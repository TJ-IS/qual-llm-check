# Alternative Designs in Information System Development

By: William R. King

## Abstract

The concept of creating and considering alternative designs in the system development process is integral to design methodology in many fields. In MIS the idea is little implemented despite the existence of some techniques that facilitate the consideration of alternative designs. Some of these techniques are reviewed and evaluated in terms of their utility in MIS.

Keywords: MIS design process, alternative designs, MIS development
ACM Categories: K.61, K.63

One of the steps in prescriptive approaches to the design of complex systems is the development and evaluation of alternative systems designs. The concept of “alternative designs” is well established in engineering and other technical design fields. For instance, Middendorf [28] specifies the development of “... possible alternative design concepts which are likely to meet the specifications” as one of the key phases of engineering design.

Implicit in the notion of alternative systems designs is the process of evaluating the alternatives to find the “best,” or an “acceptable,” design. Gibson [13] portrays this as an iterative process in which alternative designs are developed, compared, and evaluated to determine the general design that is best.

In architecture, a design field with which most of us are familiar, there is an increasing tendency to require formal alternative designs for major projects. Indeed, in this context, the best design is often formally chosen from among several submitted by competing firms [30].

Although the idea of constructing and evaluating alternative system designs has a sound theoretical and practical basis and is applied in some design fields, the principle is often ignored in Information Systems (IS) design situations [8]. Even such methodologies as IBM's well regarded Business Systems Planning (BSP) [15] produce neither the specifications for alternative designs nor a basis for evaluating such alternatives. The major reasons for this omission probably include the added cost that is involved in explicitly creating and evaluating alternative designs, the behavioral and organizational complexities involved in using alternative designs, and the paucity of tested systematic methods for describing and evaluating the alternative designs.

This paper briefly reviews the first two of these reasons for the possible lack of use of formal “alternative designs” in IS — the added cost and organizational factors — and then focuses on reviewing and comparing methods that have been developed that make use of alternative designs in the IS context. The comparisons are made in terms of a variety of measures related to the specificity and practicality of the method and its applicability in the system life cycle as well as its cost and organizational implications. Because IS designers and developers have made limited use of normative alternative design concepts, this comparative evaluation may motivate increased use in appropriate IS situations.

## The Cost of Alternative Designs

There is substantial cost incurred in developing any single proposed design for a complex system. The development of alternative designs necessarily multiplies those costs. The process of considering alternatives may also substantially stretch out the schedule for a systems development project.

If system alternatives are considered only in the conceptual stage of the system life cycle $[24]$ , the added costs are not likely to be as great as if alternatives are considered at the later “general design” stage. If alternatives are formally considered subsequently at the detailed design stage, when “blueprints” for hardware and software are being developed, the added cost of considering alternatives is likely to be even greater.

On a cost basis alone, it is not surprising that alternative system designs are often not made explicit in the process of developing an IS. Yet, if the benefits that might be realized from the use of formal alternative designs are sufficiently great, these added costs may be outweighed by the benefits that are gained. However, since those incremental benefits cannot be easily assessed in advance, it should not be unexpected that the tangible additional costs of alternative designs would often be allowed to dominate the associated, and often intangible, incremental benefits $[4]$ .

## Behavioral and Organizational Factors Inhibiting the Use of Alternative Design Approaches

Since any creative process such as system design incorporates elements of art, as well as of science, the systems designer may be motivated to preserve the mystique that characterizes the individual domain of expertise — the process of translating a complex set of user requirements into a suitable system design. The utilization of alternative designs implies that the user will play an important role in the evaluation of, and selection among, the alternatives. This user involvement serves to clarify the important tradeoffs that must be made in comparing alternatives and pierces the veil of mystique surrounding the details of the creative phase of the systems design process.

A single design, or “rifle shot” approach [8], gives the user the power to accept or reject the design, but not to consider more interesting and meaningful alternatives. Thus, the single design approach has the critical logical flaw that is inherent in any “go/no go” choice [3]. The result is that real design decision making power is retained by the designer with the single design approach, whereas it is shared with the user to a greater degree in the “alternative designs” approach.

With all that has been written about “user involvement” in IS design-development processes, the actual level of such involvement is not yet great [16]. One of the reasons for this may be that alternative designs are not integral elements of the IS development process, since the proper use of meaningful alternative designs must necessarily serve to enhance user involvement.

## A Review of Systematic Alternative Design Methods

Alternative IS designs — be they at the “policy,” “general design,” or “detailed design” levels — have many different characteristics that make them difficult to compare. The lack of a systematic method for describing alternative system designs in commensurate terms so that they may be logically compared necessitates the use of human judgment as a major factor in comparing alternative designs. However much judgment may be essential to this process, a variety of formal “alternative design” methods do exist.

This article focuses on a number of those approaches to the use of alternative designs in IS development. The methods are subsequently compared and evaluated in terms of their specificity, practicality, applicability to various phases of the system life cycle, the degree to which each influences added cost, and organizational impact.

## Budget based alternative designs

The difficulties that may result from a failure to develop alternative designs when one is faced with a budgetary constraint have been effectively described by Enthoven [10] in the context of building a home. He describes how a “requirements” approach, such as is often used in IS design, can lead to difficulties.

Suppose that I want to buy a house and, . . . I do it in the . . . traditional way. First, I determine my housing requirements without any consideration of costs. I count up the rooms I require: I need a bedroom for myself, one for each of my children, and one for my parents or other guests who come to visit us occasionally. I need a study because I occasionally bring some of my work home with me and need a quiet place to work. My wife needs a sewing room. I need a pool in the basement because my doctor has told me that I must swim every day if I don't want to have another operation on my back. Now, you might laugh when I say that I have to have a pool in my basement, but I can validate that requirement. I can argue for it very convincingly. I can produce a Doctor's certificate, and you can't prove to me that I don't need that pool. Moreover, I work at the Pentagon and I work long hours. Therefore, I need to live within five minutes drive of the Pentagon. When I put this all together, I find that I have established a requirement for a house that costs a hundred thousand dollars. Having done that, I review my financial situation and find that I am only able to spend about \$30,000. So what do I do? If I am operating under the old concept, I take the \$100,000 design and I slice off 70 percent of it and what's left is my house.

Now, clearly that's not a very sensible way to design a house. I might find that I left off the bathroom, or included the bathroom, but left off the plumbing that is required to make it work.

The requirements oriented single design approach can lead to difficulties in the IS area as well. For instance, when information systems are designed on the basis of the stated “requirements” or “needs” of users, the requirements are often overstated and run afoul of budget constraints. In one such situation, when cutbacks in stated requirements became necessary, a marketing executive was forced to incorporate only a three digit SIC (Standard Industrial Classification) code for each customer on invoice records. Despite the greater level of specificity provided by a five digit SIC code which was necessary for the primary purpose for which he had requested the information in the first place, the cutback to three digits was enforced because overall data “requirements” exceeded the available budget [20].

The net result of this process was the incorporation of a nearly useless three digit code on every invoice record. The apparent mindlessness of this is made somewhat more understandable when one realizes that the total stated requirements could not be provided under the budgetary constraint and that top management had to develop some mechanism for resolving the dilemma. When they perceived the only alternative to this overall cutback in the data requirements for all users to be a long process of negotiation over the legitimacy of each of the (possibly overstated) requirements, they adopted the simple approach of an across the board cut.

Enthoven [10] suggests in the context of his “homey” example how several design alternatives can be used in a budget based approach that avoids these problems.

The rational economic way of buying a house, . . . is to consider alternative balanced programs, each of which yields the most effectiveness possible within a budget that corresponds approximately to the availability of our resources. If I think that I have about \$30,000 to spend on a house, I should consider several alternative houses each optimized for my purposes within financial limits such as \$28,000, \$30,000, \$32,000, and perhaps \$34,000 and then I should ask myself whether the extra advantages associated with the more expensive houses are worth the extra financial sacrifices I would have to make to pay for one. It's altogether possible that they might be. For example, a larger house might have a recreation room, and this might enable me to economize elsewhere on recreation.

Thus, several alternatives constructed around the design that meets the budget “bogey” can serve as a useful guide to system design decision making. This approach preserves the design prerogative of the analyst, since the analyst produces several designs rather than one. However, it serves to highlight the available design tradeoffs and to maintain budgetary realism while providing the user with a basis for applying judgment in design decisions. This applies equally well in the IS context, and is probably far superior to the single design approach in which the analyst makes design tradeoffs on an implicit judgmental basis that is unknown to the user.

## The idealized design approach

Ackoff has discussed the notion of “idealized design” in a variety of contexts $[1, 2]$ . The idealized design notion is one of initially developing a systems design that one would like to have immediately available. Thus, the design is not an idealized system in the extreme sense, but rather one that is technologically feasible, operationally viable, and capable of being adapted and improved. “It is the system with which its designers would currently replace the system planned for if they were free to replace it with any system they wanted” $[2, p. 107]$ . The idealized design thus represents both an unconstrained goal and a standard against which alternative proposed designs may be assessed.

## The consensus LRC design approach

King and Cleland [22] have developed and used a related design notion in the IS context. This approach utilizes a variation of the linear responsibility chart (LRC) as a device to describe three alternative general designs for an IS. The alternative designs used in this approach are:

1. a normative design that prescribed how a desired system should operate (on the basis of theory, expert opinion, and other relevant prescriptions),

2. a descriptive design that describes how the existing system actually operates in terms that are directly comparable to the normative design, and

3. a consensus design that evolves from the other two as they are assessed and discussed by potential users.

The LRC model represents one useful and convenient way to depict these three alternative designs in terms that are directly comparable and understandable. The LRC may be used to describe authorities, responsibilities, initiation patterns and information flows within an organization, and between the organization and its environment, for each of a variety of subtasks in each of a variety of decision making areas.

The descriptive and normative models — described using the LRC or in some other way — are presented to the intended system users and are used as a basis for the development, through discussion and negotiation, of a “consensus” model. This consensus model, when refined, is that which is intended to form the basis for the actual design of the new system.

Of course, a model such as that of Table 1 is not an IS design. However, once a consensus general design has been agreed on, the process of developing a detailed design can be undertaken in a more traditional fashion.

This consensus LRC approach is applicable only to the general design phase of the IS life cycle and it does not have the financial discipline of the budgetary approach. It is possible for the consensus design to be totally at odds with available funds. However, in the uses made of it, the participation of designers, policy level managers, and operating managers in the consensus seeking process have served to incorporate fiscal reality into the consensus.

The highly participative consensus approach does promote the development of alternative designs that are, and will be perceived to be realistic. It avoids designs that are “technically optimal,” but which are not accepted by users and used by them in the manner intended [19].

Ackoff indicates that the benefits to be derived from “idealized design” notions and which probably apply as well to approaches such as the consensus LRC method of King and Cleland [22] include user participation, the incorporation of stylistic preferences into the design, consensus with consequent commitment of participants, the stimulation of creativity, and the enlargement of the designer’s conception of what can be implemented [2, pp. 115-122].

## Iterative design processes

While the budgetary, idealized design and LRC consensus approaches involve the creation of alternative designs, they each do so only at a single point in the systems development life cycle. Elam [9] has proposed an iterative process in which an “initial operational capability” and a “complete operational capability” for the IS are defined in two cycles. Thus, the alternative designs are developed sequentially rather than simultaneously.

Lucas [26] has proposed an “evolutionary design” process in which the number of alternative sequential designs is unlimited. The initial design takes the form of a simple model, dummy report, or simulated system that is reacted to by the users, thus permitting another iteration toward the desired system.

Prototyping is another version of an iterative design process since it involves the creation of an initial design in the form of a simple working system that is expected to be enhanced, altered, and extended. Edleman [8] has described RCA's use of prototyping. Systems described by King and Rodriguez [24] and Dutta and King [7] are prototypes that were developed in a university setting to meet the needs of two different business firms. The prototypes enabled users to develop a "feel" for the system, how it would work, and what its capabilities would be, without incurring the expense of developing an operational scale system.

Davis and Taylor [6] have described the use of a “game-system simulator” that is another form of sequential use of alternative designs. The simulator is used to refine the specifications for the actual system to be developed. System users participate in playing an abstract game that represents the application and the tentative system solution. Just as in prototyping, the potential system users can identify both their salient needs and deficiencies in the proposed design through use of the simulator.

## Multi attribute approaches to alternative designs

The development and assessment of alternative system configurations may be accomplished by describing alternative designs in terms of a set of relatively abstract attributes. These attributes may reflect either system characteristics or characteristics of the information output of the alternative systems.

Martin [27] describes how an algebraic model of system operations may be used to identify alternative system designs. This is done at a micro level by identifying those system variables in the model that are subject to control by the designers.

For instance, a simple data processing system model might contain variables such as:

\- operating time,

\- operating rate,

\- operating method, and

\- capacity.

Each of these system characteristics may be thought of as potentially taking on various values or levels. Thus, the combination of all variables, each at a prescribed level, constitutes an abstract representation of a potential system. Various alternatives are readily generated for assessment and comparison.

Another approach to the creation of alternative system designs has been described and preliminarily tested in field studies by Epstein and King [11]. This approach uses abstract system designs that are described in terms of combinations of levels of various attributions describing the information that the alternative systems designs would produce. Potential users are asked to respond to the system descriptions by indicating their relative preferences for each.

In an experimental application in two firms, managers were asked to evaluate alternative system designs, each of which was described in terms of a series of phrases that reflected levels of attributes that have been found to reflect “information value” [4]. For instance, one proposed design might be described as:

\- produces ABC report infrequently (monthly)

\- after a delay of one week

\- at moderate cost

\- most data are quantitative, etc.

An alternative design might reflect a greater frequency, less delay, greater cost, less quantitative content, etc.

In the experimental study, managers were able to express preferences among the alternative designs, even in cases where no clear logical preference (dominance) existed. This suggests that the notion of describing alternative systems in abstract multi dimensional terms that reflect either system characteristics $[27]$ or characteristics of the information output $[11]$ may facilitate the meaningful involvement of potential users in assessing alternative designs at an early stage in the design process.

## Policy approach to alternative designs

A macro level “policy” approach to system development using alternative designs has been developed by King [17] and described by King and Epstein [23]. The “policy” approach to the comparison of alternative designs is based on the idea of a set of alternative models which may be developed to represent a decision problem. Models — both those of the formal and “mental” variety — have been found to be appropriate mechanisms for specifying information “requirements” in terms of performance measures, predictive measures, model solution characteristics, functional relationships, and auxiliary information such as sensitivity measures [21].

To demonstrate this approach, consider the examples of six decision models, each of which relates a particular decision performance measure from the set $\{y\}$ , to various predictor measures $\{x\}$ , using one of a set of functional relationships, $\{f\}$ .

$$
y _ {1} = f _ {1} \left(x _ {1}, x _ {2}\right)\tag{1}
$$

$$
y _ {1} = f _ {2} (x _ {1}, x _ {2}, x _ {3})\tag{2}
$$

$$
y _ {2} = f _ {3} (x _ {1}, x _ {4})\tag{3}
$$

$$
y _ {2} = f _ {4} (x _ {1})\tag{4}
$$

$$
y _ {2} = f _ {5} (x _ {1}, x _ {2}, x _ {4})\tag{5}
$$

$$
y _ {2} = f _ {6} (x _ {1}, x _ {2}, x _ {3}, x _ {4})\tag{6}
$$

In the above expressions, $y_{1}$ and $y_{2}$ represent alternative “reasonable” performance measures, such as “profit” and “return on investment,” that might be applied in a decision situation. The x’s are predictor variables that may be combined in various ways in decision models that are to be used to predict that performance.

For instance, the six models might be various regression models that have been developed for the same decision situation using two different dependent variables ( $y_{1}$ and $y_{2}$ ) and various combinations of independent variables ( $x_{1}$ , $x_{2}$ , $x_{3}$ , and $x_{4}$ ). Alternately, the six models might be of different varieties — e.g., break-even, discounted cash flow, and payback models — that may be applied to the same decision situation. These are represented by the various functional relationships designated by subscripted f's.

The performance measure, predictor variables, and functional relationships in each model prescribe a unique set of information “requirements.”\* For instance, Model #1 prescribes a set of information requirements:

$$
\{y _ {1}; x _ {1}, x _ {2}; f _ {1} \}
$$

and Model #2 prescribes the set,

$$
\{y _ {1}; x _ {1}, x _ {2}, x _ {3}; f _ {2} \}
$$

The “policy” approach to IS design is based on the premise that these model-based information sets can be used to define other information sets which can, in turn, be used as a basis for making information system policy-level design choices $[25]$ .

A decision model information set taxonomy, which provides the basis for this approach, has four elements:

Relevant Set
Sufficient Set
Necessary Set
Essential Set

The information sets which make up the policy information sets are developed directly from the decision model based information sets. Here, these sets are defined verbally and are illustrated using the decision models (1) - (6). The basis for the definitions are subsequently described.

The Relevant Set (R) — the logical union of the information sets prescribed by each of the models under consideration for a decision problem or set of such problems. In the illustration, this set is:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \mathsf {R} = & \{\mathsf {y} _ {1}, \mathsf {y} _ {2}; \mathsf {x} _ {1}, \mathsf {x} _ {2}, \mathsf {x} _ {3}, \mathsf {x} _ {4}; \mathsf {f} _ {1}, \mathsf {f} _ {2}, \mathsf {f} _ {3}, \mathsf {f} _ {4}, \\ & \mathsf {f} _ {5}, \mathsf {f} _ {6} \} \end{array}
$$

The Sufficient Set(S) — the information set that is associated with the best model (as assessed in cost-benefit terms [19]). In this case, assume that Model #5 has been determined to be the most appropriate model for the decision situation. The sufficient set is then:

$$
S = \{y _ {2}; x _ {1}, x _ {2}, x _ {4}; f _ {5} \}
$$

The Necessary Set (N) — the information set prescribed by the lowest cost of those models that utilizes the appropriate performance measure. In this illustration, since the best model, (#5), has defined $y_{2}$ as the appropriate performance measure, Model #4 is likely to be the lowest cost of all of those models involving $y_{2}$ . Hence,

$$
N = \{y _ {2}; x _ {1}; f _ {4} \}.
$$

The Essential Set (E) — the information set associated with the lowest cost model which utilizes the “right” performance measure and also meets some prescribed level of predictive accuracy. In this case, suppose that Model #3 meets such a criterion (e.g., $R^{2} \geq 0.5$ for the case of a regression model). Then, $E = \{y_{2}; x_{1}, x_{4}; f_{4}\}$ .

These policy information set definitions have great intuitive appeal. “Relevant” information is referred to in much of the literature of MIS but it is rarely explicitly and operationally defined. These set definitions serve to operationally define “relevance” in decision model terms that conform to the dictionary definition of “relevant” as “bearing upon on applying to the case in hand; pertinent.” This definition would also appear to be consistent with that used in the American Accounting Association’s Statement of Basic Accounting Theory [4].

The Sufficient Set is based on the model that is chosen to be best for some specific application. If the application is a predictive one, the best model may be determined in terms of predictive accuracy. In other situations, some general cost/benefit tradeoff, in model terms, rather than in informational terms, is typically applied to the choice of a best model $[29]$ .

Assuming that a best model has been selected, the Necessary Set prescribes a standard that can be used for comparative purposes. The Essential Set is associated with the use of a cost minimization approach in the sense of requiring at least some minimal level of effectiveness for the decision model, and therefore, for the supporting model based information set.

Alternative general systems designs can be constructed on the basis of this information set taxonomy. For instance, one such alternative system design is that which is made up of the “data” subset of the Relevant Set, and the “software” subset of the Necessary Set. This alternative ( $A_{1}$ ) can be described in the context of the example as:

$$
A _ {1} = \{y _ {1}, y _ {2}; x _ {1}, x _ {2}, x _ {3}, x _ {4}; f _ {5} \},
$$

which specifies the appropriate “performance information” ( $y_{1}$ and $y_{2}$ ), “predictor” information ( $x_{1}$ , $x_{2}$ , $x_{3}$ and $x_{4}$ ) and “software” ( $f_{5}$ ).

Once a range of alternative designs such as $A_{1}$ have been created as a part of the IS design process, assessments may be based on qualitative and quantitative explications of the benefits, limitations, general cost considerations, and design strategy implications of the various alternatives. For instance, for design alternative $A_{1}$ , the incorporation of the “data” portion of the Relevant Set implies that great flexibility will be possible in terms of dealing with new decisions and developing new methods and models for dealing with decision problems. The development of revised regression models which incorporate new predictor variables would be greatly facilitated by the inclusion of the relevant data set rather than some more limited data set, for example. However, since the Sufficient software set has been included in $A_{1}$ , the cost of switching to different software, such as a discriminant model, would be relatively greater than it would be if the Relevant software set had also been in the design alternative $A_{1}$ .

In these terms, the illustrative system design, $A_{1}$ , is thereby characterized by great data flexibility with relatively lesser software flexibility. Such a general design alternative could rather easily be compared at the policy level with other alternative general designs.

The wide variety of general IS designs that can be constructed from the policy information set taxonomy can be assessed in terms of the costs and benefits associated with each. For instance, one alternative might be assessed to provide maximum flexibility in adapting to prospective demands for data created by new management personnel. The cost of this flexibility could then be assessed, if not in specific dollar terms, at least in terms that are comparable to other alternatives. This provides a sound basis for the judgmental evaluation of alternative designs at the policy level.

## Comparing Alternative Design Approaches

Table 2 shows the various alternative design approaches that have been discussed. They are evaluated in terms of their level of specificity, their degree of practicality, their appropriateness to various stages of the system development life cycle, their incremental cost (as contrasted with a single design approach), and the role played by users.

Although the budget based approach is primarily conceptual, it is practical and can be applied at the general design stage with modest added cost. However, users operate solely in a reactive mode as they do in a single design approach. With this approach, they react to a number of alternatives rather than to just one. Hence, its organizational impact is small.

In contrast, the concensus LRC approach specifies a detailed design methodology, is also practical, and is applicable to a somewhat different phase of the life cycle (depending on precisely how it is used). However, its added cost is high because users participate extensively in the design of the (concensus) system.

## Summary

Alternative system designs are a fundamental element of the normative processes of IS design and development. However, they are often omitted from real world IS development projects.

The omission is probably due to the added cost of alternative designs, the behavioral and organizational complexities of developing alternatives, and the lack of tested methods for using alternative designs.

This article reviews and compares a variety of design approaches that use alternative design concepts. The various approaches differ in their level of specificity, practicality, appropriateness to various stages of the system development life cycle, cost, and organizational impact. Each of the approaches has the theoretical potential to lead to a more effective system than would otherwise be developed. Some of the approaches appear to be potential mechanisms for achieving greater user involvement in system design. If such involvement is indeed of system use, these approaches may lead to systems that are both more effective and more used.

Each of these approaches has advantages and disadvantages and each might be useful in a given organization for a given IS design objective. In total, they represent a set of tools that may prove useful in better achieving the benefits of systematically designed systems that serve the needs of IS users.
