# Personality Characteristics of MIS Project Teams: An Empirical Study and Action-Research Design $^{1}$

By: Kate M. Kaiser
Robert P. Bostrom

## Abstract

Information systems for large firms are typically designed by a team comprised of both users and systems personnel. The Management Information System (MIS) literature discusses a communication gap between the organization oriented users and the more technical systems staff.

It is often hypothesized that systems personnel and users are different in terms of personality and behavior characteristics and that these differences are one of the primary reasons for the existence of a communication gap. This article summarizes a two-phased study. The first phase investigated personality characteristics of respondents from thirty-two large organizations who worked on design teams. The second phase examines, in detail, a system success and failure in one organization. Analysis was performed to see if there are significant differences on personality dimensions between users and systems personnel and to explore the relationship between these differences and system success. An operationalization of Jung's personality typology (Myers-Briggs Type Indicator) was employed.

The results show that the users involved in the systems design are very similar to their systems counterparts. Even more surprising is that the characteristics of these users are closer to the popular descriptions of systems staff than the analysts are. They also suggest that these similarities in personality types may have an impact on system success. The general implications of these findings in terms of the management of project teams and the MIS designs they create are discussed.

Keywords: Information systems, cognitive style, socio-technical systems, communication gap, MIS design, user involvement, action-research

ACM Categories: H.1.2, J.1, K.3.2, K.4.3, K.6.1, K.7

When firms develop information systems, at least two parties are involved in the process — the user-manager(s) and the systems analyst/programmer(s). This group will be termed “the solving center.” $^{2}$ The Management Information Systems (MIS) literature has discussed the existence of a communication gap between these parties [2, 4, 6, 9, 11, 15, 16, 18, 20, 40, 41, 43, 48, 56, 57, 66]. This gap can be broadly summarized with two general observations:

1. Users tend to have a broader organizational view when solving problems. They have more difficulty expressing information requirements in the structured form that is necessary for computerization.

2. Analysts tend to deal with problems in a logical framework, in part because they are aware of the requirements of the computer that will be used as an aid in solving these problems. The systems staff rarely understands or incorporates the consideration of organizational objectives and may be more interested in technical concerns.

These differences expressed in Figure 1 are not trivial. MIS projects are considerable investments in a firm's resources.

The failure to establish a semantic bridge between the different ways system personnel and users conceptualize situations may explain many system failures [23, 57]. Many of the new system development techniques are effectively attacking this problem. For example, Bostrom and Nault [12] suggest that the largest single benefit of the new structured design techniques, e.g., data flow diagrams, is the improvement in user and analyst communication via the development of a shared logical model of the system. Better mechanisms, whether they are analysis or management techniques, should be developed, or existing mechanisms improved through a better understanding of the differences that exist between system personnel and users.

There are a number of characteristics that have been suggested which may affect the success of the design process $[7, 14, 17, 21, 23, 24, 35, 55, 62, 65]$ . Many of the general characteristics mentioned in these works also relate to individual differences $[65]$ . The differences in the way users and analysts approach their role in a systems design may be a function of the kind of work they perform. However, one characteristic that might explain these different views is the focus of this article — personality.

## Individual Differences

Personality characteristics are often mentioned in the MIS popular and research literature as reflective of the differences between users and systems analysts [17, 25, 37, 41, 44, 66]. Either the skills required in each setting may attract individuals with particular personality characteristics, or the tasks that are performed in a user area or in a systems role may cause these characteristics to be developed or reinforced. This study does not explore this causal aspect but discusses the variance between the two settings.

Others have noted that there appears to be variance in cognitive styles with regard to management decision making in general $[22, 30, 36, 47, 49, 54, 59]$ and in particular with regard to designing information systems $[1, 3, 5, 8, 26, 45, 60, 64, 65, 66]$ . Though there is much conjecture that systems people are analytical and users are more heuristic in their approach to problem solving, Zmud $[66]$ points out that “empirical evidence is lacking.” “Analytic” and “heuristic” are used here to capsulize the dimensions noted earlier.

## Objectives of the Research

Because of the lack of empirical evidence, the first step is to test the hypothesis that systems people and users are different with respect to specific personality characteristics that are manifested when working on a common task. If there are significant differences, one can then investigate whether these differences are related to the quality of a design. The next step would be to see if these characteristics are a causal factor in MIS success or failure. This study addresses primarily the research question:

Are users and systems staff significantly different on personality characteristics that relate to design activities, e.g., problem solving?

The relationship between differences and systems success are also explored. The implications of both investigations to other research issues are discussed. No causal factors are investigated.

The research effort was conducted in two phases. The second phase, the action research, was designed as a consequence of the findings of the first phase, the empirical study.

## Jungian Typology and the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator

The personality typology used to classify users and systems personnel is that of C. G. Jung [33]. The Jungian typology is chosen for the following reasons:

1. the dimensions of the Jungian typology correspond to the differences between users and system personnel described earlier and hence provide an appropriate basis for comparison,

2. four of the Jungian personality types relate directly to different managerial and organizational styles and therefore can be used to compare organizational types as well as personality types [50],

3. the Jungian typology does not prescribe any type as superior or better to any of the others but instead points out that each type has major strengths as well as weaknesses, and

4. the Jungian typology has been successfully operationalized in the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator [52].

Similar dimensions measured by other popular instruments were investigated, particularly those used in cognitive style research. Robey and Taggart [54] discuss the strengths, weaknesses, and uses of these instruments, including the Myers-Briggs. As a result of their criticisms of these tools and for the reasons outlined above, the Jungian typology as operationalized in the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI) was chosen. $^{3}$

Jung [33] described three different dimensions of personality:

1. the way people relate to the world,

2. the way information is taken in, and

3. the way information is processed, i.e., decision making style.

The first dimension looks at the continuum from those who are drawn to the inner world of ideas — introverts — to those who are drawn to the outer world of people and things — extroverts. Secondly, perception describes the way people take in information. This is done by either using the five senses and relying on facts and data — sensing — or by conceptualizing with the “sixth sense” and little data — intuition. The last dimension is judgmental. People draw conclusions about the information they process either by using logic and analysis — thinking — or by using emotion and value judgments — feeling. Jung suggests that people have a tendency toward one side of each dimension and that people have a tendency toward taking information in perception or processing that information — judgment.

The MBTI, therefore, operationalizes these constructs and gives scores for a respondent on eight dimensions:

(E) Extroversion (EI Preference) (I) Introversion

(S) Sensing (SN Preference) (N) Intuition

(T) Thinking (TF Preference) (F) Feeling

(J) Judgment (JP Preference) (P) Perception

The eight dimensions are viewed in terms of continuums. Individuals are typed by the predominant dimension along each continuum, i.e., their preference along the continuum. For example, if one prefers the thinking mode over a feeling (higher score on thinking) mode of processing information, one is typed as a thinker (T). An individual's psychological type is the result of a combination of preferences, which can be stated for convenience in four letters. For example, ISTJ means an introvert preferring sensing and thinking and a mainly judging attitude toward the world.

It is important to recognize that the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator measures only preference toward a certain type. If one has a high score on one dimension, the other dimension on the continuum is viewed as complementary. MBTI does not necessarily indicate that one is deficient with regard to a particular dimension if one receives a low score; it only indicates a preference for the complementary score.

Use of the Myers-Briggs in management research has been minimal relative to its use in education and medicine [52]. Mason and Mitroff [45] suggested the four types as a useful indicator for determining characteristics of an information system's users. Differences in personality types for managers generating stories about management were described by Mitroff and Kilmann [50]. DeWaele [19] suggests the Jungian typology for the design of decision aids for marketing managers. Henderson and Nutt [28] found relationships to type and risk decision behavior in upper management. Alavi and Henderson [1] found relationships between decision style and utilization of a model based information system. Keen [36, 37] and McKenney and Keen [47] used the MBTI to describe management decision making styles. No significant difference with respect to personality and the performance of Information Analysts (IA) was found by Kaiser [34] when using the Myers-Briggs. Jacoby [32] has utilized the instrument with accountants and Hoy and Vaught [29] surveyed entrepreneurs. A sample of management consultants (of which $30\%$ were in information systems) was collected by Wade [61]. From an MIS perspective these studies report on mixed and homogeneous user populations or mixed user and systems population without noting differences between the groups. Gingras [26] performed the only study that investigated differences between users and the MIS staff. He found differences between users and systems personnel only on the thinking-feeling dimension. However, the reliability and validity of his instrument is questionable. He used a twelve-item version of the MBTI which correlated with the 126-item (Form G) version. The MBTI does not have an extensive history of use in business settings, but its use is growing. Keen and Bronsema [38] offer a thorough analysis of the MBTI and conclude that it “offer(s) an excellent solution” for studying cognitive style differences between managers and analysts. Their hypotheses are tested in this study.

## Phase One: The Empirical Study

## Methodology

MIS directors from 120 large private firms were requested by mail to participate in the first phase of this study. An interview was conducted with respondents in which systems managers in thirty-eight firms identified one project in their firm. This project was selected according to the following criteria:

budget over \$100,000,

presence of a user on the design team,

recently implemented (within six months to three years), and

inclusion of operating management as users.

The systems manager then distributed a questionnaire including the MBTI to those users and analysts who had participated in the selected projects. The managers knew that personality data was to be collected but did not know why. User response therefore was limited only to those users who were remembered by the systems manager as being a part of the design team or solving center. This may or may not have included most end users depending on how the firm or systems department structures the solving center.

A 75% response rate (100 of 134 instruments were returned from thirty-two of the firms) consisted of users and systems personnel distinguished by department of reporting supervisor. The instrument was mailed back to one of the researchers so that respondents could complete it at their leisure. To insure confidentiality no names were used; a numerical code was an identifier. To increase response, feedback about MBTI scores and the total sample was provided for those who completed the questionnaire.

## Hypotheses

The empirical study's basic objective is to test the hypothesis that systems people and users are different with respect to the specific dimensions of the Jungian typology. The particular focus of the study is on those dimensions most likely to produce conflicts if differences exist. The statistical test is to see if the sampling distribution of the users' scores is identical to the distribution of scores for the systems personnel.

The hypotheses concerning the basic personality dimensions are as follows:

H1: Systems personnel are more introverted than users. This hypothesis is based primarily on the research of Couger and Zawacki [17]. They found systems personnel have the lowest need for interaction of any profession.

H2: Users tend to be more intuitive than systems personnel. Although this is the popular conception portrayed in the MIS literature, there is very little research data to support it.

H3: Users tend to prefer the feeling dimension in processing information while systems personnel prefer the thinking dimension. Again, this is the popular notion conveyed in the literature, but only the study by Gingras [26] mentioned earlier provides support.

H4: Users tend to emphasize collecting data in (perception) while systems staff focus on processing information (judgment). Although there has been no discussion of these dimensions in the MIS literature, the analyst's role seems to have a stronger judgmental component.

As the above four hypotheses indicate, differences are expected on all four continuums.

For the purposes of this study, the middle two dimensions (sensing-intuition S-N, thinking-feeling T-F) were of special importance. The S-N dimension corresponds to the kind of “input mode” an individual prefers while the T-F corresponds to an individual’s “decision making process” preference in processing input data. Combining the two data input modes with the two decision making modes in all possible ways results in the following four Jungian personality types: sensing-thinking (ST); intuition-thinking (NT); sensing-feeling (SF); and intuition-feeling (NF). These types are illustrated and elaborated in Figure 2.

The advantage of this four type Jungian classification is that it helps make clear the conflicts between individuals. As discussed later, it can also be used to make the conflicts in organizational designs more clear. The ST and NF types are opposed to one another since they are based upon conflicting preferences for information and decision making styles $[51]$ . In the same way NT and SF are opposite to one another. The conflicts are readily apparent from a comparison of the characteristics listed in Figure 2. These conflicts are expected to be found in solving centers. The specific hypothesis is stated below:

H5: Users would show a greater occurrence of NF and SF types while systems personnel would show strong ST and NT orientations.

The Chi-square test of statistical significance was used to test for differences in user and system distribution of types.

## Phase Two: The Action-Research Design

The lack of feeling (F) types and similarities between users and systems staff in the solving center raised several questions. This caused an investigation of other variables. When investigating job level, it was discovered that only two of the 100 users were clerical. This is especially interesting since most of the systems are transaction processing ones where it is unlikely that a manager is the only end user. If there are clerical end users for these systems, they were not included in the solving center. The two clerical people are both Fs. Two related questions emerge: 1) do user populations, especially clerical, contain a higher percentage of Fs than this sample?, and 2) are user representatives on a design team more “system oriented” than the typical end user?

In order to investigate these questions, a second phase was added to the study. A case study was carried out on the implementation of an integrated Human Resource Information System in a large multi-campus university. The system was integrated in that it provides information to multiple departments and functions on each campus from a single database. The university had made four attempts to implement this system over a thirteen year period; the first three were failures, the last one a success. The last failure employed a database oriented design approach utilizing a well known database management system. This effort cost approximately one million dollars. This system was implemented in the fall of 1978 and replaced by the first phase of the successful effort in July 1980. One of the authors was involved as an action researcher in both the audit of the last failure and in the implementation of the successful system. The analysis examined personality characteristics of users and system personnel involved in the last failure (referred to from this point as the failure effort) and those involved in the successful system implementation. The reason for investigating both the success and the failure was to explore the relationship between personality differences and system success.

## Reasons for failure

Although there were many reasons for the failure effort, a few stand out (see Bostrom [10] for details).

The effort was viewed primarily as a technical change; the focus, therefore, was on the computer based system. Little attention was paid to organizational and social/people problems. The effort developed an integrative system without an integrative organization (e.g., clear interdepartmental roles and relationships) to operate and manage the system. The major problems of the environment were organizationally based and a technical solution would not solve them. In fact, the technical solution merely highlighted them and made them worse.

The computer based system was poorly designed: the online and batch user interfaces were designed primarily from a technical designer's perspective (e.g., efficiency) rather than the needs of the users (human factors considerations, etc.).

The initial information requirements were inaccurate and incomplete. Modifications to correct initial requirement deficiencies were very difficult. The deficiencies in requirements were due primarily to inadequate user involvement and poor analysis by user analysts and system personnel. In terms of involvement all relevant users were not represented and the systems personnel were not working with the end users of the system. The majority of end users were clerical and supervisory personnel. The users represented in the solving center were 1-3 levels above these end users. User analysts, systems oriented user personnel that were organizationally housed in the user area, also represented users on the project team. The poor analysis was the result of the users not having good conceptual models of their work systems. Therefore, they had difficulty in articulating their needs. Instead of helping users build good models, systems personnel and user analysts would get initial ideas from users and then systems personnel would construct the system with little feedback to users. When questions would arise, the systems staff would tend to fill in from their models rather than elicit information from users. The final models embedded in the system were, therefore, more reflective of systems personnel and user analysts' perspectives.

The project team was not managed very well. In particular, the role relationships between users, user analysts, and systems personnel were not clear or well developed.

The major reasons for failure can be summarized in one general observation: the missing ingredient in the solving center was an integrated or balanced problem solving perspective. A technical perspective was adopted where a socio-technical one was needed. In addition, the needs and viewpoints of an unrepresentative set of users and user analysts were primary inputs into the design process, when input from a more representative set of users' perspectives was needed (clerical end users). The successful implementation effort drew from these mistakes and incorporated procedures to insure a more integrative/balanced perspective.

## Reasons for success

The Socio-Technical Systems (STS) design methodology was utilized to insure equal attention to technical and social (people, structure, etc.) systems issues $[10, 17]$ . A prototyping design approach was utilized to implement a purchased software package. The prototype approach, combined with the use of structured design methods, allowed the development of good user models and needs statements.

The structure of the solving center included a steering committee and a number of project design teams. The higher level indirect users who were designers in the failure effort became members of a steering committee that made decisions on policy, resources, and organizational issues and monitored the activities of the project teams. Users and user analysts were selected for design teams to insure adequate representation of end users.

The selection of systems personnel, users, and user analysts to be members of the design team was based on their relative technical and social skills. The selection criteria were to maintain a balance of good technical and social skills on the team. This was accomplished by having people that processed both types of skills and a combination that excelled in one or the other.

The Jungian framework was applied to the development/training of the project team. $^{4}$ The newly formed project team was given the Myers-Briggs instrument. The personality type information was shared, allowing project team members to examine their underlying values, assumptions, and the strengths/weaknesses of their problem perspectives. This type of sharing helped create a climate for integrative problem solving.

## Results

The consensus of the users, systems personnel, and user analysts involved was that the adoption of an integrated problem solving perspective reflected in the procedures above was critical to the success of this system. The rather lengthy background material presented above was prerequisite for adequate understanding of the analysis of personality differences of users and system personnel involved in these projects. The tables indicate the number of percentage of users (percentages in parentheses), user analysts, and system personnel exhibiting a particular type. For example, sixteen or 52% of the users were feeling (F) types, while the three user analysts on the failure effort were thinking types (Table 3). Although only twelve, or 39%, of the users were ST types, nine, or 60%, of them were on the failure design team; whereas only seven, or 33%, were involved in success design team (see Table 4).

The other two key factors were the use of the socio-technical design approach and the utilization of the Jungian framework as a team development and training tool. The findings indicate that the development of an integrative problem solving perspective is critical to successful implementation.

## Discussion

The results from the first phase of the study indicate that users are very similar to their systems counterparts on the Jungian dimensions. In fact, users appeared to be more “systems oriented,” as traditionally defined (ST type), than their systems counterparts. Due to the lack of Fs in the solving centers, one would expect it to be relatively free of conflict due to personality differences. It is, therefore, very difficult to support the contention that a communication gap exists between users and systems personnel in the solving center.

The first phase raised the question of why there has been so much conjecture in the MIS literature about the differences between these two groups. The second phase of the study produced a possible explanation. In the failure effort described, the users assigned to the solving center were not end users or representative of the end users. This implies that organizations are choosing to bridge the “communication gap” by selecting user representatives for project teams that are similar to their system counterparts. This strategy is reflected by users with job titles such as user analyst and user liaison and by the exodus of system personnel into user areas.

The findings from the case analysis would suggest that those classified as users in the first phase sample are more “system oriented” than the typical end user. This implies the lack of involvement in systems design by users who probably are most affected by information systems and who possibly are very different in personality characteristics from the users who collaborate with system personnel in developing information systems. These differences indicate that user representatives may not adequately represent the end user. It appears, therefore, that MIS departments are shifting the communication gap, conflict situation, etc., from the user representative and system person to the user representative and end user. Other explanations of the results must also be considered. This study assumes the validity not only of a Jungian typology (some psychologists would argue against this classification) but also of the operationalization of this typology by the MBTI. Some alternative explanations for the heavily skewed sample and similarity between systems staff and users are:

1. both Jungian and MBTI validity are questionable, and

2. systems staff and users are indeed different but not on the dimensions measured by the MBTI. Other studies should employ instruments that might replicate this research which looks at both systems staff and users who work together.

If one assumes that the MBTI is valid in this research setting, the research design issue is relevant for phase one. The users in this sample were chosen to participate in the research by a systems manager. This individual may have excluded users who were involved in the systems design and had contact with the solving center but not necessarily systems management.

However, based on all available information, the best explanation seems to be that only a certain type of individual represents the user department in a solving center — an individual similar to the systems staff. More research is needed, however, to substantiate this finding. The comparison between the success and failure projects suggests that an integrative problem solving perspective within the solving center is directly related to system success. Subsequent research needs to investigate ways for ensuring an integrated perspective. The three ways identified by this research were: 1) the use of the socio-technical design approach, 2) the use of the Jungian framework as a project team development and training tool, and 3) the selection of project team members to ensure a balance of Jungian personality types. It is also possible that other types of interventions might have affected success. Future research should explore other intervention strategies. In order to provide some background for these future research efforts, the following section will discuss the implications of the findings.

## Implications

The ways that MIS designers (users and system personnel in a solving center) view organizations, their members, and the function of an MIS within them are critical to a successful MIS effort $[10, 27]$ . These views are imbedded in a design methodology or approach which guides the development and implementation of an MIS. Mitroff and Kilmann $[50, 51]$ contend that the four middle types (ST, NT, NF, SF) of Jungian framework introduced earlier provide a useful scheme for characterizing the way people view organizational settings.

They argue that these four types provide a set of underlying dimensions, and hence a systematic rationale for defining the basic characteristics of different forms of organizational design and decision making. One can then speak of an ST organization or an NF information system design, etc. The characteristics outlined in Figure 2 can, therefore, be used to describe types of organizations, systems designs, and individuals. This approach provides a more individual oriented alternative than Theory X and Y [46], one more like Theory Z [53]. The important finding, however, is that a particular type (e.g., ST) of individual will prefer a similar type (ST) organization, and in a designer role, that person will attempt to create this type (ST) of organization.

Assuming that the findings are accurate, 89% of designers are STs (54%) and NTs (35%). ST organizations correspond to a design that has traditionally been called bureaucratic. NT organizations, like ST organizations, are also impersonal. However, NT designs emphasize “the global, long term goal orientation of the organization while maintaining a loosely structured role in order to accomplish these goals” [50].

In contrast, the NF organization, sometimes referred to as an organic design, “explores the global, long-term goal orientation of the organization (INTUITION), and the informal, social, human resource potential of the organization's members (FEELING)" [50]. On the other hand, the SF design specifies the hierarchical structure emphasizing its benefit to members, i.e., ability to meet their needs.

The implication of the findings is simply that computer related technology is being used to help support and develop ST/NT organizational designs and information systems. To recall an earlier point: the Jungian framework does not refer to one type as generally better or more relevant than other types. There is no connotation that an ST or NT is “better” than an SF or NF. Each type has strengths and weaknesses. The essential question is: what design is most appropriate for a given organizational environment? It appears that many end users of these systems are NFs and SFs. As discussed earlier, NF and SF designs are direct opposites or in conflict with ST and NT designs, respectively. Because end users are not actually involved in the design process, these conflicts do not arise until after the system is implemented. Perhaps these conflicts can explain many of the system implementation problems that occur. It is important that these conflicts surface and be resolved in the design process. An MIS design approach is needed that integrates all perspectives.

There is a need for NF and SF individuals and MIS designs to be integrated with ST and NT ones. Opposite types can complement each other. For example, Taggart [58] outlines how the four different types are most effectively utilized in the different MIS development phases. Mason and Mitroff [45] emphasize the general growing trend toward the legitimization of SF and NF individuals and organizations. The need for SF and NF perspectives is growing stronger. The authors believe this integrated perspective is critical to successful utilization of computer related technology. One can draw upon the strengths of all four perspectives.

One problem with the MBTI is that it does not adequately assess the balance or integrativeness of individuals, i.e., their ability to express both ends of the personality dimensions. For example, if one has a high score on the N dimension, the S dimension is viewed as complementary. It may not mean that one is lacking in S characteristics; it only indicates a preference for the N dimension.

Most users and systems personnel have the potential to develop a more integrative perspective. Although MBTI data does not measure the degree of integrativeness, it seems that solving center participants did not possess an integrative perspective for two reasons: first, the strengths of their preference scores indicate minimal integration; and, second, the strong thinking orientation in a solving center would make it very difficult for feeling perspectives to be expressed.

One effort that did create an integrative problem solving climate was the system success effort described in the second phase of the study. The Jungian framework was a very useful tool for helping to create the appropriate climate.

## Conclusion

This two phase study was a step in investigating personality differences between users and systems staff. The MIS literature contains much conjecture about the differences between these two groups. The results, however, indicate that user representatives on project teams are very similar to their systems counterparts on the Jungian personality dimensions. Even more surprising was that these user representatives are closer to popular descriptions (ST personality type) of systems staff than the analysts are. The data indicate a plausible explanation. It appears that these user representatives are not the actual end users of the systems and are different in personality characteristics from these end users. These findings imply that organizations are shifting the communication gap from the user representative and system person to the user representative and end user. This strategy leads to a more harmonious design process but a high probability of implementation problems. This scenario provides the best explanation of the results but not the only one. Future research efforts need to clarify the unresolved issues raised by this research, particularly those dealing with the effects of personality differences, or lack of, on the management of project teams and the designs they create.

In terms of the Jungian typology, solving centers are dominated by thinking types. The low number of feeling individuals implies that systems designs are being created lacking a people oriented, dimension. An integrated perspective (ST/NT/NF/SF) is critical to successful utilization of computer related technology. This contention was supported by the case analysis of a system success and failure.

The Jungian framework can help identify differences between individuals and MIS/organizational designs, i.e., make explicit the conflicts and value issues inherent in the different personality types (ST vs. NF, for example). Accepting these differences in people and organizational designs, one can utilize the framework to design procedures or mechanisms to insure an integrated perspective within a design solving center. Some examples of mechanisms are outlined below.

1. The adoption of an MIS design methodology that has this integrated perspective built into it. The approach known as Socio-Technical Systems (STS) design is an example [10, 11].

2. The Jungian framework could be applied to the development of project teams. For example, a newly formed project team can be given the Myers-Briggs instrument. Using the personality type information, a training exercise can be developed to help project team members examine their perspectives as well as create a climate for integrative problem solving. Although the framework could also be used for selection of project team members, its best application seems to be team development and training.

3. Those educating users and system personnel have focused most of the training on ST and NT methodologies. There is a need to train for SF and NF skills as well — perhaps even more. This is starting to happen but only on a small scale [51, 63].

In the turbulent economic and social environment of the coming decades, computer related technology holds promise as a key to organizational survival and growth. The biggest challenge to the users and system personnel applying this technology may well be integrating the strengths of complementary perspectives into their views.

## About the Authors

Kate M. Kaiser is an Assistant Professor of MIS at University of Wisconsin-Milwaukee. She received a B.A. and M.B.A. from Kent State University and a Ph.D. in MIS at University of Pittsburgh. She was previously a member of the Faculty of Management at McGill University. Her research focuses on the systems development process, and characteristics of systems-related personnel. Bob Bostrom is an Assistant Professor of MIS and Special Internal Consultant at Indiana University — Bloomington. His time is split between academic efforts and the support of administrative computing efforts at IU. He holds a B.A. and MBA from Michigan State University, an MS in Computer Science from SUNY at Albany, and a Ph.D. in MIS from the University of Minnesota. He has extensive consulting experience in the areas of database management, MIS design, and the design of the MIS department.

Professor Bostrom is currently engaged in research to determine the applicability of the Socio-Technical Systems Design Methodology to the design and implementation of computer based information systems and to the management and design of the MIS department. At a more basic level, his research focuses on humans as information processors and model makers. His recent efforts have focused on the application of the new field Neuro-Linguistic Programming (NLP) to MIS tasks. NLP is a model of the mapping processes that our brains use to organize our dealings with our environment.
