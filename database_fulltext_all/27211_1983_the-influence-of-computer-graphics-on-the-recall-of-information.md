# The Influence of Computer Graphics on the Recall of Information

By: Collin J. Watson
Russell W. Driver

## Abstract

This article describes an experiment that examined the influence of computer plots of three dimensional graphics on the recall of information. Three dimensional graphics and a tabular mode of presentation were used to convey information to two experimental groups of subjects. The graphics were produced by using perspective projections. The outcome was that the three dimensional graphics did not result in greater recall of information than did a tabular presentation for the task that was assigned.

Keywords: Computer graphics, MIS management, information recall, three dimensional graphics, information systems

ACM Categories: I.2.6, I.3.4, I.3.7, B.4.2

## Introduction

Organizational expenditures for information systems (IS) have increased dramatically in recent years. However, research concerning the influence of IS remains relatively scarce. Several studies have called for additional research in IS [2, 4, 11]. One aspect of IS that has drawn attention to researchers and users has been the mode of presentation [1, 14]. This refers to the form by which information is conveyed to the user [15]. Computer graphics is a mode of presentation that has elicited considerable attention in IS [1, 8, 14, 28].

Previous studies indicate that the format in which data are presented to users is critical to providing information for making decisions $[15, 28]$ . One technique often used by demographers in depicting information is mapping. When a computer system is used to produce plots $[5, 23]$ , information can be displayed via computer plots of three dimensional maps $[5, 7, 26]$ . The three dimensions are obtained by employing orthographic or perspective projections.

Computer plots of three dimensional graphics is a form of presentation that has been available for several years $[7, 23, 26]$ , and the use of graphics generally has been recommended in the literature $[16, 21, 24]$ . However, no one has yet studied the effectiveness of less conventional modes of presentation in IS, such as three dimensional graphics. Previous studies of graphics have investigated traditional line graphs and bar charts, as opposed to more recently developed graphics, such as computer plots of three dimensional graphics $[1, 12, 14, 28]$ . It appears likely that three dimensional graphics obtained by orthographic or perspective projections will be used increasingly in IS. Therefore, experimental research concerning the efficacy of employing three dimensional graphics is particularly important to IS users.

The purpose of the experiment reported in this article is to investigate some aspects of the efficacy of using computer plots of three dimensional graphics as a mode of presentation in IS.

## Previous Studies

Advantages that have been delineated for graphical techniques are that (1) they can be effective in creating interest and in appealing to the interest of the user, (2) relationships can be clearly grasped and easily remembered using these techniques, (3) time can be saved, since large masses of data can be visualized at a glance, (4) a comprehensive picture can be presented that makes possible a complete and balanced understanding of the problem, and (5) tools such as these can bring out hidden facts and relationships and can stimulate, as well as aid, analytical thinking and investigation [18].

Zmud concluded, from an experiment that compared tabular, bar chart, and line-type graphical modes of presentation, that graphical presentation was preferred [27]. In that study, multiple comparisons indicated, among other things, that graphical presentations (1) were judged more relevant than tabular and bar chart presentations, (2) resulted in more accuracy than tabular presentations, and (3) were more readable than tabular and bar chart presentations. Benbasat and Schroeder found that the use of a graphical display of a time series of data within a gaming experiment reduced costs for the decisions being made [1].

Lucas and Nielsen [14] and Lucas [13] hypothesized that a graphic mode of presentation would result in greater learning or performance than tabular presentations, but found no superiority for graphics in gaming experiments, for the most part. They suggested that the type of graphic displays utilized may be important. Mason and Mitroff indicated that mode of presentation is important in management information systems (MIS), and that a graphics presentation is a more personalized mode than tabular presentations [15]. They suggested that graphics may be more effective in some information contexts. Gerrity, in a descriptive study, suggested that graphic display functions have a continuing attraction for the user [8]. Keen and Scott Morton examined several decision support systems and found that they reflected an assumption that graphical displays are more useful in the problem solving process than static tables of numbers that lack patterns and are hard to assimilate [12].

Schutz in an experimental study of graphics, found that line-type graphs resulted in more accuracy and better speed of performance than vertical bar or horizontal bar graphs $[19, 20]$ . He also found that a single multiple line graph generally resulted in more accuracy and better speed of performance than several single line graphs. Hitt determined that, in general, color coded and numerically coded graphics were superior to several other coding methods $[10]$ .

Several studies have suggested expanding research efforts in MIS. Lucas indicated that the findings in various studies of computer graphics to this point are rather modest, and that many small studies will likely be required to gain a greater understanding in this area $[13]$ . Chervany and Dickson suggested the use of experimentation $[2]$ , and Dickson, Senn, and Chervany suggested that there exists a paucity of research efforts in MIS of all types, including laboratory experiments $[4]$ . They also expressed a need for continued experimental research. Additional research is required in the area of graphics since findings concerning graphics are somewhat contradictory (compare Lucas and Neilson $[14]$ with Benbasat and Schroeder $[1]$ , and Zmud $[27]$ ).

The studies discussed above focused on graphics as a two dimensional mode of presentation. Most of these studies suggest that, in general, the use of graphics is advantageous $[2, 8, 12, 15, 28]$ . Additionally, three dimensional graphics are thought to be beneficial. Shostack and Eddy suggest that computer graphics, including three dimensional graphics, can be used to communicate information in a more comprehensible, intelligible, and usable way to the user $[21]$ . Takeuchi and Schmidt suggest that three dimensional computer graphics can both save managers time, by simplifying the interpretation and communication of information, and assist managers in the decision making process $[24]$ . This article focuses on an experiment that examines the influence of computer plots of three dimensional graphics on the ability of users to recall information presented in IS.

## Conceptual Framework

The conceptual framework for this study emanates from cognitive processes [9, 17]. People seem to store information in memory by representing the information using two general types of codes. One type of code is an analogue representation. An example is a map of the United States. The other type of code is an analytic representation, and an example is the names of the states [9]. Information that is in memory is encoded, stored, and later recalled [17].

During the encoding of information, two factors appear to be important for later memory performance. The first factor is how well the memory codes discriminate among items of information, and the second factor is the effectiveness of the retrieval cues. It appears that a person must perform mental activity that sets up a discriminating memory code for the information. The memory code must also be associated with retrieval cues [9].

Recall of information that is in memory is accomplished by cues that are contained in a recall request. The cues are associated with and are used to generate potential information targets, and then the cues are used to help identify or discriminate the appropriate target. Visual, analogue representations of information are often more discriminating than are analytic representations, such as words $[9]$ . When items are recalled multiple times (i.e., immediate recall and delayed recall) the number of items recalled is greater for pictures than for words, presumably because pictures are more discernible than words $[6]$ .

The ability to recall information depends on two major factors. First, the information must be organized in some associative structure. Second, a recall strategy must be adopted that uses cues and the associative structure to generate the desired information [9].

When information is presented in the form of computer graphics, such as maps as compared to tabular presentations, a more discernible analogue representation should be coded in memory. The information is organized in an associative structure by the format of the graphic, and recall cues can be linked to the associative structure by using imagery. Recall performance is therefore expected to be superior for information presented using three dimensional computer graphics than for information presented using tables.

The previous research and conceptual framework, discussed above, provide the hypotheses examined in this study. Operationally, the first hypothesis is that the recall of information immediately after the information is presented in the form of computer plots of three dimensional graphics will be greater than the recall of the same information presented in tabular form. The second hypothesis is that the recall of information following a delay after it is presented in the form of computer plots of three dimensional graphics will be less than the delayed recall of the same information presented in tabular form.

## Method

The experiment employed a basic two comparison groups design. The independent variable for the experiment is the mode of presentation of information. The dependent variables are the degree of recall of information immediately after it is presented (i.e., immediate recall), and the degree of recall after a period of elapsed time (i.e., delayed recall).

## Subjects

Subjects were 29 junior and senior level business administration students at a large university. Several were also lower level managers. The subjects consented to participate and performed the task dutifully.

## Experimental procedure

The stimuli or treatments for experimentally testing the influence of mode of presentation on immediate recall and delayed recall of information took two forms: (1) computer plots of three dimensional graphics, and (2) a tabular presentation. The stimuli were prepared by determining the state of practice location for a medical college's physicians for the years 1964-1969. Computer plotted three dimensional maps of the distribution of these physicians among the states were produced $[5, 23]$ . The map for the physicians who graduated in 1965 is presented in Table 2 as one example. Seven maps, one for each year and one for all the data from all years combined, embodied the same information as presented in tabular form. The relative frequency of the distribution of the physicians among states is depicted by the height of each of the states, so that the seven maps allow the characteristics of the distributions over the years to be discerned.

Experimental data were obtained at two sessions. At the first session two groups of subjects were presented the information. One treatment group was presented the information in the form of computer plots of three dimensional maps of the data (e.g., Table 2), and the same information was presented to the other treatment group in tabular form (i.e., Table 1). The subjects were instructed to study the information for one minute, after which they would be asked to respond to questions about the information. At the end of the study period the stimuli were taken from the subjects. A list of the top six states, in terms of the percentage of physicians located in the state, was then presented to the subjects as a recall cue. The list was not rank ordered. Subjects were then asked to rank the six states in order, from highest relative frequency to lowest, based on the information they had previously studied. No mention was made that another session would be held at a later date.

Rank order correlations were computed for the rank order specified by each subject for the top six states, and the actual rank order of those states. The rank order correlation for each subject is a measure of the degree of recall immediately after the information was presented. Degree of recall is therefore defined as the ability of a subject to give correct rankings for the task assigned. This measure of recall is similar to standard measures of recall [9]. Ranking tasks, such as this one, are often a part of IS use processes.

At the second session the subjects were reminded of their participation at the first session and were identified as the “maps” or “table” group members, based on the mode of information they had been presented. They were then given the same list of the top six states (not in rank order), and were once again asked to rank order those states, based on the distribution data that had been presented to them during the first session. Four subjects were not present at the second session.

The ranking obtained from each subject during the second session was also used to compute a rank order correlation. These correlations are a measure of the information presented four weeks previously that subjects were able to recall. Delayed recall is defined, then, as the ability of the subject to give correct rankings for information presented at a previous time for the task assigned.

The two groups that had received the computer graphics and the tabular presentations of information at the first session were tested for equality of mean rank order correlations using a Student-t statistic. ## Discussion

This investigation does not lend support to the notion that computer plots of three dimensional graphics as a mode for conveying information in IS, will result in increased recall, both immediate and delayed, of that information, when compared to the more traditional tabular mode of presentation. Computer plots of three dimensional maps were not superior to the tabular presentation of data with respect to the degree of recall of information.

Lack of support for the superiority of graphics in this experiment is comparable to recent findings by Lucas and Nielsen [14], although the types of graphics employed in the studies were different. Lucas and Nielsen suggested that perhaps the graphics they utilized were not beneficial because they were somewhat hard to read, and because of the slow speed of the graphics terminals they used. The graphics used in this experiment were maps that were clearly plotted using a pen plotter. The lack of beneficial effects for graphics in this experiment cannot be ascribed to those attributes.

However, these findings should not be construed as a general condemnation of the use of graphics in IS, because many forms of graphics are different from the one employed in the experiment. Additionally, the authors used recall, both immediate and delayed, as the dependent variable. This is only one variable with which to evaluate the presentation of data. Such variables as understanding the data, integration of the data with prior knowledge, speed of comprehension, and degree of appeal to the interest of users, are examples of other important variables with which to judge the value of a graphic presentation. Future research could consider other dependent variables such as these.

The degree of recall was not substantial for several of the subjects. A reasonable explanation for the somewhat low degree of recall by the subjects is that recall is associated with the “level of processing” during the encoding of information. The information that was presented in this experiment underwent visual processing by the subjects. An auditory processing task or a semantic processing task would increase the degree of recall [3]. An example is a task that included a discussion or a task that explored the meaning of the information presented. Training sessions may have been beneficial as well. However, the main purpose of the experiment was to examine differences in recall between the treatment groups, not the absolute level of recall of the subjects.

Other considerations regarding this experiment were that the delay between measurements, selected as four weeks, may have been too long, and there were no cautionary steps taken to avoid contamination of subjects between the measurements of recall. The three dimensional graphics did not result in recall superior to that for the tabular presentation even though the states that had higher practice location frequencies on the computer graphics were identified by name, which would seemingly be favorable to the graphics. Individual differences have been shown to influence cognitive responses to information, and may influence recall of information that is conveyed in different modes of presentation [28]. Future research could more closely attend to such influences on cognitive processes during the recall of information.

In summary, greater recall of information did not result during an experiment when information was presented to subjects in the form of computer plots of three dimensional graphics than when information was conveyed in tabular form, contrary to the hypotheses. In general, the results of this study imply that systems designers should be discriminating as they select computer graphics to be included in IS, because the computer graphics may not enhance the use process with regard to the ability of the user to recall the information being presented. It is suggested that users be involved in the decision to include computer graphics, such as three dimensional maps, in IS in order to enhance the use process.
