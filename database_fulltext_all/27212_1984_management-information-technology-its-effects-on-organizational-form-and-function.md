# Management Information Technology: Its Effects on Organizational Form and Function

By: Lawrence W. Foster
Graduate School of
Business Administration
Michigan State University
East Lansing, Michigan

By: David M. Flynn
Environmental Activities Staff
General Motors Corporation
Warren, Michigan

## Abstract

Advancements in management information technologies in the past half decade are bringing to organizations forms and functions unanticipated even a few years ago. The revolution in personal communication and computation power is changing organizational roles and tasks and is offering increased effectiveness and productivity to organizational designers who choose to take advantage of technological innovations. This article articulates the nature of those changes and their impact on a particular organization within the General Motors family.

Keywords: Human factors, management
ACM Categories: H.1.2, H.4.2, K.4.3

## Introduction

The unparalleled advances in management information technology in the past half decade are bringing wholesale changes in organizational form and function unanticipated even a few years ago. Within the seeds of this revolution in technology lies potential for change as potent and ubiquitous as that brought about by changes in manufacturing technologies during the days of the Industrial Revolution. Through innovative management information technologies, new and unexpected relationships between individual and individual, and between individual and task are restructuring organizations into forms impossible prior to the advent of the technologies. When introducing integrated management information system technologies that provide virtually instantaneous intraorganizational communication, electronic information storage and retrieval, and personal computing resources for decision making, organizations are confronting significantly altered organizational forms. This article is drawn from the experience of one particular organization. Its purpose is to give future system designers, organizational designers, and researchers of organizational form and job design the benefit of one experience. In such a way the chances of capturing the shifting roles and functions in an accepted quasi-experimental methodology are enhanced; for not until the changes are better anticipated can traditional pre-intervention measurements be developed.

In General Motors' Environmental Activities Staff the dawning of the personal computing/communicating era has brought major changes. Within eighteen months of its introduction, integrated management information technology quickly spawned increases in organizational efficiency, effectiveness, creativity, and innovativeness. Many of these increases were caused by changes in traditional organizational forms. Many, in fact, would have been impossible without changes in traditional roles and relationships brought about by the new technology.

The General Motors Environmental Activities Staff is a policy-development, research, and regulatory compliance group reporting through a vice-president to a group vice-president of the corporation. It employs approximately 180 managerial, professional, and clerical-technical personnel. The professional group is made up

MIS Quarterly/December 1984 229 largely of graduate engineers and physical scientists; the managerial cadre is drawn from the professional ranks.

Environmental Activities' charge is to design and recommend policy to senior company officials on environmental, safety and fuel economy issues. The staff prepares position papers detailing corporate stands on environmental and safety issues, researches and prepares testimony of corporate officials before legislative and administrative bodies, and is responsible for overall policy consistency. In addition, the staff is also responsible for ensuring that the corporation's plans and products meet all governmental regulations and corporate requirements. Thus, the staff continually monitors environmentally-critical data and disseminates those data both throughout the corporation and to the appropriate governmental agencies. A separate responsibility is the continual monitoring and reporting of all world-wide legislation dealing with vehicle safety and emissions standards and the accompanying maintenance of current regulation manuals throughout the corporation. Environmental Activities is organized in a "product" configuration. Reporting to the vice-president are the directors of four distinct areas: vehicle emissions, vehicle safety, plant emissions, and international regulations.

In 1981, the vice president of the Environmental Activities staff chose to make a major commitment to utilization of management information technologies. She saw in that technology the potential to integrate the planning effort of the staff in a more complete and timely fashion than had previously been possible. System design, vendor selection, and equipment delivery were completed and the integrated information processing and communication system came on-line in 1982. Members of the organization were provided with personal computers, sophisticated graphics/text work stations, printers, and a library of software. These personal computers were linked through a communication network that allowed a variety of processing and transfer capabilities including electronic mail, electronic file construction and maintenance, hard copy to electronic format transfer, etc. Familiarization and operations training were provided to all individuals in the organization. The changes in organizational performance and the changes in traditional relationships have come about at an accelerated pace.

## Changes in Relationships

During the first year of operations, changes within the organization have taken place three interrelated areas. While none of the three can be completely isolated from the others, the areas together do provide foci for future research in information system implementation. Each has a somewhat different focus of affect.

\- Major changes and redefinitions of organizational communication.

\- Major changes in the use and purpose of organizational hierarchies.

\- Major changes in task performance and task structure.

## Major changes and redefinitions of organizational communication

Table 1 shows the nature and direction of changes brought about by the implementation of management information systems in the Environmental Activities staff. Changes are apparent in three areas of organizational communication: in the absolute number and direction of communication in the purpose and nature of the communication, and in the degree of impersonalization within the communication network.

After implementation of the system, members of the organization found themselves constructing much more elaborate networks of communications. Before implementation, a significant percentage of the organizational communications between individuals was vertical and was composed largely of interactions required in the performance of organizational tasks. Communication channels were narrow and directed much more toward the support of vertical relationships than of lateral ones. This changed following implementation as organizational members found increased utility in constructing wide-ranging lateral networks that tied together individuals with complementary skills and common problems. Much of the resulting improvement in organizational effectiveness can be attributed to the synergism provided by the combination of instantaneous communication networking capability and personal computing power offered by the system.

Secondly, individuals spend a greater proportion of time communicating task-relevant information than they have done previously. Electronic communication media do not lend themselves particularly well to social chat, yet social protocol is an important part of face-to-face communication. In fact, refusal to engage in socially-oriented, supportive behaviors carries strong messages of personal disapproval and, when done by a leader, significantly lowers the level of work group satisfaction. The integrated information system, however, allowed for rapid interactive communication to take place without requiring the stylized protocol for face-to-face communication that develops in the culture of any organization. Thus, individuals found that a much larger amount of their system communications were task-focused than was true when interactive communications could take place only via telephone or face-to-face contact.

Ironically and unexpectedly, the system also brought about an apparent improvement in the level of “personal” feelings which grew up between members of the organization who had been newly linked through the information communication network. When organizational members could make initial contacts with others via a virtually impersonal communication medium they were more willing to do so than when new relationships could be formed only face-to-face. However, once that initial contact had been made and an electronic communication pattern developed over time, face-to-face contacts were personally less threatening. Thus, the number of personal contacts within the organization actually increased due to the system implementation. People quite literally could meet as friends instead of strangers.

## Changes in the uses and purpose of organizational hierarchy

Changes in the use and purpose of the organizational hierarchy have at least two dimensions in Environmental Activities. First, structures of relative hierarchical position are losing their traditional grip on the organization. From the former hierarchy of position power there is developing instead a hierarchy of competency. Individuals who demonstrate competence have become visible through the system. Power and resources now flow increasingly to the obvious centers of competence instead of to traditional hierarchical loci.

Second, and somewhat similar to protocol changes in communication patterns, is a change in organizational protocol. In General Motors, as in any organization, there is an established and elaborate protocol of rank. Though hierarchical protocol is attenuated to a degree by the management information technology relationships, the traditional protocol remains. An organizational member does not, for example, pop into the vice-president's office for a quick word. Such protocol cannot, and should not, be maintained within the new management information system, however, for it is too confining. It is not uncommon, for example, for an organizational member to pop into the vice-president's office electronically on several occasions each day. Similarly, the information technology allows those in the upper reaches of the hierarchy to dip directly into the organizational ranks for direct access to information or expertise — and to do so without visible violation of the established chain of command. While the traditional hierarchical protocol remains, it no longer controls all organizational relations. A second, less formal, more spontaneous, task-directed system of hierarchical interaction has developed in conjunction with, and complementary to the formal traditional protocol.

## Changes in task performance and task structure

Not surprisingly, investment in management information technology has also affected task performance and task structure. The major influence accrues from increases in the actual prime time that critical workers have available for managerial or creative task performance. These workers, often termed “knowledge workers,” are the office-based professionals and managers who possess unique expertise, experience, and/or decision-making power that makes them especially critical to the achievement of organizational goals. Prior to system implementation these knowledge workers spent a sizable portion of their time performing tasks which might as easily have been performed by others less critical to the overall success of the organization. Routine analysis, data input and retrieval, and document preparation all are examples of tasks that now are done by workers less critical to goal attainment. With those jobs redistributed, knowledge workers have increased time and energy for tasks that they are uniquely qualified to perform.

For example, prior to the introduction of the management information technology, one skilled engineer was dedicated totally to developing and monitoring corporate position on waste water discharge. Today, technology allows that man to send scientific information directly and simultaneously to plant engineering locations, to “electronic” files, and to a continuously updated “electronic” water-quality manual. Not only is information now sent much more quickly, but it is sent without errors introduced by non-professional or less-skilled technical personnel in repeated data entry operations. More importantly, the expertise this one skilled worker brings to problems of waste water management through the incremental time savings is estimated already to have made major savings in plant design and construction expense.

Increasing the prime time of knowledge workers has a second beneficial effect. As tasks are shared between workers there grows both a new appreciation for and understanding of the purpose of the tasks. In Environmental Activities the perception of the tasks has shifted from the notion of a task as a series of sequential but isolated jobs to one of shared responsibility for successful overall task completion. The task perception of subordinates has become less one of simple support for a hierarchical superior and more one of joint proprietorship for the work of the group. It comes as no surprise that the intrinsic attractiveness of the job is greatly enhanced for subordinates and that higher levels of motivation and satisfaction yield both personal and organizational payoffs. Within Environmental Activities, secretaries, who before had spent their time typing single-page hard copy, have taken on routine data analysis and matrix manipulations. Others have moved to support knowledge workers with a range of computer graphic augmentations previously available only at a much greater cost in time and money. One young worker, for example, found her job growing from merely typing manual updates for each eight-hour day, to full responsibility for the maintenance of an internal manual of international regulations. Utilizing enlarged data management and enhanced graphics capabilities, this woman has become a “publisher.” Not only does she feel far more satisfied and productive in her new role, but the firm is able to avoid significant annual costs in printing expense.

Finally, tasks have become more fluid and dynamic. Managerial information technology clearly frees time previously hostage of repetitive support tasks. As time is freed for numerous workers across the organization, managers have had an opportunity to reassess and to redefine jobs within the organization. This redefinition takes a number of forms.

(1) Jobs may be redefined into natural work units which allow workers to feel increasingly that they “own” a task in the organization.

(2) Jobs may be consolidated in a manner that frees additional prime time for critical knowledge workers.

Well-run organizations find that greater organizational payoffs come from investing incremental time savings in the effectiveness of the organization rather than from trying to increase efficiency through reduced head counts. The time of skilled managers and professionals is a resource of tremendous value, far more important to the long-run health of the organization than are salary savings. Where this is not true, in fact, managerial and professional performance should be carefully scrutinized. When an organization can gain more by recovering salary savings than by reinvesting time savings, the managerial and professional ranks are, by definition, failing to return the investment made in them.

An organization can tailor the reinvestment of time savings to meet its changing needs by using the flexibility provided by the information management system to pair problems to the individual most able to redress them. Where management and professional skills can be profitably leveraged, organizations gain from reinvestment of time. Specifically, a savings of two hours in the day of each of four knowledge workers yields greater long-run payoffs than does the consolidation of those savings into a single eight hour block which can then be disinvested (laid off).

## The Thisby Farce

Of course, not all changes brought about by management information technologies result in improved organizational performance. Some may actually impede acceptance of the technology. One such change is, borrowing from Shakespeare, the Thisby Farce. In Midsummer Night's Dream, Flute, one of Shakespeare's most appealing characters, demonstrates loudly when forced to play the role of Thisby — the female lead. "NAY, FAITH, let me not play a woman; I have a beard coming!"

The Thisby Farce is no stranger to organizational life. Secretaries use keyboards, executives don't; real executives dictate to secretaries. Yet the official role of secretaries and other similar staff functionaries is historically one of facilitating communications in a way that frees time of knowledge workers for concentration on organizational objectives. Technology now fills that role efficiently and effectively. An executive working with a keyboard can communicate almost instantaneously with anyone or everyone in the organization. He or she spends far less time inputing information via keyboard than writing or dictating information, waiting while it is recorded in hard copy, proofing that hard copy, then waiting again while additional copies are made and physically transported to those requiring the information. The latter is pretty much the same technology used by Napoleon to communicate information to his commanders.

A variant of the Thisby theme is the Crown Prince(ss) Syndrome. In this, the executive or professional does not “type” because of perceived incongruence with his or her current status in the organization. In appearance, affectation, and effect this Prince(ss) Syndrome mirrors the Thisby Farce. It is, however, less gender specific though it can take a particularly resistant form among women managers and professionals who have already spent long years being mistaken for secretaries.

Some degree of Thisby Farce and Prince(ss) Syndrome may be inevitable companions of information management implementation. Research shows their effects can be ameliorated, however. A key first step is training for system users. "Keyboard familiarization" training is an initial first step along with an introduction to system use. Also important is the availability of software which, regardless of its centrality to the required tasks, is user friendly and provides the naive user with feelings of success and mastery of the system. The more complex, less friendly software can wait: The first order of business is seduction, not execution. Third, a critical part of introductory training programs is the active and visible involvement of top management.

At GM, the vice president of Environmental Activities sent a number of clear signals when she and her executive director were the first organizational members to undertake initial training. First, she made it clear that “typing” is a skill neither gender- nor hierarchically-specific. It is, instead, a skill as critical for work at all levels of the organization as literacy or the ability to speak into a telephone. A second signal was that no executive or professional in the organization was too busy (or too smart) to delay or delete the training sessions. Third, she created the clear expectation that information management technology was to become a permanent and ongoing part of daily life in Environmental Activities — it will neither go away, nor can it be avoided. Michael Jensen, the president of Banco International de Colombia, addressed the same point when discussing his personal use of the bank’s information management system in a recent Wall Street Journal article. “When I do it myself,” he says, “I set a style for the organization. I’m showing that computers and information are critical to the bank” [1].

## The Got-cha Factor

Another potentially disruptive effect of information management technology is the got-cha effect. Unfortunately, information management systems are not panaceas. They are only tools. The technology can make good employees better; it cannot make a poor manager into a good one. Following the implementation of an information management system, information is much more visible and much more widely shared than was true when information streams were limited to a hard-copy medium. Consequently, mistakes are often more visible as well.

The technology will not make poor organizations function better but it will very likely show that they contain poor performers. System implementation allows data to be consolidated in combinations previously unavailable in hard copy. Production reports can be aggregated with sales reports. Engineering costs can be aggregated by market trends. New combinations of data suggest new ideas or often show old ideas overlooked or only partially realized. The new combinations of data can also show poor management — the got-cha factor. The same Wall Street Journal article tells of the CEO of an oil-equipment supply company who discovered, through a newly operational system, that the price of one of his company's specialized products was far below that of his competitors. A quick analysis showed that the company could have made an additional two million dollars by bringing its prices closer to the competition's. His reaction: "If I can find this out in an afternoon or two, where was the manager of this division? What was he doing all that time?" The article goes on to tell of the division manager whose career was severely damaged by the accident.

## Conclusions

Information management technologies bring unique changes to organizations and to the life that goes on within them. These new technologies directly alter the tasks of managers and professionals and how they go about the accomplishment of organizational objectives. In many ways these new technologies bring revolution to the managers' suite for the first time in history. The effects of this revolution are already far-reaching and will reach farther each day. Like the changes the Industrial Revolution brought to the work place, the changes of information management technologies will not all be positive, but clearly the overall effects will, like those of the Industrial Revolution, be overwhelmingly beneficial. These changes are upon us now. If managed correctly, they will bring increasing flexibility and adaptability. Through greatly enhanced information processing capabilities, organizations will be able to respond to environmental changes, and to achieve the environmental/organizational congruency necessary for long term success.
