# Requirements, Needs, and Priorities: A Structured Approach for Determining MIS Project Definition

By: John L. Batiste
A.O. Smith Data Systems
Brown Deer, Wisconsin

By: John T. Jung
A.O. Smith Harvestore® Products, Inc.
Arlington Heights, Illinois

## Abstract

Critical Success Factors (CSF) [1], Application Transfer Team (ATT) [2], Business Systems Planning (BSP) [3], and Executive Application Survey (EAS) [4] are some of the more highly publicized methods used to perform requirements definition for information systems. A key element in these techniques, and often the most difficult to secure, is the total manpower commitment required to perform them correctly. These analysis methods are attractive to management, but we have not always been successful in selling those approaches to senior executives due to the time commitments they require.

Requirements, Needs, and Priorities (RNP) combines the more effective concepts of CSF, BSP, and ATT with traditional requirements analysis while minimizing the time and effort required to reach project definition. The objective is to bring executive recognition of MIS needs from a maybe perspective to one of definite yes or no. This article will describe the RNP procedure and explain the components for successfully executing a study.

Keywords: Methodology, life cycle, requirements definition, critical success factors, business processes, operational rank

## Introduction

The A.O. Smith Corporation is a diversified manufacturer and supplier of products and services for the transportation, water heating, water supply, air conditioning, agricultural, financial, chemical, power generation, wastewater, and petroleum industries. As one of the 500 largest publicly-owned manufacturing companies in the United States, A.O. Smith employs nearly 10,000 people. Its products are manufactured in 21 plants in the U.S. and abroad.

A.O. Smith Data Systems was created in 1961 as a central information processing resource for the entire corporation. Staffing now stands at over 200, and an additional two to ten MIS personnel are employed outside of Data Systems at each of its manufacturing companies. Traditional applications and technologies have led to mature systems for most business functions. Total annual MIS budgets are near \$14 million.

A.O. Smith Harvestore® Products, Inc. (AOSHPI) and A.O. Smith Data Systems work together in a customer/vendor relationship. Responsibility for effective management of the information resource is split. Data Systems manages central mainframe applications. AOSHPI performs local system operations and development. Responsibility for distributed applications is shared.

Our challenges are to respond to a changing business environment and to integrate fourth generation technologies into existing information systems. Having spent considerable time and effort using the traditional approaches to MIS project definition with mixed results, a specific and planned attempt was made to find a better way. Data Systems' first modified Critical Success Factor (CSF) [1] study was completed in mid 1981. Results were better than those achieved with traditional methods, but still required refinement. Encouraged by the wider scope of the CSF concepts and lessons learned from the end user perspectives of Application Transfer Team (ATT) [2] and Business Systems Planning (BSP) [3], continued work brought us to the current definition of Requirements, Needs, and Priorities (RNP).

RNP offers a pragmatic approach for entering the system development life cycle. It identifies high level information system concerns and provides an understandable structure of relationships and needs. BSP also accomplishes this, but includes a detailed analysis of data requirements. We defer that analysis to a technical design project, removing much of the detail from the RNP procedure.

In general, we believe BSP is too extensive; we have already established our MIS direction and strategy. BSP is a recognized and valuable process, but we have been reluctant to begin that procedure when it is apparent that many of its resulting benefits are already in place. For us, BSP presents an opportunity with marginal return from the effort. ATT is useful, after the initial requirements are known, to firm up specific needs and estimate cost/benefit relationships. As you will see later, CSF analysis brings high level focus to our approach; however, it does not sufficiently handle some of our operational concerns. CSF analysis can provide a surprising improvement to the scope of a study and to the understanding of relationships between business processes. For our purposes, CSF's are the first step in defining potential problem areas, but we needed a more detailed method for breaking the problem definitions into workable MIS pieces. Executive Application Survey (EAS) [4] is similar to RNP but, as with ATT, it is geared to matching a requirements definition with the characteristics of existing application software. The essence of our requirements definition dilemma is that typically, we know too little about the business issues to permit the authorization of approaches such as ATT and too much to warrant the time and expense of BSP.

In short, there are several good methodologies that are applicable before the development or purchase point in the systems life cycle, but our experience indicated that we could do a better job in defining the business problems and understanding the impact of the application on the organization prior to making those decisions. Requirements, Needs, and Priorities overcomes many of the shortcoming we perceived in alternative methods.

## What Is RNP?

RNP is an analysis methodology. It involves understanding the processes that are present in operating the business, the factors that are critical for success in those processes, and the obstacles that prohibit or impede the attainment of success. Its function is to develop high level MIS project definition to overcome those obstacles.

The procedure is organized into three phases: an executive session, a task force analysis workshop, and an executive presentation. The function of the executive session is to gain perspective on the business problem and to define the detail and scope of the study. Task force analysis resolves the problem issues, and develops a set of recommendations for further action. The executive presentation is the decision making step where recommendations to proceed or not to proceed are delivered to the executive sponsor. The phases may be completed within three weeks. In selecting a study area, it is important to understand how the system applications are structured. At A.O. Smith, application systems are defined and managed according to business function characteristics. Major categories of function include customer order service, marketing/distribution, manufacturing, and finance/accounting. Further breakdown of function is appropriate in most cases. For instance, customer order service typically includes the order entry, sales analysis, sales commission, and forecasting applications.

An RNP study is initiated after there is sufficient cause for information systems evaluation in one of the functional areas. Sufficient cause may be due to cost/benefit relationships drifting out of range, frequent system improvement requests, problems with accounting, the shop floor, or sales, newer database management approaches, or additional business function requirements. As an example, one of our studies began after reviewing an order entry system that was ten years old and had been constructed with technologies approaching twelve to fifteen years in age. The cost/benefit relationship was no longer attractive, and we were having difficulty in responding to change requests from the users of that system.

## The roles of the participants

Staffing the study requires the chief executive, the executives affected by the business problem, six to eight task force members, a moderator, and a scribe. Necessary materials include flip chart paper, easels, marking pens, and tape or pins. An off site meeting room may also be considered a requirement in order to minimize interruptions and to serve as a work area where charts and notes can be maintained throughout the study.

The chief executive endorses the RNP study through a letter to the participants ten days to two weeks before the first session. The letter states the nature of the business problem, its importance, and the dates and location of the sessions. It demonstrates that the study has executive attention and that results are expected.

Executives have two assignments in the RNP analysis. First, they bring a higher level perspective; their view of the business is generally broader than that of operating management. Second, they are the audience for the executive presentation and must approve implementation of the recommendations. Executives are present for the executive session and the executive presentation. They are not required in task force analysis.

Task force members are the experts from the area of the business under review. They perform the analysis, bring their concerns to the process, and learn from each other as the significance of problems is discussed.

A moderator leads the study. The moderator's job is to get the steps of the procedure completed and keep discussions moving. However, the moderator must not influence what is discussed. Discussion content is the responsibility of the task force.

The scribe is a note taker and helper. The scribe records lengthier details and assists in displaying completed charts for reference by the task force.

## The executive session

Introductory remarks explain the RNP procedure to the participants, and clarify their roles. The purpose of this phase is to establish the scope of the analysis. This is accomplished by modeling the business area to be studied, determining what is critical for success in that environment, and identifying the associated problems that will be addressed. The executive session is completed in four to six hours.

Participants prepare for the executive session by collecting their concerns and needs pertaining to the business problem. A review of personal objectives and departmental goals is normally the starting point for this preparation.

The first task is to construct a process flow model. The model describes the business processes that occur in the course of normal activity for the area of the study problem. It establishes process scope. In constructing the model, emphasis is placed on business processes without regard to who performs the activities. The business functions accomplished are listed below the respective processes.

The model shown in Figure 1 represents the processes that may occur from the time a product is conceived to the time it is shipped and follow-up activities are performed. A model for a shop floor control analysis would be different. The subject of each RNP study determines the content of the model.

Next, we identify the Critical Success Factors for each process in the model. In our definition, CSF's are no more than statements of those activities that must be performed well in order to be successful. Each stage of the process flow model is examined separately, and its CSF's are recorded and posted.

Critical Success Factors analysis is essential to our process. While not as rigorous as Rockart's approach, RNP does focus on individual managers and their information needs through the executive session and task force analysis. Many of the CSF's found in our studies have their origin in the four prime sources pointed out by Rockart's MIT team (i.e., structure of the particular industry, competitive strategy or industry position/geographic location, environmental factors, or temporal factors).

CSF's are an excellent means of setting the stage. They serve as a basis to recognize MIS issues that are important to the business and to separate essential requirements from the nonessential. Admittedly, we emphasize only those factors that have MIS significance. The majority of our procedure is occupied with what to do about the MIS problems that are obstacles to success.

Problems that exist in achieving success are then explored for each CSF. A problem is defined as anything that is lacking or gets in the way of accomplishing a CSF. When a problem is identified by a task force member, it is thoroughly discussed, assigned a reference number, recorded, and posted. For example, the timely scheduling of shipments may be a critical factor for quality customer service; inaccurate or missing order promised dates may be a problem in achieving that CSF.

The viewpoints of executives are of particular interest. Do not be surprised if problems not relevant to MIS solutions surface. They are recorded and become a part of the executive presentation.

The last step of the executive session is to rank the problems. Each participant votes once on each problem, indicating whether they consider the issue to be a major problem, a problem, or a lesser problem. This distinction in problem severity is sufficient for our purposes.

RNP is a workshop process, and the executives and task force members have been informed that the final presentation will not be a formal written report. Our purpose in RNP is to define and justify an MIS project, not draft the requirements document. The details and formal documentation for the project are pursued after the RNP recommendations are accepted. This reduces the cost of the study and delays the clerical effort until a time when the need to perform that work has clearer justification.

## Task force analysis

Task force analysis is a three to five day workshop involving list and matrix exercises designed to digest the problems and develop solutions. The exercises contribute to clarifying the issues by looking at the problems and processes from several perspectives. Additionally, organizational impact is studied to show relationships and determine who has responsibility for portions of the application system(s).

Task force analysis begins with a review of the CSF's and how they fit in the processes. The list of problems from the executive session is posted, and the matrix exercises are briefly described.

Organization-process, the first matrix, is shown in Table 3. It establishes organizational scope for the study problem. The processes that were defined in the executive session are listed across the top and the organization (departments) down the left hand side. The cells of the matrix are filled as a result of group discussion. Responsibility for the information in a process is indicated by R, major use of the information in a process by U, and occasional interest in the information by I. Ideally, task force members represent the departments and can speak for the activities performed.

Organization-process shows what parts of the organization are involved with each of the processes and identifies the extent of involvement.

Now that the full scope of the analysis is clear to the task force, we are ready to consider the information issues. The development of quality MIS recommendations requires a solid understanding of the information relationships within the organization and among the applications. That understanding is bolstered through a simplified input/process/output exercise, the next step in the workshop.

Table 4 shows how input/process/output is applied to each stage of the process flow model. The information that enters a process is listed, followed by the process performed on the information and the resulting information output(s). Many of the outputs become inputs to the next (or another) process. One input/process/output worksheet is prepared for each stage of the process flow model. Input/process/output identifies the relationships of information across the processes.

Activities to this point have been preparatory, yet key to the analysis. We have established scope (and possibly scope exclusions) through a statement of the business processes that will be considered. The portions of the organization that are affected have been identified, and the information used in those processes is defined.

Now that we better understand how the processes map across the organization and the nature of the information flow within those processes, it is time to revisit the problems that were identified in the executive session. Table 5 is a problem-process matrix. Its purpose is to help the task force understand how the problems relate to the processes.

Problem-process identifies those areas that require the greatest amount of attention. Processes are listed across the top and problems down the left hand side, by reference number, in free-for-all rank order. Each problem is reviewed with respect to how it impacts each process. The matrix is filled by using pound sign (#), X, and slash (/) notations to indicate a major problem, a problem, or a lesser problem, respectively. A blank entry indicates no impact. Again, a relatively soft distinction in the degree of problem severity is sufficient for organizing the thought process. Task force consensus determines #, X, or /.

The resulting matrix is examined for patterns of # and X. Difficulties with a particular process or problem group may be apparent. The problem-process matrix is a discussion vehicle, used by the task force to determine which business processes are being affected by each problem. This places the problem discussion in an environment of total process consideration.

It may be necessary to rearrange the rows of the matrix for a better grouping of #, X, and /. This will, of course, take the problem list out of its free-for-all ranked order, but the resulting patterns would shed light on which processes are experiencing the greatest degree of difficulty and show relationships between problems. For that exercise, the sequence in which problems are listed is not important. The emphasis is on problems and processes, not on any particular problem list arrangement.

The development of problem-process has an enlightening effect on the task force. Each member came to the study with his/her own needs and grievances. By using processes as the environment to discuss problems, many have found support for their cause, determined that they may be the source of someone else's problems, or more fully realized why they are having difficulties in achieving success.

In Table 6, an organization-problem matrix highlights problem ownership and forms the basis for operational priority. At this point, the task force has a good understanding of the CSF's and the problems that exist in achieving success. They have also gained an appreciation for how the problems related to the processes and to what extent information is produced or required by each process. The organization-problem exercise summarizes the impact of the problem issues on the departments, and it produces a problem priority list that more accurately identifies the business system needs and their degree of importance. The cells of organization-problem are filled with # (major problem), X (problem), and / (lesser problem) notation to indicate severity. The technique used is one of acceptance and/or consensus. Normally, problem ownership is readily claimed by the department(s) involved and the degree of impact is easily established. We have not experienced a case where consensus could not be reached on ownership or severity.

It is important to note that the voting procedures that establish the #, X, or / entries for problem-process and organization-problem are independent events. There is no attempt to coordinate the values of the first with the values of the second. The entries arrived at for organization-problem area a result of the shared discussions and the associated change in perspectives that came about through each of the preceding RNP steps.

The operational rank, shown at the bottom of the matrix, is established by assigning a value of three to #, two to X, one to /, and then totalling each column. In light of the activities that have preceded this step, it is unlikely that operational rank and free-for-all rank will be identical.

Establishing the relative significance and impact of problems is one of the concerns normally addressed in MIS studies. Discussion generated throughout our analysis tempers individual judgements on the issues. In free-for-all, participants express their needs as related to their experience. After exposure to the executive view of CSF's and problems, the thoughts of other department members, and the nature of interrelationships of information across the processes and organization, a new perspective is realized. This is the heart of RNP!

At this point, the CSF's and problems are well established in relation to the organization and the processes. They represent the requirements and needs of RNP. The operational rank identifies the priorities.

A comparison of the two rankings is shown in Table 7. The comparison is developed to better appreciate how the importance of problem issues has changed after considering the forces at play in the business process and the organization.

Duplicate numbers in the ranking columns are due to identical scores in the vote tallies. In our examples, a number one free-for-all problem became the number four problem in operational rank, fifteen became seven, and a group of problems (ranked three, six, and eight by free-for-all) are now operationally ranked at eleven.

We do not believe that the operational rank merely represents a new bias introduced by the study method. The substance of all discussion has been directed at understanding how the problems and obstacles affect the business and to what degree they impact each of the processes. If those discussions have been conducted in good faith, then there is ample reason to assume that operational rank does, in fact, represent a more realistic assessment of the information system issues.

With this new focus, it is time to propose solutions to the problems. Solutions are accepted from all participants and vigorously discussed. In our experience, a revitalized sense of purpose arises due to insight gained in the analysis.

Solutions to the problems are stated in broad terms, with emphasis on how a new or upgraded system could address the problems that are obstacles to succeeding in the processes. The analysis has demonstrated how the issues are interrelated. Many of the proposed solutions are stated so that a process or process group is encompassed. This tends to produce recommendations of greater substance, when compared to matching problems and solutions on a one-for-one basis. Once the solutions are established, they may be organized and evaluated by comparing their anticipated result and the problems associated with the CSF's in each process. If further clarification of the solution impact is required, a solution-process matrix may be developed to highlight relationships.

In organizing the solutions, it is likely that a few of the problems can be eliminated. Problems that are contingent on other problems are a part of the puzzle. For instance, a problem in order tracking may not be an issue if a problem in order maintenance is handled properly. Repairing two or more issues in one pass is a rewarding experience. Searching for that opportunity is a key step in this phase of the procedure.

Since the participants have worked hard to get to this point, it is fitting that they have a say in the executive presentation. The task force develops the structure of the presentation. Their recommendations are arranged in three categories: administrative/policy issues, major system efforts, and small projects. It is not difficult to achieve this breakdown.

A task force member is chosen as spokesperson. Remember, the moderator is no more than a facilitator. The task force owns the project, and they will speak for the analysis and recommendations through the spokesperson.

In closing task force analysis, a critique of the exercise is conducted. The critique consists of no more than ten to twelve questions answered on a scale of one (low) to five (high). Typical questions might be “To what extent did you find the RNP process useful?" or "Did the RNP study change your opinion of the requirements for this project?" A blank area is included for free form comments and suggestions to improve the procedure. The critique verifies the value of the study and opens the methodology for enhancement.

## The executive presentation

The executive presentation requires four to six hours to complete. It is an oral report, led by the spokesperson, and task force members are present for responding to executive questions. Presentation aids are a matter of personal choice. Use whatever works best in your organization.

Findings from the task force analysis are highlighted, and changes in the importance of problems are explained. The recommendations are presented in a framework of business process impact, and the executive sponsor is asked for permission to proceed with the recommendations.

At this point, the RNP study is complete.

## Observations

As far as the mechanics of the methodology are concerned, three observations are in order.

First, it is important to guard against moderator bias. The role of the moderator is to act as a catalyst. This must be done without injecting views on any of the study topics. Violating this rule is tantamount to 'leading the witness.' The moderator must be content with recording facts, orchestrating the process, and facilitating the activities of the task force.

Second, some concern has been voiced regarding the amount of chart work. The authors acknowledge that issues are looked at from several viewpoints; these perspectives are important. On the other hand, an RNP study achieves a significant requirements focus within three weeks. Given the commitment in time required by some alternative approaches, the chart work seems to be well worth the investment.

Third, we have found that the use of a personal computer or information center spread-sheet program aids in the recording and review of the lists and matrices. Some of the materials developed in this fashion are then usable at the executive presentation.

## Conclusion

To date, we have conducted two RNP studies. The recommendations arising from those studies have been accepted and implemented. As a result, we have initiated software evaluation, vendor selection, and functional design activities with greater confidence and direction than previously experienced,. In both cases, RNP has proven to be an effective and economic structure for determining high level MIS project definition.

The concept of operational rank is valuable. Relative ranking schemes aside, three potential pitfalls are avoided by using RNP:

1. A new system defined on the basis of a free-for-all rank may incorrectly identify the most critical problem(s) from an operational standpoint. In our example, a number one free-for-all problem (history tracking) is only a component of the more important issues in the number one operational problem, 'what-if' analysis (see Table 7).

2. The free-for-all approach may cause inappropriate attention on the wrong business process. In our example, the most troubled process from an operational standpoint is product requirements; in free-for-all, it is follow-up.

3. In using free-for-all, there is a possibility of concentrating efforts in the wrong departments as further systems analysis progresses. This can be seen by reviewing the responsibility content in the organization-process matrix.

This does not imply that all the other MIS requirement definition methods are guilty of neglect. It serves only to emphasize the necessity of taking an analysis past the list of problems stage, and how effectively RNP handles that need.

In developing RNP, our purpose was to create a method for high level MIS project definition that was applicable early in the development cycle, did not require undue expenditures of manpower, and did not miss important aspects of a businesses problem. We believe we have succeeded.
