## Abstract

An emerging body of research in group decision support systems (GDSS) provides evidence that computer technology can and does impact the quality of decision making in groups. Most GDSS research is oriented toward examining the effects of a computer system on group outcomes, typically decision quality or group consensus, with the process itself often treated as a “black box.” The research reported in this article addresses the need for a closer, micro-level examination of group process. An important group variable, namely influence behavior, was isolated and examined at various levels and by multiple methods.

A model of specific GDSS effects on influence behavior was developed, based on an information exchange view of decision making and on the impact of a GDSS as a communication channel. Based on the research questions of interest in the study, several propositions and hypotheses were advanced and empirically tested on a specific implementation of a GDSS. Results were analyzed both quantitatively and qualitatively. The major empirical findings of the study showed no significant difference between the overall amount of influence behavior attempted in computer-supported versus unsupported groups, although significant differences were found in the pattern of influence behaviors, i.e., the different types of behaviors used. In addition, the distribution of influence behavior was more even in GDSS groups than in unsupported groups on one of two measures used. Empirical findings partially supported the research model, with indications that decision-making groups need more active guidance in understanding how to adapt computer support technology to their view of decision-making processes.

Keywords: Group decision support systems, decision support, decision making, interaction process

## Introduction

Group decision support systems (GDSS) are computer-based systems concerned with group communication and how to improve it (Bui and Jarke, 1986; DeSanctis and Gallupe, 1987; Poole and DeSanctis, 1987). For this reason, research on GDSS and its impacts can be rooted in theories of communication, which explain the mechanisms and processes involved in the exchange of ideas (see Bormann, 1975). According to basic communication theory, the essential elements of any communication system are a source, a channel, and a receiver (Cherry, 1978; Shannon and Weaver, 1949). The source selects signs and encodes them as physical signals, which the receiver decodes according to a shared sign system. A communication channel may be described by particular properties such as its capacity, whether noise is present, and so on. The basic model of two-person communication can be extended by the addition of multiple communicators and multiple channels. Individuals may communicate through non-verbal channels, for instance, by using pitch, intonation, or gestures to supplement or substitute for verbal messages. Thus, a communication channel may be more appropriately thought of as a set of potential channels, and the choices that individuals make about the use of those potential channels affect the characteristics of messages sent. Natural, face-to-face human interaction contains verbal and non-verbal communication channels. Additional channels may be made available through particular kinds of technology, as in the case of drawing.

During interpersonal communication, members of a group attempt to exert influence over one another through available channels. Thus, channels are important because they are mechanisms for influence. Full and egalitarian participation by all group members is generally considered desirable to ensure complete information exchange and more vigilant information processing (Janis and Mann, 1977), at least in tasks where such exchange is desirable. The extent to which egalitarian participation is possible in a group is a function of the extent to which potential communication channels allow actual use by all members of a group for providing and receiving information.

One of the distinctive characteristics of computer-supported, face-to-face meetings is that they provide an electronic channel of communication to supplement natural human interaction. A rationale underlying the potential effectiveness of GDSSs has been that the electronic communication channel promotes more equal participation and influence on outcomes by group members (DeSanctis and Gallupe, 1987; Huber, 1984a). The purpose of the research described here was to develop a conceptual model of specific GDSS effects on influence behavior and empirically test several research questions suggested by the model. This study was motivated by the importance of opening the “black box of process” (Todd and Benbasat, 1987) and looking at how a GDSS impacts group interaction. The focus is not on outcomes but on the group interaction process itself. This study builds on tradition in small group research by utilizing proven research methods and examining an important group variable, and it extends knowledge of group behavior by applying these techniques to the new area of GDSS usage.

## The Research Model: GDSS Effects on Influence

The study of influence has a long tradition. Several lines of research can be distinguished. Perhaps the longest is research on persuasion, starting with the studies of Hovland and colleagues in the 1950s (e.g., Hovland, et al., 1953) and extending through to present studies of persuasion, cognition, and influence (e.g., Petty, et al., 1981; Roloff and Miller, 1980). A second line consists of studies of interpersonal influence and compliance-gaining strategies (e.g., Marwell and Schmitt, 1967; Seibold, et al., 1985). Influence has also been studied as one aspect of leadership behavior (Stogdill, 1974). Finally, there is a newer tradition of research concerned with influence over group processes (Putnam, 1981). While all four perspectives hold important insights for GDSS research, we decided to focus on influence over group processes for two reasons. First, since the basic premise of a GDSS is to aid the group in structuring and managing its process, processual influence seemed the logical starting point. Differences in influence between computer-supported and unsupported groups were most likely to emerge on this process dimension, if they emerged at all. Second, the management of processual influence was expected to be an important element of the effectiveness of GDSSs in practical situations. Understanding patterns of process influence could help in designing more effective GDSSs.

Figure 1 shows a model of specific GDSS effects on influence behavior. The model is based on the work of Hoffman (1965) and others (McGrath, 1984) who have articulated the nature of influence processes in group interaction and outcomes. Members start their “group life” as equals, in the sense that they have equal access to a set of potential communication channels, as defined by the technological environment. Over time, a differential pattern of actual channel use may develop, given different preferences or skill levels with respect to channel use. For example, one person may be more adept at verbal expression than another, or may prefer speaking to writing. As a group develops over time, its characteristic use of communication channels is reinforced and becomes a part of the group’s structure. In this study, the technological environment consisted of a GDSS provided as a support tool for face-to-face, as opposed to dispersed, meetings. The standing group structure was characterized by group sizes of three and four members and an existing influence structure defined by the group’s prior meeting history. Four channels of communication were available: verbal, non-verbal, written, and electronic, with each channel defined according to the skills required to use it, its features, capacity, and impact on the receiver.

Consider the following example of how this specific model explains the interplay of GDSS technology with influence patterns in groups. First, a new communication channel is made available to a group via the introduction of a GDSS. One feature of the specific GDSS is that it allows for anonymous entry and display of ideas. Within the group structure are individuals who dominate other channels, and whose messages, therefore, dominate the interpersonal exchange process. That dominance reinforces the existing group structure. With the introduction of the GDSS, a highly available, low exposure channel is made available, and otherwise non-participating members begin to use it. These members begin participating in the interpersonal exchange process by expressing their ideas and arguments via the system and by making procedural moves with the system. Examples of the latter might include moving the group from one agenda item to another or changing a screen display to influence the topic of discussion. Participation loops back to change the standing group structure by changing role relationships and members' expectations about one another. The loop from the exchange process back to the GDSS would occur with an intelligent system that is able to monitor group process and change itself to match member needs. The present research focuses on the use of these four communication channels as revealed in the group interaction process; it does not examine the looping process back into group structure or the technological environment. Examination of the looping process would require analysis of group process over time, an analysis intended for later work that will build upon the more fundamental work reported here.

## The Research Constructs

As used in this study, influence implies attempts to move, affect, or determine a course of action (Bass, 1981); the influence construct has a heavy procedural component. Behaviors that represent influence in decision-making groups might include agenda-setting, discussion of group goals, summarizing of group opinions, attempts to implement action, and general discussion of process. These behaviors can be defined and captured at the micro-level of verbal interaction. But influence has an aggregate, group-level meaning as well; groups may resist or ignore individual influence attempts or give heavier weight to non-verbal cues than to verbal ones. To capture the full richness of the influence construct, this analysis looks at influence from several perspectives.

Expressed influence behavior $^{1}$ is defined as individual verbal, non-verbal, written, and/or electronic actions that attempt to affect or determine the course of group behavior. $^{2}$ Thus, influence behavior has a strong goal orientation component. Several means through which influence behavior may be attempted are defined as follows. Verbal behavior consists of the use of language as traditionally defined (the form and meaning of the spoken word). Non-verbal behavior typically means specific categories of action other than the use of language, for example, paralanguage, body motion, proxemics, body contact, and use of artifacts (Duncan and Fiske, 1977). An additional category of non-verbal behavior used in this article is non-verbal procedural behavior, defined as actions that reorient or change the group's visual attention through use of supplementary visual aids. Non-verbal procedural behavior may be either electronic (through use of a public screen driven by a GDSS) or manual (through use of a flipchart). Group messaging behavior consists of the use of written messages to the group, either via writing comments on a flipchart or sending group messages electronically to a public screen.

Not all uses of the above behaviors constitute influential uses; influence behaviors were defined as only those actions that attempt to affect or determine the course of group behavior. One important way that influence is manifested in verbal behavior is through the use of procedural statements (Putnam, 1981). Five specific categories of verbal behavior are of interest in this study:

1. initiation behavior, which deals with setting or initiating agendas;

2. goal-oriented behavior, concerning group goals or group jurisdiction;

3. integrative behavior, which summarizes and integrates contributions of others;

4. implementation behavior, representing an action orientation toward getting the task done; and

5. process behavior, dealing with procedural movement of the group.

These five categories are clearly a subset of all possible influence behaviors, but are of particular interest here for several reasons. These behaviors have primarily a procedural orientation, and interaction and discussion on procedural matters are related to effectiveness in group decision making, as shown by Hirokawa (1980). Procedural communication “occupies a substantial portion of group talk time, performs vital meta-message functions, and serves as indices of leadership emergence and decision-making processes” (Putnam, 1981, p. 332). In addition, a GDSS is expected to have its greatest impact in such procedural areas through emphasis on structuring behavior.

To summarize, influence behavior is defined in terms of verbal, non-verbal, and group messaging actions that attempt to determine or affect the course of group behavior. Verbal behavior that represents influence can be subdivided into five categories of procedural statements. One further construct builds on these definitions. The pattern of influence behavior is a broad concept that represents the variation among group members in their use of different means of influence (verbal, non-verbal, group messaging) and in their use of different categories within those means (the five categories within verbal behavior). The concept of a pattern of influence behavior allows for an aggregate analysis of influence and permits integration of multiple views of expressed influence behavior. These behaviors are analyzed in terms of amounts, i.e., sums of stated behaviors of group members.

Influence behavior is also analyzed in terms of its distribution, or variance among group members. Two concepts — prominence and achievement — are used. Prominence is the degree to which a group member is individually prominent, forceful, or individually assertive. Achievement is the degree to which a group member is goal-oriented, aids attainment of group goals, or shows high task ability (Schneider, 1970).

## Hypotheses

Several critical aspects of a GDSS explain its potential impacts on influence behavior: (1) the technology provides a new channel of communication; (2) that channel is equally available to all (giving continuous air time to each group member), thus we would expect group members to take advantage of it; $^{3}$ (3) the anonymity of the channel means that external status characteristics are neutralized (note, however, that there may be new forms of status influence that arise because of channel characteristics); (4) the anonymity of the channel also means that the group focuses more on issues and ideas rather than on the persons who present them; (5) inhibitions are typically lessened over a continuously available channel; and (6) the GDSS provides a particular kind of structure to the group interaction. The research questions formulated from these observations and the conceptual model are stated in the form of propositions and hypotheses. The effects of computer support are proposed in terms of their contrasts with manual, i.e., non-computer-based, support systems for groups.

## Proposition A:

The total amount of influence behavior expressed in computer-supported (CS) groups will not differ from that expressed in manual groups, but the distribution of that behavior across group members will be more egalitarian in CS groups than in manual groups.

H1: The total amount of expressed influence behavior in CS groups will not be significantly different from the amount of expressed influence behavior in manual groups.

H2: The distribution of expressed influence behavior across members in CS groups will be more even than the distribution across members in manual groups.

A new communication channel presents an added means by which influence may be attempted, hence more influence attempts may be expected when that added channel (the GDSS) is presented to a group. However, it may be that groups use the added channel as a substitute for existing channels, and that the total amount of influence behavior remains the same. Given the lack of evidence to support a strong assertion in either direction, hypothesis 1 is stated in non-directional form.

Several attributes of the electronic channel support a directional expectation for hypothesis 2, i.e., that distribution of influence behavior will be more even in CS groups. Equal availability of the GDSS channel supports the expectation of equal distribution of influence attempts through that channel. The anonymity of the channel presents additional justification, since external status characteristics are not carried on the electronic channel, and inhibitions are typically lessened over an anonymous channel. The GDSS also takes over some of the task information processing for the group. This removes some potential for informational influence that would have been exerted by the individual who otherwise would have done that processing.

## Proposition B:

The pattern of influence behavior in CS groups will differ from the pattern of influence behavior in manual groups.

The pattern of influence behavior in a group is defined as the variation among members in their use of different means and categories of influence. Influence behavior patterns are expected to vary according to the communication channels available to a group. Given that computer support represents a different and additional channel of communication among members, one would expect different patterns to emerge with groups that are supported by computer technology.

Proposition B is exploratory in nature. Through both quantitative and qualitative methods, CS groups are compared with manual groups in terms of relative loads on various means and categories of influence behavior. Patterns in the use of means and categories of influence behavior between CS versus manual groups are examined. CS groups, for instance, were expected to use more process behavior than manual groups, based on the power of the technological intervention being stronger in computer groups.

## Research Design, Variables, and Measurement

## Overview of the experiment

Given the early state of knowledge of GDSS effects on group process, a controlled laboratory experiment was undertaken. Three- and four-member groups met for one session each. $^{4}$ Their task was to evaluate a set of applicants to an international studies program, agreeing on a success rating for each applicant and selecting a subset of the applicants for admission. Some groups had computer support, while others did not. Computer support consisted of terminals for each group member, a public display screen, and specially designed software that provided structure for the meeting and allowed individual entry and group display of ideas, evaluations, and so on. Data was gathered from videotapes of the sessions, self-report instruments, and computer logs. Properties of the environment, outside of technological support, were controlled to the extent possible, with all groups meeting in a conference room setting. Length and type of group history and subject characteristics such as age and educational level were similar for all groups.

Subjects consisted of undergraduate (junior and senior) business majors enrolled in a daytime introductory information systems course at a large university. Participation in the research was voluntary, but for those who did participate, course credit worth 5% of the grade was given. Bormann (1970) and McGrath (1984) have pointed out the drawbacks of using zero-history groups and the inconsistent findings that may result. Therefore, subjects were chosen according to their availability and a guarantee of ongoing future interaction and continuing commitment to the group. Groups were assigned randomly to experimental conditions. Subjects chose their own group membership at the beginning of the school term, but the great majority of subjects were strangers to one another and selection was effectively random. The experiment took place about halfway through the term, after the groups had met several times on other class projects.

## Research design and variables

Type of technological support was the independent variable, with two levels. Half of the groups were provided with a computer-supported (CS) rational problem-solving agenda, while the remaining groups were provided with the same agenda in a manual (paper-and-pencil) form. Both types of groups received the same group decision support features, including collection and display of ideas, rating, ranking, and voting. In manual groups, a paper agenda substituted for the computer agenda and a flipchart substituted for the public screen. The structure of the experiment itself was kept as equivalent as possible between the two treatment conditions. Both CS and manual groups were given training on use of the agenda.

A number of baseline groups were run to assure the appropriateness of the structure given in the two experimental conditions. Baseline groups were not provided with any computer or manual technological support. They constituted naturally interacting groups, free of any intervention or instruction on how to approach the task. Task instructions and task materials (other than agenda instructions) for baseline groups were equivalent to experimental groups. Baseline groups were contrasted with the manual groups, but not included in the data analysis for testing of the hypotheses since the benefit of a second type of control group was not sufficient to justify the high cost of the coding techniques used for analysis.

The dependent variables were: amount of influence behavior, distribution of influence behavior, and pattern of influence behavior. Three measurement methods were used:

Method 1: coding of verbal acts of each individual into categories representing verbal influence behavior (the word "act" denotes one instance of a behavior, usually an utterance of speaking turn by a group member);

Method 2: observer rating of members' influence behavior within the group;

Method 3: measurement of influential use of the CS or manual structure equivalent via:

3a: counting acts of non-verbal procedural behavior; and

3b: counting acts of group messaging.

Total amount of expressed influence behavior was measured via Methods 1 and 3, and operationalized as the arithmetic sum of verbal influence acts, non-verbal procedural acts, and group messaging acts. Verbal influence acts were captured via the Procedural Message Coding System described in the next section. Non-verbal procedural behavior consisted of influence attempts that could not be captured through verbal coding. The objective was to capture communication channel use beyond the spoken word. In CS groups, non-verbal procedural behavior included changing the current display on the public screen or deleting an item (a problem, criterion, or alternative definition) from the system. The frequency of these acts was counted via analysis of computer log files. In manual groups, the corresponding behaviors were counted by observation of videotapes. Behaviors consisted of changing the currently displayed page of the flipchart or crossing out material on the flipchart. Group messaging meant writing messages for public display. In CS groups, an individual could type a message at the private terminal for anonymous display on the public screen. In manual groups, the message could be written on the flipchart. A group message is influential because it focuses the attention of the entire group. This category was separated out from other messages in part to see how groups would use the group communication feature of the computer system. Distribution of expressed influence behavior was measured via Method 2 and operationalized as the within-group variance of influence ratings that were assigned to individual group members by unbiased observers, as detailed below. Pattern of influence behavior was measured via Method 1 and operationalized as the within-group variance of amount of verbal influence behavior by category, i.e., the arithmetic sum of expressed influence behavior within each of five influence categories in the Procedural Message Coding System.

## Verbal Interaction Coding System

The coding system used in Method 1 was the Procedural Message Coding System developed and validated by Putnam (1981) as a measure of procedural messages during ongoing group interaction. Procedural messages are statements that guide the work of the group, including what the group is doing, where it is going, and what it should do. Putnam's first five categories record different types of procedural messages, while the latter five categories record different levels of continuing interaction (e.g., continuing task-related discussion, changing from detail to detail, digression to socio-emotional issues). In this study, every verbal act was coded into one of Putnam's 10 categories, but only the procedural messages (the first five categories) formed the basis for influence measurement, given the reasoning outlined earlier. The focus was on the first five categories because these are explicit comments that attempt to manage the group process. The five categories of procedural messages were:

1. Initiation messages — requesting or suggesting deadlines, agendas, or lists of activities. Examples: "Let the meeting begin." "Let's start with the applicants who are obvious candidates for rejection." "Let's come back to that one later." "OK, go back to define alternatives."

2. Goal-oriented messages — requesting or making statements about group goals or group jurisdiction. Examples: “We are only supposed to pick three applicants.” “That’s not part of our job here.” “Is this what we are supposed to be doing?” “We have to come up with collegiate success scores on all these.”

3. Integrative messages — summarizing and integrating contributions. Examples: "Essentially, it looks like we agree that applicant A is out of the picture." "Based on what everybody said, this applicant meets our criteria." "I think we have all been saying the same thing here."

4. Implementation messages — suggesting or requesting division of labor or implementation of a course of action in getting the task done. Examples: "I would give this one a rating of 30." "Should I record a reject for the second applicant?" "Why don't you write on the flipchart." "Let's delete applicant #3."

5. Process messages — requesting or suggesting procedural direction. Examples: “Can we go on now?” “Does everyone understand what we’re doing here?” “I think we should move ahead.” “Do you agree?” “I hope we’re doing this right.”

Two coders worked together using this system until they achieved reliability, then completed the remaining transcripts separately. $^{5}$ Inter-rater reliabilities for the five categories, calculated as a percent of agreement, ranged from .80 to .91. An overall reliability of 0.839 was calculated via Scott's pi (Scott, 1955), which corrects the possibility of chance agreement. These reliabilities are well within the standards of prior research (Lasswell, et al., 1952; Thorndike, 1950).

It should be noted that the Procedural Message Coding System, as implemented, codes influence bids rather than actual influence per se. That is, the system codes members' attempts to influence the group by setting goals, agendas, procedures, etc. There is no indication of whether these attempts are successful or whether the group rejects them. To get some indication of the degree to which bids for influence represented actual influence, we analyzed members' reactions to influence behavior in six randomly selected groups. The second author and another coder classified responses to procedural influence behaviors into four categories: (1) acceptance of influence bid; (2) open rejection of influence bid; (3) group ignores influence bid (cases in which the bid was made but not acknowledged by the group); and (4) ambiguous response (in which no determination of group response could be inferred). In the few cases where the two coders disagreed (less than 5% of the cases), classifications were decided by consensus. For 383 bids coded from the six groups, 274 (71%) were accepted, 62 (16%) were rejected or ignored, and 47 (13%) received ambiguous responses. This suggests that, in general, influence bids also represented actual influence in the groups.

## Observer Rating System

The observer rating system complemented the micro-level interaction coding with a more group-oriented level of analysis. Two independent raters observed selected segments of videotapes of the group meetings and rated group member behavior on two scales of influence behavior: prominence and achievement (Schneider, 1970). The raters also assigned an overall rating of influence for each group member. The ratings were based on observation of 3 five-minute intervals of interaction from each third of each discussion. These were global ratings, designed to capture the general character of interaction rather than the cumulative number of events captured by the procedural message coding system. Therefore, it was not necessary to code the entire transcript. $^{6}$

The “Spread N” rating technique was used (Bass, 1949), in which N votes were assigned to N group members in the appropriate combination. If a rater thought one group member completely outclassed all of the others on prominence behavior, for example, then that member would receive all N votes, while the rest of the members received zero votes. If all members were judged equal, they all received one vote. Any other distribution of the total votes was possible. For example, in a four-person group, a completely even distribution of influence would be a 1-1-1-1 pattern, while complete domination by one individual would be a 4-0-0-0 pattern. Inter-rater agreement was calculated as 0.823 using Cronbach’s alpha, so the two raters’ judgments were averaged together. The distribution of influence behavior was then calculated as the within-group variance of the averaged influence votes on each scale. The variances were converted into an index for ease of comprehension (index value = 1 - [actual std.dev./std.dev. where 1 person completely dominates]). $^{7}$ Indexes on three observation scales were calculated: one for prominence, one for achievement, and one overall. A value of 1.00 in the index represents a completely even distribution of influence, and a value of 0.00 represents complete domination by one member.

## Research Task and Procedures

Virtually all group researchers agree that group performance cannot be studied generically without regard to task, and that an individual's performance is without question affected by the type and characteristics of the task (McGrath, 1984). McGrath provides an integrated task framework, called a task circumplex, that formed the theoretical basis for task selection in this study, as well as in the larger research project of which this study is a part (Poole and DeSanctis, 1987).

12-category version suggested that the fewer the categories, the less the proportion of interaction that had to be sampled to achieve a given level of validity. For the two-scale rating system used in this study, Billings, et al.'s results suggest that the samples utilized in this study were sufficient to yield valid indices of interaction.

The research reported here used an intellective type of task, which has potential for eliciting all stages of group behavior. Alternatives were known and had to be evaluated by group members, and the group had to find a correct solution to a problem. In the CS condition, a GDSS aided the group in their search for that “right” answer by providing alternatives generation and evaluation capabilities. The type of task used in this study was expected to benefit from full participation as individuals cross-checked one another’s answers and applied their full resources to the problem.

The task required subjects to choose from an applicant pool a given number of individuals to be admitted to an international studies program. $^{8}$ Subjects were provided with a profile of each applicant consisting of scores on four personality measures that related to success in the program, as well as two distractor variables not directly related to success in the program. The four personality measures, or cues, were scores on tests of attitudes toward self-concept, social success, premarital sex, and independence. The distractor variables were gender of applicant and whether the applicant had travelled abroad previously. These variables were included to make the task sufficiently difficult and to make it parallel to other tasks in the larger research project. Each of the four personality measures was more or less linearly related to program success, although the degree of that linear function varied for each measure. Thus, the causal knowledge of the components of success in the program was certain, but its particular nature had to be discovered or learned by subjects. Decision quality could then be measured by the difference between the predicted success rating for each applicant as assigned by the group and the correct predicted success rating as determined by the four personality measures.

The first step of the experiment was a training session, on an individual basis, to give subjects practice with the relationship between personality measures and program success. Each subject was given a set of 50 cards; the front of each card showed the scores on the cue and distractor variables on a hypothetical applicant. The subject predicted program success from this information, and then checked the accuracy of the prediction from the actual program success score on the back of the card. The second step of the experiment was also performed individually. Subjects were given test information on a new set of 30 applicants and were requested to make predictions on success and admission decisions. No feedback was provided in this step.

The final phase was performed in groups. CS groups were trained to use the computer system and manual groups were trained to use their paper-and-pencil agenda. All groups were instructed that they were required to use the provided agenda, but that they could use it in any order or way they wished. Manual groups were instructed to use the flipchart to record group ideas or decisions. It should be noted that although both types of groups were instructed at the beginning of the meeting to use their support systems, the experimenter did not intervene during a meeting if groups failed to comply with instructions. In short, both CS and manual groups had access to a support system and the communication channels associated with it, but were given freedom during the meeting to use it as they wished. Certainly, more usage could have been compelled through experimenter intervention, but the objective here was to investigate how groups used the technology in a reasonably free choice environment — an environment that was parallel to the way such technology would be encountered and used in organizations. This allowed us to measure the amount of choice of access to varying communication channels, which was an important dependent variable. Following training, each group was given applicant information as before and was requested to agree on a predicted program success score and an admission decision for each applicant. Only three out of 16 possible applicants could be admitted, with one assigned to a waiting list. This task was pilot-tested over a period of four months on nearly 60 subjects to ensure appropriate difficulty and differential learning effects.

A warm-up task was included in the procedure to observe evenness of participation by group members prior to the experimental treatment. Since subjects were live groups with an existing history, prudence dictated an observation of their influence structure prior to applying the GDSS intervention. The warm-up task (taken from Dickson, 1965) was required for all groups and consisted of a short discussion on a task that required the group to agree on a paint vendor for a hypothetical company.

## Experimental Setting

The setting for the CS groups consisted of a specially-designed and equipped “decision lab” containing a conference table, four chairs with a private computer terminal next to each chair, and a large-screen monitor at one end of the table, which acted as the public screen. A camera with a wide-angle lens was placed in one corner of the room. A chair for the experimenter was in the opposite corner, out of the way of group interaction. Manual groups met in a different room from the CS groups, but the physical configuration was quite similar, with the exception that no computer equipment was present. A flipchart took the place of the public screen.

The computer support system, called “Software Aided Meeting Management” (SAMM), $^{9}$ was developed by a research team at the University of Minnesota for a series of studies on the impact of computer-supported meetings. SAMM is written in the C programming language and runs under the Unix operating system. The system is described in DeSanctis and Dickson (1987) and DeSanctis, et al. (1988), and is being used in a long-term program of research at Minnesota (Poole and DeSanctis, 1987). Figure 2 shows the main agenda of the version used in this study.

Manual groups had no electronic channel and no automatic calculation of their votes and ratings, as was provided by SAMM.

## Results

A total of 32 groups was analyzed (14 in each of the two experimental cells, plus four baseline groups). The average age of subjects was just under 23 years. Forty-nine percent of the subjects were female and 51 percent were male. On average, subjects' level of experience in working in groups was 2.33 on a 5-point scale (5 = "very high"; 1 = "very low"); subjects averaged 1-3/4 years of full-time work experience. The groups had met on previous class assignments an average of 6.4 times prior to the experiment.

## Statistical analyses

H1: The total amount of expressed influence behavior in CS-supported groups will not be significantly different from the amount of expressed influence behavior in manual groups.

The total amount of influence behavior was measured by summing (1) verbal acts in the five categories of Putnam's procedural messages coding system, (2) non-verbal procedural acts, and (3) group messaging acts. The higher the number, the more influence behavior took place. Table 1 shows the descriptive statistics for total amount of influence behavior.

A non-directional t-test of the difference in means resulted in p = .134 (t = 1.55, df = 26, power = .34). The t-test results are consistent with the first hypothesis, providing support for the expected finding that there is no significant difference between the total amount of influence behavior expressed in CS groups versus manual groups. It is possible, however, that there was not sufficient power to detect this effect, so we cannot be absolutely certain whether this conclusion holds. Analysis of three subsets of the total amount of influence behavior shows that manual groups rarely used the public communication devices provided. CS groups averaged 13.7 non-verbal procedural acts, while manual groups averaged only 0.6. Group messaging averaged 0.6 by CS groups and zero by manual groups.

## H2: The distribution of expressed influence behavior across members in CS groups will be more even than the distribution across members in manual groups.

The distribution of influence behavior was measured by calculating the within-group variance of the average influence ratings that were assigned to individual group members by observers of the session videotapes. Table 2 shows the descriptive statistics for prominence, achievement, and overall indexes. Table 3 shows the results of directional t-tests.

The t-test results from Table 2 support the second hypothesis that the distribution of influence in CS groups is more even than in manual groups with respect to the prominence measure. The results fail to support the second hypothesis with respect to achievement and overall measures, but low power (less than .20) was a problem with the achievement measure. As a matter of interest, we also tested the distribution of influence via amount of verbal influence behavior. A directional t-test of the difference between CS and manual groups on within-group variance of verbal influence acts showed no significant difference $t=0.84, df=26, p=0.20$ .

## Qualitative analyses

Qualitative analyses, summarized in the form of brief case studies, were developed for six groups: two CS, two manual, and two baseline. CS groups were arbitrarily named C1 and C2, manual groups were M1 and M2, and baseline groups were B1 and B2. Selection criteria for the case study groups were: (1) at least one group from each treatment should be a high-performing group (performance of the group exceeded highest-performing individual), and (2) at least one group from each treatment should have a relatively uneven distribution of influence (overall index less than .70) and the other group a relatively equal distribution (overall index greater than .80). The reason for these criteria was to explore differences between groups of even and uneven influence distribution, as well as look for links to high performance. The qualitative analyses addressed four questions:

1. Through what channels and in what ways was influence exerted and distributed in the group? Possible forms of influence include status dominance, verbal skill, verbalization amount, non-verbal skill, procedural skills, facility with the electronic channel, quality of argumentation, and so on.

2. How did the group react to influence attempts on the part of individual members or coalitions? Did the group generally ignore, accept, or revise influence moves?

3. What was the group's decision path? How did the group move toward its decision?

4. What was the group's view of the structure (in manual groups) or computer system (in CS groups? How did the group characterize the structure/system and how, if at all, did members integrate it into their decision-making process?

In baseline group B1, an essentially egalitarian group was able to improve group over individual performance by simple pooling of individual opinions, without substantive discussion or extensive group interaction. In baseline group B2, two competing members exerted influence over the rest of the group, and in the course of their competition, improved group performance by bringing issues up for discussion and urging participation. Both groups had relatively high performance and followed similar paths, starting with discussion of criteria, moving to scoring of applicants one by one, and finishing with deciding which applicants to admit. But the nature of their interaction and influence behavior differed markedly. Group B2, with its uneven influence distribution but greater interaction, was able to improve performance more than Group B1. Equal exercise of influence did not necessarily enhance performance, although it may have had other beneficial group effects.

In manual groups M1 and M2, confusion over use of the provided structure (the agenda) dampened the group's smooth functioning. The key seemed to be a difference in views — of the agenda as instructions to be carried out in rote fashion, versus the agenda as a tool to apply with judgment under appropriate conditions. Overall, performance in the manual groups was generally less than in either baseline or CS groups, and the question arose whether the provided structure was suboptimal. The case studies of groups M1 and M2 suggested that the problem was in the use of the agenda rather than a deficiency in its form.

In CS groups C1 and C2, uses of the system varied. C1 accepted the system as a tool and used it advantageously to surface discussion and support group process; C1's performance was also very high. C2 ignored the system's potential, defining it as inappropriate for its task, and group performance was quite poor. Group C2's reaction to the system suggested that the task used in the present research did not always show the full power of the system because the task's boundaries were well-defined. Criteria and alternatives were already defined for the group, thus focussing the usefulness of the system on the evaluation process. This explains why a group might perceive the system as merely an elaborate calculator.

In manual and baseline groups, influence was expressed primarily through the verbal channel, in verbal skills and procedural suggestions, whereas in CS group C1, the computer system was used for influence in procedural moves and directing group attention. The qualitative analysis pointed to several factors about the use of manual structure versus the computer system. The structure provided to manual groups may have interfered with their performance to the extent that they were unable to appropriate the structure as a decision process tool. Rote following of agenda steps was not conducive to performance.

CS groups varied in how they appropriated the system for their use. In some cases, the computer system was used to influence the group through redirection and focussing of group attention. But a lack of acceptance of the system as a tool for decision support occurred in one group, resulting in confusion as to which direction to take. Apparently, training on the computer system was not so much an issue as how the group viewed and accepted the system.

## Discussion

This study began with the development of a conceptual model to explain the effects of a GDSS on influence behavior in groups. The total amount of influence behavior in CS groups was not significantly different from manual groups, although the absence of an effect here could be attributable to insufficient power. The study also found distribution of influence to be more even in CS groups on prominence, as we anticipated. Several counterarguments to these results might be addressed. First, did the CS groups receive sufficient training in the use of the SAMM system? The training period for the CS groups lasted just over 20 minutes; Watson, et al. (1988) and our experience with SAMM suggest that 20 minutes was sufficient training for this software. Second is the issue of low power on statistical tests. If we presume similar effect size, the fact that a significant difference was observed on some measures (prominence and four of Putnam's categories) implies sufficient power to detect an effect, and we have confidence in the results for hypothesis on total amount of influence. Finally, was there sufficient usage of the system? We provided access and did not force usage — because the study aimed to give groups the opportunity to use or not use the system, as they saw fit, and the question was whether groups opted to use the system. This was important for the experimental manipulation. The fact that usage was fairly low was not surprising, since the SAMM system does not substitute for discussion but rather supplements and provides a platform for discussion. This type of computer support represents a conservative test of the hypotheses versus a system where individuals would have to do a lot of keyboarding before they could talk.

Nothwithstanding the issues addressed above, we believe the results provide support for our model of the impact of the GDSS on influence behavior in groups. The statistical findings supported the hypothesis of no difference in the total amount of influence behavior in CS versus manual groups. However, CS groups did use significantly more non-verbal procedural behavior than did manual groups. In CS groups, members used the system to communicate with one another and to provide a focus for group attention. Manual groups used the flipchart rarely and in a mechanical fashion — as a recording device rather than a communication or influence device.

The findings on distribution of influence were mixed. Observer ratings on prominence showed that CS groups had a significantly more even distribution of influence than did manual groups. Observer ratings on achievement were in the expected direction but the difference was not significant. For CS groups specifically, the achievement index showed a significant movement toward a more even distribution from the warm-up to the experimental task. The fact that prominence ratings showed a significant effect is particularly interesting. Prominence represented the degree to which a group member was individually prominent, forceful, or assertive. Members who spoke frequently or caused others to react were rated high on prominence. The presence of the electronic communication channel, which was continuously available to each member, evened out individual assertiveness. These results are consistent with the underlying model for this research and support the current thinking among researchers that GDSS use should encourage even participation in group decision making (Applegate, 1986; DeSanctis and Gallupe, 1987; Huber, 1984a). In groups supported with manual forms of structure, there may be individuals who do not choose to participate fully, for reasons that might include their inhibitions or lack of skill in expressing themselves over available communication channels. The electronic channel in CS groups is easily available, and its anonymity should represent a low threat form of communication, providing an opportunity for expression of ideas and arguments that would otherwise not exist. Individuals who would not otherwise assert themselves through certain channels such as verbal, can do so through the electronic channel, thus evening out what might have been unbalanced prominence in a group. The lack of a significant difference on the achievement measure might be explained by the generally high motivation and task orientation of all group members in this study. Most individuals showed goal orientation and attainment by working individually and together to decide on the predicted scores required by the task instructions.

Several significant differences were observed in this study between CS and manual groups in their use of influence. CS groups had a significantly higher proportion of verbal influence behavior to total talk than did manual groups. Given that there was no significant difference between the two types of groups in amount of verbal influence behavior, this test indicates that CS groups spent proportionately more of their communication attending to procedures than did manual groups and therefore less time in substantive task discussion. This could mean that the CS groups had more problems getting organized than did manual groups, which resulted in relatively more procedural discussion. It may also indicate that substantive task discussion in the CS groups was more efficient or effective; this, in turn, implies that the procedural efforts of CS groups were more effective than those of manual groups. In view of the fact that CS groups outperformed manual groups (see Zigurs, 1987), the latter interpretation seems more plausible.

Another aspect of the difference in pattern of influence between groups is in the use of categories of verbal influence behavior. CS groups used significantly more initiation and process statements than did manual groups. Process statements reflect requests or suggestions for procedural direction and are concerned with how to use the structure. Initiation statements consist of requests or suggestions for action and are concerned with acting on an agenda item or activity. Both a concern with how to use a group support system (the GDSS, in this case) and taking subsequent action through that system are desirable, again to the extent that group outcomes are enhanced.

Another significant difference between CS and manual groups was that CS groups used significantly less goal-oriented and integrative statements than did manual groups. Cooperation in working toward group goals has been shown to enhance group effectiveness (Shaw, 1981). A focus on goals and a concern with summarizing progress and integrating group member contributions should have a positive effect on decision quality (Gouran, et al., 1978). Just the reverse happened in this study because the groups that used the most goal and integration statements were the groups that performed least well. Did the CS groups satisfy goal-orientation needs through the system, resulting in less need for verbalization about goals? This seems unlikely, since most groups defined the problem (goal) at the beginning of the meeting and never referred back to it. The four baseline groups subjected to interaction analysis had even fewer goal-oriented comments than CS groups, suggesting that manual groups may have used many goal statements because they were confused about goals. Evidence indicates that confusion in manual groups arose from difficulties with integrating task and agenda structures.

Although the focus of this study was on the communication process, the link with outcomes is important. What, if any, advantages did computer support offer over manual or no support in the task used for this research? Huber (1984a) and DeSanctis and Gallupe (1987) argue that a GDSS should improve information exchange and, therefore, group outcomes. Prior research has demonstrated increased effectiveness in GDSS groups over manual or no support for creativity and problem-finding tasks (Applegate, 1986; Gallupe, 1986; Lewis, 1982), but little performance advantage for a GDSS in preference tasks (Watson, et al., 1988; Beauclair, 1987). The findings from this study are mixed. Computer support helped to even out influence behavior, but it did not result in higher quality decisions than were made in manual groups. It appears that task demands are an important determinant of how computer support is viewed and used. The characteristics of the intellective task used in this research (e.g., the extent and type of structure inherent in the task) appeared to affect the way in which groups used the technological support provided. The manual groups had difficulty incorporating both the task structure and the process structure (i.e., the manual agenda) into their decision-making routine. As a result, the performance of manual groups suffered. The electronic structure, in the form of the GDSS, proved more adaptable than the manual structure, and performance in CS groups was essentially the same as in freely-interacting, baseline groups. The effectiveness of a group's adaptation of support technology, then, is partially a function of the match between the group's view of task demands and its view of the technology. That is, the key to significant effectiveness increases in computer support of groups may be in both the adaptability of the system to the task and the adaptability of the group in their view of the system's utility and meaning.

These observations suggest several questions for future research. How does the impact of the GDSS on influence behavior change as a group gains sophistication with the system over repeated usage? Does the technology have a lesser effect on groups that are well-established? It is possible that well-established groups will not use the technology because their procedures are already well-formed, although the task may change how well-established a group is. How does the distribution of influence change in groups with formally appointed leaders? Does the GDSS help to overcome a drift toward groupthink? Are leaders able to “take over” a group via the system? How rich is the communication carried over the electronic channel?

These issues also raise the question of how the system is perceived by users. The software used in this study was built on a rational model of decision making. What impact does this design have on “fuzzier” group processes (e.g., notions such as trust and mood)? Is the electronic channel believed more or less than verbal and non-verbal channels? Does the physical presence of the technology affect the mood of the group? Answers to these questions would have a bearing on whether and how members use the system to influence the group.

The results from this study provide some support for the argument that computer support of group decision making results in more even influence in groups. However, it appears that CS groups did not take full advantage of the characteristics of the electronic communication channel, which resulted in less than anticipated effects. A greater intervention in group process via training or facilitation may be required to provide groups with a view or frame of the system so that they can effectively adapt the electronic communication channel to their own group process.

## About the Authors

Ilze Zigurs is an assistant professor in the Division of Management Science and Information Systems at the University of Colorado, Boulder. Dr. Zigurs holds a Ph.D. in management information systems from the University of Minnesota, where she was a member of the Group Decision Support Systems Project team. Dr. Zigurs' primary research interests are in the use and impact of computer and communications technology in the support of collaborative work. Her research has been published in the Proceedings of the Hawaii International Conference on System Sciences and will be forthcoming in the Journal of Decision Support Systems.

Marshall Scott Poole is associate professor in the Department of Speech-Communication at the University of Minnesota. He received his Ph.D. from the University of Wisconsin. His research interests include social impacts of technology, group and organizational communication, conflict management, and communication research methods. He has authored or edited four books and numerous articles and is a member of the editorial boards of Human Communication Research, Communication Monographs, and Communication Research.

Gerardine DeSanctis is associate professor of management information systems at the University of Minnesota. She received her Ph.D. from Texas Tech University. Her research interests concern the design of systems to support managerial decision making and the effects of information systems use on individuals and groups within organizational settings.
