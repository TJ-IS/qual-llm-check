# Service Support Levels: An Organized Approach to End-User Computing

By: Robert L. Leithelser
Assistant Professor
School of Business Administration
University of Wisconsin-Milwaukee
Milwaukee, Wisconsin

By: James C. Wetherbe
Professor of MIS
School of Management
University of Minnesota
Minneapolis, Minnesota

## Abstract

The rapid growth of end-user computing is a double-edged sword: it offers new opportunities for improving the effectiveness of organizations, but it also creates new risks for a firm's information resources. This paper describes an organized approach for effectively managing end-user computing. It is based on the definition of “service support levels” which link support services to responsibilities. These levels form the basis of a cooperative effort between the MIS department and the end user.

Keywords: End-user computing, personal computing, information management, information policies

ACM Categories: C.5.0, C.5.3, D.2.9, D.4.4, H.1.2

## Introduction

MIS departments are struggling to keep up with the demand for computing. The traditional methods for producing systems have been overwhelmed and long lead times have resulted. Promising computer applications are being deferred or dropped because of the delay. In a survey of user managers' computing needs, Alloway and Quillard [2] found that the number of desired, but not formally requested systems was 160% of the number of systems in development or in the development backlog. They concluded that even with dramatic improvements in development efficiency, MIS departments alone could not meet users' computing demands.

This unmet demand has combined with the fall of hardware costs and improvements in software design to increase the amount of computing being done by end users. Benjamin [3] estimated that for a typical large U.S. manufacturing firm, end-user computing had grown from virtually nothing in 1970 to 40% of computer processing capacity in 1980. He projects it to grow to 75% by 1990. Rockart and Flannery [11] found 50-90% annual growth in time-share computing by end users as compared to 5-15% growth in traditional computing.

The rise of end-user computing has not gone unnoticed. In a survey of leading MIS executives, consultants, and researchers [8], the “facilitation and management” of end-user computing was ranked as the second most important MIS management issue for the 1980's. Only MIS planning was ranked higher. The need to effectively manage end-user computing is being widely felt.

This article reviews the opportunities and risks presented by end-user computing. It then presents an approach to managing end-user computing that uses the concept of service support levels. This approach offers organizations the opportunity to gain the benefits of end-user computing while minimizing its risks.

## End-User Computing Opportunities and Risks

There is much discussion of end-user computing in the academic and popular press, but no generally agreed upon definition. In this article, end-user computing is defined as:

“... the use and/or development of information systems by the principal users of the systems' outputs or by their staffs” [13, p. 3].

End-user computing has been motivated by the inability of the MIS department to meet computing needs. End users choose to do their own computing because [13]:

1. lead times on development requests are shorter,

2. end users have more control over system development and use,

3. services are not available from the MIS department,

4. MIS department procedures are not appropriate for small applications,

5. the MIS department is not perceived as being concerned about users' needs,

6. end users want to learn about computing,

7. end users gain more flexibility,

8. the information systems developed better meet users' needs, and

9. development costs are lower.

The rapid and continued growth in end-user computing is evidence that users believe the benefits from doing their own computing are substantial.

Besides the advantages for users, there are also benefits for the MIS department. First, the shortage of systems development personnel can be relieved. This allows MIS executives to use their expensive human resources on larger, more technical development projects. Second, if users know their requirements, they can implement them directly into a system. This allows users to avoid going through the time consuming and error-prone process of communicating requirements to an outside developer. Finally, system implementation becomes the responsibility of the users. Their ownership of the system is thereby insured and a major stumbling block to successful system implementation is removed.

Taken together, these benefits to both users and the MIS department are substantial.

Unfortunately, end-user computing has a down side. The organization's hardware, software, and data are valuable resources which can be lost or diminished if not properly developed and protected. End users, acting independently, cannot always be expected to use these resources in ways that are optimum for the whole firm. Since end-user computing bypasses the monitoring and control mechanisms built into MIS department computing, there is no formal check on user behavior. To illustrate these risks, Davis [7] presents three scenarios where end-user computing leads to problems for an unwary firm.

A faulty corporate model leads to a disastrous acquisition which later forces the firm into bankruptcy.

Errors in retrieving data for an analysis cause a substantial underestimation of the cost of introducing a new employee benefit. The actual costs are so high the firm is forced to cancel the program with disastrous results for employee morale.

An employee who developed an important end-user application leaves a company to establish his own business. Since the system was not documented no one in the organization knows how to use or maintain it.

These scenarios appear to be hypothetical, but similar situations actually exist. In his survey of end-user computing, Benson [4] found problems with inadequate documentation, poor data backup procedures, and a lack of data security.

MIS executives might argue that they should not be held accountable for end-user mistakes and failures. And for certain kinds of computing and certain knowledgeable end users this position may be defensible. In the majority of cases, however, the expertise and experience of the MIS department make it the obvious organizational unit for protecting the computing resources of the firm. Top management reasonably looks to the MIS department for leadership in computing, no matter who develops or uses the system.

The range of risks that result from end-user development is suggested by Davis [7]:

1. The elimination of the separate analyst functions of technical expert, independent reviewer of information requirements, and enforcer of standards and policies.

2. The inability of end users to completely and accurately specify system requirements without the tools, techniques and experience of the analyst.

3. A lack of end user knowledge about, and commitment to quality assurance in system development.

4. The development of continuously changing (unstable) systems, where stable systems are needed.

5. The encouragement of private information systems at the expense of shared corporate computing resources.

6. The expensive accumulation of unneeded information.

Most firms will find it too dangerous to ignore these risks. Clearly then, success in managing end-user computing requires the facilitation of beneficial computing, while minimizing risks to the firm.

Rockart and Flannery [11] present recommendations for both of these management goals. To facilitate end-user computing, they suggest: (1) establishing a distributed organizational support structure, (2) providing a range of software tools, (3) developing a broad educational program, and (4) establishing efficient procedures for locating and transferring data. Risk coordination recommendations include: (1) identifying critical applications, (2) controlling through user (not MIS) management, (3) using MIS experts to assist end users in making computing decisions, and (4) developing standards for hardware and software and creating incentives for following them. These recommendations provide valuable guidelines for MIS executives but they do not describe a systematic approach to end-user computing. Such an approach is important for achieving effective facilitation and coordination.

Before proposing an organized approach, it is useful to lay out four general responses that an MIS executive can take towards end-user computing [10]. These general responses represent the range of specific actions which are available to executives.

1. Sink or swim: Don't do anything — let the end user beware.

2. Stick: Establish policies and procedures to control end-user computing so that corporate risks are minimized.

3. Carrot: Create incentives to encourage certain end-user practices which reduce organizational risks.

4. Support: Develop services to aid end users in their computing activities.

Each of these responses presents the MIS executive with different opportunities for facilitation and coordination. The sink or swim response is the default action. It does nothing to increase the level of end-user computing or improve its quality. All of the risks mentioned above apply, while the benefits to end users and the MIS department are solely a function of end user efforts.

The restrictive, stick response forces coordination at the cost of some computing opportunities. Many of the risks associated with end-user computing could be reduced by effective policies, standards and procedures. These same policies, standards and procedures also are likely to reduce the incentives for end users to do their own computing; i.e., lead times are increased, user control and flexibility are reduced, and development costs are higher. A heavy reliance on standards/policies and their enforcement is a hallmark of an emphasis on coordination [e.g., [1]).

The carrot response motivates end users to reduce the risks to the firm associated with their computing. By allowing end users to retain their computing freedom, it maintains an important incentive for end-user computing but gives up some certainty about the reduction of computing risks. This response targets a specific benefit or risk, rather than providing a complete program of support.

The support response is designed to increase end-user computing through the provision of services. These services are carefully designed to increase beneficial computing and to foster coordination. As long as end users choose to use or not use services, they will retain control over their computing activities and will view services positively. This choice will provide a check on the MIS department because services that focus too much on coordination and not enough on facilitation will simply not be used.

Each type of response is appropriate in some situations, but a combination of responses is necessary for management on a broad scale. The next section describes an organized approach to defining these combinations.

## Service Support Levels

An effective approach to managing end-user computing achieves both facilitation and coordination. Service support levels do this by (1) defining computing responsibilities, (2) providing a framework for designing support services, and (3) allowing end users to retain as much control as possible over their own computing.

Service support levels are formal divisions of computing responsibility between end users and the MIS department. These divisions are based on a small set of critical computing decisions that are made by end-user management. The way managers make these decisions commits them to accept certain responsibilities and allows them to turn over others to the MIS department. Since end-user management make the decisions they are free to choose the amount and kind of support they feel they need. This freedom to chose provides a check on the MIS department. If the MIS department is to meet its objectives of coordinating and facilitating end user computing it must develop and deliver support services to meet needs.

An approach based on service support levels offers several other advantages. First it reduces "finger pointing" by clearly specifying department responsibilities. When a microcomputer malfunctions everyone knows who is responsible for fixing it. Second, it provides a structure for the design and delivery of end-user services by the MIS department. This structure allows a range of support options to be provided from a common pool of services.

For example, the same consultant can have clearly specified duties at each support level. Third, incentives are created for end users to improve their computing practices and thereby reduces the computing risks to the firm. By clearly stating what has to be done to qualify for better MIS service, end-user management are in a better position to make trade-offs in their computing decisions. Finally, it provides a means for the MIS department to coordinate end-user computing. The same staff that provides support services also monitor and report on end-user activities. Some end-user computing will remain outside of this coordination efforts, but those activities are recognized to be the responsibility of end-user managers.

Establishing service support levels requires four steps:

1. defining the levels,

2. dividing computing responsibility at each level,

3. designing services to meet MIS support level commitments at each level, and
4. implementing service support levels.

The process may be applied to each of the principal computing resources: hardware, software, and data.

A concrete illustration will highlight aspects of the approach. The purpose of the example is not to present a business case but to illustrate the definition process and give examples of its outputs. Due to unrelated business circumstances, the levels defined here have not yet been fully implemented and their success cannot, therefore, be reported. The authors know of several other companies that have used versions of the support levels approach, but this example best illustrates the main concepts. Some modifications have been made to the original level definitions to improve the clarity of the presentation.

## Defining Service Support Levels

An important part of the process of instituting support levels is determining the critical decisions which will define the levels. These decisions must be few in number and generally perceived as affecting computing accountability. A small number of decisions insures that the requirements for obtaining levels is understandable and obvious to end users, top management, and MIS staff.

One obvious means of defining levels, and thereby dividing responsibility, is to base it on the relative amounts of control that departments have over the processes that affect a computing resource. For example, if the user manager purchases a piece of hardware then the responsibility for insuring that the equipment meets department and corporate needs should rest with that manager. Similarly, if the MIS department formally reviews and approves an application program, then the MIS department should be accountable for any subsequent quality problems. These indicators of control offer a fair means of assigning responsibility. Firms must consider their own unique computing environments when defining the qualifying decisions.

For example, if a company strives to integrate its application systems, then a decision emphasizing compatibility could be defined. Concerns regarding security, sharing of applications, and documentation could lead to other qualifying decisions. Many sets of qualifying decisions should be analyzed, but no set can be final until it is formally accepted by end-user, MIS, and top managements.

Control and compatibility are the basis for the qualifying decisions in the example firm. As Table 1 shows, separate decisions were defined for hardware, software, and data resources. Other firms may elect to establish support levels for only one or two types of resources (e.g., software only) or may combine two types of resources (e.g., software and data) and use one set of decisions. The qualifying decision sets given in Table 1 have general applicability and offer a good starting point for other firms.

Levels are defined by assigning outcomes to qualifying decisions. Figure 1 maps the example firm's decisions to specific support levels. Five levels are defined for each of the types of computing resources and range from almost complete MIS department responsibility (Level I) to almost no MIS department responsibility (Level V). Note that all possible combinations of decision outcomes need not be used. For example, at this firm all MIS department purchases of hardware and software for end users must be registered. This policy drops all combinations of purchase/develop? = "MIS" and registration? = "NO." Similar logical or policy restrictions apply to other combinations of decision outcomes so the number of possible levels is reduced. Notice also that qualifying decisions can be related. One data level qualifier in Figure 1 depends on decisions made for the software that processes the data.

In this example, the qualifying decisions were completely determined before the support levels were defined. In practice the process is iterative, with tentative decisions being suggested and tested for their ability to define useful levels. They may then be retained, modified, or discarded.

To be effective, the number of levels should be small (less than 10) while the qualifying decisions should be powerful, easily recognizable, and readily understandable. Review and modification are necessary to achieve a good balance among these factors. Once the levels have been defined and agreed upon, the specific responsibilities can be determined.

## Dividing Computing Responsibilities

After defining support levels, the next step is to divide up specific responsibilities. This is a shared task for end users and MIS departments. One method for doing this is to first brainstorm a list of required computing activities. Make the list as long as possible before cutting it back to the 12 to 20 most important activities. These activities should then be defined as clearly as possible. This formal definition will avoid later questions about what was actually meant by the assignment of an activity. Table 2 lists the activities and definitions generated by the example firm.

Next, a two-dimensional matrix is created to link the decision defined levels with the required computing activities. Figures 2, 3, and

4 contain these matrices. For each activity and level an assignment of responsibility is made. In our example an "X" indicates that the MIS department has the major responsibility for that activity. The absence of an "X" means that the major responsibility is assigned to the end users. For example, the MIS department has major advising responsibility at all hardware levels, but major maintenance responsibility only at the Turnkey Level (Level I, see Figure 2).

Firms may elect to use a more finely partitioned system for assigning responsibility. For example, an "F" could indicate full responsibility, an "S" shared responsibility, an "L" limited responsibility, and an "N" no responsibility. A single level of hardware support may then include:

F — full MIS responsibility for hotline/debugging,

F — full MIS responsibility for purchasing,

S — shared MIS responsibility for training,

L — limited MIS responsibility for documentation, and

N — no MIS responsibility for backup.

This division of responsibility is more precise but the levels become more complicated to develop and administer.

The assignment of activities at a particular level involves many considerations. The first is that there should be a range of MIS support options. At one end of the range the MIS department should be assigned all or most of the activities, while at the other end all or most should be given to the end user departments. In the example the range goes from "Turnkey," where the user department limits its principal computing activities to just using the system, to "Advising," where the department assumes almost all responsibility and uses the MIS department only for advice.

Once the range is established, assignments in the middle levels can be made. Some activities are linked to qualifying decisions. For example, one of the qualifying decisions for hardware and software resources (Figures 2-3) concerns compatibility with MIS systems.

The determination of this compatibility was defined in Table 2 as a support activity. The logical linkage between the decision and the activity was the basis for assigning the activity's responsibility. The linkage between the registration question with the list resource activity is less obvious but also exists and was used. Identifying such logical linkages is an important part of assigning activity responsibilities to support levels.

No division of activities will be optimal the first time. It is important for the MIS department to keep track of the support level choices being made by end users. More incentives may be needed to attract end users to higher levels of support. Since these higher levels also provide for better coordination of computing it may be in the organization's best interests to shift support responsibilities in order to create those incentives. Any change must be approved by the MIS department, end users, and top management.

## Designing Support Services

Once the responsibilities for computing activities have been determined, the MIS department can use them to design its support services. User departments can also use them to develop their own policies and procedures.

The services offered by the MIS department in our example include: (1) general consulting, (2) product support, (3) hotline/help desk, (4) technical support, (5) quality assurance, and (6) end-user training. These services are based on those suggested by Hammond [9]. Figure 5 shows a mapping between support activities and the six services.

All activities must be assigned. Notice that in doing a mapping it is allowable to assign multiple services to one activity. An activity is then broken up into logical parts so that responsibility can be clearly defined. For example, the development activity is shared by General Consulting, Product Support, and Quality Assurance. The first service is concerned with analysis, the second with design and implementation, and the third with testing and certification. Each of the six resulting services is described below.

The general consulting service is assigned advising, development, and purchasing assistance activities. Its focus is on problem definition and analysis. General consultants help end users define their requirements and steer them to the appropriate packages or development tools. They assist end users in making build vs. buy decisions, and provide guidance in planning end user development activities. The general consultants' responsibilities necessarily involve them in end-users' decisions about support levels. In these choices they may try to influence the users, but they cannot make their decision. The general consultant's job is done when the building or installation of the application system is ready to begin. Then they refer end users to a product support staffer.

Product support personnel assist users with development, documentation, resource listing, and maintenance. They also do research into new products and the possible uses for them. Personnel specialize in particular high level development tools and/or in certain kinds of applications. After referral from the general consultant they become involved in the design, building, and maintenance of the application system. The degree of involvement depends on the support level selected by the end user. Most development needs can be met by these product specialists, but when difficult technical problems arise they can call in technical specialists.

Hotline/Help desk services support end users who have short, immediate problems. A hotline service uses a special phone number that end users call to get short answers to specific questions. Responses are immediate and callers do not have to leave their terminals or computers. The very nature of telephone communication, however, limits the kinds of problems that can be handled. Problems that can't be handled over the phone can be taken to a help desk. This service requires an MIS staff person to be at a particular location during particular times. The end user must go to the desk location, but once there can have a much richer and longer exchange of information. Program listings, for example, can be shared over a help desk but not over a phone. More time-consuming problems are referred to the technical or product support staffs.

Technical support includes backup, some maintenance, data transfer, and recovery activities. These activities are aimed at specific technical problems which require specialized expertise. For example, communications specialists are needed to solve problems resulting from incompatible protocols. Database specialists are necessary for file conversions and database restructuring. Technical specialists are usually not contacted directly by end users, but are called in by product support or hotline/help desk personnel.

Quality assurance is involved with compatibility, development, and documentation activities. Implementing a quality assurance program requires designing a review process that reduces the risks while also considering the special needs of end-user computing. A process that is expensive and time consuming will defeat the very purpose of end-user computing. A balance between efficiency of service and risk reduction must be struck. Quality assurance efforts are especially important here because of the key role they play in determining support levels in the example.

End-user training is the final support service. This training ranges from establishing a general awareness about end-user computing to explaining the details of how a specific database query language works, including an explanation of support services and levels. The mechanisms available for training include classes, demonstrations, on-line training systems, user groups, and a newsletter. The demand for training in firms that support end-user computing has been tremendous so creating an effective program is essential.

## Implementing Service Support Levels

For the MIS department, implementing support levels requires establishing a mechanism for officially assigning a computing resource to a support level, creating a process to maintain support level definition, and developing a means for delivering end user services. If the qualifying decisions have been properly selected, it should be easy to determine the level that a piece of hardware, a software package, or a particular database belongs to. Once the approach has been explained to the users they should be able to make their own determination about the applicable support level. An official assignment of a support level could be part of the registration process. This works for the example firm because any software or data that is not registered is automatically placed in the lowest support level. Only the two unregistered hardware levels would not be distinguished.

Since the definition of level qualifying decisions, the specification of computing activities, and the assignment of responsibilities are cooperative endeavors, so too must be their review and revision. A committee of MIS staff and users is an appropriate body for maintaining support levels. One of the first action items for this committee is to define procedures for submitting, reviewing, and gaining approval for any changes. The committee would also settle disagreements between the MIS and user departments.

The most obvious approach to delivering services is to use an information center. An information center is an organizational unit, usually part of the MIS department, whose principal function is to facilitate and coordinate end-user computing by offering support services. Large numbers of information centers already exist. In two different surveys, 60-70% of respondents claimed that their firms would have information centers by the end of 1984 [6, 13]. Empirical descriptions of existing information centers can be found in [5, 12, 13].

Most information centers are not large enough to justify separate full time personnel for each of the six defined service areas $[13]$ . Therefore, individual staff members must be responsible for multiple services. General consulting and product support, for example, might be combined. Technical specialists are only needed for special problems so they may be shared with other MIS units. Whatever the arrangement, care must be taken to insure that the staff have the necessary combination of technical prowess, business sense, and interpersonal communication skills. The quality of the service staff is as important to success as the proper definition of service activities $[5]$ .

## Selecting a Support Level

Crucial to the success of the support level approach is the ability of end-user managers to make appropriate decisions about levels of support. In making this choice, managers should consider the benefits of the support offered and the trade-offs of additional time and expense that result when MIS services are used. Beyond this cost/benefit assessment, end-user managers should ask themselves three questions.

## 1. Do I have the resources required to perform the set of activities not covered by MIS services?

The X's in Figures 2-4 indicate activities that are the major responsibility of the MIS department. It is the responsibility of the user department to perform the other activities. Different departments have different levels of computing expertise. Rockart and Flannery [11], for example, found knowledgeable end-users in many departments. To answer this question, managers of user areas must (1)

know what their internal computing resources are, and (2) decide whether they want their own personnel spending substantial amounts of time doing support activities. Choosing a particular support level involves choosing a level of internal support activity.

2. How much risk is involved with this application and how much am I willing to assume? Some computing applications are risker than others. If the risks are high it might be in the end-user manager's best interests to share some of these risks. Assessing the level of risk associated with a particular application is not always easy. End-user managers will want to consider:

\- the size and complexity of the application,

\- the number and sophistication of the users,

\- the familiarity of end-user developers with this type of application, and

\- the stability of the application environment.

The MIS department can help end-user managers by providing training and written procedures that can assist them in making the risk assessment.

## 3. Are other organizational units involved in this application? Should they be?

Rockart and Flannery [11] found a substantial number of end-user timesharing applications which extended beyond single departments. When different managers become involved with an application, coordination problems arise. Since the MIS department is concerned with meeting the overall needs of the firm, it is an obvious choice for coordinating multi-departmental computing. Selecting support levels with high MIS department involvement is recommended for interdepartmental systems.

## Concluding Remarks

The growth of end-user computing offers MIS executives the opportunity to improve the performance of their firms. To do this requires a new attitude and a cooperative approach to working with end users. No longer can the

MIS department simply dictate which application systems are to be built and how they are to be developed and used. It must now compete for computing business with services that meet the needs of users. Given the right support services, MIS executives can take the lead in increasing the amount of beneficial computing done in their firms.

The growth of end-user computing also creates new risks for MIS executives. Until the new cooperative arrangement is structured, the MIS department will be open to every kind of claim by end-users who run into trouble while computing. Without a clear specification of responsibilities, top management will, rightly or wrongly, hold the MIS department accountable for these computing claims. MIS executives must reduce the risks of end-user computing to themselves and to their firms.

Obtaining the benefits of end-user computing, while minimizing its inherent risks, requires a clear delineation of responsibilities and an effective service program. This paper described an organized approach for doing this based on the establishment of service support levels. With end-user computing becoming the dominant kind of computing in this decade, MIS executives need such an approach for dealing with the demands of their new computing environment.
