# A Stochastic Dominance Approach to Risk Analysis of Computer Systems

By: Gerald V. Post
J. David Dlitz
Department of MIS and Quantitative Methods
Oakland University
Rochester, Michigan

## Abstract

Auditors and systems analysts are increasingly called upon to determine the impact of a disaster striking the computer system. Current risk analysis methods rely on some variation of expected value analysis. The expected value method suffers from serious drawbacks in this application because probabilities of disaster are difficult to estimate and the loss distributions are likely to be highly skewed. This article presents an improved methodology for dealing with EDP risk analysis and contingency planning. It is based on the concept of stochastic dominance and it provides a more accurate comparison of the various contingency plans by dealing with estimates of the entire loss distribution. This methodology also focuses on the differences between contingency plans, rather than on the cause of the disaster. The application of this methodology is illustrated for the case of a hypothetical medium-sized bank using aggregated data.

Keywords: Contingency planning, risk analysis, stochastic dominance

ACM Categories: H.2.2, J.1, K.6

## Introduction

Recently auditors and system managers have become increasingly concerned with the effects on the business and its information system if a disaster were to strike the main computer center. In particular, system managers are being called upon to make arrangements for backup facilities to restore a damaged system. As a final step in systems analysis, an analyst may be advised to perform a risk analysis of the various components of the systems, and then construct a contingency plan that details the actions that would be taken if a major disaster were to occur. Conventional risk analysis methods rely heavily on probability estimates generated by some form of expected value (EV) analysis. (For examples, see NBS [11, 20, 21], Lobel [13], and the Federal Reserve [10].)

In this article, we present a superior methodology that can be used to compare various contingency plans. The basic premise of our approach is that contingency plans should be compared under the assumption that a disaster has occurred. We use a method called stochastic dominance to compare these plans, and examine its relative advantages over expected value analysis. The method is then illustrated with an example of a “typical” bank.

## Risk Assessment Measures

As noted in existing literature [4, 13, 16, 18], current contingency practices are based on three types of backup arrangements:

\- offsite duplication of equipment and information,

\- reciprocal arrangements with “sister sites,” and

• purchase of backup services from a specialized firm.

Each of these strategies has advantages and disadvantages which must be weighed in developing a contingency plan that is right for a specific organization. The methodology presented in this article focuses the attention on the comparison of these alternatives, rather than on the type of disaster that may occur.

## Expected Value Analysis

In developing a risk analysis and contingency plan, the system manager performs several steps. First, each component of the information system is described. Each individual component is then assigned a value that represents its importance to the system. Next, the analyst assigns a probability that each component will be damaged or destroyed by a disaster. These values and their probabilities are then combined to estimate the expected value of loss for different types of disaster. That is, if a major fire occurs, the system could expect to lose an estimated amount of resources. The next step in risk analysis is to create a contingency plan. This plan describes exactly what steps are to be taken when a disaster occurs. Finally, the contingency plan is tested and revised periodically.

There are some advantages to the current methodology. Specifically, working through the risk analysis steps forces the analyst to identify the specific components that are the most susceptible to disaster, and those that are critical to the operation of the system. Similarly, generating and testing a contingency plan shows the analyst where problems are likely to occur when disaster strikes. Also, note that testing a contingency plan will graphically demonstrate the value of the information system to management.

The stochastic dominance methodology proposed in this article replaces the valuation steps of the EV method. Hence, it is still necessary to examine the system for critical areas and a contingency plan must still be prepared and tested.

## Disadvantages of Current Methodologies

From the viewpoint of the analyst, the greatest difficulty with EV analysis lies in estimating the probability of losing a component of the system. For example, a Southern California installation would need to know the probability of an earthquake occurring, the probability that the quake strikes the computer center, and the probability that the disk drives are damaged when the quake strikes. From a theoretical standpoint, the greatest difficulty with EV analysis lies in its assumption of symmetric distributions.

In general, EDP catastrophes are relatively rare events, implying that it will be difficult to estimate probabilities. Further, the probability distribution of losses arising from these disasters is likely to be highly skewed, presenting problems for traditional decision-making paradigms. For example, there may be many circumstances that cause relatively minor problems, (e.g., loss of power), but there is usually a very small probability of a major disaster destroying large portions of the EDP system. EV analysis will attempt to average these events, blurring the differences between the two extremes, thus implying homogeneous solutions.

Another technique suggested in the literature is “fuzzy cost-benefit” analysis (for example, see $[12, 17]$ ). This technique calls for the development of a set of “fuzzy descriptors,” rather than dollar cost estimates, to describe benefits and costs of a particular alternative. These descriptors are then used to develop a general analysis of alternative contingency plans. Besides suffering from the same problems as EV analysis, fuzzy cost-benefit analysis does not provide much detail regarding the magnitude of the potential losses. Fuzzy descriptors are subject to considerable variation in interpretation. Thus the very characteristic which purportedly makes the technique useful represents its greatest drawback.

The fundamental problem with currently practiced EV analysis is that it cannot directly answer the primary question: "If disaster strikes, which type of backup facility should we use?"

## A Stochastic Dominance Approach

## Overview

This methodology consists of two fundamental changes to the expected value approach to risk analysis. First, instead of emphasizing probability estimates for different types of disasters, the primary objective is to compare the costs associated with various backup and recovery options. Second, stochastic dominance analysis evaluates the entire loss distribution instead of just the first moment.

The first step in this approach is to assume that a disaster has already occurred. The next step is to analyze the effects of the disaster at various points in time. Recovery from a disaster proceeds through three stages: initial disaster, contingent operation, and restoration. In large installations it may also be useful to model subsets of each of these stages so that this model can be modified to fit specific cases. Elapsed time in each of these stages can be measured in increments of weeks, days, hours, or minutes. The choice of the increment will depend on factors such as the size of the installation, the time it takes to respond to a disaster, and the importance placed on the information system. For example, a large institution that relies heavily on their information system may want to analyze disaster and recovery in hours. However, most installations should find days to be an acceptable scale. For an example, see Aasgaard, et al., [1].

Once the scale has been set, the analyst must examine all of the areas of the system that are susceptible to losses if the EDP system is damaged or destroyed. In particular, the analyst must identify how well the system and the business will perform in each of the three stages, and identify which business areas will suffer losses. To simplify the presentation we will be discussing relatively major disasters in this article. However, the methodology can be applied to less extensive losses by considering individual components separately and adjusting the time frame as necessary.

## Stages of Disaster

The initial disaster stage is defined as the time period between the initial loss of EDP capability and actual operation of the contingent system. The contingent operation stage begins when the contingent system begins operating, and ends when the primary data center is first restored. The restoration stage consists of the time period necessary to fully recover the information system to normal operations. It arises mainly because some applications were deferred in the earlier stages, and this processing backlog must be eliminated before operations are back to normal. ## Loss Functions

When a catstrophe strikes, some information functions will be unavailable, implying that there will be initial costs. These costs would be expected to compound, resulting in losses that increase for every day (elapsed time-unit) that the institution is operating without the EDP capability. There is a limit to the losses because the losses ultimately depend on revenues, and in the worst case scenario, the institution could simply shut down. Hence, the Stage I loss function with respect to time will be increasing at a decreasing rate. Empirical studies support this description. For a graphical example, see an illustration created by Masstor Systems Corporation that was reproduced in Miles [15].

In general terms, losses in Stage I will fall into three major categories. First, there will be increased labor costs because some functions will have to be performed manually. Second, and probably the largest source of costs will be the loss of business due to the inability to perform transactions and queries. Third, there will be costs due to an increase in errors because some information will not be available.

When the contingent operation is activated, the losses should stop increasing — assuming that the contingency system functions. However, in most cases, the backup system will not be efficient enough to recover the backlog and return operations to normal, so there will still be some operations not performed. There will be additional operating costs due to dislocated personnel and the cost of using backup facilities. Therefore, costs will level off in Stage II but will not decrease.

The categories of Stage II losses should be identical to those of Stage I. In particular, there will be extra labor costs because some functions will have to be performed manually, and because contingent operations may require more personnel and management to handle unfamiliar equipment, locations, and new problems that arise. In most cases there will still be problems accessing some of the data because lower priority items will be deferred until the complete system is restored.

Once the primary EDP center has been restored Stage III begins and full recovery commences. At this point costs should decrease as the backlog is reduced, and the additional costs of running from a backup facility are reduced. It is assumed that Stage III begins when the primary EDP facilities are restored, hence there will be a delay before operations are shifted back from the contingent facilities. Again, empirical cases support this description of the loss function. For an example involving a credit union, see the case reported in Computerworld [5].

In some cases a particular stage could be extremely short or nonexistent. For example, in a manual contingency plan (i.e., no electronic backup facilities) the operation will usually stay in Stage I for a longer period of time, and then move directly to Stage III. Similarly, if there is a sophisticated hot site that is an exact duplicate of the primary facility, Stage II will tend to be very short.

## Contingency Plan Alternatives

The primary objective is to evaluate each of the alternative contingency plans with respect to the three disaster stages. Observe that each plan will affect losses in a stage in two different manners. First, the amount of time spent in each stage will vary for different plans. Second, the size (and type) of losses in each stage will depend on the type of plan chosen.

For most institutions there are four main alternative contingency plans. First, the institution may revert to manual operations until the information system can be restored. Second, critical EDP operations may be moved to a “sister” facility that has excess capacity. Third, operations could be moved to a shell site that contains only the necessary environmental and communications equipment. Fourth, operations could be moved to a hot site that contains all of the necessary equipment to run the information system, including the CPU(s), storage devices, and output devices.

There are other decisions to be made, such as whether or not the hot site should be owned by the institution or rented from commercial firms. However, each of these options can be evaluated in a similar fashion, so the focus of this article will be on the four primary options.

Finally, note that some losses can be covered by insurance. However, there are two problems with insurance. First, there is a cost in the form of premiums. This fixed cost must be compared to the other probabilistic losses. More importantly, insurance does little to actually recover the information system. That is, you could have a large amount of insurance coverage, but still be out of business. Also, there is no insurance for loss of good-will when disgruntled customers select another business. In a similar vein, system maintenance (including data backup) may influence the probability of a disaster occurring, and whether or not it is possible to recover. However, once a disaster has occurred, it is too late to change these policies, so the direct effects on the choice of contingency plan will be minimal.

## Comparison of Alternatives

When comparing the alternatives, the reduction in loss due to a well-developed contingency plan must be weighed against the cost of maintaining the plan. For the case of manual operations and sister site arrangements, it is assumed that there are no annual subscription or maintenance costs. For the services of a backup EDP firm, the costs of the plan can be estimated to be the present value of perpetuity, with the annual subscription fee being the amount of the perpetuity. The present value of this perpetuity is the primary cost of these services. Since disasters are rare events, any operating costs for the contingent system or start-up fees incurred during the disaster are amortized over an infinite horizon, and are thus small enough to be ignored.

When the loss functions have been described, the next step is to define probability distributions describing how long a business might remain in a given stage. A computer simulator then generates a sample of losses for each of the contingency plans specified. These plans are then compared with stochastic dominance methods. Essentially, this method examines the entire distribution of losses rather than just the expected value.

## Probability Functions

If it were known exactly how long the information system remains in each stage, the problem of evaluation of alternatives would be trivial. The difficulty is that time estimates are speculative. However, if we specify these conjectures in probabilistic terms, we may evaluate each alternative from a statistical perspective.

There are several ways to specify the probability functions. From an applications perspective, a convenient way is to choose a distribution that is defined in terms of a modal (most likely) value, a minimum value, and a maximum value. Although a triangular distribution could be used, there is a precedent for the use of a beta probability distribution. This distribution has a number of advantages, such as its ability to take on variety of different forms, including a high degree of skewness. Compared to the triangular distribution, it does not have to be estimated in pieces. Finally, it has gained wide acceptance as a probability distribution of activity times in the PERT literature (see Donaldson [7], MacCrimmon [14], and Swanson [19]).

## Definition of Stochastic Dominance

For a decision maker faced with a set of risky alternatives, decision theorists have developed an important principle called the dominance principle. Only the basic interpretation of stochastic dominance is presented in this section, but a more detailed description is presented in Appendix A, and [22].

If alternative $A_{1}$ is more desirable than alternative $A_{2}$ , for every viable utility function, then $A_{1}$ is said to dominate $A_{2}$ . There are several forms of dominance. If the cumulative probability distribution for alternative $A_{1}$ always lies below (to the right of) that for $A_{2}$ , it is called first degree stochastic dominance (FSD) and denoted as $A_{1} >_{1} A_{2}$ . In contingency planning, we will be measuring costs, so $A_{1} >_{1} A_{2}$ would mean that we would prefer alternative $A_{2}$ . $A_{2}$ is preferred because the probability is higher than we will pay lower costs. Graphically, FSD exists when the cumulative distribution functions do not intersect. If they do cross, then the FSD results are indeterminant. FSD is a very strong form of dominance, and its usefulness is somewhat limited.

When we assume that decision makers are risk averse, we can use a weaker, but more useful version of stochastic dominance called second degree stochastic dominance (SSD) that is denoted $A_{1} >_{2} A_{2}$ . With SSD, if the cumulative distributions intersect, we still might be able to determine which alternative is preferred. Roughly stated, $A_{1}$ will dominate $A_{2}$ when the area under the cumulative distribution for $A_{1}$ is less than the corresponding area for $A_{2}$ . In terms of contingency planning, it might indicate that plan $A_{1}$ yields higher costs for all but one set of circumstances. If this one case is not too severe, SSD would indicate that, in general, plan $A_{2}$ is better than plan $A_{1}$ .

The main advantage of using stochastic dominance for contingency planning is that it examines the entire loss distribution, rather than just the first moment. It also provides a way to display the distribution, so that the decision maker can visually compare difficult contingency plans. Further, it is useful to examine the tails of the distributions, especially since the probability distributions are only estimated, and errors will occur more often in the tails. Such comparisons are facilitated with the graphical presentation of the distributions.

## Advantages of This Methodology

The primary advantage of the stochastic dominance (SD) methodology is that it is designed to answer the specific question of what type of contingency plan should be used if a disaster strikes. The whole point of the analysis is to help system managers choose between alternatives. This method focuses the attention on potential solutions, rather than causes of problems. EV, and other methods that rely on causes of problems are incomplete because they cannot possibly examine all potential causes of disasters.

The second advantage of the SD method is that losses are measured in terms of the business unit and expressed in units of time. The SD method changes the focus of the problem so that the MIS manager does not have to estimate the probability that a natural disaster might occur and damage a portion of the information system. Instead, the manager estimates how long it will take to recover from a disaster, and how much the business will suffer during that time period. Simply stated, the

SD methodology allows the MIS managers to analyze the MIS function and its relationship to the business, rather than try to forecast the weather.

Finally, because the methodology relies heavily on computer analysis, it lends itself to allowing changes and examination of the underlying assumptions. Hence, the modeling process provides better support for making decisions.

## Case Study

## Overview

In this section we illustrate the stochastic dominance technique by applying it to a hypothetical medium-sized bank (e.g., a bank with about \$300 million in assets). Alternatively, this example could be treated as a subset of a much larger bank by using it to represent the portion of the data processing center that handles customer demand deposits.

It must be emphasized that results reported in this section of the paper are not intended to be a general statement regarding the efficacy of one particular contingency plan over another. Data input into the model represents aggregate data taken from a variety of sources and represent our best estimates of various parameters for a medium-sized commercial bank. We prefer to use this generalized bank model, rather than an actual case study, because it would not be practical to include all of the details of a particular bank into a journal-length paper, and it would tend to obscure the methodology. It should not be very difficult to extend the analysis presented here to a more detailed level which can be used in actual practice. The model can be applied to larger banks, but special consideration must be made for the clearinghouse transactions performed by these institutions. A simple extension is to replicate this model for added processors.

We chose to apply the SD approach to the case of commercial banking because:

\- a bank's primary piece of physical capital equipment is its computer system, - banking is a service industry whose primary product is the processing of financial information,

\- the bank's information processing services consist primarily of management of dollar flows which are obviously very important to the economy, and

\- the more than 14,000 banks operating in this country provide ample opportunity for potential application.

Data on bank labor costs, balance sheet items, and operating revenues are derived from Bank Operating Statistics, 1982, [8] and Federal Reserve Bulletin, [9]. Information on bad check rates was provided by the Bank Administration Institute [2]. Data on subscription costs for EDP backup services, as well as information on disaster recovery times was obtained through interviews with representatives of EDP services firms [6]. ## Initial Disaster: Stage I

Costs in Stage I will depend on three major factors:

\- increased labor costs,

• revenue loss due to inability to perform transactions (e.g., posting accounts, wire transfers, etc.), and

\- loss due to clearance of bad checks.

These costs are estimated in the following manner. It is assumed that each employee works an extra four hours per day (a 60 hour week) at time-and-a-half. This daily excess labor cost per employee is multiplied by a subset of the number of employees to arrive at daily excess labor cost for the bank. This result is then multiplied by the number of days spent in Stage I to obtain total Stage I excess labor cost.

Costs incurred through clearance of bad checks may be obtained by multiplying an estimate of the bad check rate by an estimate of the dollar amount of checks cleared per day by the bank. Multiplying the result by the number of days spent in Stage I yields an estimate of total bad check cost.

When the bank cannot perform EDP functions, it cannot efficiently process transactions. Since this service is the primary function of a bank, EDP disaster implies a significant revenue loss to the bank. Moreover, this problem would be expected to compound, resulting in losses that increase for every day that the bank is without EDP capability. Thus, we model daily revenue loss as an increasing function of the number of days spent in Stage I. Specifically, we estimate revenue loss by assuming that after ten working days in Stage I, revenue has declined to approximately ten percent of its pre-disaster level. This criterion was chosen by reasoning that the bank will be essentially insolvent after two weeks of Stage I operation. This assumption implies the following function:

Transaction Loss = R [1 - e-.023t] where:

R = pre-disaster daily revenue

t = number of days spent in Stage I.

Daily transactions loss is calculated for every day spent in Stage I, and the results are accumulated into a total transactions loss for Stage I. The transactions loss estimate is then added to the excess labor and bad check costs to yield an estimate of total Stage I loss.

In addition to these costs, the bank is also probably borrowing funds to cover its (uncertain) reserves position. In most cases these extra borrowing costs are insignificant due to the relatively short time for which the funds are to be borrowed. However, for large institutions they may become the dominant cost. For example, consider the Bank of New York which had to pay \$5 million in interest costs over three days when a software problem prevented the transfer of government securities [3].

## Contingent Operation: Stage II

Daily excess labor and bad check costs are assumed to continue as they were specified in Stage I. Daily transactions loss is assumed to be constant and fixed at a level equal to that incurred during the last day of Stage I operation. Daily losses are multiplied by the number of days spent in Stage II and are added together to obtain total Stage II loss.

## Restoration: Stage III

Daily loss for this stage is estimated in the following manner. First, total daily loss is fixed at Stage II levels for the first 25 percent of time elapsed in Stage III, with 25 percent being an arbitrarily determined fraction of total Stage III time. For the remaining Stage III time, total daily loss is assumed to decline geometrically, reaching 10 percent of its initial Stage III value at the end of the elapsed Stage II time, with 10 percent being an arbitrarily small amount. These assumptions imply the following function:

The main purpose of modeling the bank's losses is to express dollar loss as a function of the number of days spent in each stage — disaster, contingent operation, and recovery. Admittedly, the model specified above is simple and highly stylized, but it is adequate to illustrate the framework described in this article. More elaborate models, tailored to specific banks, could be developed. The next step in model development is to model the probability distributions of time spent in each stage.

## Costs of Alternative Contingency Plans

The reduction in loss due to a well-developed contingency plan must be weighed against the cost of maintaining the plan. In the case of manual operations and sister bank arrangements, it is assumed that there are no annual subscription costs, et cetera. For the services of an EDP firm we estimate the cost of the plan to be the present value of a perpetuity, with the annual subscription fee being the amount of the perpetuity. The present value of this perpetuity is the primary cost of these services. Since disasters are rare events, any operating costs for the contingent system or start-up fees incurred during the disaster are amortized over an infinite horizon and are thus ignored.

## Contingency Plan Assumptions

As indicated previously, the four alternative contingency plans considered are: 1) no plan, or reverting to manual backup; 2) sister bank arrangement; 3) shell facility provided by an EDP services firm; and 4) hot site facility provided by an EDP services firm. For a large bank, a sister bank arrangement may simply represent a shift of processing from a disabled center to a computer center run by a different branch of the holding corporation. Manual backup and sister banking arrangements are assumed to have no periodic subscription fees associated with them.

Purchase of the right to use an empty shell facility from an EDP services firm may be obtained at a cost of approximately \$6,000 per year. In calculating the present value of this stream of payments, we have adopted 15 percent as the rate of discount, although any appropriate rate may be used. Alternatively, a sensitivity analysis could be performed to determine the effect of alternate discount rates on the results. Discounting the subscription fee at 15 percent over an infinite horizon yields a present value of \$40,000, which will be factored into shell facility costs.

The subscription paid for the right to use a hot site depends upon the size of computer system required. For our purposes, we assume that it will be necessary to use a large system (e.g., an IBM 3083). Access to the large system may be maintained for an annual fee of \$48,000, which discounted at 15 percent yields a present value of \$320,000. Alternatively, access to a smaller system (e.g., an IBM 4341) may be maintained for an annual fee of \$19,200, which discounted at 15 percent yields a present value of \$128,000.

The portion of the analysis that will be most dependent on the type of information system and the business is the number of days spent in each stage. In this example, estimates of workdays spent in each stage of disaster and recovery for each contingency plan are shown in Table 2. It should be noted that the important characteristic of these estimates is not their absolute values, but the relative values of the estimates for a given plan vis-a-vis the other alternatives.

The time estimates associated with manual backup are based on the assumption that disaster recovery times may be exceedingly long and there is no Stage II (steady-state) operation. With manual backup, daily disaster costs mount very rapidly, and they do not begin to fall until the main system is restored. Total disaster cost will be exceedingly high under this alternative.

The time estimates associated with the sister banking arrangement reflect the fact that, when the sister bank is operating a system similar to the victim bank, Stage I costs may be reduced. However, Stage II will tend to drag on, since there will be a capacity problem at the sister bank's facility. Stage III will be shorter than with the manual backup because the backlog of transactions will be smaller.

The advantage of a shell site is its ready access. However, there is no equipment at the site, hence we assume Stage I to be relatively long. Shorter Stage II and III times for this alternative reflect the fact that once the equipment is in place, the system will function close to normal.

Time estimates for the hot site reflect the fact that, notwithstanding the high subscription costs, this is definitely the superior alternative in terms of operations. Compatible equipment exists and is ready for use the moment a disaster strikes. Stage I is short in duration, backlog is minimized, and this results in smoother transitions from initial disaster to contingent operation to recovery. The main drawback to this alternative is the annual subscription fee.

## Simulation and Results

Three hundred EDP disasters were generated for each of the four contingency plans. Cumulative distributions of total dollar loss were created from this exercise. FSD and SSD were then applied to the cumulative loss distributions. The FSD results indicate that the losses from using a manual backup system dominate all of the other alternatives. Also, the losses from the shell site dominate those of the hot site. SSD adds another dominance relation by indicating that the losses while using a shell site dominate losses from using a shared arrangement with a sister bank. Hence, the hot site and sister bank alternatives are preferred over shell sites, and manual backup. The cumulative loss distributions for the four plans are shown in Figure 2.

## Comparing the SD and EV Approaches

Risk analyses were performed for the same four contingency plans using the expected value approach. The results are given as expected losses (including the present value of subscription fees paid for the shell and hot sites), and they are presented in Table 3.

The expected value approach ranks the plans in the following order (best to worst):

\- hot site

\- sister bank

\- shell site

\- manual operation.

Comparing these results with the SD results yields some Interesting observations. The EV approach ranks the hot site over the sister banking alternative. However, SD does not distinguish between the two methods. The reason is because SD considers the entire distribution, not just the average loss. The implication is that which method is better will depend on how well we accept the data that was used in the analysis. For example, if we are more concerned about small losses, we may choose a sister banking arrangement, even though expected loss is greater under the sister bank alternative.

Note that it is important to perform sensitivity analyses so that the performance of the choices can be examined under alternative assumptions. For example, in an earlier analysis we considered the case of a smaller bank and the sister bank arrangement tended to have lower losses than other alternatives. As noted above, one of the advantages of this method dology is that the computerized model encourages the decision maker to examine numerous alternatives from different perspectives.

## Conclusion

Most managers and analysts agree that disasters have the potential to cause severe losses if they strike corporate data centers. However, because disasters are (fortunately) rare events, it is difficult to determine how many resources should be devoted to contingency planning. Traditional risk analysis methods have exacerbated the problem by focusing on the causes of disaster. In contrast, the stochastic dominance method presented here directly addresses the question of which alternative contingency plan should be chosen. This model also evaluates the alternatives in terms of their direct effect on the business, hence the analyses and conclusions are understandable by managers with little or no MIS background.

One of the strengths of this method is that it recognizes the importance of time. As a result, the evaluation of alternatives parallels the development of the contingency plan. Alternatives are evaluated on the basis of how they would perform if a disaster strikes. This methodology will produce both a fully developed contingency plan and a consistent evaluation of the alternatives.

There is a need for more research into this methodology. In particular, it is necessary to develop full-scale models for organizations in different industries. There are three major topics that need to be addressed. From a theoretical standpoint, it would be interesting to see if there are inherent differences in contingency plans between different industries. From a more practical standpoint, analysis of firms within industries should be performed to create industry probability parameters that can be used by individual managers as guidelines for their own planning. Finally, extensive work with actual cases may be able to eliminate certain alternatives that consistently result in higher costs.

# Appendix A Definitions of Stochastic Dominance

To define first degree stochastic dominance (FSD), suppose that the decision maker is choosing between two risky alternatives, f and g. Let F and G be the cumulative distribution functions of outcomes corresponding to alternatives f and g, respectively. Then, FSD is defined as:

$F >_{1} G$ if and only if $G(x) > F(x)$ for all admissible x.

To define second degree stochastic dominance (SSD), we need to assume that decision makers are risk averse. Next, we need to define two functions as:

$F^2 (x) = \int F(y)\mathrm{d}y,$ and

$G^2 (x) = \int G(y)dy.$

Then, SSD can be defined as:

$F > _{2} G$ if and only if $G^{2}(x) > F^{2}(x)$ for all admissible x.

There are some useful properties of the SD definitions. The first thing to note is that if $F >_{1}G$ then $F >_{2}G$ . That is, if FSD holds, then SSD holds as well. The converse is not necessarily true. Secondly, both FSD and SSD support transitivity. That is, if $F >_{1}G$ and $G >_{1}H$ then $F >_{1}H$ .

One of the nice features about FSD is that it can be observed from plots of the cumulative distribution functions. The definition states that if the plot of F always lies above (to the left) of G, then G dominates F. In this paper, the x-axis is denominated as costs, so if G dominates F, it means that there is a higher probability that alternative G would lead to higher costs. If the lines cross, FSD is indeterminate and we have to examine SSD. Unfortunately, SSD is more difficult to determine visually, but some generalizations usually hold true. If the cumulative distribution plots intersect once at low x values, SSD is usually indeterminant, because it implies that one plan is better for small disasters and another for larger disasters. However, if one line crosses another for a short interval and then returns, SSD will usually still hold. In any case, the dominance is calculated more precisely by the simulator.

The main advantage of stochastic dominance analysis is that it requires a computer to perform the simulations and calculations. On the other hand, the calculations are not very complex, so it should not be difficult for an MIS manager to find the necessary resources.

There are three main steps to performing the SD analysis. First, the computer simulates several disasters from the specified distributions. Second, this data is converted to cumulative distribution functions and examined for first and second degree dominance. Finally, the cumulative distribution functions should be plotted. There are commercial programs available that perform the second two tasks, but it will be necessary to generate the simulated disasters for each institution individually.
