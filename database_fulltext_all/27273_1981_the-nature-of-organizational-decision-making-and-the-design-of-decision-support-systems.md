The nature and function of a Decision Support System is heavily influenced by the organizational environment in which it is used. In this third article of the Decision Support System Series, George Huber explores the potential contribution of DSS in the context of four organizational decision making environments.

# The Nature of Organizational Decision Making and the Design of Decision Support Systems\*

By: George P. Huber

## Abstract

Today's Decision Support Systems (DSS) are almost invariably designed to function in rational, or rationalized, decision making environments. Many organizational environments, such as political environments or garbage can environments, are more accurately portrayed, however, with models other than the Rational Model. Can DSS be useful in such environments? What are the boundary conditions for the application of DSS? These are the questions addressed in this article.

Keywords: Decision support systems, information requirements, decision making, organizational behavior

ACM Categories: 1.3, 2.11, 3.52

Very, very few of the world's managers have access to a Decision Support System (a DSS, as defined by the books, articles, and marketing materials that use this term). On the other hand, every manager has a “decision support system,” (a dss, a system consisting of the information sources and decision aids that the manager draws upon as the occasion requires). Further, today's DSS assists managers in understanding only a small portion of their decision environments. Increases in the scope of their databases and in the variety of the decision aids that they offer will enable tomorrow's DSS to assist managers in understanding a much larger portion of these environments. In particular, tomorrow's DSS will assist managers as they attempt to more fully understand and account for the organizational environments in which their decisions are made. This is inevitable. Everyday observations and an abundance of scientific evidence make it clear that organizational environments have a great impact on managerial decision processes and choices. Those who design user-specific decision-aiding technologies, such as DSS, will not allow this important determinant of decision making behavior to go unattended for long.

The purposes of this article are to examine the nature of organizational decision environments and to examine the nature of the DSS that might be specifically designed to assist managers who find themselves operating in these environments. Before proceeding to these tasks, it will be useful to put DSS into some perspective. First, let us remind ourselves of what we all know but sometimes forget: every manager has and uses a management information system (an mis), a combination of information sources and channels, and procedures for drawing on these sources and channels. Such systems typically consist of the trade magazines, file cabinets, sales reps, recollections, telephones, organization charts, and the other resources and programs that the manager uses when seeking to answer a "What is . . .?" question. Keeping this fact in mind, we see that a Management Information System (MIS) is the portion of a management information system (mis) that deals with computer-stored, and perhaps processed, data.

Second, let us remind ourselves that every manager also has and uses a decision support system (a dss), a combination of his or her information system, and a set of decision aids for examining the desirability of possible choices. Such a system might include decision rules, policy manuals, recollections, and staff analysts among the programs and resources that the manager uses when seeking to answer a “What if . . .?” question. With this fact, too, in mind, it follows that a Decision Support System (DSS) is the portion of the decision support system (dss) that is computer enhanced. (Conventional use of the term also implies that the relationship between the computer and the decision maker is highly interactive, i.e., that the DSS includes a personal terminal that accesses an online processor.)

Third and last, let us recall that “a main argument of the DSS approach is that effective design depends on the technician’s detailed understanding of management decision processes,” and that ideally this understanding results in a DSS that is custom designed to reflect the decision maker’s personal style and organizational environment [14]. With this perspective as a base of departure, the nature of organizational decision environments and the nature of DSS designed to assist managers making decisions in these environments will be examined.

## Organizational Decision Making

Organizational decisions are decisions made on behalf of the organization. The decision making units may be as small as an individual, e.g., a manager, or as large as the entire organizational membership. While personal goals often influence organizational decisions, the decisions themselves are legitimized to other units and agencies as fulfilling organizational needs. As Chester Barnard put the matter, “Organizational decisions do not relate to personal purposes, but to organization purposes,” [1].

Organizational scientists have learned a great deal about how organizational decisions get made, and it would be foolish not to draw on the results of their work as we examine the complex topic of organizational decision making and the various organizational decision environments. One of the contributions of these scientists is a set of four conceptual models for portraying and interpreting organizational decision environments: the Rational Model, the Political/Competitive Model, the Garbage Can Model, and the Program Model. These four models can serve us in three ways. First, they can provide a framework for interpreting the decision making that goes on in the organizations in which we work. Second, in that DSS are generally more useful if they are custom designed for the decision maker's environment, the models can help us interpret and articulate the organizational aspects of that environment. Finally, as we shall see, the models can suggest the nature of the information and decision aids that might be useful in specific types of organizational environments, and thus might be helpful in the design of “specific DSS.”

In the next section of the article the nature of the decision making environments characterized by these models will be examined, and in the succeeding section the implications of the models for the design of DSS will be examined. Let us keep in mind as we proceed that by definition all models, including these models, are abstractions of reality. They are simplifications that allow us to highlight certain features of complex situations. Thus, while in a given situation, one model might be more valid than the others, in almost all important situations, all four models have validity. Part of what goes on is interpretable with one model and part with another. The great majority of environments have their rational aspects, their political/competitive aspects, their chance or garbage can aspects, and their predictable program aspects.

The Rational Model will be presented first, as the other three models are invariably presented as relating to it in some way, e.g., as an alternative or as a complementary elaboration.

## The Rational Model

The Rational Model portrays an environment where organizational decisions are consequences of organizational units using information in an intendedly rational manner to make choices on behalf of the organization.

As we have all observed, organizations attempt to create and maintain within themselves decision environments describable with the Rational Model. In addition, they attempt to convince their members and outside observers that they are successful in this regard. Of course, they are only partially successful in either of these attempts.

A number of sets of writings are related to the Rational Model. One consists of reports on the actual use of normative procedures, such as operations research or program evaluation, in organizational decision making $[10, 13]$ . Another consists of reports on the apparently rational use of information by individuals in simulated organizational environments $[11, 22]$ . The third set of writings consists of authoritative descriptions of the nature of organizational decision making $[18, 21]$ .

Although the Rational Model is an extremely important model, both because it is publicly ascribed to and because it is often the target of those who espouse alternative models, it is easy to forget that it is simply a model, a fragmentary representation of what actually occurs. It is also an inadequate basis for the design of the relatively encompassing Decision Support Systems that will become more commonplace as this decision aiding technology evolves from its present embryonic state.

## The Political/Competitive Model

The Political/Competitive Model portrays an environment where organizational decisions are consequences of the application of strategies and tactics by units seeking to influence decision processes in directions that will result in choices favorable to themselves.

Each of us has no doubt encountered organizational decision environments that were readily describable with the Political/Competitive Model. The model highlights the fact that different participants in the decision process often have different goals. There are a good many writings related to the model, although a composite model or theory is not yet available. Some of these writings concern organizational power, particularly how such power is acquired [19, 23, 25]. Others describe field studies where the decision processes and outcomes are interpreted as having political bases [9, 26]. They include studies of capital budgeting that show it to be a “somewhat distorted, political process, far less analytical than the normative literature suggests.” [21]. Expositions on the role of politics in organizational decision making are provided by March [16], Pettigrew [24], and Lerner [15]. Writings on conflict and bargaining are also related to the literature on organizational politics, but with few exceptions [27, 35] these writings are not based on studies undertaken in the complex and hierarchical organizations where DSS are most typically found.

The relationships between the Rational Model and the Political/Competitive Model are apparently not well understood, either by organizational scientists or by managers themselves. Certainly it is fair to say that not everyone agrees about the nature of the relationships. At the one extreme are those who believe that appeals to “the public interest,” or “the stockholders’ interests,” and also the rational/analytic studies that seek to find means for fulfilling these interests, are merely weapons used by members seeking to achieve their own interests. At another extreme are those who believe that political behavior is a rational approach for determining the relative weight to be given to subgoals in the development of “the organizational goal,” and that such behavior fits within an enlarged definition of the Rational Model. Still another point of view is that organizations can design their reward systems such that their members will make choices in keeping with organizational objectives rather than personal objectives, and in this way eliminate the need to consider organizations as competitive environments.

## The Garbage Can Model

This is a relatively new model. Although the basic concept was alluded to in 1963 [5], the model itself was not fully explicated until 1972 [3]. The essence of the Garbage Can Model is that organizational decisions are consequences of intersections of problems looking for solutions, solutions looking for problems, and opportunities for making decisions. These three variables, problems, solutions, and opportunities for decision making, e.g., annual budget reviews, and changes of corporate leadership, along with a fourth, participants, are portrayed by Cohen, et al. [3], and later by March and Olsen [17] as being tossed into and churning about in a garbage can.

The Garbage Can Model highlights the roles of chance and timing in determining organizational choices. In spite of the appearance of superficiality imparted by its name, this is an important model if for no other reason than that it is apparently very useful in interpreting choices in certain organizational settings, and yet it relies so little on available behavioral or normative theories of decision making. In several aspects the Garbage Can Model describes the environment of the “unstructured” decision processes observed by Mintzberg and his associates $[20,21]$ .

## The Program Model

In 1888, James Bryce observed that “to the vast majority of mankind nothing is more agreeable than to escape the need for mental exertion. . . to most people nothing is more troublesome than the effort of thinking,” [2]. Fifty years later Chester Barnard made a similar observation in his book, The Functions of the Executive.

The making of decisions as everyone knows from personal experience, is a burdensome task...Accordingly, it will be observed that men generally try to avoid making decisions, beyond a limited degree when they are rather uncritical responses to conditions [1].

In 1978, Herbert Simon won the Nobel prize for, among other professional contributions, his writings suggesting that organizational decision making was significantly and adversely affected by the cognitive limitations of the human decision maker [18, 29]. In 1966, Anthony Downs suggested that the nature of a manager's task environment was not conducive to the making of high quality decisions [6], a point of view that seems supported by Mintzberg's studies [20].

This evidence and authoritative testimony leads one to ask, “When rationality and analysis do not guide organizational decision making, what does?” The Program Model identifies two factors that seem to determine many organizational decisions. One is “programs.” Everyday observations and a good many scientific studies remind us that decision making behavior in organizations is affected by standard operating procedures, group norms, budget limitations, and other forms of action-directing or action-constraining organizational “programs.” The other factor that seems to guide decision making in a wide variety of circumstances is the previous “programming” of the decision makers. Again we have our own observations and an abundance of scientific evidence attesting to the fact that decision making behavior is influenced by programming. Examples include professional training, planned and accidental reinforcements of past decision related behavior, on-the-job training, and other forms of cognitive or motivational “programming.”

The Program Model emphasizes the effect of these programs and programmings on organizational decisions. In its most rudimentary form, the model portrays a setting where organizational decisions are consequences of the programs and programming of the units involved. Since an organization's programs are slow to change, and since the effects of programming are hard to erase, decision making behavior at time t is largely predictable from decision making behavior at t-1, irrespective of whether this is appropriate.

At this juncture it may be useful to repeat what we noted earlier, that these models are alternative ways of interpreting organizational decisions, and that in most important decision situations all four have validity. $^{2}$ The great majority of significant organizational decision settings have their rational aspects, political aspects, predictable process aspects, and chance or garbage can aspects. This fact suggests that in many instances information and decision aids other than those commonly associated with the Rational Model might be useful additions to the DSS, and that DSS designers should consider including such information and aids in the DSS that they create. To further this consideration, in the next few pages the nature of the information and decision aids that might be useful in various organizational decision environments will be examined.

## Information Requirements and Analytic Aids

## For the Rational Model

Today's DSS are almost all designed to serve in organizational settings that can be portrayed most appropriately with the Rational Model. The encompassing list of data requirements for this model follows. Of course, not every decision requires all of the data types listed.

## Basic Data

1. What are the alternatives? (e.g., Who are the possible suppliers?)

2. What are the future conditions that might be encountered? (e.g., What are the possible interest rates?)

3. What are the criteria to be used in evaluating alternatives? (e.g., Is overhead a required component of the cost analysis? Is community reaction to be considered?)

## Elaborating Data

4. What are the probabilities of the future conditions?

5. What are the relative importances of the various criteria?

## Performance Data

6. What are the payoffs, or costs, associated with various outcomes?

7. What are the constraints on the payoffs or costs? (e.g., Is there a minimum rate of return? Is there a budget limit?)

Analytic aids for the Rational Model settings include those familiar to everyone, such as break-even and rate-of-return models, linear programming algorithms, and simulation languages and models. In almost any complex situation, the answers to some of the above questions are matters of judgment. Everyday organizational experiences would lead us to expect that in at least some instances the combined judgments of several people would be used to answer an individual question, in order to obtain a more complete or exact answer. Further, in other instances we would expect that no one person or unit would provide the judgments needed to answer all of the relevant questions; few people are omniscient. Together these points remind us that organizational decisions almost always involve inputs from more than one participant, even in the case of the Rational Model. For the most part, then, complex decisions made in organizations generally involve several participants, and thus are organizational decisions in the sense of collective participation as well as in the sense of being made on behalf of the organizations. $^{3}$

In some cases the numerical data for DSS in the Rational Model setting are directly retrievable from computer accessed databases, and the proportion of DSS for which this is the case will increase dramatically in the next several years, as database management systems and interfacing software make their impact. Of some interest will be the inclusion within tomorrow's DSS of existing computer aided, judgment eliciting technologies, such as computer automated Delphi studies [35] or computer enhanced Social Judgment Analysis [8], for obtaining information such as the identity of alternatives or estimates of the probabilities of future conditions. Of similar interest will be the increased computer storage and accessing of non-numerical data. With regard to “What are the alternatives?,” for example, it is inevitable that a great deal of information about the identity of vendors and suppliers, and their most up-to-date offerings and prices, will be computer accessible using the desired product or service as a key word retrieval cue, just as non-numerical information about job candidates and job openings is available on today's computer aided employment systems.

## For the Political/Competitive Model

With respect to the Political/Competitive Model, the organizational science literature suggests that decision makers operating in environments aptly described with this model frequently deal with the following questions:

1. Who are the parties involved in the decision process?

2. What is the potential influence of each of these parties?

3. Which alternatives do, or should, each of these parties, or my competitors, favor?

4. How can I influence these parties, or my competitors?

5. Which strategies might my competitors choose, and how likely are they to be chosen?

It is interesting to note that DSS are currently being used in environments characterized by the Political/Competitive Model. For example, in labor-management negotiations, and in utility municipality negotiations (over utility rates), online DSS are being used to assess the impact of proposals and counter proposals during negotiation. Similarly, DSS are being used in negotiations between contractors and subcontractors, and in mergers and acquisitions, for determining the benefits to both parties that will be derived from various financial arrangements.

It seems likely that future DSS will be of particular assistance in answering the first of the above questions, especially in situations such as those described by Shumway, et al. [28], where there are many parties involved in the decision making process. We can expect that they will contain, for example, the identities of all those executives with authority for approving funding or enactment of the decision, the identities of all members of committees involved in the organizational decision processes, the identities of the staff advisors of each of the committee members or executives, and the phone numbers and addresses of all these parties.

Other DSS will be useful in answering the second, third, and fourth questions. For example, solicitors of government contracts, marketers of industrial goods, lobbyists, heads of government agencies, and perhaps even corporate division managers have choosing among strategies for influencing other decision makers as a primary responsibility, and do this in settings that are readily portrayable with the Political/Competitive Model. In order to assist such decision makers, it is likely that tomorrow's DSS will be even more capable than are today's in providing information about the nature of previously approved contracts and the success rate of competitors, for soliciting contracts; the nature of previously made purchases and the success rate of competitors, for marketing industrial goods; political affiliation, voting record, and primary supporters, for lobbying; the missions, mandates and budgets of superordinate units, for obtaining authorizations; and new corporate policy or strategy proclamations, for packaging divisional budget requests.

Finally, with respect to the fifth question concerning the competitor's strategies, it should be noted that there are DSS today that support the decision making of managers operating in formally competitive environments. For example, the computer-accessible databases of professional sports managers contain information about the probabilities of certain strategies being adopted by the opposing teams, information developed from historical databases. Besides convenience, having these several types of information available in an online DSS will allow for easy updating, and datedness is an important problem in hard copy maintenance of information of this nature.

With respect to decision aids, we might suppose that game theory would be useful to decision makers operating in a Political/Competitive environment. This supposition seems not to have been fulfilled in practice, however, perhaps because of the restrictive assumptions necessary for most game theory models to apply. It does seem, though, that in the future decision aids might be devised that will aid in employing information such as that described above. For example, today's industry lobbyists use information concerning how individual legislators feel about each of a bill's key features as they make the tradeoffs and compromises necessary to ensure passage. Especially when the number of key features and/or the number of legislators is large, computer-actualized optimization algorithms would be of immense benefit and could be easily designed. It may be that mathematical studies of coalition formation have already addressed this question.

## For the Garbage Can Model

Let us turn now to the Garbage Can Model. Recall that this model emphasizes environments where decisions are largely intersections in time of problems looking for solutions, solutions looking for problems, and opportunities for decision making. A further thrust of this model is that in many settings the nature and occurrence of problems, solutions, and decision making opportunities are not fully predictable. What, then, are the questions that a decision maker operating in such a setting might wish to have assistance in answering? It seems that they would include the following:

## Problems and Opportunities

1. What are the problems and opportunities that might be forthcoming?

2. How likely is it that these problems and opportunities will occur?

3. When might they occur?

## Solutions

4. What are the potential solutions that might be forthcoming?

5. How likely is it that these solutions will occur?

6. When might they occur?

## Decision Making Opportunities

7. What are the potential decision making opportunities that might be forthcoming?

8. How likely is it that these opportunities will occur?

## 9. When might they occur?

At the present time the answers to these and related questions are generally obtained through the organization's noncomputer-aided information system [12]. Could DSS, like those now in existence, be of assistance in answering such questions? The answer is "Yes" in situations where the occurrence of an event is a consequence of other forecastable events. For example, current DSS can provide probabilistic estimates of income streams and capital availability and also probabilistic forecasts of certain events leading to cash outlays, such as equipment failures. Thus, they can forecast the intersection of anticipatable "solutions" and "problems," and in this rudimentary manner be useful in organizational decision environments that can be described with the Garbage Can Model.

In some respects, however, the above example misses the point; the unique thrust of the Garbage Can Model is its focus on events that are not readily anticipated. This simultaneous consideration of 1) the dynamic, probabilistic nature of the environment characterized with the Garbage Can Model, and 2) the fact that much of the information managers seek is used to build a mental model of their environment [20], suggests that for managers operating in a Garbage Can environment, a primary need is for a DSS that would help in efficient environmental scanning. Such decision aids are, of course, within easy reach of today's technology. For example, we can expect tomorrow's DSS to store trade journals, newspapers, segments of television broadcasts, and to access these and other media through key word searches. In other words, they will be time-saving and effectiveness-enhancing aids to "the manager (who) perpetually scans his environment for information" [20]. Similarly, especially considering the fact that "a good part of the information the manager collects in his monitor role arrives in verbal form" [20], we can expect tomorrow's DSS to contain the names, attributes, and locations of people, both inside and outside the organization, who are experts on certain aspects of the environment, and for it to conduct searches of this file on the basis of key words.

Somewhere between a DSS's use for forecasting events in modelable stochastic environments and its use for aiding managers in obtaining information with which to build models of their environments, is its potential use in monitoring the environment for preselected opportunities or problems. For example, routine monitoring of stock market transactions, patent applications, and financial reports could identify potential investment opportunities or possible candidates for corporate acquisition.

With respect to the inclusion of analytic aids for DSS designed for Garbage Can environments, it may be that some forms of search theory would be useful. For example, it is likely that decision trees will be incorporated in future DSS for determining the merits of dealing with an available “problem” or “solution” versus waiting to see if the problem goes away of its own accord or if the solution is replaced by another of greater utility.

## For the Program Model

The Program Model is the last to be discussed. Recall that this model emphasizes the fact that in many environments organizational decision making behaviors are largely the consequences of programs and programming, and that further, because these are slow to change, the model suggests that organizational decision processes and outcomes are largely predictable from historical data.

Managers whose organizational environments can be described with this model have both a special challenge and a special opportunity. The special challenge is to make sure that the decision processes that they manage do not become dysfunctionally routine and inflexible. While programs often increase the user's efficiency they also tend to "stay in place" and to be employed long after the circumstances that initiated their use have changed. Thus, "when an organization discovers a solution to a problem by searching in a particular way, it will be more likely to search in that way in future problems of the same type" [5]. Similarly, an organization's structural characteristics and reward systems create routine routings of information [12], and in this way lead to routinization of decision making behavior.

Although there seem to be no scientific studies that directly test the hypothesis that decision-aiding technologies themselves lead to routinization of decision making behavior, it seems reasonable to believe that, to the extent that the technologies are found helpful, their helpfulness will reinforce their use. Thus, while the challenge for all managers is to avoid allowing the calcification of decision making processes in their units, this challenge is especially great where the overall organizational decision environment is validly described using the Program Model, and may become even more important with the implementation of a Decision Support System.

The special opportunity for managers operating in Program Model environments is that routineness leads to predictability, and this, in turn, increases the acquirability and reliability of information that can be used in managing decision making efforts. Examples of such information include:

1. the frequency distributions of the time necessary for subordinate or adjacent units to produce decision-related information or recommendations,

2. the expected accuracy and precision of this information, including data concerning the nature and expected magnitude of the biases that may be involved, and

3. the frequency distributions of the time and cost for decision-implementing units to carry out their assignments.

Data of this nature are relevant to the decision support system (dss) of any manager. It seems that they could also be an important component of a Decision Support System (DSS). Certainly, today's MIS already contain "standard times" for organizational activities varying in scope from operator hand movements to construction of subassemblies, and DSS already monitor the relationship between forecasts and outcomes, and use the findings to update the DSS's exponential smoothing forecasts and models. We can expect that data of this nature will be commonly accessible in tomorrow's DSS, especially for those designed in Program Model environments. Further, tomorrow's DSS will more frequently contain decision aids that will facilitate capitalizing on the availability of these data. For example, we can expect them to more frequently contain Gantt charts and Critical Path and PERT-Cost algorithms and displays.

## Concluding Comments

Our own observations and an abundance of scientific studies make clear that organizational decision environments 1) vary greatly, and 2) have considerable impact on the decision making behavior of individuals. One purpose of this article was to examine the organizational science literature that describes these various environments, and in this way help us to organize our thinking about decision making and also to interpret the organizational aspects of the environments of the managers for whom we design specific DSS.

A second purpose was to examine the nature of the information and decision aids that might be useful in these various environments. The results of this effort make clear that as the technologies for designing DSS continue to evolve, the nature of organizational decision environments will more and more be seen as a variable that should determine the nature of Decision Support Systems.

## About the Author

George P. Huber is a Professor of Business and also of Industrial Engineering at the University of Wisconsin-Madison. His research has focused on decision making and information processing at the individual, group, and organizational levels. He has over fifty publications dealing with these topics. His current research interest is the design of organizational information systems and decision support systems.

Professor Huber has held full-time managerial positions in industry, government, and higher education, and has had extended consulting relationships with a variety of organizations in both the private and public sectors.
