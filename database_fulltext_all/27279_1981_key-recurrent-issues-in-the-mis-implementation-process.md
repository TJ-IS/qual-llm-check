# Key Recurrent Issues in the MIS Implementation Process\*

By: Michael J. Ginzberg

## Abstract

Previous analyses of the MIS implementation process have focused on the conduct of individual process stages as explanatory of successful or unsuccessful implementation. Process stages, however, involve multiple issues, and it is unlikely that all issues are equally important for good performance at a stage. This article attempts to identify generic implementation issues — those issues which arise at many process stages. An empirical analysis identified six such issues, and suggested that three of them were quite important for differentiating between successful and unsuccessful MIS implementations.

Keywords: MIS development, MIS implementation, organization change, user involvement
ACM Categories: 2.0, 3.3, 4.0

## Introduction

In recent years, a number of researchers have studied MIS implementation as a process of change [6, 8, 16]. The conclusion of each of these studies was that implementations are more likely to succeed if they closely follow the dictates of a normative model of change — e.g., the Lewin/Schein model [13], the Kolb/Frohman model [7], or the diffusion of innovation model [15].

Each of these models is organized as a sequence of stages, and analyses of MIS implementation using these models have examined the relative importance to overall project success of good performance at each stage. There is evidence to suggest, however, that process stages do not represent unitary concepts $[6]$ . Rather, each stage is composed of a number of issues requiring resolution, and good resolution of one of the issues does not necessarily imply good resolution of the others. Thus, it is difficult to say whether “good” performance at a particular stage requires good resolution of all issues or only of some issues, and if the latter is the case, which issues are most important?

Empirical analyses to date have not focused on this level of specific issues or types of activities occurring within, and perhaps across, stages, though Zmud & Cox [17] in a recent article highlight the importance of two specific issues — involvement and training. The purpose of this article is to attempt to identify generic issues which arise during implementation — i.e., issues which arise recurrently throughout the implementation process — and to assess the relative importance of these issues for determining implementation success or failure.

## Identifying Generic Implementation Issues

The implementation literature provides consistent evidence of the importance of only two generic issues for success in MIS implementation. These are management support $[2, 12, 14]$ and user involvement $[3, 9]$ . Further, more recent evidence even calls into question the necessity for substantial user involvement $[1]$ . Thus, there is not a well articulated list of generic implementation issues to serve as a starting point, and identification of these issues must be by induction.

Data collected from thirty-five users of twenty-seven information systems were used to identify generic implementation issues. The data consisted of each user's assessment of how well each of seventy-two statements described the implementation process for the system (see Appendix A for further description of the data). One of these statements concerned overall satisfaction with the system, and was used to differentiate between successful and unsuccessful implementations. The remaining seventy-one statements described specific implementation issues suggested by the Kolb/Frohman model of change as shown in Figure 1.

The Kolb/Frohman model is organized as a sequence of seven stages, and the issues outlined by the model are tied to particular stages. However, consideration of the issues suggested some recurrent themes; that is, similar issues were raised at a number of stages. It was believed that these recurrent issues might be the key issues requiring resolution in order to assure implementation success.

To assess whether these seemingly recurrent issues did indeed constitute “themes,” a principal components analysis was performed on the seventy-one statements describing implementation issues (see Appendix A for a description of this analysis). The analysis indicated that six factors should be extracted. These factors are described in Table 1, and the specific items which constitute them are listed in Appendix B.

The procedure used to construct factors (see Appendix A) was designed to assure that factors were interpretable rather than to extract independent factors. Indeed, each factor does appear to have a dominant theme, and both the factor titles and the factor intercorrelations suggest that these themes are not independent (see Table 2). All six factors differentiate to some extent between successes and failures, and for four of them the difference is significant (see last column of Table 1).

Each of the factors does present a recurrent issue, as each includes issues arising at multiple stages in the Kolb/Frohman model (see Appendix B). The issues presented by these factors are not identical to those most commonly suggested in the literature — i.e., support and involvement — but they are quite similar. In fact, they can be viewed as refinements of these more general prescriptions. For example, management can support a project in many ways and to a variety of degrees. Some of these may prove to be adequate and some inadequate. Indeed, Bean et al. [2], found that one level of support, top management's selecting projects, had no impact on implementation success or failure. Organizational commitment to a project, however, implies a substantial degree of support, and this may be a more meaningful way to characterize the requirements for project success.

## Relative Importance of Generic Implementation Issues

Each of the six factors identified represents an issue or type of activity which normative models of the implementation process highlight as being important to successful MIS implementation. Further, successful implementations report better handling than unsuccessful implementations, on average, of each of these issues. Even so, this does not tell us anything about the relative importance of these issues in determining implementation success. We can begin to address this question by examining how well each of the factors can differentiate between successful and unsuccessful implementations.

For each of the factors, a classification rule was developed that “best” differentiated between successes and failures, i.e., the rule that correctly classified the largest percentage of the total sample of thirty-five users. In cases where multiple classification rules produced the same overall result, the rule which best classified the smaller group (failures) was selected. The results show reasonably good identification of successes for all factors, but considerable variation in their ability to separate out failures (see Table 3). Clearly, Factor 5, Commitment to change, is best able to differentiate between successes and failures, while Factor 2, Organizational commitment to the project, is least able. In the case of each factor, some of the failures score as high or higher than some of the successes, and vice versa. Thus, good handling of any one of these issues does not appear to guarantee success, nor poor handling, failure.

While no individual factor was sufficient to completely distinguish successes from failures, perhaps some combination of factors could do so. To test this, the factors were used as the independent variables in a linear discriminant analysis. Two analyses were run using the SPSS [11] discriminant analysis subprogram. One, using the DIRECT option, forced inclusion of all factors in the final discriminant function. The other, a STEPWISE analysis, admitted only those factors which significantly improved discriminating ability.

Although the overall discriminant function from the stepwise analysis is significant (p = .0026) as are each of the factors included in it, other criteria might be used to evaluate it. First, its classification performance can be compared with that of the best single factor, Factor 5. For the total sample, both the discriminant function and Factor 5 classify the same number of cases correctly (82.9%). However, they differ in their ability to correctly identify failures — 70% correct for Factor 5 alone, 90% correct for the discriminant function. Figure 2 demonstrates this difference graphically. For Factor 5 alone, there is a substantial overlap of the scores of successes and failures. In fact, there is no part of the range of factor scores on which one can unambiguously identify a case as a failure. For the discriminant function, there tends to be more “bunching” of scores within the two groups, and, all but one of the failures fall outside the range of all but one of the successes. Thus, the addition of Factors 1 and 2 does improve the ability to identify unsuccessful implementations.

Another criterion for assessing the worth of a discriminant function is suggested by Morrison [10]. The proportion of cases correctly classified by the discriminant function can be compared to the proportion of cases one would expect to be correctly classified by chance. This latter proportion is a function of the number of cases actually in each group and the number of cases classified into each group. For two groups it is:

$$
P = \pi \alpha + (1 - \pi) (1 - \alpha),
$$

where,

$$
\begin{array}{c} \pi = \text { the   proportion   of   cases } \\ \text { actually   in   group   1,   and } \end{array}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{l} \alpha = \text { the   proportion   of   cases } \\ \text { classified   into   group   1. } \end{array}
$$

For the analysis presented above, P = 54.29%. The percentage actually classified correctly was 82.86%, and the difference between these two proportions is significant (t = 3.39, p < .001).

Frank, Massy, and Morrison [4] point out, however, that when the same data are used both to fit the discriminant function and to test its ability to classify cases, an upward bias in discriminating ability is introduced. One method they suggest for estimating the size of the bias is to run the discriminant analysis on a random sample of cases similar to the original set of cases, but for which there is no expected difference between groups. This was accomplished by randomly reassigning the thirty-five cases into the two groups, using random two-digit integers to determine group membership. This resulted in group proportions roughly equal to those in the original sample. A discriminant analysis was then run on this simulated sample. This procedure was repeated five times, and the results are shown in Table 5. On the average, 68.57% of the cases in the simulated samples were correctly classified, compared to 50.97% which would be expected purely by chance (see Equation 1). The ability to classify correctly 82.86% of the cases in the original sample indicates that the discriminant function does possess a real ability to differentiate successes from failures, though it is not as powerful as the initial analysis might suggest.

One final bit of evidence concerning the value of this discriminant function should be mentioned. One of the respondents specified as being a success, on the basis of the quantitative answer to the dependent variable questionnaire item, wrote a number of comments on the questionnaire form about the system, its performance, its fit to organizational needs, etc. All of these comments suggest that the project was in fact a failure. The discriminant analysis, too, classified this project as a failure. This provides some slight additional evidence concerning the ability of this discriminant function to differentiate between successes and failures.

## Discussion

This analysis does appear both to have identified generic or recurrent issues in the MIS implementation process, and to have differentiated among these issues in terms of their relative importance to implementation success. Three issues are highlighted as being central to successful MIS implementation. Careful analysis shows that these issues are related to the popular nostrums, support and involvement. Indeed, they amplify our understanding of the types of support and involvement that are needed.

Two of the issues concern commitment (Factors 2 and 5). The first is commitment to the project — doing what is necessary throughout the stages of system development, installation, and use to assure that the problem is understood and that the system developed solves that problem. Both users and management must develop this commitment, as this increases the odds that they will take appropriate actions at each project stage — from pre-design through post installation — to assure the project's success.

The second commitment issue is commitment to change. Many new information systems imply a substantial degree of change to the organization, and an organization unwilling to accommodate that change is unlikely to be successful in implementing the system. This issue should be particularly important for decision support systems, which typically imply a greater degree of change than do other information systems [5].

Thus, the two commitment issues, while related, are not identical. The first concerns taking those actions necessary to assure that the system is a good one, and provides a solution to the organization's problem. The second concerns the willingness of those involved to make the changes in behavior, procedures, etc., that are necessary for the system to work. Both issues require action by both management and system users. And, the data indicate that successful handling of both issues contributes to project success, that neither one alone is sufficient.

The third issue highlighted by this analysis is the extent of project definition and planning (Factor 1). Detailed consideration of organizational needs, project impacts, training requirements, and evaluation criteria, as well as care in specifying the roles of project team members, are all parts of this factor. The more thorough the planning effort, the less likely are unforeseen circumstances which could endanger the project. This factor identifies some of the key questions toward which the users' involvement in the project should be directed. And, like the commitment factors, this factor too should be viewed as placing demands on both management and users.

As noted earlier, the coefficient of Factor 1 increases somewhat in the stepwise analysis, that is, when Factors 3, 4, and 6 are omitted. This suggests that project definition and planning might be less important in comparison to the commitment factors than the relative magnitude of the coefficients would suggest. Thus, we should recognize that while procedures for project planning and management can contribute to project success, they cannot make up for a lack of commitment. Perhaps this explains why some organizations continue to experience MIS implementation difficulty even after they have adopted a formalized system development methodology.

These three issues provide an understanding of the MIS implementation process which goes beyond that provided by previous analyses. Earlier analyses, e.g., [6, 16], highlighted the importance of the Termination or post installation, stage for project success. While it is inevitably true that the quality of the implementation process at the Termination stage is strongly related to success or failure, knowing this does not provide us with a great deal of leverage for assuring success.

By the time a project reaches Termination, much of the damage, or good, is likely to have been done already. In fact, it may be that the quality of the Termination stage largely reflects what occurred at earlier stages. The three recurrent issues identified in this analysis represent issues arising primarily at earlier stages of the implementation process, and thus perhaps conditioning what can happen at Termination. Indeed, it is worthwhile noting that two of the factors identified as generic implementation issues, Factors 4 and

6, relate primarily to the Termination stage, and successes and failures do score differently on these factors. However, when all six factors are considered jointly, these two factors do not contribute to our ability to differentiate successful implementations from unsuccessful ones.

## Implications and Conclusions

Identification of key recurrent implementation issues which can differentiate successes from failures is not important for after-the-fact identification of successes and failures. These results are useful, however, for two purposes. First, they provide guidance about which issues are particularly important, and hence deserve a disproportionate amount of attention during the implementation process. As indicated above, three issues stand out. Thus, the probability of success in implementing an MIS could likely be increased if special attention were paid to 1) gaining commitment to the project, 2) gaining commitment to any changes necessitated by the new system, and 3) assuring that the project is well defined and plans are clearly specified.

The second purpose for which these results are likely to be useful is in providing advance warning of impending failure. Since all three issues highlighted by this analysis begin to arise at early project stages, these issues could be monitored in order to identify likely trouble spots. While such monitoring would not likely result in a perfect separation of probable successes from probable failures, the data suggest that the great majority of impending failures could be identified, while misclassifying only a modest percentage of the cases already on the road to success. A probable failure, once identified, could be given special attention to assure that the key implementation issues were indeed successfully resolved. Since the cost of this special attention would almost always be much less than the cost of eventual implementation failure, the rules for classifying projects should favor classification as a probable failure in ambiguous cases. Clearly, further investigation focusing on 1) the best way to track these issues, and 2) appropriate rules for classifying projects is required.

# Appendix A: Factor Analysis of Implementation Process Data

Each of the seventy-two statements about the implementation process was rated by the respondents according to how well that statement described the project. Ratings were on a five point scale, ranging from “very characteristic of the project” to “very uncharacteristic of the project.” These data cannot be assumed to meet the requirements for a parametric measure of association, i.e., interval scaling cannot be assumed. Further, the number of respondents (thirty-seven were used for the factor analysis) was much smaller than the number of variables (seventy-one). Thus, it was necessary to proceed with caution in conducting any factor analysis, and to recognize the limitations of the data.

Six separate principal components analyses were performed using the SPSS factor analysis sub-program. They differed in 1) the measure of association used — Pearson product-moment, Kendall's Tau, or Spearman's Rho, and 2) the number of points on the response scale — three point versus five point; three point scales were created by treating identically all responses on a given side of the scale mid-point — the neutral response. Pairing each measure of association with each of the response scales yielded six combinations. Initially, each analysis was allowed to extract all factors for which the eigenvalue exceeded 1.0. On the basis of the increase in the percentage of variance explained, “elbows,” all six analyses suggested that six factors be extracted. With thirty-seven respondents, extraction of six factors should be feasible.

The six analyses were rerun to extract six factors only, and the solutions were rotated using the VARIMAX criterion. Items loading on the rotated factors with an absolute value of 0.5 or greater were identified, and the factors thus extracted were compared across the six analyses. The results showed considerable similarity across analyses. Two factors, 1 and 2 in this article, appeared in all analyses with only minor differences in the specific items included. There was substantial similarity across all three analyses using the five point scale, with the two analyses using non-parametric measures of association being almost identical. There was less consistency across the analyses using the three point scale, with the Pearson three point analysis being the most different from the others. In the case of all three measures of association, more of the variance could be explained when the data were left on their original five point scale than when they were collapsed to three point scales.

The results suggested that the analysis was relatively less sensitive to the measure of association than had been expected. However, since that data did violate the requirements for a parametric measure of association, it was decided that one of the non-parametric measures would be used. Surprisingly, analyses using the three point scale yielded factors that were less easy to interpret, and explained a smaller percentage of the variance than did the corresponding analyses using the five point scale. Thus, the analysis using Spearman's Rho on the five point scaled data was selected, as it explained a larger percentage of total variance (50.5%) than did the corresponding analysis using Kendall's Tau (45.3%).

The factors from this analysis were then reviewed once more to determine how well the individual items loading on each factor correlated with one another. Items found not to be highly intercorrelated with most of the other items were eliminated from the factor. This resulted in the removal of one item from Factor 6 and five items from Factor 3.

Scores on the six factors were calculated without regard to the factor loadings, except for sign. While this results in factors which are not orthogonal, they are more easily interpretable.

## About the Author

Michael J. Ginzberg is an Associate Professor of Computer Applications and Information Systems at the Graduate School of Business Administration, New York University. He received an S.B. and Ph.D. from the Alfred P. Sloan School of Management, M.I.T., and an M.B.A. from Iona College. Prior to joining the faculty at New York University, he was a member of the Faculty of Business at Columbia University. His research interests include selection, design, evaluation, and implementation of information systems. He has published articles on these topics in Interfaces, MIS Quarterly, Omega, Sloan Management Review, and TIMS Studies in the Management Sciences.
