# User Experience with and Assessment of Participative Systems Design

By: R.A. Hirschheim
Templeton College
Oxford University
Oxford, England

## Abstract

This article reports on the second half of a two-part study addressing user experiences with participative systems design. The concept of participative design is outlined and compared to more conventional approaches. User experiences are noted and the issue of evaluation is explored.

Keywords: Participative systems design, user involvement, methodology evaluation, systems design.

ACM Categories: K.4.3, K.6.1

## Introduction

Over the years, numerous people have advocated the need for involving the workforce in decisions whose outcomes would affect them. The rationale for this position is straightforward and has been written about extensively. More recently, worker participation has been discussed in the context of computer-based information systems implementation. Several studies report that the use of participation in systems design has had positive effects $[1, 18]$ . Others argue that user participation in the systems development process can be dysfunctional in that it can lead to political problems $[11]$ , is inherently manipulative $[6, 13]$ , is extremely difficult to operationalize $[4, 14]$ , and leads to systems which are not only sub-optimal, but take much longer to develop $[5]$ .

The disagreement between those who feel participation is beneficial and those who see it as detrimental is not easily resolved as these positions are generally strongly held and based on the interpretation of limited, and often contradictory data. Proponents and opponents hold their beliefs more on intuition than on empirical grounds. Attempts to offer a more definitive statement on the value of participation in the systems development process either suffer from methodological difficulties or have been inconclusive [9,12,20,21,23]. A recent report by Ives and Olson provides a comprehensive review of the empirical literature on user involvement and comes to the same conclusion [10]. The authors state, “...much of the existing research is poorly grounded in theory or methodologically flawed; as a result, the benefits of user involvement have not been convincingly demonstrated” [10, p. 586].

It is upon this basis that a project was set up in the summer of 1981 to attempt some form of assessment in three areas. First, to survey the literature and provide a framework through which participative systems design could be discussed. Second, to detail the use of participative methods in the United Kingdom. And third, to empirically assess the value of such methods by drawing on the experiences of organizations which have used them. Study was undertaken in the first two areas during the period October 1981 — October 1982; the results of which are reported in Hirschheim [7]. The last area of study was finished in November 1983; its findings are the subject of this article.

## Overview of Previous Research

## First phase

The first phase of our research found that although considerable literature on participative design does exist, little of it is evaluative. Most of what does exist could be categorized as intuitive or conceptual. For example, participation is beneficial because it allows the skills and knowledge of the individuals who must use the system to be incorporated into it. Empirically based evaluative studies are small in number, and the results usually are inconsistent with one another. The relationship between user participation in systems design and system use, system quality, and user attitudes is far from clear.

It is necessary to make operational the concept “participative systems design.” Having users participate in the systems development process is an insufficient basis by which to classify something as participatively developed, since all systems have some degree of user interaction. Participative design embodies more than just user involvement; it involves users taking the lead in the development process. Moreover, it differs from conventional approaches in two primary areas — content and involvement.

## Content of Participation

The content of participation refers to the subject matter under consideration. Participative design enlarges the scope of the design from involving only technical matters to involving technical and social considerations. This is the socio-technical systems notion presented by Bostrom and Heinen [2,3] and others [6,22,24]. Most conventional methodologies pay little more than lip service to the social aspects and are not normally equipped to handle them. Content is one of the key items which differentiates participative design from the concept ‘user involvement’ as the latter is discussed in the literature.

## User Involvement

Participative design not only broadens the base of user involvement, but also places the responsibility and control of the design process in the hands of the users. Mumford [15] notes three types of involvement:

1. consultative — where the users are consulted about what they want but where the bulk of the decisions are left to some other group;

2. representative — where a group of users is elected to represent the needs of their co-workers in the design process; and

3. consensus — where the users not only make the decisions, but assume full responsibility for their implementation.

Ives and Olson [10] refer to this latter type as “involvement by doing” or “involvement by strong control.” Participative systems design relies most on the representative or consensus forms of involvement which allow the users more responsibility and authority over the development process. This again differs from conventional methodologies which allow only a relatively small degree of user involvement. Figure 1 depicts the continuum between content and involvement, placing some of the better known systems analysis and design approaches (including the participative ones) in their respective positions.

## Second phase

A number of conclusions can be drawn from the data collected in the second phase of the project (which had as its goal the elicitation of details on the use of participative methods). First, participative systems design was a far more complex notion than the literature suggested. Indeed, its name failed to connote both the substance and context of its intention. Second, there was little similarity between the organizations who had used participative design, the types of applications they used it for, the individuals involved (sponsors, users, DP personnel, etc.), and the like. ## Current Research

## Research method

Since knowledge about the value of participative methods was limited, it was recognized at the outset that this research would have to be more exploratory in nature. It needed to be more descriptive then prescriptive, and it needed to be based on the knowledge of those who were in the best position to assess participative design — the individuals who had used it in the past. The research method chosen was based on Mintzberg's [19] notion of “direct research” — an empirical approach (in the sense that it is guided by experience and observation) involving the use of semi-structured interviews, anecdotes, and observation to form descriptive and emergent theory. The method is largely inductive, concerning itself more with discovery than hypothesis corroboration or refutation.

The research used semi-structured interviews as the means to draw out the experiences and opinions of those who were most knowledgeable about participative design. Individuals who had been engaged in one or more participative exercises in their organizations were the primary source for the interview data. Additionally, informal discussions and observation were used to supplement the semi-structured interview data.

An interview guide (see Appendix A) was developed and used to assess: (a) the nature of participation (exploratory questions); (b) whether there had been any post-implementation evaluation (cost/benefit questions); (c) experiences with conventional systems development methodologies (other approach questions); and (d) details relevant to assessing the value of participative design (evaluative questions). The guide was used in structuring the interviews. Great flexibility was offered the respondents to express whatever they wanted in any particular order they wished. Interviewee responses were jotted down during the interviews and were used for more comprehensive write-ups later. This was done in place of tape recordings which, it was thought, might inhibit discussions. Information provided by the respondents was guaranteed to be anonymous, and organization details were kept in confidence. Interviews normally lasted between one and three hours.

Twenty individuals were interviewed in eight organizations. These organizations were chosen from the twenty which had participated in an earlier phase of the project. The eight chosen were those with the most experience with participative design. Six had used the representative form of participation, and two the consensus form. There were no apparent similarities between the organizations in terms of industry type, size, age, structure, etc., nor were there similarities in the types of systems developed. In addition to the twenty individuals formally interviewed, many others took part in informal discussions.

It is important to note that the research method chosen for this study is of a more qualitative nature and contrasts sharply with the call for more rigorous (and quantitative) research as advocated by, for example, Ives and Olson [1]. This departure is a manifestation of a fundamental difference about research which has been the subject of intense debate among social scientists and, more recently, the information systems community [17]. Although this debate is an important one it cannot be discussed here. See Hirschheim [8] for a treatment of the subject. For the purpose of this project, a qualitative research method was deemed most appropriate.

## Research findings

User experiences seem to support the position of participative systems design proponents in a number of ways. Benefits were claimed in the areas of improved communication, lessened resistance to new systems, decreased implementation time, and increased productivity.

Increased productivity was widely reported. The number and severity of design errors was reduced and better functional procedures were incorporated into the system, this being achieved by incorporating the skills and knowledge of the system's users into the design.

Less resistance to change was another reported benefit. Participative systems design helped reduce resistance because the affected parties were involved in the design process. Seminars which highlighted the rationale and mechanics of the approach were also thought to have helped reduce resistance. Most of the systems developed under the participative philosophy were thought to be more user friendly. This was probably due to the views of the operators and users being taken into account in the design phase.

A number of respondents claimed that implementation time had been significantly reduced. Participative design generally resulted in a relatively problem-free implementation, as most of the major problems were brought to the surface at the design stage and resolved then. More time was spent on the design phase, but on the whole, total time from conception to operation was not increased. Participative systems design altered the distribution of the time spent on the various phases of systems development, but left total time either reduced or unchanged.

Finally, it was found that most of the organizations surveyed did not carry out formal postimplementation evaluation of their systems. The most frequently cited reason is that such evaluations are time consuming and require a substantial amount of resources. In one particular organization, formal evaluation is never done except when there is a high degree of dissatisfaction with the system or if the cost of the project significantly exceeds the allocated budget.

## Perceptions of Interest Groups

Various organizational interest groups viewed participative design in different yet favorable ways. Some examples are given below:

Management services: Some systems designers saw the approach slowing down the design process, but generally found that implementation was faster and easier. The resultant systems were thought to be more robust. Some systems designers saw their jobs becoming easier in the sense that they could afford to spend time on several projects rather than just one, as they acted primarily as technical consultants to the design group.

Line department and users: For many of the users in this category the approach represented an opportunity for early involvement in the system. This enabled their ideas and views to be heard. Many users found that the approach led to improved communication between the systems personnel and themselves. There was also a general feeling that the systems developed were the ones they really wanted.

Senior management: The general feeling was that the participative approach was a better way of designing systems — less autocratic and more humanistic. Participative systems design was also seen as a means of providing better feedback from the users/operators about their real needs. It was perceived as “the coming thing.”

Trade Unions: There was a positive feeling on the part of the unions. They perceived participative systems design as providing a forum in which their views were included, such as changes to the work situations of their members.

## Determinants of Success

The success of the participative systems design process depended on two important roles and the support of top management. The crucial roles were those of sponsor and facilitator. A sponsor advocates and encourages the use of the approach as well as being responsible for its use. This role is usually filled by someone senior in the organization who is committed to the use of participative design. In a sense, the sponsor is the driving force behind participative design, and can therefore be seen as the champion of the approach.

The facilitator, on the other hand, helps the participative process flow smoothly, especially in the initial stages. In some cases the facilitator may be an outside consultant; in others, a systems analyst or a manager within the organization fills this role. Both the sponsor and facilitator play a large part in ensuring the successful application of the approach.

All the organizations surveyed reported strong support from top management. This support included:

— having a personal interest in the project — keeping in touch with the steering and design groups to monitor the progress of the design process,

— expressing a willingness to consider recommendations resulting from the participative process,

— providing funding for the project,

— agreeing to have external consultants (facilitators) to help get the project running, and

— agreeing to allocate sufficient resources for the project, particularly in terms of manpower.

## Perceived Benefits

Staff reduction was by far the most frequently cited quantitative benefit. Most of the organizations surveyed practice a no-redundancy policy, so staff reduction refers to redeployment of staff and early retirement. Of course, it is likely that staff reduction would have occurred with or without the use of participative design. The main difference, however, is that it is a “better” way of achieving staff reduction. With participative design, the path of systems development was known at an early stage by all the effected parties, and as such, they had more time to look for alternative positions within the organization.

A number of qualitative benefits were perceived by the respondents. Participative design was reported to promote better and more effective communication within and between the effected departments. In one instance where the system was initially manual, the staff had kept to themselves, each one “doing their own thing.” With participative design there was much more communication and interaction, discussing each others’ tasks and information requirements, since they had to use the same new system.

Participative design was also thought to lead to an increase in line management's confidence in higher management. With participative systems design, line management felt that there was an opportunity for getting senior management to listen to them, resulting in fewer conflicts between the two levels.

Systems designed participatively were claimed to be easier to operate. This phenomenon can be attributed to the familiarity of the users/operators with the system, gained by their involvement in the design process. Better and more appropriate information was noted by one respondent because information could be more easily obtained. Also reported was a general feeling that systems designed participatively were more likely to be accepted by the users/operators.

A majority of the respondents felt that the number of requests for modification to these systems was lower in comparison with systems developed conventionally. No quantitative data on this was available, as it proved difficult to compare different types of systems. It was also generally felt that systems designed participatively required less effort to maintain in terms of resources.

It was noted, however, that users in some organizations were increasingly requesting enhancements to their system. Participative design had led users to become more aware of, and receptive to opportunities (e.g., advancements in information technology) as well as giving them a greater feeling of ownership of the system. This active involvement and feeling of ownership generally led to a higher degree of commitment by the users and operators. They tended to be less critical of its faults. One respondent noted that errors displayed on the VDT were met with less hostility.

In all the organizations surveyed, job satisfaction of those involved was reported to have increased. Most employees found that there was a greater variety in the tasks they performed and in some cases, increases in their latitude in making decisions. Working conditions had been enhanced, both in terms of the nature of their tasks and the working environment. Improvements in the working environment took the form of greater emphasis on ergonomics, taking into account such issues as office lighting, ventilation, and physical location of VDTs. It must be noted, however, that it is extremely difficult to tell whether the increases in job satisfaction were due to the application of the participative approach, the introduction of a computer-based system, or even external factors (such as a significant increase in wages or the introduction of certain fringe benefits for those affected by the change).

There was general agreement among the respondents that participative systems design provided a “learning experience,” thereby enabling users to better manage and adapt to change. The findings of the survey generally support the proposition that as a result of the learning gained in the design process users/operators effectively required less initial training. This was particularly true for smaller departments with fewer staff. When the representative form of participation is used, the design group represents the members of a large department, and the proposition is less likely to hold. The reason is simply that although members of the affected department are kept informed of the developments of the project via reports and feedback mechanisms, the skills of the design group members cannot be passed on to the rest of the department. In this case, the learning experience argument is really only directly applicable to members of the design group. (Members of the design group can, however, help the other members to understand the project by preparing guidance notes, lectures and seminars.)

Most of the organizations which experienced work pattern changes believed that the changes were for the better in the sense that the amount of routine work had been reduced. Again, it is difficult to know whether this was a direct result of participative design or simply the introduction of a computer-based system.

## Perceived problems

One problem mentioned by a number of respondents was that the participative approach leads to delays in the design phase. For participative systems design to be practical then, the delays must be offset by a relatively problem-free and quick implementation. Another difficulty experienced was that some problems which arise during the design phase may not be resolved quickly. The problems normally relate to work design and industrial relations. In such cases the technical systems designer may be unproductive during the time when the problems are being resolved. Although participative systems design facilitates active feedback to reflect the real needs of the users, it was claimed that too much time was spent on minor issues. This was one of the reasons for the lengthened design phase.

One organization claimed that the successful application of the approach was dependent on the capabilities of the external consultant (facilitator). In this organization, it was found that since the consultant was not a member of the organization he could easily move within the organizational hierarchy to solve problems. People tended to be more open and honest with the consultant. When the consultant left it was necessary to appoint someone in the organization to take over the role. This proved problematic as people were less open to the internal replacement. They feared certain disclosures might be reported back to management.

Difficulty in persuading and convincing all concerned that participative systems design was feasible was another problem. It was particularly true if the notion of participation was novel to the organization. People were initially suspicious and skeptical about the process.

Not surprisingly, finding sufficient time and opportunity to bring people together for discussion and consultation was found to be difficult. Also, the size of the design group was an important factor in determining the success of the approach. Too large a number of members in the design group (e.g. 10 or more people) led to problems of coordination and control. In a few instances there was a feeling of “not getting anywhere.”

One organization found it difficult to decide on the most appropriate time to introduce participation in the development process. If participation is advocated at the early stage of conception, many users may not be able to conceptualize a system which is not in existence; introducing it at a later phase may result in the users being left out.

## Application

It is worth noting that the approach was generally used for large and complex systems. A number of respondents felt participative systems design may be impractical to use for small systems. Also, one respondent observed that participative systems design worked better with higher grade staff who are more interested in their jobs and on improving their work situation through participation.

In general, it was found that the design of scientific or specialized engineering-type systems tended to be biased towards technology. This was assumed to be true because there is less scope for manual tasks and therefore less emphasis on work design. Commercial-type systems, on the other hand, tended to allow greater opportunity to consider work-design around the supporting technology. On the whole, both technical and social aspects were included in the design process of this kind of system.

## Discussion

It is apparent from the comments of the respondents that there exists a general consensus regarding the value of participative systems design. Most are favorably disposed to it and advocate its use in systems development and implementation. Organizations do not, however, carry out post-implementation evaluations on their systems. Nor do they set quantifiable objectives at the outset which a system could be evaluated against after it is implemented. Therefore, any system and method evaluation has to be of a more subjective nature.

One measure which the study thought would provide an objective assessment was the quantity of system modification requests. Savings were predicted in the number of requests for system change based on the assumption that systems not meeting user requirements would generate more requests for modification. It was not the case, however, that participatively developed systems led to fewer change requests; in fact, some even led to an increase. This was explained by one respondent who noted that systems which were designed participatively were likely to generate more requests for enhancements because the users were more aware of the opportunities available. Thus, requests for change cannot be related to how well the system met its requirements. The only statistic which would be meaningful was one where requests for change were somehow categorized as either enhancements beyond initial system requirements, or modifications to meet those requirements which had not been met initially.

Even with the failing of the more objective measure it is apparent that users assess the value of participative systems design to be high. This is a key evaluative result of the project. It must be recognized, however, that the subjective nature of evaluation makes these results more descriptive than normative. In an area where little prior knowledge existed, this approach seemed the most sensible. Future research should concentrate on developing more objective evaluation criteria as well as a research method which lends itself to more quantitative measures.

# Appendix A. Questionnaire

## Exploratory Questions

1. How would you define the type/nature of the participation as used in your organization?

2. What are your general feelings about participative systems design?

3. What/who prompted the use of participative systems design in your organization?

4. How do the different interest groups in your organization view participative systems design?

## Cost/Benefit Questions

5. Have post implementation evaluations been carried out on the systems designed participatively? If so, how were these carried out? If not, why not?

6. In general, do you find that participative systems design is more cost-effective than other conventional, non-participative approaches to systems development?

7. What quantitative and qualitative benefits were achieved, if any?

8. What proportion of the participants have now left the organization or been promoted within the same or other departments as a result of the increased knowledge gained through the participative process?

## Questions on Other Approaches

9. What systems design methodologies have you used in the past?

10. What are your general feelings about conventional non-participative approaches?

## Evaluative Questions

11. Has there been a large number of requests for modification to the system(s) developed participatively?

12. How much maintenance, in terms of resources (finance, manpower, time), is required by systems developed participatively? How does this figure compare with that for systems (of similar nature) designed conventionally?

13. How much participation was involved and at what stages of the systems development process?

14. Was there senior management support for participative systems design? If so, what was the form of the support and was it visible?

15. Has job satisfaction increased?

16. It is frequently claimed that participative systems design allows users to learn from their experience, thereby enabling them to better manage/adapt to changes. Is this true?

17. Has initial training and content been noticeably reduced?

18. It is frequently claimed that systems designed participatively are flexible. Is this true?

19. Has technical support from the DP department or outside consultants been sufficient to facilitate efficient design of the technical component(s) of the system(s)?

20. Have there been any major (noticeable) changes resulting from participative systems design (e.g. changes in work pattern, organizational structure, etc.)?

21. What problems were encountered in using participative systems design?

22. Has the organization attempted to evaluate participative systems design? If so, how?

23. What is your overall assessment of participative systems design?
