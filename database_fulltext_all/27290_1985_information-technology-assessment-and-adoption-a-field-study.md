# Information Technology Assessment and Adoption: A Field Study

By: Sid L. Huff
School of Business Administration
The University of Western Ontario
London, Ontario

By: Malcolm C. Munro
Faculty of Management
The University of Calgary
Calgary, Alberta

## Abstract

This article presents the results of a field study examining the strategies and mechanisms used by major companies for identifying, assessing, and adopting new information technology. The principle finding is the identification of several generic models which reveal the driving forces for new technology adoption. The article also describes phases in the adoption process, organizational roles, and information gathering mechanisms. This new line of research in MIS parallels and builds upon technology transfer research and marketing studies in the area of organizational buying behavior. The purpose of this work is to assist organizations with the challenge of coping with rapidly changing information technology.

Keywords: Technology transfer, information technology adoption, organizational impact

## Introduction

The idea that our society has begun an information revolution is a popular one. This information revolution is happening largely because the cost and capabilities of information technology are rapidly changing. Improvements in microchip manufacturing, key to practically all information technology hardware, have paved the way for an astounding variety of new information-related products, services, and processes. These, in turn, present opportunities for organizations to improve their efficiency and productivity by updating older information handling methods. They also present opportunities for increasing organizational effectiveness by providing totally new ways to improve revenues and reduce costs. The results of the information technology choices made by managers will dramatically affect individual and corporate futures.

The identification and assessment of these information technology options is a complex task, usually involving several individuals or units in an organization. It is not a task which can be delegated to a single person. “Attempts of single individuals to stay on top of all major developments in technology as well as applications have, by and large, proved unsuccessful. This includes the most capable of the senior practitioners and accounts for much of the difficulties experienced by almost all of the ‘monolithic’ (data processing) organizations in American industry” [4, p. 23-24].

This article reports on a study of the organizational processes involved in information technology assessment and adoption (ITAA). In particular, data from a series of field investigations are analyzed and a set of simple process models are proposed. These models constitute generalizations of the ITAA approaches taken by the organizations studied. The models describe the major approaches to ITAA — both the activities themselves as well as management of the activities — as observed in the companies studied.

“Information technology” refers here to the broad range of technologies involved in information processing and handling, such as computer hardware, software, telecommunications and office information, and includes such “technologies” as new systems development methodologies. “Assessment and adoption” refers to the organizational policies, strategies, processes, and tasks employed, either explicitly or otherwise, by an organization in its efforts to identify, acquire, and diffuse appropriate information technology.

## Previous Research

There are very few reported studies of information technology assessment and adoption in the information systems literature. However, some related areas such as organizational buying behavior, organizational behavior, research and development management, and long range planning provide useful background information on this subject.

Research in the area of organizational buying behavior seems to provide the closest parallel to ITAA. An example is the model of industrial buying behavior proposed by Robinson, Faris and Wind [9]:

1. Need recognition

2. Determination of solution characteristics

3. Description of solution characteristics

4. Search for sources

5. Acquisition of proposals

6. Evaluation of proposals and source selection

7. Selection of order routine

8. Performance evaluation

Both ITAA and organizational buying are concerned with the investigation and acquisition of material in an organizational setting. Organizational buying however is much broader, being concerned with all types of material, not solely information technology. Robertson, Faris and Wind's phase model of the industrial buying process is representative, and illustrates the applicability of organizational buying research findings to ITAA.

Another particularly useful framework for the present study is the “buying center” concept, and the roles involved therein, as delineated by Webster and Wind [11]:

1. Users — those members of the organization who use the purchased products and services,

2. Buyers — those with formal responsibility and authority for contracting with suppliers,

3. Influencers — those who influence the decision process directly or indirectly by providing information and criteria for evaluating alternative buying actions,

4. Deciders — those with authority to choose among alternative buying actions, and

5. Gatekeepers — those who control the flow of information and materials into the buying center.

Both the Robinson and Faris model and the buying center concept shed light on the ITAA process, but both are untested in the specific area of information technology assessment and adoption. Thus, it cannot be assumed that they are accurate or complete for the purposes of this study, but they do offer a useful starting point.

In the field of research and development management, a considerable body of research has been completed on the topic “technology transfer.” In general, technology transfer studies typically focus on communication patterns and information flows into and within the R & D lab. However, important differences exist between R & D labs and information systems departments which may limit the relevance of the former research. For instance, technology transfer in R & D labs is a highly active, almost daily event and hence such events are amenable to frequency counts over the short term $[1]$ . However, in an information systems department, and in the larger organization generally, the technology transfer process appears to occur less frequently. Also, R & D labs are generally populated by research-oriented staff, whereas IS departments usually include only a few such individuals.

In the information systems area itself, Lientz and Chen [7] have proposed a normative, four-step approach for assessing new information technology:

1. Definition of what the organization does and wants to do in terms of information services, and the current information services environment.

2. Analysis of technology trends and forecasts in the areas of hardware, software, and communications, and assessment of this technology in terms of price, timing, size, speed, reliability, and so on.

3. Assessment of (1) and (2) above at the project level, and at the organization level.

4. Further refinement and assessment of the above as it relates to specific application areas.

Lientz and Chen do not discuss alternative models, nor do they evaluate their model in terms of cost vs. benefit, sensitivity to contingencies, etc. They do, however, discuss two case studies in organizations and relate their field observations to their model.

Recently, Zmud has reported “a study which examines the influence of centralization and formalization on the initiation, adoption, and implementation of modern software practices, i.e., methods for the design, development, and maintenance of computer programs and the management of these activities” [12, p. 1421]. Zmud’s paper indicates that information technology is not limited to hardware and software, but can include such things as system development methods (“modern software practices”) as well. Similarly, Huber [6] has completed a study of information sources and their role in initiating innovation in R & D projects, another important aspect of the ITAA issue.

In summary, with few exceptions we are unable to report studies which directly focus on information technology assessment and adoption. Literature in the area of organizational buying behavior, the concept of the buying center, and the roles involved suggest issues to explore in the context of ITAA. Similarly, the study of communication patterns and information flow reported in the R & D management literature suggest possible directions for study in ITAA. Finally, the mass of technology transfer literature is not very helpful in understanding ITAA processes in organizations.

## Research Approach

The fundamental question motivating this research was, “How can organizations properly manage the process of assessing and adopting new information technology?” We began with the assumption that such processes would be described in the information systems literature and that the literature would point the way to further refinement through verification and hypothesis testing. This assumption was invalid however. This situation suggested that our first research initiative on the question must consist of a description of the process, a necessary step for the generation of theory relevant to the research. Thus, this work falls not in the realm of verifying an existing theory and hypothesis testing, but in the purposeful, systematic generation of new theories from the collection of qualitative field data.

This study follows the tradition of grounded theory-building [5]. Systematic data collection is followed by a data analysis which seeks concepts in the data collected. The concepts, in turn, are combined with existing concepts that are clearly useful. The “results” of the research consist of a presentation of the concepts which emerged from the data and a demonstration of the applicability of the concepts borrowed from research in related areas. This approach appears to be “the most adequate and efficient way to obtain the type of information required and to contend with the difficulties of an empirical situation” [5, p. 18].

Data collection was accomplished by means of a questionnaire generated through interviews in a two-company pilot study. A series of interviews in each company provided a framework within which specific questions were formulated. For the main study, ten companies were selected from the Financial Post listing of Canadian private sector corporations. Only very large organizations were included on the assumption that the ITAA process would be more mature in such firms.

The person initially interviewed in each company was the senior information systems executive. Such an individual usually held the position of vice president or director. Interviews were semi-structured in that the questionnaire was used to guide the interview but individual questions were mainly open-ended. Sample questions from the questionnaire are shown in Appendix 1.

The initial interview served to identify additional individuals who had more direct, functional responsibility for ITAA-related activities in the organization. Such individuals usually held positions such as manager of IS planning, manager of technical systems, or coordinator of special projects. Occasionally, user managers with a particularly strong interest in information technology were also identified.

These persons were interviewed using the same questionnaire as before. However, since their work is often more central to the ITAA process, the discussion tended to focus on details of the ITAA stages. Such individuals are frequently insightful with regard to the true meaning of organizational policy with respect to information technology. For example, in one company corporate policy explicitly required the application of “leading edge technology.” Discussion with a technical systems manager revealed that this policy held only inasmuch as the technology adopted came from a single vendor. In other words, the firm’s real policy was to adopt the most advanced technology available from their current vendor. Such a policy may have a dramatic effect on the scope of ITAA activities.

Upon completion of the interviews in a company, a field report on each interview was prepared. The field report was written with the purpose of creating a permanent, detailed record of the interview. While each field report was organized according to the categories implied by the questions asked, no analysis was undertaken at this point.

Following completion of the field studies and reports, the entire set of reports (i.e., the data collected) was examined and analyzed. The purpose of the analysis was to reveal common observations, organizing concepts, and reasonably accurate generic descriptions of the processes observed.

## Results of Field Report Analyses

A critical factor in understanding any process is a knowledge of the forces which drive it. This knowledge facilitates effective intervention in the process. Thus, our most significant result from analyses of the field reports is a description of the driving forces underlying ITAA processes in the organizations studied. These driving forces are issues and technology.

Practically every organization is faced with a large number of current issues with which it must deal in some fashion. A number of such issues emerged in these organizations and were dealt with partially or entirely in terms of technology; in particular, information technology. ## Issue driven model

In an issue driven environment the ITAA process is closely geared to the corporate and systems planning processes. It is in these planning processes that the various issues of current organizational importance are discussed and eventually prioritized and funded. A thorough planning process makes it quite unlikely that important organizational issues will be overlooked.

Each issue that emerges from this planning process then becomes a focal point for a search for solutions. Often this search will center on information technology. In effect, a small scale ITAA process will be carried out for each such major issue. Within the scope of each issue, a technological scanning procedure is performed. Thus, it is unlikely that important technological options or opportunities applicable to the issues will be overlooked. In this model, technological scanning outside the realms of the current issue is ad hoc and incomplete, and the possibility for overlooking opportunities is much higher.

The actual mechanism for carrying out the ITAA process for a given issue will often take the form of a small study team. The study team performs information search via literature, vendor contacts, internal gatekeepers, counterparts in other organizations, etc. Other activities the team would typically perform include internal systems analysis, cost-benefit analysis, hands-on product evaluation, and product tryout in the organization. The study team's work typically ends with a report and recommendation to senior management.

The issue driven model could also be characterized as a “top-down” approach, inasmuch as the process really starts with the delineation and prioritization of issues at the senior management level (corporate and systems planning), then proceeds to more and more detailed levels of analysis. The key skills for successful use of this approach appear to be the ability to identifying those issues that are most critical to the organization, and to identifying technical solutions. In one firm we studied, middle-level IS managers were charged with generating such issues as part of their jobs, and were evaluated on the “quality” of the issues they produced.

There are other important distinguishing characteristics of the issue driven model. Typically, the thorough planning process necessitated by this approach resulted in fairly heavy expenditures on planning activities; however, it is quite efficient in that, once issues are determined, the search for information regarding solutions is well focused. There is relatively little need for seeking high-level “sponsorship” for new technology when it is introduced, since the development of the issues during the planning cycles generally produces sponsorship early on [5]. High degrees of organizational slack and flexibility are not required as the approach is relatively well structured. Good problem-directed information search capabilities are required, although ongoing technological information scanning across-the-board is less important.

## Technology driven model

In the technology driven organization, thorough corporate planning is not an important activity, and indeed there may well not be any corporate planning at all. Rather, the ITAA sequence progresses from identification of an interesting technology, often by individuals relatively low in the organization, to the location of an organizational problem to which it can be applied. In this sense, the technology driven model is a bottom-up approach to ITAA.

This approach is clearly not comprehensive with respect to organizational issues; important issues may well be overlooked. However, the technology driven approach is quite thorough with respect to awareness of potential technological opportunities and options. Scanning of the technology information sources tends to be quite thorough. Firms that follow this model are usually well endowed with technical gatekeepers [8] on the IS staff; their libraries of technical journals and books are usually much larger and more up-to-date than in other firms; their interest in technical conferences is greater; they are basically better informed as to newly available, as well as still experimental information technology.

There appear to be two key success factors associated with the technology driven approach. One is the need for thorough scanning of the many sources of information about new technology. This requires a relatively large amount of organizational slack and flexibility within the IS department to free up the time required for the scanning activities. The other key success factor is the ability of IS managers to secure strong, high-level sponsorship for new technology. Sponsorship does not develop naturally out of the planning process, as it does in the issue driven model, but rather must be sought out and cultivated if the IS group is to be successful in actually introducing and diffusing new technology.

This approach is less costly overall than the issue driven approach, because of the lower expenditure on planning. However, the higher level of slack required in the IS department partially offsets this savings. Key players tend to be technical gatekeepers and analysts, as they are the generators of the ideas for new technology to be introduced to the organization. As such, evaluation of staff centers on their ability to locate and develop application ideas for technology rather than their ability to locate and develop important organizational issues.

## Opportunistic model

Many organizations are purposefully not comprehensive with respect to either their issue identification procedure (planning processes) or their technology identification procedure. Such firms typically perform some technology scanning, but it is unorganized and not managed. It may, for example, be thorough with respect to a few technologies and neglectful of others. Similarly, there may be some corporate and systems planning, but it is usually one year budgeting, not comprehensive long-range planning. The ITAA process in such firms seems to fall somewhere between the two models previously discussed. Such firms may be pictured as a “sea” of partially developed issues and partially investigated technology options, with neither playing a leading role. Occasionally a matchup between issue and technology occurs. Such coming together of technology and issue has more of a serendipitous flavor to it in this environment than is the case with the two previous models. This is analogous to the “garbage can” process model of decision making [2]. There is also a “learn-as-we-go” ethic common to many such organizations; rather than investigating either issues or technology in depth before proceeding further these firms tend to take the ITAA process a step at a time, feeling their way in a somewhat cautious, relatively unstructured mode.

None of this is to say that the opportunistic process is necessarily worse than either issue driven or technology driven processes. For one thing, the opportunistic approach is lowest of all three models in terms of organizational overhead or need for flexibility and slack. There is no large expenditure of resources for either planning or technology information scanning and evaluation. There is also a lower need for specialist skills, such as planners, or technical or applications gatekeepers.

The key skills in the opportunistic environment appear to be generalist skills: the ability to keep a broad perspective, to be aware of the important issues facing the organization, and to possess a basic, though not necessarily in-depth, understanding of information technology. The most important skill is the ability to recognize opportunities for bringing together appropriate information technology with issues. A key problem is the risk that an important issue or technological solution is totally overlooked or unseen.

If issue driven behavior is top-down and technology driven is bottom-up, then opportunistic behavior might well be termed “middle-out.” Issues and technological solutions meet in the middle, are matched up, and move out into the organization.

The key players are the IS management team, and frequently the middle-level user managers. Middle-level managers often play important roles in helping the IS managers clarify and flesh out the issue-technology matchups. The need for sponsorship for introduction of new technology will vary from case to case; some issues may come with an appropriate sponsor, others may not [3].

Although our field study was too limited to be conclusive, it is likely that the opportunistic model predominates in practice. It lies on the path of least resistance for organizational development. Unless an organization were to take special steps, expend extra resources, etc., to develop its planning abilities or to develop its technological assessment abilities, it would likely take on the characteristics of the opportunistic model.

## Normative ideal model

In our field investigations we have yet to encounter an organization which actually follows the normative ideal approach. This model is based on the framework proposed by Lientz and Chen [7]. We have chosen to label the approach “normative ideal,” because Lientz and Chen argue that it is the approach that fully rational firms (with few resource constraints) ought to follow, and it represents an approach which would be an ideal standard or norm for most organizations if resources were not a constraint. Lientz and Chen do, in fact, cite two firms whose ITAA practices approach this standard [7, p. 48].

In the normative ideal model, the ITAA process begins with a comprehensive assessment of both the current information services environment and future goals of the organization with regard to information services. This assessment is carried to the level of current and future applications. In general, the assessment should provide “a sense of what will be needed from technology to support the organization” [7, p. 46].

Next, forecasts of the availability of new technology are reviewed. Each of the major technology areas (e.g. hardware, system software, applications software, communications) is included and assessed according to a variety of criteria. For example, all technology would be assessed for price and timing. Memory and processors would also be assessed for size and speed; auxiliary memory for transfer rate and access time, and so on.

With both the organizational and technological reviews complete, a mapping of technology assessments to organizational needs at both the individual application level and organization level is done. The focus of the exercise is to assess the impact of the technology in terms of meeting information needs. Then this impact assessment is refined, with special attention being paid to currently important application areas in the firm. Finally, the assessment is used as the basis for developing a long-range information services plan.

The major strength of the normative ideal model is that it is, in principle, comprehensive with respect to both information technology and organizational issues, problems, and opportunities. Lientz and Chen note, for example, that the technological assessment includes not only hardware, software, and communications, but also technical and user personnel and vendors and suppliers.

The countervailing weakness, however, is that this approach is very costly. It is costly in an absolute sense due to the organizational and technological reviews required. It is also costly in that the technological review is likely to cover technology of little interest to the organization. A related problem in this regard is that the technology forecasting literature is vast, posing the dilemma of being selective yet still complete.

A key skill in this approach is the ability to identify appropriate technology to support each of the potential application areas. This skill is particularly vital if technology for an application does not seem to be available; a decision made on whether to rely on internal research efforts to solve the problem or whether to postpone the application pending the availability of a matching technology.

## Additional Findings

There are usually a number of different perspectives from which to view a process. Our review of the literature indicated that other researchers in related fields have found it insightful to view organizational processes, such as ITAA, in terms of the various phases that occur as the process unfolds. An examination of the roles played by individuals or groups of people who participate in the process has also proven useful. Finally, since dealing with information technology is an information intensive activity, understanding how organizations deal with information gathering activities would also seem useful. These perspectives are briefly discussed below.

## Phases of ITAA

Many of the recently proposed phase models are based on work done over twenty years ago by Rogers [10]. Rogers proposed a phase model of innovation, which includes five activity phases: awareness, interest, evaluation, trial, and adoption.

We have found, in the organizations studied, that a modification to this model seems to provide the best description of the phases of the ITAA process. Split Rogers' adoption phases into two parts: implementation and diffusion. In this way, the characteristics of an organization's first full-scale adoption of a new technology (e.g., the use of a fourth generation computer language for a major system development project) can be distinguished from those of later adoptions (e.g., the use of the language on later projects, or by system development groups elsewhere in the organization).

Descriptions of each of the six phases observed are as follows:

1. Awareness: The innovation is first heard of but full information about it is lacking;

2. Interest: Missing information is deliberately sought out;

3. Evaluation: Use of the innovation is mentally considered and a decision is made whether to try it;

4. Trial: Practical, usually small scale testing is made to assess the value of the innovation to the present and future situation;

5. Implementation — The trial is extended to the first full use of the innovation, usually involving one or a few sub-units of the organization;

6. Diffusion — The innovation is extended, together with possible modifications, to additional organizational units.

Application of the six-phase model is relatively straightforward. The model provides an effective means of separating a somewhat continuous weave of events into separate activities. Such activities can then be isolated, described, and better understood.

An elaboration by way of a brief example will help to clarify the six-phase model of the ITAA process. One of the firms studied had just implemented executive workstation technology for the senior managers (president plus eleven other managers). The following was observed:

1. Awareness of the executive workstation technology had developed in the executives mainly from talking with managers in other firms and from reading the general media. Awareness had developed in the IS managers from reading the trade press.

2. Interest was kindled in the senior executives from an in-house vendor briefing and from an inspection of a similar facility in the vendor's own offices. The IS staff's interest developed more subtly, as an outgrowth of awareness (as a result of reading more and more about it) and as a result of interest shown by the senior executives.

3. The evaluation phase involved the IS executives and staff. Information on various products was gathered via vendor literature, vendor presentations, and discussions with peers in other firms.

4. The trial phase (as defined above) was non-existent. Once the evaluation had been completed, a decision was made by the board of directors to purchase a particular technology (including terminals, software, communications gear, manuals, training, and support) from the company's mainframe vendor.

5. The implementation phase of this project was carried out in a fairly traditional fashion. A software contract was signed with the vendor, necessary equipment was purchased, and the system was installed on one of the company's computers.

6. Diffusion of the technology throughout the population of potential users in the firm had not yet occurred. At some point in the future, the firm planned to expand the use of the system by adding more workstations and providing access to other managers, both at head office and in field locations.

Using the six phases to fully describe the ITAA process in an organization yields a richness of detail beyond this report. In fact, each phase in itself revealed useful opportunities for management intervention and improvement.

## Roles in ITAA

Our field study uncovered a number of different actors in the ITAA process. ITAA roles generally cut across the process phases, although certain roles tended to be predominant in certain phases. In searching for a categorization of these roles we found that of Webster and Wind to be useful. A slight modification of Webster and Wind's buying center role set seemed to capture all the ITAA-relevant roles we observed. The role set suggested by our field studies includes six roles: 1) user, 2) influencer, 3) decider, 4) gatekeeper, 5) planner, and 6) sponsor. To illustrate, consider the executive workstation example earlier.

1. Users in this case were the twelve senior executives — at least as far as the initial implementation is concerned.

2. Influencers included members of the IS department (principally the IS manager), as well as outside parties (vendors of appropriate software packages, including the company's mainframe vendor).

3. Deciders were ostensibly the members of the board of directors. The board voted to adopt the particular technology in question.

4. Gatekeepers were those individuals with “expert” knowledge of the technology involved as a result of their special interests, talents, and position in the firm. In the executive workstation example, the newly appointed office systems manager had been studying the whole range of office information technology in depth, and played the role of gatekeeper for the executive workstation project.

5. The planner role was played by IS department staffers who carried out various parts of the assessment and evaluation procedure and planned the implementation.

6. The sponsor role was filled by a senior level manager with considerable organizational “clout,” as well as respect — a person who served as a change agent to pave the way for the internal changes that accompanied the introduction of new information technology. In the workstation example, the sponsor was the entire senior management group, which had formed a consensus on the issue.

Clearly, the six roles do not have to be played by six different parties. In particular, the gatekeeper is often one of the influencers. In smaller firms the planner, decider, and gatekeeper roles may be collapsed, and all played by a single individual. The sponsor role may not be filled at all in some cases. Nevertheless, viewing the ITAA process as a set of six key roles helps to clarify and understand the process as it unfolds in its full complexity in real organizations.

## Information gathering in ITAA

The information gathering task is especially difficult because the determination of what items are important often cannot be made by a junior employee. However, senior staff people are usually overloaded with other, more time-critical concerns, so journal scanning is frequently skipped. This is exacerbated by the fact that information gathering is a type of long-range capital investment (intellectual capital), which is notoriously easy to postpone when short-term pressures build.

Several mechanisms for dealing with the information gathering problem were evident in the study sites. The major ones are categorized below.

## Employee Curiosity

A substantial number of people who work with information technology are curious about new developments, and take it upon themselves to read about them — often outside of regular working hours. Employers can, and often do, rely on their employees to use their own resources to stay well informed and to apply that information fruitfully in their work. This process is encouraged in various ways — through journal subscriptions, formal technical discussions, and “perks” such as travel.

## Repetition Factor

Some of the companies studied indicated that they knew when some new topic was important enough to examine indepth because it would have been written about so many times, from dozens of different perspectives, that they couldn't possibly have missed hearing about it. And, conversely, if a new item did not achieve such a high level of redundancy, then “chances are it's not worth knowing about anyway.”

## Other Organizations

We observed a surprising amount of intercompany sharing of information in the IS technology domain, even in organizations that are quite secretive in other ways. User groups such as Guide, Share, Decus, etc., all openly share information about new or different ways of using a certain vendor's information technology. Informal sharing with acquaintances in other firms was another common mechanism. Some of the organizations indicated that this intercompany, peer-to-peer transfer was a very important form of information gathering and probably the most efficient. Three 20-minute telephone conversations could produce more useful information than weeks spent in other information gathering modes. However, it was also pointed out that inter-company sources were best for specific, well-defined questions (e.g “what has been your experience with software product ABC?”) as opposed to more general environmental scanning activities.

## Vendors

One of the interesting ways in which the organizations differed concerned the extent to which they voluntarily constrained their information gathering to products offered by, or compatible with the offerings of their existing hardware vendor(s). In one firm, new information technology essentially meant new product offerings from their mainframe vendor. Such total reliance by an organization on its main hardware vendor solves some of the problems cited above, but creates others. Keeping informed becomes a matter of staying cognizant of upcoming vendor offerings, and planning their introduction and use. Of course, there is also a greater chance of overlooking a potentially valuable new information technology in such firms; the quality of vendor information was frequently cited by respondents as being relatively low.

## Summary

We have indicated that the general ITAA process can be described by one of four distinct models. These models emerge from the relative emphasis that organizations place on each of two key driving forces — technology and issues. Table 2 summarizes the models, phases, roles and information gathering processes observed.

At this point it is perhaps tempting to draw conclusions. However, in our view such a step is premature. Additional study is necessary to properly understand the nature of the important variables and their relationships. This study, and the resulting descriptions provides a basis for formulating future research.

Further steps in this research program are to (a) improve and verify our initial findings and analyses about the fundamental ITAA models, and (b) begin to apply the models so as to make predictions, establish testable hypotheses, and generally extend the results beyond the representations themselves.

For example, in the course of this study we became aware that a lot of new information technology is being absorbed by organizations through processes other than the formal ones upon which we focused. In particular, the end user computing phenomenon has given rise to considerable interest in a range of software, terminals, and microcomputers. In many organizations, information centers have been established to support, encourage, and/or control end user computing. On the assumption that information centers play a major role in the absorption of these new technologies, our next effort in expanding our understanding of ITAA would be to observe and describe ITAA as it occurs in organizations with information centers.

Management of the process of information technology assessment and adoption is of critical importance to organizations today. Practicing managers require a benchmark against which they can compare the activities and performance of their own organizations. The long-range intent of ITAA research should be to provide validated descriptions and models which facilitate such comparisons, and to recommend ways of managing the ITAA process more effectively. This study, and the descriptions herein, represent a first step in the process of generating the theory upon which such recommendations can be based.
