# The Impact of Role Variables on IS Per- sonnel Work Attitudes and Intentions

By: Jack J. Baroudi
New York University
Graduate School of Business Administration
90 Trinity Place
New York, New York

## Abstract

This study examines the antecedents of job satisfaction, commitment, and turnover intentions for 229 information systems (IS) personnel employed within several industries. The antecedents studied include boundary spanning, role ambiguity, and role conflict. A model of these variables was built and tested using path analysis. Role ambiguity was found to be the most dysfunctional variable for IS personnel, accounting for 10.3%, 20.2% and 22.2% of the variance in turnover intentions, commitment, and job satisfaction. This information is used to make recommendations to IS management. Finally, recommendations and directions are suggested regarding future research.

Keywords: MIS management, IS personnel, job satisfaction, role conflict, role ambiguity, boundary spanning

ACM Categories: K.6.1

## Introduction

Qualified information systems (IS) personnel are a costly and scarce resource. The effective management of this resource is important to the success of both the IS department and the increasingly computer-dependent organization. Yet the management of IS personnel is extremely difficult, with much of this difficulty traced to the turnover of IS staff.

A 1982 industry survey revealed IS personnel turnover to be 20%, with the increase in demand for new personnel growing at 15% every year [15]. Therefore, almost half of the IS staff changes every two years. In a field such as data processing where it is estimated to take 18 months for an employee to become maximally productive [7, 12], 20% turnover is excessive.

Given the current turnover problem facing the IS profession, it is surprising that only a handful of studies have explored turnover and its antecedents among IS professionals. A review of this limited literature reveals two variables which consistently appear to be important determinants of IS personnel turnover; these are job satisfaction and organizational commitment $[5, 6, 41]$ .

Beyond their relationship to turnover, job satisfaction and organizational commitment have other important consequences for the effective management of IS personnel. While no causal relationship has been established, job satisfaction and job performance have been found to be positively correlated [36]. Additionally, a lack of job satisfaction is related to increased absenteeism, ill health, and grievances [22]. Commitment to the organization leads to increased effectiveness [34, 38], and has been found to relate positively to organizational adaptability [3]. Adaptability is important for IS departments as they operate within a rapidly changing environment; they must be able to adapt to the new technologies in order to effectively serve the organization.

## Critical Antecedent Variables

Boundary spanning, role conflict, and role ambiguity have all been found to be important antecedents of commitment, job satisfaction, and turnover. Boundary spanning refers to the individual's crossing of intradepartmental and interorganizational boundaries in order to perform his/her job [2, 42]. Van Sell, et al, [43] define role conflict and role ambiguity as follows. Role conflict occurs when an individual receives conflicting job performance information or is expected to do too much. Role ambiguity refers to the lack of clear and precise information regarding what is expected of the role incumbent. Van Sell, et al, report that role conflict and role ambiguity have been found to positively relate to boundary spanning, and also to increase turnover and job dissatisfaction. The dynamics of boundary spanning, role conflict, and role ambiguity as they operate on IS personnel are described below.

The IS department and other business functions, such as sales and accounting, can be viewed as self-contained units with well-defined boundaries. The gathering of information necessary for the analysis and design of computerized systems requires IS personnel to interact with individuals outside the IS department which necessitates the crossing of departmental boundaries. Systems which cut across many different organizational functions will require IS personnel to cross many boundaries. If a system requires the purchase of new hardware, software, or other outside vendor services, inter-organizational boundaries may be crossed as well.

As IS personnel span organizational or departmental boundaries they increase the number and types of individuals with whom they must work. Individuals in other departments may have orientations and expectations that are very different from those of the IS professional. Additionally, IS personnel have to balance the vague, and sometimes conflicting desires of users when designing a system; particularly one that cuts across several functions. The outcome of these activities may result in role conflict and role ambiguity.

## Prior Research

There are several MIS studies which suggest that role conflict and ambiguity are particular problems for IS personnel. Woodruff [44] examined the personality profiles of 202 IS personnel and found that they possessed significantly higher needs for achievement, cognitive structure, and autonomy, and a lower need for change than workers in general. The high need for cognitive structure and low need for change are indicative of individuals with relatively little tolerance for ambiguity.

While not specifically addressing IS personnel, there are several organizational behavior studies that confirm that IS personnel may be particularly sensitive to role conflict and role ambiguity. Kahn, et al, [18] found that individuals with a high need for cognition (a construct similar to cognitive structure) displayed a positive relationship between role conflict and job related tension, indicating that IS personnel are prone to the adverse effects of role conflict and ambiguity. Morris and Snyder [30] report that satisfaction and organizational commitment are strong negative correlates of inter-sender role conflict for individuals with high autonomy-related needs.

Abdel-Halim [1] reports that jobs in mediating (service) technologies with high Job Diagnostic Survey (JDS) scores are more vulnerable to role conflict and ambiguity. (Couger and Zawacki [11] found that IS jobs scored high on the JDS.) The evidence is highly suggestive, therefore, that systems professional with their high JDS job scores, high need for achievement, autonomy, and cognition, and low need for change are particularly prone to the adverse effects of role conflict and role ambiguity.

Two studies have directly investigated the role characteristics of system designers and their effects on work-related outcomes. Bostrom [9] concluded that high levels of role conflict and ambiguity were associated with system designers' positions. He also found that both ambiguity and conflict were negatively associated with designer job satisfaction. Goldstein and Rockart [14] reported that role conflict and ambiguity were negatively correlated with general job satisfaction, growth satisfaction (the amount of personal growth and development one receives from one's job), satisfaction with co-workers, and satisfaction with supervision. In general, Goldstein and Rockart found that the correlations between job satisfaction and role variables were consistently higher than the correlations between job satisfaction and job characteristics (e.g. task identity, skill variety, identity, feedback and significance).

## Research Model

This study attempts to weave together the findings of prior research into a model (Figure 1) which examines the process by which IS personnel develop turnover intentions. Turnover intentions, rather than actual turnover, are considered for several reasons. First, turnover intentions have been found to be the best predictor of actual turnover [26]. Second, it is known that in periods of general economic slowdown when alternative jobs may not be readily available, actual turnover may be severely restricted. Therefore, even though personnel may be generally unhappy and intend to quit, this may not be possible until the economic climate improves and alternative jobs are more plentiful. Given the poor economic climate at the time of this study, it was felt that turnover intentions would provide a better measure of personnel disaffection.

In general, the model suggests that the boundary spanning requirements of the job lead to role conflict and role ambiguity, which, in turn, negatively impact job-related attitudes (satisfaction and commitment) and ultimately lead to turnover intentions.

A number of the relationships articulated by the model are quite complex as there can be both direct and indirect effects with opposite impacts. For example, boundary spanning is expected to lead to role conflict and role ambiguity. Role conflict and role ambiguity are stressful experiences which, in turn, reduce job satisfaction. Thus boundary spanning has an indirect negative effect on job satisfaction through role conflict and role ambiguity. However, the model also predicts a positive direct relationship between boundary spanning and job satisfaction; boundary spanning can add a dimension of increased interest and autonomy to the job, thus increasing job satisfaction. Both effects may operate simultaneously as a more interesting job may also be more stressful.

Path 1: The greater the number of boundary spanning activities IS personnel engage in, the greater their intentions to quit.

Adams [2] suggests that boundary spanning incumbents experience weakened organizational bonds due to their physical and psychological distance from other organizational members. These weakened bonds are reflected in greater intentions to quit. As IS personnel span boundaries they are exposed to a greater number of alternative job opportunities both within and outside the organization.

Increased awareness of alternative employment opportunities may heighten IS personnel intentions to quit.

Path 2: The greater the number of boundary spanning activities IS personnel engage in, the greater their job satisfaction.

Prior research [19, 20, 23] has reported a positive relationship between boundary spanning activities and job satisfaction. This relationship may exist because boundary spanners have inherently more interesting work and thus should be more satisfied.

Path 3a: The greater the number of boundary spanning activities IS personnel engage in, the greater their role conflict.

Path 3b: The greater the number of boundary spanning activities IS personnel engage in, the greater their role ambiguity.

Boundary spanners often assume the role of change agents by attempting to influence the behavior of individuals whose preferences may be in conflict. This can lead to role conflict for the change agent. Keller and Holland [19] posit that conflicting and misunderstood expectations for role performance are often sent to boundary spanners. Additionally, Keller, et al., [20] state that since both individuals internal and external to the organization have expectations for boundary spanners, these expectations may be inconsistent resulting in role conflict, or vague and unclear resulting in role ambiguity.

Path 4: The greater the number of boundary spanning activities IS personnel engage in, the less their commitment to the organization.

Similar to Path 1, this path is based on the work of Adams [2] who argues that boundary spanners experience weakened organizational linkages. Commitment is an organizational linkage. Therefore, boundary spanning activities weaken the individual's organizational commitment.

Path 5a: The greater the role conflict of IS personnel, the less their job satisfaction.

Path 5b: The greater the role ambiguity of IS personnel, the less their job satisfaction.

Many studies have found that role conflict and ambiguity decrease job satisfaction [9, 13, 14, 28, 43].

Path 6a: The greater the role conflict of IS personnel, the greater their intention to leave.

One response to increased role conflict and ambiguity is to withdraw. A primary withdrawal mechanism is to leave the organization. Researchers have found that both role conflict and role ambiguity are positively related to the propensity to leave the organization $[24, 26, 43]$ .

Path 7a: The greater the role conflict of IS personnel, the less their commitment.

Path 7b: The greater the role ambiguity of IS personnel, the less their commitment.

Just as role conflict and ambiguity increase an individual's intention to leave, it is expected that they will also weaken an individual's attachment to the organization, resulting in reduced commitment. Many studies have found role conflict and ambiguity to be significant and important detractors to organizational commitment [8, 13, 28, 29, 31, 43].

![](/api/attachments/JYJ9SBCS/fulltext/images/e92112edf333a7770f63b4b0ab9b4f7d78322607527fba9a339dd56a75419fd3.jpg)

Path 8: The greater the job satisfaction of IS personnel, the less their intentions to leave.

Jamal [17], Arnold and Feldman [4] and Steers and Mowday [39] all found that reduced job satisfaction increases an individual's intention to leave. Additionally, Bartol [6] found that job satisfaction significantly reduced actual IS personnel turnover. In general, strong support exists for this hypothesized relationship.

![](/api/attachments/JYJ9SBCS/fulltext/images/65903bc71b238259f6e6016747d70e64e094b8c7c3239fed673805e3cf76d525.jpg)  
Figure 1j. Research Model

Path 9: The greater the job satisfaction of IS personnel, the greater their commitment.

The preceding hypothesis is based on work by Brief and Aldag [10] who view the facets of job satisfaction — work, promotions, supervision, people, and pay — as antecedents of commitment. Their empirical results support this position.

![](/api/attachments/JYJ9SBCS/fulltext/images/8aad2368b4e2d20818da295e143c0ad19bf1bbddae801f1816ab74ba1c5b5861.jpg)

Path 10: The greater the commitment of IS personnel, the less their intentions to leave.

This hypothesis is consistent with the literature that views organizational commitment as an organizational linkage. The more strongly linked an individual is to an organization the less likely it is that the individual will plan to leave. This view is strongly supported by empirical research $[4, 16, 29, 38, 39]$ .

## Measures and Methodology

In constructing the instrument to test the model presented earlier, measures with extensive histories of reliability and validity were employed whenever possible. Accordingly, the Job Descriptive Index (JDI) [37] was used to measure job satisfaction. A scale developed and extensively tested by Rizzo, et al., [33] was used to measure role conflict and role ambiguity. Organizational commitment was measured using an instrument developed by Porter and Smith [32], and intention to quit was measured via three items developed by Mobley, et al., [27].

There was concern that the role ambiguity scale needed to be expanded. Schuler, et al., [35] have suggested that the commonly used operationalization of role ambiguity is inadequate and should be expanded to include task and reward ambiguity. The task and reward scales were built by decomposing Schuler's definition into several items for each of the two constructs.

Because it is possible that IS personnel may be significantly committed to the department, but not the organization, the definition of commitment was expanded to include both department and organization. However, testing of the two commitment measures revealed a very high correlation; use of either measure separately, or the two measures combined into one scale, did not significantly alter the results. The analysis suggested that the inclusion of the department commitment measure was unnecessary.

To gather boundary spanning data a measure developed by Miles and Perrault [25] was adapted and used. The Miles and Perrault scale measures the boundary spanning activities of laboratory research and development personnel. For use in the IS personnel setting, it was necessary to generalize the questionnaire by replacing all references to the laboratory setting with non-situation-specific terminology. ## The study participants

Nine companies from the New York/Boston area participated in the study, with data collected from a total of 229 individuals. The 229 subjects represent an overall response rate of 85%. The missing 15% represent individuals who were sick or on vacation the day the survey was administered. Response rate per company did not differ significantly from the 85% average, thus no systematic bias was expected.

The study participants included applications programmers, programmer/analysts, analysts, and project leaders in centralized IS groups. Additionally, to assure that the study participants were engaged in comparable tasks, only those individuals working on the development of systems to be used within the organization were included. ## Testing the trimmed model

Two statistical tests can be applied to determine how well the trimmed model captures the relationship in the sample data; testing for omitted third variables, and reconstructing the correlation matrix. First, correlations among the residuals (the amount of unexplained variance for each variable) were computed and none were found to be significant at the .05 level. This provides evidence that the trimmed model does not omit any important third variables that might account for the relationships described by the model.

The second test rebuilds the original correlation matrix from the trimmed path model. To reproduce each correlation coefficient the direct, indirect, and common cause effects were calculated and summed. (A detailed description of these effects and how to calculate them can be found in Stump and Hartman [40].) Table 6 presents the three effects, the original correlations, and the amount of unexplained residual for each reconstructed correlation. A rule of thumb is that the difference between the original and reconstructed correlations should be no more than .05.

The effects not explained by the model are generally below the suggested level; however, there are four residuals large enough to warrant further attention. The two largest deviations involve the path from role conflict to commitment and the path from role conflict to intention to quit. These deviations indicate that a direct negative relationship between role conflict and commitment may exist. Had a direct effect been included in the trimmed model this would have greatly reduced the difference between the reconstructed and original correlations for both the path from role conflict to commitment and the path from role conflict to intention to quit.

The large residual for the path between boundary spanning and job satisfaction suggests that the model does not accurately reflect the true relationship between these variables. While boundary spanning does have a negative effect via role conflict, it also must have a positive effect not captured by the model, since the original correlation was positive. It is possible that the model should be expanded to explicitly include job characteristics such as job interest and job autonomy which boundary spanning may enhance, thus increasing job satisfaction. The residual for the path from boundary spanning to intention to quit, while above the .05 cutoff, is quite small (only .08) and thus most likely does not indicate any serious failing of the model.

Six of the paths hypothesized by the original model were supported by this analysis. Five direct paths hypothesized between the study variables and intention to quit were not supported. However, with the exception of boundary spanning, all of the variables were found to influence intention to quit in the expected direction although the relationships were indirect, operating through other variables. The failure to find a direct relationship between job satisfaction and intention to quit was rather surprising given the body of research which shows a relationship between job satisfaction and turnover. This may be an artifact of the measures used, as commitment and intention to quit measure very similar constructs.

The trimmed model illustrates that role ambiguity and role conflict are extremely dysfunctional, reducing job satisfaction and commitment which, in turn, increases the individual's intention to quit. Role conflict was found to be partially attributable to boundary spanning activities of IS personnel.

## Regression analysis

As only moderate support was found for the causal ordering of the variables proposed by the model, a secondary analysis was performed utilizing multiple regression to test the ability of the antecedent variables to explain variance in the outcome variables. The secondary analysis should demonstrate that even though only partial support was found for the causal model of Figure 1, the antecedent variables are indeed important and are able to explain significant amounts of the variance in the dependent variables. As the literature reviewed in this article quite clearly indicated that a number of the independent variables were causally related, hierarchical regression was employed.

The first regression treats intention to quit as the dependent variable and enters the independent variables in order of causal precedent (i.e. variables expected to “cause” another variable are entered first). Boundary spanning is entered on step 1, followed by role conflict and role ambiguity. Role conflict and ambiguity are entered simultaneously, as the path analysis indicates that a relationship exists between these two variables but does not provide evidence of the causal direction of the relationship. Job satisfaction was entered on step 3, followed by commitment. The results of the regression analysis are presented in Table 7a.

## Recommendations for IS Management

The IS manager who is concerned with building a satisfied, committed staff and who desires to control turnover may view these findings as providing strong support for the need to reduce role conflict and role ambiguity for his or her staff wherever possible. Given the very dysfunctional nature of role ambiguity it is important to identify its sources. While the trimmed model does not identify the causes of role ambiguity, interviews held with a number of the study participants did reveal some of the potential sources.

Many study participants cited department reorganizations and policies that created unclear reporting structures (“I really am not sure which project manager is now in charge of my project”) and uncertain job priorities (“I have so many different projects that I don’t know what to do first”) as being problematic. Reward policies were also a source of much ambiguity. A number of the firms in the study were experiencing poor profitability and thus were providing low raises and bonuses for personnel. However, the reasons for the poor raises were often not communicated to the IS personnel, leaving them with the feeling that rewards were arbitrary and ambiguous.

Because IS is a dynamic field with rapidly changing technology and tools, it may be impossible or undesirable to eliminate all role ambiguity as companies attempt to take advantage of new and unfamiliar technologies. However, IS management should be cognizant of the dangers of role ambiguity and make certain that the sources of the ambiguity are not a result of poor management practices and policies within the IS department.

Role conflict, while also a problem faced by IS personnel, was partially traced to their boundary spanning activities. Such role conflict may be unavoidable as IS professionals attempt to serve the multiple user communities of the organization with the limited resources of the IS department. Training to help IS personnel cope with this role conflict may be helpful for the existing staff. In the future, the IS manager may want to consider recruiting and selecting those individuals who have tolerances for both role ambiguity and role conflict, as these may be unavoidable conditions of the IS professionals' world.

## Future Research Directions

Although this study has been useful in forwarding our understanding of IS personnel, several limitations to the research exist.

While the study employed carefully constructed measures (most with lengthy histories of reliability and validity) and employed powerful statistical techniques, the data were cross-sectional. Longitudinal studies are needed to fully address the issue of causation.

The regression analysis demonstrated the importance of the antecedent variables included in the study; however, the path model received only partial support. Better models of IS personnel turnover intentions and behavior are needed. Future models should explicitly include job characteristics and IS management practices and policies. Such models will more fully explain the attitudes, intentions, and behaviors of IS personnel.
