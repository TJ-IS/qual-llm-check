## Abstract

A field survey was conducted to study the factors associated with the use of data processing chargeback information in organizations. The aim of this research was to identify the organizational and budgetary characteristics associated with how the output of a chargeback system is used by user-managers to control their data processing costs. It was found that involvement in budget preparation, accountability for meeting the data processing budget, and cost variability of the charges were the most important factors to consider when designing data processing chargeback systems.

Keywords: Chargeback, chargeout, cost control
ACM Categories: K.6

## Introduction

In the last few years, chargeback systems for data processing services have gained popularity in organizations using data processing equipment. In 1978, Informatics, Inc. [10] reported that 52% of its subscribers had already established chargeback systems, while in 1980 Drury [6] found that 58% of the organizations he studied used them.

A chargeback system is a mechanism that, among other things, helps users allocate financial resources among projects. It offers a framework for top management to assess the relative competitive efficiency and capability of the DP department, and provides information on the costs of adding or deleting computer applications $[13]$ . It does not ensure the organization of an adequate return on investment for its information systems expenses, but as with any budgetary and administrative mechanism, it should help sustain the organizational profitability.

From a data processing department point of view, chargeback systems have varying objectives: cost recovery, cost control, demand control, resource allocation, and user involvement in information systems development $[5, 9, 14, 21]$ . Until now, however, it has been very difficult to demonstrate the effectiveness of chargeback systems with respect to these objectives, and particularly with those related to users $[6, 16, 17]$ .

A possible explanation for the non-conclusive results with respect to user related objectives may be due to the lack of importance given to the actual use of the chargeback information by the user-manager. As with any type of information system, it is necessary for the chargeback information to be used if any positive effect is expected. Past studies omitted to measure the extent to which the chargeback information was used by the user-managers to analyze and control their data processing costs. This may be a major reason why past research did not find any important impacts of chargeback systems on organizations in terms of effectiveness. It is suggested (Figure 1) that the use of the chargeback system by the user-manager acts as an intermediate variable in a general model explaining the effectiveness of the chargeback system in the organization.

The lack of empirical evidence on the effectiveness of chargeback systems may also be due to the inadequate or incomplete characterization of the chargeback system itself and of its environment. Differences in the data processing budgeting procedures and chargeback system characteristics may be important enough to hamper or enhance the effectiveness of this kind of system.

The aim of this study is to identify the data processing budgetary and chargeback system characteristics associated with the use of chargeback information (i.e., data processing budget status reports) by the user-managers to control their data processing costs. It focuses on the relationship between chargeback system characteristics and chargeback information use described in the first half of the general model presented in Figure 1. It also analyzes the differences between development and operations costs to study if the same characteristics are important with respect to the use of chargeback information. Therefore, the major purpose of this study is to identify factors influencing the use of chargeback information. It does not intend to study the relationship between the use of the chargeback system and chargeback system effectiveness, which could be studied in further research.

## Background

Past studies on practical aspects of data processing chargeback systems are neither numerous nor very conclusive. One of the first studies on the topic, if not the first, was Nolan's [16] study which tried to relate chargeback systems characteristics with user-manager attitudes toward information systems in organizations. Nolan measured the maturity of data processing chargeback systems by four variables: the understandability of the reports, the degree of control held by the user over the charges, the user's accountability for performance, and finally, the user's responsibility for costs and benefits. Unfortunately, he was not able to show a clear-cut relationship between the maturity of the chargeback system and user satisfaction with the information for which they were billed. His model explained only 4% of the variance in the attitude of the user-managers toward the information system. Furthermore, Benbasat et al., [1] could not support Nolan's assertion that the introduction of a chargeback system in an organization is related to the maturity of the data processing activities.

A further study by Olson and Ives [17] also failed to establish the existence of a relationship between the quality of the charges (bills) and user attitudes toward information services. They found, however, that users were more satisfied with their chargeback system when they were billed on a resource basis (e.g., cpu time, lines printed) than when they were billed on an overhead basis.

From another perspective, Drury [6] found that the characteristics of the organizations charging users for data processing services differed from those not charging users for data processing services. Furthermore, higher user cost-awareness and computer awareness were observed in organizations charging their users for data processing activities.

Another group of studies was concerned with the pricing mechanisms in the data processing chargeback environment. Although many theoretical papers have been written on the topic, no ideal solution has yet emerged. Indeed, there is still no recognized rule to guide the choice among the different chargeback methods such as actual costs, standard costs, direct or marginal costs, and flexible pricing $[3, 5, 14, 21]$ . The only agreement among researchers and practitioners in that respect is that data processing charges to the users should vary in proportion to use.

Overall, studies on data processing charge-back systems give only parsimonious information on the usefulness of these systems. Further, almost nothing is said about how they should be designed and implemented, i.e., the characteristics these systems should possess to be effective.

## Research Approach

## The Research Model

Based on the literature on budgeting and chargeback systems, five variables were identified as potential predictors of the use of the data processing chargeback information by user-managers (Figure 2): accountability, authority, and involvement variables represent organization and user characteristics; quality of information and cost variability represent budget status report characteristics.

Figure 2. The Research Model

User accountability — the extent to which user-managers are held responsible by their superior for meeting their data processing budget. Accountability is the process, the need and the obligation that organizational members provide their superior with a reasonable justification for their actions. Accountability has long been considered an important aspect of the budgeting system. More recently, accountability has been suggested as an important dimension of any information and control system [12]. In this research, accountable users are those who are asked by their superior to explain variations in their data processing budget, and to act to correct problems.

User authority — the users' power to decide on the quantity and type of services they receive from the data processing department. Although it is generally recognized that responsibility should not exist without authority, there is no evidence that the rule is consistently applied in organizations and in the management of data processing activities. Even in situations where the user are held responsible for results, it is important to measure the real authority the user-managers have over data processing activities, since a lack of authority would discourage them from effectively managing their data processing budget.

Cost variability — the extent to which the charges are proportional to the services received. Users prefer to be charged on a resource rate basis rather than on an overhead basis $[17]$ , but it is not clear now these different pricing mechanisms influence the effectiveness of the chargeback system with respect to cost control. Top MIS executives have already suggested that cost variability is an important aspect of chargeback systems, $[18]$ but the relative importance of charging users in proportion to the services received in order to encourage the users to control their data processing budget is as yet unknown. Cost variability is a measure of the extent to which charges are variable as opposed to fixed.

Quality of billing information — the meaningfulness, accuracy, and timeliness of the data processing charges as perceived by the users themselves. The quality of the information contained in information systems reports has long been considered an important aspect of information systems effectiveness. Therefore, the quality of the information provided on the bills produced by the chargeback system should be a strategic dimension in the subsequent use of the charges to control data processing costs.

User involvement — the extent to which the user-manager participates in budget preparation prior to budget approval. User involvement in information system design and implementation is an important dimension of information system success and is related to the subsequent use of the system by the user. Similarly, the users' involvement in budget preparation is expected to be an important dimension in predicting the subsequent use of the charges by the users to control their data processing budgeted costs.

DP chargeback information use — the extent to which user-managers analyze and control their data processing expenses during the budgeted period. Chargeback information use refers to cost control activities related to the analysis of data processing budget variances and the search for a solution. These activities are carried out by the user-managers upon receipt of the data processing charges. This is the dependent variable of the model.

## Hypotheses

In total, five independent variables (user accountability, user authority, cost variability, quality of information on the charges, and user involvement) were expected to explain variations in one dependent variable (the level of use of chargeback information by the user-manager). All independent variables were expected to show a positive correlation with the use of chargeback information. The following hypotheses were studied separately for development/maintenance and operations costs in accordance with earlier research on chargeback systems [17]. There were two sets of five hypotheses, one for development costs and one for operations costs. Stepwise multiple regression was used to test the hypotheses.

## H1: User accountability for meeting his/her DP budget is positively related to the use of the chargeback information.

This suggests that users who are held accountable for their data processing budget are more likely to make use of the information provided on the chargeback billing than those who are less accountable for results. In the latter case, users will tend to neglect or even ignore chargeback information.

## H2: User authority over DP activities is positively related to the use of the charge-back information.

Users will be concerned about their data processing budget only if they can exert control over the spending of the data processing resources for which they are billed. If, for example, their spending of data processing resources depends upon inputs from other departments over which they have no control or authority, then it is likely that the users will make less use of the chargeback information.

## H3: Cost variability of the DP charges is positively related to the use of the charge-back information.

To make the cost control activity worthwhile from the user's point of view, it is necessary that a decrease or increase in operations or development activities be reflected in the total data processing charges. If a high proportion of the charge is overhead, a decrease in operations might not show up in the total bill received by the user. In this case, the user will probably make little use of the chargeback information since the efforts he/she makes do not result in substantial cost reductions. On the other hand, if costs are proportional to the services used, it is possible for the user to try to control his/her data processing budget.

## H4: Quality of the information on the DP billing is positively related to the use of the chargeback information.

It seems important that the information provided on the billing be understandable to the users, if the data processing department wants them to control their data processing costs. Too often the information provided on operations charges does not lend itself to decision making. Difficulty with discerning the meaning of the charges and associating them with specific control activities do not encourage the use of chargeback information by the user-manager.

## H5: User involvement in DP budget preparation is positively related to the use of the chargeback information.

Involving users in budget preparation should bring positive results. Participants in this process acquire a better understanding of the budgeting process and its importance for the organization. Furthermore, the implicit commitments made during the budgeting process should incite them to analyze and control their budget during the budgeted period. Uninvolved users do not have this incentive.

## Measures

A questionnaire was used to collect the data needed to test the hypotheses. All scales used for construct measurement were multi-item, 5-point Likert-type scales except for the authority scale which was a 7-point Likert-type scale, and the quality scale which was a 7-point semantic differential. The accountability, involvement and use scales were adapted from Swieringa and Moncur [20]; the quality scale was mostly based on Swanson's quality of information scale [19]. The authority and cost variability scales were constructed by the author. Samples of all scales are presented in Appendix 1. In addition to the multi-item scales used to test the hypotheses, individual-item scales asking for an overall evaluation of each construct were developed by the author. They were 7-point Likert-type scales anchored at each end.

The scales' reliability was tested using Cronbach's alpha measure. High levels of reliability were observed. The final number of items in each scale along with their Cronbach alpha are found in Table 1.

Multi-trait, multi-method correlation matrices were used to determine convergent and discriminant validity of the constructs. The data coming from the multi-item scales represented the first method, whereas the data obtained from the individual-item scales measuring the same constructs represented the second method. For convergent validity, the range of correlations was .59 to .77 for development costs, and .50 to .79 for operations costs constructs. For discriminant validity, only one correlation out of ninety was slightly higher than the limit set by the validity coefficient. This correlation concerned the accountability for development costs construct. These levels of convergent and discriminant validity are considered acceptable as defined by Lawler [11], and Gunderson and Nelson [8]. These results suggest that there is only a low probability of a common method variance problem that could have been introduced by the use of a questionnaire as the unique data collection tool. More information about the validity of the instrument is available in Bergeron [2].

A total of 1,246 organizations were contacted. They were invited to participate in the study if they satisfied the selection criteria: to charge internal users for data processing services on a regular basis and in proportion to the services they use (i.e. not a fixed overhead charge).

These results support those of Nolan [16]. However, by comparing the level of $R^2$ , it seems that the use of chargeback information can be predicted more easily than the user's attitudes toward information systems researched by Nolan. The $R^2$ found in this study were of 65% and 68% while Nolan obtained an $R^2$ equal to 4%. This study also has the advantage of identifying which specific variables contribute to the use of the chargeback system while Nolan grouped the variables together.

## Discussion

The results of the tests of hypotheses for both operations and development costs will be discussed simultaneously since most of the accepted relationships for operations costs were the same as for development costs. The importance of user accountability, user involvement, and costs variability with respect to chargeback information use will therefore be stressed, along with the suggestions to improve the level of use of chargeback information by user-managers.

## Accountability, Involvement and Cost Variability

User accountability for the data processing budget, user involvement in budget preparation and cost variability of the data processing charges were found to be the predictors of chargeback information use for both operations and development costs. Therefore, it is suggested that these strategic dimensions be focused on in order to increase the level of use of data processing chargeback information for operations costs.

As far as development costs are concerned, increases in user accountability for meeting his/her data processing budget and user involvement in the budgeting process represent the targets of any attempt to increase the use of the chargeback information by the user-manager. It is interesting to note that accountability is usually considered in accounting literature as an important dimension of budgeting systems. This would suggest that data processing budgeting systems and chargeback systems behave in a similar fashion to any other type of budget in an organization.

A concern for the data processing managers who wish to apply these research results is how to increase the involvement and accountability of their users. The development of a strategic plan for data processing resources $[15]$ and the formation of a committee for priorities $[4, 7]$ could help users realize the importance of being involved in the budgeting of their computing resources. A budget committee could also approve the data processing budgets and make sure these budgets are not overspent. Furthermore, the user's management of budgeted resources could be included in the periodic performance evaluation of the user. By doing so, the importance for the user to meet his/her budget should be emphasized. Finally, as a general rule, it would seem appropriate that the level of user accountability for the data processing budget fit the organizational policy in regard to user accountability for other kinds of budgets.

As far as cost variability is concerned, the fact that this variable is a predictor of information use for the operations budget only and is not one for the development budget is rather difficult to explain. It may be that the formulation of the question impedes appropriate testing of the relationship. It is possibly more difficult for a user to evaluate if the charges he/she gets for development activities are proportional to the amount of development services he/she receives than it is for operations costs for which units of activities are easier to observe and quantify. On the other hand, it may be that proportionality of the charges for development activities is simply less important in the prediction of chargeback information use.

## Authority and Quality

The rejection of user authority over data processing activities and of the quality of the information on the charges as important dimensions of data processing chargeback systems was quite unexpected. As far as user authority is concerned, a possible explanation for its rejection may be that having authority is a necessary but insufficient condition for allowing the user to control his/her data processing costs. Indeed, from a budgeting control point of view, increasing user-manager involvement in budget preparation and holding him/her accountable for results are more important than simply giving him/her the necessary authority.

It was also surprising that the quality of the information on the data processing bills was not an important dimension of data processing chargeback system use, given the importance usually attributed to this aspect by practitioners [18]. A possible explanation may be that if the users are accountable for results, they will have to use the data processing bills to control their costs, whatever the quality of the information provided on the bills. The users may be dissatisfied with the information received concerning their data processing expenses but this does not mean that they will not use the information to control their costs.

On the other hand, high user involvement in the budgeting process may compensate for the low quality of the information on the data processing charges. Indeed, the user's involvement allows a better understanding of the meaning of the budget and its associated bills. The user may still consider that the information provided is not of a high quality, but he/she understands it and knows how to use it to control data processing costs.

## Limitations

This research carries some limitations. First, it concerns only organizations charging their users on a monthly, or more frequent basis. Results could have been different with organizations charging their users quarterly or annually. For example, the quality of the information on the budget reports of the surveyed organizations was generally good. It is possible that an organization charging its users on a frequent basis takes more care about the quality of the information than an organization charging its users on an annual basis only.

Second, the values obtained are users perceptions and are not objective measures. It is possible that the respondents to the questionnaire have biased their responses. Indeed, having much authority, being involved in budgeting process, and using the budget reports to analyze and control expenses are all positive traits of an effective manager. While this social desirability response set does not affect the size of the correlations, it might have artificially increased the real level of each variable. Thus the reported level of each variable should be used with caution.

Third, there could be a common method variance problem due to the fact that all the data were collected through the same instrument, a questionnaire. If this is the case, the size of the correlations and of the regression coefficients might be inflated. Specifically, a common method variance problem could explain the high zero-order correlation between accountability, involvement and use which appeared on the same page of the questionnaire. Conceptually, accountability and involvement are not expected to be so highly correlated. The tests for discriminant and covergent validity applied to the data collected through the questionnaire tended to discard the possibility of a common method variance problem. However, the research results might have been more valid if objective data collected through another instrument had been available.

Fourth, although the study has been designed to be representative of user-managers of medium and large United States organizations doing business in any industrial sector, results are still drawn from a sample which might not be representative of the whole population. User-managers who did not complete the questionnaire might represent different sub-populations from which different conclusions could have been drawn. This is, however, a common threat to any survey research.

## Conclusion

The aim of this study was to identify the conditions under which data processing chargeback information is used by user-managers. The results obtained from this research suggest that it is possible to design and implement data processing chargeback systems that will be effective in having the users control their spending of scarce data processing resources. For development and operations costs, it is suggested that users be held accountable for meeting their data processing budget and that they be involved in the preparation of their own budget. In addition, it is important to charge the users in proportion to the services they use for operations costs only.

In future, research on chargeback systems should take into account the extent to which users use the chargeback information in their budgetary control activities. As explained in Figure 1, the use of chargeback information acts as an intermediate variable between the characteristics of the chargeback system and its effectiveness. While this study has demonstrated the existence of a relationship between characteristics of the chargeback system and use of the chargeback information, it is expected that research studies oriented toward the identification of chargeback systems effectiveness will gain by taking into consideration the use of chargeback information as an explanatory variable. Future research should, therefore, control for chargeback information use.
