# Job Characteristics as Indicants of CBIS Data Requirements

By: Pamela Hammers Specht
Department of Management
College of Business Administration
University of Nebraska at Omaha
Omaha, Nebraska

## Abstract

A test of the relationship between job characteristics and data requirements was conducted. Information system users in five public sector service organizations participated in a cross-sectional field survey. Results indicate that some job characteristics are related to data manipulation requirements, while others are related to data quality. The results support past findings outside the CBIS field that complex, nonroutine tasks with low analyzability require less precise information and more information processing than simple, routine tasks.

Keywords: Information systems design, information requirements, data manipulation, data quality

ACM Categories: E.1.4, H.1.1, K.6.1

## Introduction

The determination of data requirements has been a concern of both computer-based information system (CBIS) and organization theorists. User involvement in the design of the CBIS and user personality dimensions have been identified as useful for determining data requirements. However, determining needs with these two analysis techniques has been problematic [23, 33, 45]. Organization theorists [31, 32, 38, 43], as well as some CBIS writers [5, 9, 10], have suggested that individual job characteristics might be important determinants of data requirements. But there has been little empirical research on this issue, especially at the detailed level.

The present study addresses this research gap by investigating the relationship between job characteristics and CBIS data requirements. This study contributes to both the CBIS and organization theory literature. The CBIS literature deals considerably with design models [e.g., 26, 34, 37]. The majority of these models take a behavioral approach with emphasis on variables such as user involvement. (A more detailed critique of CBIS models is contained in Specht [35].) This study extends the literature to a basic level of organization design, indicating that job characteristics are useful in studying CBIS requirements.

## Literature Review

Specific job characteristics (job complexity, job level, and decision making purpose) and data requirements (quality and manipulation) have been linked in the CBIS and organization theory literature. These variables are examined below.

## Job Complexity

Indicants of job complexity in the literature include task environment complexity and cognitive complexity. The number of different environmental elements (e.g., customers, competitors, government regulations) involved in the job [13] and the number of information sources in the environment needed to make a decision [30] are examples of task environment complexity measures. Cognitive complexity includes job characteristics such as task variety, autonomy, etc. This study uses cognitive complexity measures because they relate to a broader spectrum of job types. All jobs do not involve contact with the external environment, but all jobs have various degrees of cognitive complexity.

Cognitive measures of job complexity most often appearing in the literature are task interdependence, variety, significance, identity, and autonomy. Because task analyzability has been linked with data requirements $[15]$ , the current study added analyzability to this list. Job complexity dimensions used in this study are defined below.

1. Task analyzability — the availability of concrete knowledge about task activities.

2. Task interdependence — the degree of dependence on others in order to carry out one's own tasks.

3. Task variety — the number of exceptional cases encountered in the work requiring different methods or procedures for doing the work, and the routinization, repetitiveness, stability or rigidity of the work.

4. Task identity — the degree to which a job requires the completion of a “whole” and identifiable piece of work.

5. Task significance — the degree to which the job has a substantial impact on the lives or work of other people.

6. Task autonomy — the degree to which the job provides substantial freedom, independence, and discretion to the employee in scheduling work and in determining the procedures to be used in carrying it out.

No studies appear in the CBIS literature linking job complexity to user data requirements. However, several organization theorists have empirically linked the cognitive measure of job complexity to variations in data requirements [6, 7, 39, 40, 42]. These studies show that jobs that are more complex require different data characteristics than simpler, more routine jobs. As job complexity increases, uncertainty and the time required to make decisions increase. Therefore, data that is of high quality (e.g., precise, accurate, timely, and relevant) would be needed to decrease the uncertainty and minimize the time required to make a decision. Daft and Macintosh [15] support this contention with their finding that low task analyzability, an indicator of job complexity, requires precise information. High quality data also become more important as task significance and interdependence increase, because these two indicators of complexity broaden the impact and importance of decisions. However, Daft and Macintosh found that another indicator of complexity, task variety, was not related to need for precise data. Because of this finding, and the fact their work was not done in a CBIS design context, a hypothesis relating job complexity to the need for data quality needs to be tested.

As job complexity increases, the need for manipulated data also may increase. Manipulated data include analyzed data (e.g., means, ranges) and decision aids (e.g., graphics). Similar to quality data, manipulated data decreases the time required to make a decision. Daft and Lengel [14] indicated that complex tasks require more information processing than non-complex tasks. This contention might mean that more data manipulation is required in complex tasks. Daft and Macintosh [15] indicated that as job complexity increases, the need increases for personalized, rather than impersonal, information. Exception reports, a form of data manipulation, might satisfy this need for personalized information. The relationship between job complexity and the requirement for manipulated data needs to be tested.

## Job Level

Data requirements may differ by job level. As the level increases from clerical to managerial, problems differ in uncertainty and complexity. Problems at the higher managerial level tend to be less programmed, stimuli less structured [11, 25]. Gorry and Scott Morton [21] indicated that top and middle managers have different CBIS needs, because of the variance in types of activities carried out while using CBIS reports.

Adams [2] found that top managers had more need for relevant data in their CBIS reports than did middle managers. (Relevant data is narrow in scope, only pertaining to a specific task. Users may want irrelevant data that covers topics unrelated to the immediate task to aid them, for example, in identifying additional tasks to be undertaken and in making creative decisions.) Adams indicated that this finding may have resulted from top managers' reports containing less relevant data than middle managers' reports. His sample, like Gorry and Scott Morton's, was limited to managers.

If data needs differ between two management levels because of a variance in activities, there may also be a difference in data needs across all job levels. As job level increases from clerical to managerial there should be an increasing need to manipulate high quality data to decrease decision complexity. Individuals higher in the organization hierarchy would have more nonroutine tasks to perform, necessitating more data manipulation [15]. This suggests that there may be a positive relationship between job level and requirements for quality and manipulated data.

## Decision Making Purposes

Dickson, Senn, and Chervany [16] hypothesized that when decision making purpose is considered in CBIS design, decision quality is enhanced. However, research relating decision making purpose to data needs is sparse. Findings indicate that even though various managers may be utilizing the same data, different combinations of data characteristics should be selected to fit the different decision making purposes of the users [5, 19, 21]. For example, Adams and Schroeder [3] found that CBIS data used by middle managers for planning could be less accurate, less precise, older, more aggregated, and less descriptive than data used for operations or budgeting and control. Anthony [4] indicated that strategic planning data could be less accurate (p. 67) and less precise (p. 43) than management control data.

Using this literature and Anthony's framework (strategic planning, management control, operation control), this study suggests that as strategic decision making increases, the need for quality data decreases and the need for data manipulation increases. This study also suggests that managerial decision making is positively related to the need for quality data (from Anthony [4]), and for data manipulation. These statements regarding planning and managerial decision making need to be tested because other literature is not in agreement. Lucas [27] and Adams [2] did not find variations in desired report characteristics by decision purposes. Lucas's study, however, actually found no difference in types of data being supplied to managers who had varying types of decisions to make. Variations in user needs and satisfaction with the data received were not explored.

Although planning and managerial decision making were emphasized in the studies just discussed, CBIS data requirements also may differ for operations or routine clerical decisions. (For this study, Anthony's "operations" decision was renamed "routine clerical" to more clearly reflect the meaning of the term.) Routine clerical decisions are simple, straightforward and follow definite procedures. Therefore, with routine clerical decision making, quality data should be more obtainable and data manipulation should not be needed. This reasoning is supported by Daft and Macintosh [15] who indicated that the more analyzable a task, the greater the need for precise information.

## User Data Requirements

CBIS literature provides the data needs of importance to users. Table 1 presents a summary of these data requirements in two categories, data quality and data manipulation. Both of these categories were addressed in this study. Few studies have been conducted relating requirements for quality and manipulated data to job characteristics. Aggregation, timeliness, and preciseness were found to be related to decision making purpose among managers [3]. Daft and Macintosh [15], observed that the amount of data required was related to task analyzability and variety. Data relevancy was related to job level by Adams [2].

## Methods

The primary method for testing the hypotheses was a cross-sectional field survey using a questionnaire. Five public sector service organizations in a midwestern state were contacted as possible research sites and all agreed to participate. The organizations were a state university, a state university hospital, a state and county health department, and a state human services department. To collect qualitative information on the research variables, fifteen in-depth interviews were conducted at the five sites. In addition, five interviews were conducted during instrument development.

In public sector service organizations there are many generation points for CBIS reports. For instance, reports are generated by the federal, state, and local governments, other agencies, regulatory bodies, and in-house IS departments. These CBIS report producers often do not know who the users are because they send the reports to a distribution point. In addition to the proliferation of generation points, many employees receive more than one report. Multiple reports can even come to one employee from one producer of reports. CBIS characteristics, such as aggregated data, can also vary from report to report.

To simplify the sample selection procedure and to make it more accurate, a monthly budget report generated by the state, with data from agencies receiving state funding, was used. The report was a computer print out and was particularly useful for this research because it was used for many different decision purposes by employees with varying degrees of job complexity at all organizational levels. Because many users received more than one report, the users were told in a cover letter what report to refer to in answering the survey. Names of the state monthly budget report varied across organizations, however all reports presented data in identical categories (e.g., personnel costs), and data varied by program within each organization. The data for the CBIS reports were generated in the same manner for each research site and at the same time every month.

The sample selection process resulted in a population of 558 users of the budget report at the five research sites from which a random sample of 200 was chosen. The initial questionnaire mailing was followed two days later by telephone calls to all sample members urging their participation. A second mailing to nonrespondents was made two weeks after the first mailing, followed again two days later with telephone calls encouraging participation. Of the 200 questionnaires mailed, 140 or 70% were returned; 127 or 63.5% were usable for data analyses. Thirteen questionnaires were not used because either they were returned blank (4) or only partially completed (9).

## Instrument Development

Widely accepted scales did not exist for measuring all the job characteristics and data requirements studied. Therefore, several steps were undertaken in instrument development. These included an extensive literature review to examine previous operationalizations of the study's variables, semi-structured interviews with CBIS users and analysts, face validity and internal consistency checks. Face validity of the questionnaire was checked with formal interviews of five individuals in public sector organizations. The positions (Billing Clerk, Director of Special Programs, Executive Assistant, Dean, Accountant) held by these individuals were widely divergent, reflecting the eventual sample composition. For each item on the questionnaire, each interviewee was asked to interpret the item and to explain his/her response to the researcher. Internal consistency of multi-item scales was determined by pretesting the questionnaire with 21 users in two public sector organizations. All but one standardized item alpha ranged from .70 to .91. Another reliability analysis was conducted after all data had been collected. (Results are reported in Table 6.)

## Variable Measurement

## Job Complexity

The Job Diagnostic Survey (JDS) [22] was used to measure the job complexity dimensions of task interdependence, variety, significance, identity, and autonomy. Task analyzability items and additional task variety items were taken from instruments developed by Van de Ven and Delbecq [41] and Daft and Macintosh [15]. Construct validation had previously been conducted on these instruments [44]. All items measuring job complexity, except the JDS items, are presented in the Appendix.

Subjects described their jobs while using the budget report on seven-point scales. Each of the six dimensions comprising job complexity was measured by averaging responses to three to five questionnaire items. The dimensionality of job complexity tends to vary by sample [17]. Therefore, a factor analysis was conducted on the sample data. Results showed that two factors were unambiguously defined by five of the six job complexity measures. Only task identity failed to load clearly on either factor. Based on the results presented in Table 3, three indices of job complexity were formed. The factor that accounted for most of the common variance was labeled job complexity. The other two factors were task analyzability and task identity. Job complexity was the average of the responses to the items measuring task interdependence, variety, significance, and autonomy. Task analyzability was the average of five items (see Appendix); task identity the average of three JDS items. The correlation between the job complexity and task analyzability indices was r = .39 (p<.001), between complexity and task identity was .05 (NS), and between analyzability and identity was -.18 (p<.05).

## Job Level

Each respondent was classified in one of three job levels by the researcher using position titles, and by a manager at each of the five research sites. Interviews at the five research sites netted information that these three job levels and definitions were meaningful. There were no disagreements between the researcher and managers regarding the classifications. The three levels are defined below.

Level 1. Clerical — nonprofessional employee, may have supervisory responsibility over other nonprofessionals

Level 2. Nonmanagerial — professional employee with less than one FTE supervisory responsibility

Level 3. Managerial — professional employee with supervisory responsibility for one or more FTE's

Of the 127 usable questionnaires for analysis, 32 (25.5%) of the respondents were classified as clerical; 51 (40.2%) were nonmanagerial; 44 (34.6%) were managerial.

## Perceived Decision Making Purposes

Respondents were asked to indicate, using a seven-point ascending scale from “very inaccurate” to “very accurate,” how accurate eleven statements were in describing the kinds of decisions they made while using the CBIS budget report. The user could be making strategic decisions, managerial decisions, and routine clerical decisions. The exact items for the three decision making purposes are presented in Table 4.

A factor analysis was run on the three decision making purposes, resulting in two factors (Table 4). The clerical decision making items loaded on one factor, and the managerial and strategic decision making items loaded on the other factor. However, the original three decision making purposes were retained because of the high likelihood that the results of the analysis were due more to the sample composition than to the lack of three unique decision purposes. Because the correlation between managerial and strategic decision making was .62 (p<.001), the conclusion can be made that the sample consisted of individuals who made both managerial and strategic decisions when using the CBIS budget report. Only two factors or decision making purposes, then, were identified because factor analysis uses the correlation matrix as the data.

An additional factor analysis was conducted to determine if the individual managerial and strategic items loaded on the decision making purposes they were originally designed to measure. With the number of factors preset to two, the analysis was run. Items loaded on each factor matched the decision making purpose they were designed to measure. Therefore, as a result of the two factor analyses, no change was made in the items measuring each decision making purpose. Routine clerical decision making was measured with the average of four items. An average of five items assessed managerial decision making, and an average of two items measured strategic decision making. The correlation between clerical and managerial decision making was .28 (p<.001), between clerical and strategic was .17 (NS), and between managerial and strategic .62 (p<.001).

The set of requirements can be represented as two primary data needs: (a) the need for data quality, and (b) the need for data manipulation. ## Findings

All data were analyzed at the level of the individual subject. Tests for site differences in the job characteristics were run, and no significant differences were found across the five research organizations. Table 6 shows the means, standard deviations, Cronbach alphas and selected zero-order correlation coefficients for the study variables. Alphas ranged from .62 to .85, which represent acceptable reliabilities [29]. Job level and the specific dimensions comprising data quality (e.g., timeliness) and data manipulation (e.g., analysis) do not have computed alphas because they are not multi-item scales. All correlations between any two job characteristics, not reported in Table 6 were, less than .40 except for two. The correlation between job complexity and job level was .47 (p<.001) and between managerial and strategic decision making was .62 (p<.001).

Results of the hypotheses testing are presented in the boxes in Table 6. No support is seen for hypothesis 1, and mixed support is seen for hypothesis 2. The requirement of users for data quality in a CBIS report was inversely related in a statistically significant manner to task analyzability, but was not related to task identity and job complexity. The requirement for data manipulation was significantly related to job complexity, but not to task analyzability or identity. Respondents, then, who perceived there was little concrete information available about their task activities (task analyzability) needed lower quality data than those who perceived there was more concrete information available. Therefore, task analyzability was inversely coded to match job complexity. (As task analyzability decreases, job complexity increases.) Respondents who perceived their jobs to be complex needed more manipulated data than those who perceived their jobs to be routine.

No significant associations were found between need for quality data on a CBIS report and job level, or between need for special request reports and job level. Hypothesis 3 was therefore unsupported. Table 6 does show support for hypothesis 4. A statistically significant positive relationship was found between job level and the need for data manipulation. Managerial respondents were more likely to require data manipulation than clerical respondents.

Some support was found for hypotheses 5 and 6 which related decision making purpose to CBIS requirements. The requirement of users for quality data was positively related to managerial decision making and positively, not negatively as hypothesized, related to strategic decision making. User requirement for manipulated data was, as hypothesized, positively related to managerial and strategic decision making. Finally, clerical decision making was positively related to need for quality data, but was not significantly related to need for data manipulation. Managerial decision making was the only decision making purpose that had a statistically significant relationship with the need for easily obtainable special reports.

Respondents, then, who make many strategic decisions (forecasting and goal setting) require higher quality, manipulated data than those who make few strategic decisions while using CBIS budget reports. Respondents who use those reports for reviewing activities going on under them, evaluating performance, detecting problem areas, and solving nonroutine problems (managerial decision making) also need higher quality, manipulated data, and more special reports than those who do not make managerial decisions. Respondents making routine clerical decisions (decisions involving a definite procedure) need higher quality data than those who make few such decisions while using budget reports.

## Discussion

## Results

The central question addressed in this investigation concerned whether job characteristics correlated with data requirements. The answer to this question is visible in the results presented. The job characteristics of cognitive complexity, job level, and decision making purpose are all related to data requirements. More specifically (from Table 7), CBIS users making routine clerical decisions need quality data, while users dealing with uncertainty (managers with complex jobs who are making strategic decisions) need less manipulated data. Finally, CBIS users making managerial decisions with little information regarding their activities (low task analyzability) need easy access to special request reports.

Contrary to what was hypothesized, job complexity and job level were not related, in a statistically significant way, to data quality requirements. In addition, clerical decision making was not inversely related to the need for data manipulation. Interviewees at all job levels, and with all degrees of job complexity, indicated a need for quality data on any report. Therefore, the requirement for timely, precise and relevant data existed for users at the low as well as the high ends of job complexity and job level. On the other hand, the need for manipulated data (such as decision aids) did not exist for routine clerical decision making.

Also contrary to what was hypothesized, strategic decision making was not negatively related to the requirement for quality data. The hypothesis was based on a study conducted by Adams and Schroeder [3]. They found that CBIS data used by managers for planning could be of lower quality than CBIS data used for operations or budgeting and control. Present results can not exactly be compared to the Adams and Schroeder study because of some differences in decision making purposes identified. However, this study did find that as decision making purpose moved from clerical to managerial to strategic, correlations of each purpose with data quality requirements decreased from .35 to .22 to .20. A test for differences between dependent r's revealed that the difference between the correlations involving managerial and strategic decision making (.22 minus .20 = .02) was not statistically significant. However, the differences between the correlations involving clerical and strategic decision making (.35 minus .20 = .15) and involving clerical and managerial decision making (.35 minus .22 = .13) were statistically significant. Based on these tests, managerial and strategic decision making do require less quality data than clerical decision making, but there is no difference in quality requirements between managerial and strategic decision making.

Anthony [4] indicated strategic planning data is less precise and less accurate than management control data. His contention may be correct for some strategic planning information, but not for data obtained from CBISs. From this study, CBIS users involved in strategic planning wanted as much high quality information from their CBIS reports, especially from budget reports, as did users making managerial decisions.

The finding of a positive relationship between strategic decision making and data quality requirements also may be due to unique requirements for strategic decision making in public sector human service organizations. Quality data for forecasting and setting goals may be obtainable and therefore expected in public sector service organizations, but not in private or non-service organizations, but not in private actions of competitors often make forecasting and setting goals difficult for private, non-service organizations because quality data on competitors is not obtainable. Public sector service organizations, as the ones sampled here, are not faced with as much competition and therefore are not faced with the problem of obtaining data on competitors.

## Practical Application

An example of how this study's results could be used to improve CBIS report design is based on a case pieced together from the interviews conducted to supplement this research. A detailed CBIS report which identified services given at a mental health in-patient facility was being used by clerks for third party billing, by middle managers to determine appropriate staff deployment, and by upper management to plan for future resource allocation to services. Because detailed, precise information was not required by the middle and upper managers, staff were assigned the task of aggregating, analyzing and producing decision aids to meet the managers' information needs. The findings of this study have alerted the organization to differing CBIS needs based on job level and decision making purpose and have promoted the recommendation that a new CBIS report be designed to provide the analysis and decision aids required by the managers. Implementation of this recommendation may save the facility money by freeing staff to work on projects to which the CBIS cannot contribute.

As this case illustrates, the results of research matching job characteristics with data requirements have practical applications to CBIS design. Additional studies should result in the development of normative statements to aid organizations in enhancing their capacity to handle information by the use of CBISs. The current findings suggest that if a specific CBIS report is utilized by employees with various degrees of job complexity, at various levels in the hierarchy and with different types of decisions to make, then the report designer will have to include various levels of data quality and manipulation to meet user needs. More specific design guidelines can be developed from Table 6. For instance, the greater the task interdependence of the user, the greater the user's need for timely data. This study also found that the JDS [22] (measured job complexity) and Anthony's framework [4] (measured decision making purpose) can be used to identify CBIS data needs. Coupled with job level, these two tools could be used successfully by designers to enhance CBISs.

## Limitations

Limitations exist in this study. The findings are based almost entirely on self-report measures. Job level was the only objectively measured variable. If objective measures for the variables of job complexity and decision making purposes were used, the proposed relationships might not hold. The argument, however, could be made that whether an employee uses a report is based not on objectively derived design critiera, but on perceptions of the fit between the report and their information needs. Therefore, subjective measures such as the ones used in this study are probably appropriate.

Another limitation to this study is that the survey instrument gathered information on one type of report in one type of organization. The study results may not generalize to non-budget reports or to private sector organizations. Fifteen interviews, though conducted in public sector service organizations, did address many types of reports with comparable results. Interviewees were asked to describe their tasks while using each of the CBIS reports they currently received, and to explain their information requirements for each report. Relationships found in this study held, regardless of report used. Results of this research also reflect findings from other settings, such as Lucas's study [27].

Private and non-service sector CBIS users share many of the same task responsibilities (i.e., accounting, planning, support activities) as the sample members. Because some of the responsibilities of private and non-service sector users could be dissimilar from sample member responsibilities, (e.g., using a CBIS report to make a decision regarding inventory allotment) external validity of this study might be negatively affected. However, this research does provide a needed extension to prior work on CBIS report design and data requirements by finding that users of the same report have different requirements on different job characteristics. Research encompassing a wider variety of organizations and CBIS reports will have to be conducted to ascertain whether the results are generalizable.

Future research directions also might involve investigating other organizational characteristics. Specht and Robey [36] have suggested that organizational environment, size, formalization, and centralization might be studied to determine if they are related to CBIS requirements. For example, the stable vs. changing dimension of environment may be a valuable data needs analysis tool. Tushman [40] operationalized this dimension as the rate-of-change of techniques or skills the organization needs to carry out its work and the rate-of-change of scientific, technical, or market domains. These two sources of variability seem to be intuitively related to information needs, but such a relationship has only been studied by organization theorists [e.g., 30], not CBIS theorists. As CBISs become more sophisticated and are designed to provide a richer media, additional research will be needed to determine what job characteristics are related to use of this new type of CBIS. For example, how are job characteristics related to the use of expert systems? Longitudinal research is also suggested to determine if job characteristics are predictors as well as correlates of information processing needs.

# Appendix

Items measuring job complexity were taken from the Job Diagnostic Survey (JDS) and other sources as indicated in the “Variable Measurement” section of this article. The non-JDS items are presented below. Subjects were asked to respond to each item on an ascending seven-point scale ranging from “to a very great extent” to “not at all.”

## Items measuring task analyzability

1. To what extent is there a clearly defined body of knowledge or subject matter which can guide you in doing your work?

2. To what extent is there an understandable sequence of steps that can be followed in doing your work?

3. During the course of your work, to what extent do you come across specific difficult problems that you don't know how to solve?

4. To what extent is it possible to learn enough about your job to handle all of the problems that come up?

5. In some jobs, things are fairly predictable. In others, you are often not sure what the outcome will be. To what extent are you generally sure what the results of your efforts will be?

## Items, in addition to JDS items, measuring task variety

1. To what extent are the work decisions you make different from one day to the next?

2. If you wrote a list of the exact activities you would be confronted with on an average work day, to what extent do you think these activities would be interrupted by unexpected events?

3. In doing your job from day to day, to what extent do you adopt different ways of doing things?
