# The Effects of 3D Imagery on Managerial Data Interpretation

By: Jong Min Lee
College of Business Administration
Yonsei University
Seoul, Korea

By: James MacLachlan
William A. Wallace
School of Management
Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute
Troy, New York

## Abstract

The new technology of computer graphics makes it possible to create images on the screen that literally appear to pop out into the room. This is accomplished by creating separate images for the left and right eye and filtering them so that each eye receives the appropriate image. In the context of a scattergram, the binocular disparity information is interpreted by the brain as the z axis of a three dimensional x-y-z scattergram. In this article a convenient anaglyph method of accomplishing this on a standard IBM PC is presented. Also, a multi-color method using polarized projection is described and tested. The effectiveness of stereoscopic presentations is investigated within the context of managerial decision-making problems. When used to present continuous data in an x-y-z scattergram, the stereoscopic presentation resulted in greater accuracy and faster answers. However, when used with 3-D block diagrams to present semi-discrete data of the type usually presented by crosstab tables, the technique was less successful than a conventional crosstab presentation.

Keywords: Computer graphics, management information systems, decision making
ACM Categories: 1, 1.3

## Introduction

Graphics are an inherent part of managerial decision making and have been relied on for years to analyze and present data. Recent years have seen a great deal of interest in computer graphics, with a particular concern with its use by managers $[4, 8, 11]$ . The value of graphics in improving managerial productivity is a subject of debate among researchers and practitioners alike. However, computer graphics in a wide variety of forms has become an established component of the information technology viewed as part of decision support systems. Technological advances will soon enable a manager to add another capability to his/her system of computer graphics: stereoscopic (3D) images. What, if any, will be the utility of this new capability in providing decision support for managers?

Graphics is one way to analyze quantitative information. It visually displays measurements by using one or more media including points, lines, numbers, words, symbols, and colors $[10]$ . Recent advances in computer technology can create stereoscopic images to convey information. This technology adds a depth dimension to the present displays, facilitating the visual portrayal of complexity. This capability could be useful where simple x-y graphics do not provide sufficient information because there is a z dimension which interacts with x and y.

Consider, for example, the problem of determining the most effective use of advertising and promotional pricing for a chain of stores. The manager could look at scatterplots of advertising vs. sales and promotion vs. sales, but this would fail to reveal the fact that advertising is much more effective when it is accompanied by promotional pricing. Likewise, promotional pricing generates more sales when accompanied by advertising. This relationship between advertising, promotion, and sales could be shown graphically by a three-dimensional scatterplot.

Even more to the point, as far as the decision maker is concerned, would be the relationship of advertising and promotion to profit. A three-dimensional graphic will show that profit peaks at a certain level of advertising and promotion, and that peak would represent an optimal level. This information could not be captured in two-dimensional graphics because the variables are non-linear and interactive.

The manager might also be concerned with how to divide the advertising budget between newspaper and radio advertisements. The usual way of looking at this is to plot newspaper advertising dollars against sales and plot radio advertising against sales. This would not show the probable interactive effect — when newspaper advertising is used to provide details and radio is used as reminder advertising the combined effect could be greater due to interactive effects. This would be revealed by a three-dimensional graphic display from which a manager could determine the optimal mix of newspaper and radio for a given budget constraint.

This article, as an exploratory study, discusses the physiological basis for perceiving in three dimensions. It then describes the technological means for creating images that will be perceived as three-dimensional; selects one, the polarizing method, for experimentation, and presents the results of that experimentation.

## The Stereoscopic Method

Stereoscopic images were first created by Charles Wheatstone in 1833 [2]. His device used mirrors to enable the left and right eyes to see separate drawings which contained binocular disparity information (Figure 1). This was the forerunner of the stereoscope, which was popular in Victorian times, and today's popular toy called the View-Master.

## The Polarizing Method

Operating on the same principle, stereoscopic movies have been created by projecting separate pictures for the left and right eyes. Two projectors equipped with polarizing filters are used, and the viewers wear glasses containing polarizing lenses. This polarizing system causes each eye to see only its intended image. Movies of this type have included House of Wax and the more recent 3-D version of Jaws.

## The Time-Division Method

In the time-division method the two parts of the stereo pair are alternately presented to the viewer's eyes at a very high rate, about 60 cycles per second. Using a device which is synchronized exactly with the screen alternation, each of the viewer's eyes is stimulated by the appropriate half of the stereo pair, creating what is, in effect, a continual image impression on each retina. These two retinal images, when fused in the brain, will yield a perception of three-dimensional relief.

Because the images on a CRT do not vanish immediately this method may provide unsatisfactory results. The method will be more promising once the problem of CRT “ghosts” is solved and the technology for the required electro-optical synchronization is developed. The development of fully synchronized, lightweight “switching glasses” which allow sufficient light penetration will make the time-division projection method a viable mode of 3-D presentation.

## The Lenticular Sheet Method

The 3-D images created through the use of lenticular sheets are commonly used in such commercial products as phonograph albums, tourist postcards, and illustrations and advertisements for magazines. They are autostereoscopic and unidirectional, because they require no glasses or other selection devices and because they provide information about only the horizontal and not the vertical parallex.

The thickness of a lenticular sheet, as diagrammed in Figure 2, is usually such that its rear surface and focal plane coincide. The stereo pair is printed beneath the lenticular sheet in fine strips, so that the left and right eyes will perceive different parts of the printed image, thereby creating a 3-D relief effect.

## The Anaglyph Method

The easiest way to achieve binocular disparity stereoscopic images is through the anaglyph method. This involves drawing two separate images on top of one another. Typically this involves a green image for one eye and a red image for the other eye. The viewer then wears glasses which contain one red lens and one green lens. The green lens allows the green light to pass but blocks the red light, and the red lens passes red light and holds back green. Thus, it is possible for the left and right eyes to see separate images from the same surface. This is readily accomplished on an IBM PC by drawing one image in red and the other in green (they will be yellow where they overlap). As a result, true binocular disparity stereoscopic images can be perceived on a PC when the user wears red/green glasses.

The anaglyph method, however, does not make it possible for the PC user to perceive multiple colors. As discussed earlier, there are four ways in which colored stereoscopic images can be generated on a CRT screen:

1. Stereoscopic method. A different CRT is used for each eye. They are seen through a device equipped with mirrors so the image appropriate to each eye is reflected to it, in the same style as Wheatstone's stereoscope. Two PC's could work in tandem to create the images, or a single PC could generate two frame buffers driving two CRTs.

2. Polarizing method. The image for the left eye is created and photographed. Then the image for the right eye is created and a different photograph is made of it. Two projectors, which are equipped with polarizing lenses, are used to project these images onto an aluminized screen. As in stereoscopic movies, the image is viewed by an audience wearing glasses with polarized lenses.

3. Time-Division method. The two parts of the stereo pair are alternately presented to the viewer's eyes at a very high rate, about 60 cycles per second, on a computer CRT screen. The viewer wears special glasses with lenses which become transparent only when the image that should be viewed through that lens is on the screen.

4. Lenticular Sheet method. A lenticular lens is placed over a CRT screen in such a way that the left and right eyes see a different set of pixels. The viewer does not need to wear special glasses.

## Research Design

Of the four methods just enumerated, the lenticular sheet method would be the most desirable for use on personal computers because it requires that no special glasses be worn. However, the technology is not yet perfected to the point that it is being offered to the public in a CRT version. Therefore, in our research we chose to use the polarizing method. We would expect that results reported here could be generalized to the lenticular sheet method since both technologies represent a way of creating a binocular disparity 3-D image in full color.

Because of its ease of application, we also chose to include the anaglyph method in our calibration experiments. To apply that technique in the field, the user needs only to have a PC equipped with color display and a pair of glasses with red and green lenses.

## Choice of Equipment

The IBM PCjr was selected because it is capable of 16 colors in medium resolution mode as compared with 4 colors in that mode using the standard IBM PC. Recently, however, IBM introduced the enhanced graphics adapter for their PC. Using that adapter, the standard PC is capable of the extra colors available on PC-jr. Both computers have a resolution of 320 pixels horizontally by 200 pixels vertically in medium resolution mode.

When using anaglyph technology, both machines are equally effective because the four colors available on the standard IBM PC can include the necessary red and green against a black field. The PCjr offers the possibility of 4 colors in high resolution mode with a resolution of 640 by 200 pixels. However, in that mode the vertical lines are half the width of horizontal lines and lines drawn at angles can have severe “staircasing” problems due to varying pixel sizes along the line. Therefore, the potential higher resolution advantage of the PCjr for anaglyph operation did not materialize.

For the polarizing method, it is important that the image from each projector be in precise registration. This requires special control at three critical junctures:

1. The slides must be mounted in perfect registration. We used a system available from the Wess Plastic Company of Farmingdale, New York, which punches registration holes on the film exactly where desired. These registration holes mate with pins in the glass slide mounts available from the company.

2. The slide projectors must register each slide precisely. The Kodak Ektagraphic III-A is the only example we are familiar with which addresses this problem. It works by implementing a second arm which pushes the slide mount laterally against an internal surface of the projection cavity.

3. Projection must be free of "keystoning." This was accomplished by using a special anti-keystoning lens. That is, a top to bottom variation in width of the projected image due to the projector axis not being at a $90^{\circ}$ angle to the screen.

## The Applications

A prime application of 3-D stereoscopic graphics is the presentation of three dimensional scattergrams. Pilot testing revealed that a convincing 3-D effect could be created. The effect can be enhanced by incorporating four other depth cues: brightness, color, relative size, and lines of perspective.

Cognitive psychologists have demonstrated that a difference in brightness, in two otherwise identical objects, will lead a viewer to perceive them at different distances. Against a dark background, for instance, a bright object will appear to be nearer to the viewer than will a dark object of identical size and shape [3]. We incorporate this effect in the scattergram by having brighter colors in the foreground.

Regarding color, as Egusa [5] has demonstrated with experimental subjects, red is the hue most often perceived as being nearest to the viewer, followed by green and blue. Oyama [7], testing for figural dominance, obtained similar results, with red being the most dominant hue and blue the least. Thus, the relative dominance of hue may be related to its utility in creating the illusion of spatial depth. The effect is incorporated into the scattergram by having warmer colors in the foreground and cooler colors in the background — red is closest, greens midway, and blue farthest away.

Relative size is used as a cue by letting small crosses represent the points of a scatter-gram. Closer points are represented by larger crosses.

Finally, lines of perspective have been incorporated into the scattergram by creating a border which frames in the field of points (Figure 3). The frame forms a 3-D box which encloses the points. The z axis is delineated by colored grid lines along the edges of the box. In keeping with the color scheme described, warmer colored grid lines along the edge of the box represent closer distances.

![](/api/attachments/NMKCCGTN/fulltext/images/81aba8ca02d16e9c1c30118307f355759b7bae9c3ffb73b806295deb30f1e2a0.jpg)  
Figure 3. Example of 3-D Frame

The grid enhances the sensation of depth by creating a kind of texture on the walls of the box, thus operating as a pictorial depth cue. A further depth cue is provided by lines of perspective. The vanishing point of the lines forming the corners of the box is at the center of the screen; i.e. if these lines were extended indefinitely they would converge at the center of the screen just as, when looking along a railroad track toward the horizon outdoors, the track lines appear to converge at a point in the center of an observer's field of vision.

The grid also serves the usual function of grids in any plot — they pinpoint the location of data points against a constant frame of reference.

## Scatter Plot Pretest

In the calibration pre-test, ten subjects were shown four anaglyphic version scatter plots and were asked several questions about their perceptions of the relative depths and positions of objects within the display, such as whether object A was closer or farther than object B. The anaglyphic process uses differently colored filters placed in front of each eye; we used red and green filters for this purpose, and drew the screen images in red and green. This provided a simple method of gaining the 3-D illusion, but the effect is monochromatic. In similar fashion, 14 subjects were shown five polarized versions of scatter plots and were questioned as they viewed.

Among the 14 viewers of the polarized scatter plots, a single subject gave incorrect answers to two questions about the relative positions and depths of objects within one scatter plot. Among the anaglyph group, six persons from the group of ten were able to respond correctly to such questions without providing contradictory answers. Kendall's coefficient of concordance between theoretical and perceived rankings of distance was significant at p = < .00005 for the polarized test and p = .0232 for the anaglyph test. The anaglyph display suffered from some ghost imaging caused by imperfect filtering of the opposite eye images by the red and green filters, and this may have accounted for its poorer performance. Also, there was not the reinforcing cue of color in the anaglyph version.

## 3-D Block Diagram Pretest

A similar calibration test was done concerning a 3-D block diagram. Anaglyphic subjects were shown four block models and polarized subjects were shown three block diagrams. As they saw the images, subjects were asked questions about distances represented in the diagrams. In both the anaglyph and polarized versions, all subjects responded correctly when asked whether a given object was nearer or farther than another object. The universal success of the subjects in this case is probably due to the assistance provided by the redundant depth cue of the linear perspective in which the block models were drawn. ## Experimental Design

In order to test the utility of 3-D graphics, it was necessary to compare the results obtained from experimental subjects who were presented with two and three dimensional presentations of similar complexity. Slide presentations of polarized scatter plots and block models were the primary test materials. Tasks were chosen in which 3-D graphics had a potential advantage in that x, y and z axes were needed to represent the data in a single exhibit. The scatter plot represented the situation where all three axes represent continuous variables; such data is difficult to portray in alternative two-dimensional methods. The block model represented a situation where data was continuous on only one axis, the other axes representing discrete integer values; such data can be portrayed reasonably effectively by alternate two-dimensional methods.

Forty five subjects were recruited from an undergraduate course in marketing management. They were paid for their participation. Subjects were randomly divided into four equal groups. One subject's data was eliminated from the final calculations because the subject's attention to the experiment was apparently low. The grouping is shown in Table 1.

Two management scenarios were presented to each subject. Scenario A, used with scattergram data, was based on the auto market. Scenario B, used with block diagram data, was based on the real estate market. Representations of the data corresponding to each scenario were produced in two versions, two and three dimensional.

Members of A2 were tested on a two-dimensional version of scenario A first and a three-dimensional versions of scenario B second, and so on. This arrangement was intended to provide experimental balance and to eliminate any effect caused by ordering, whether of scenario or of dimensionality.

Subjects were given a warm-up period with each display mode before being tested in order to familiarize them with two- and three-dimensional projected graphics and to allow them some time to learn to fuse three-dimensional stereo pairs. It was hoped that by providing an identical warm-up period for the two-dimensional displays it would be possible to neutralize any possible distortion of emphasis.

Before the first model was presented, the background of the scenario was presented to the subject. In order to eliminate any variation in emphasis or tone, tape recordings were used rather than a live voice.

Each experimental session was recorded using a second tape recorder. Thus, both the accuracy of subjects' responses and the time they required could be counted as significant experimental data without letting subjects know they would be timed. The actual timing was done later by playing back the tape recording and using a stop watch to determine response times.

Because the 3-D representations make it possible to compact the data to be analyzed into a single display, and because the 3-D exhibits make it easy to visualize the interactive effects in the data, it was anticipated that in the 3-D versions subjects would be both faster and more accurate. It was anticipated that the effect would be strongest in the case of the continuous data case represented by the 3-D scattergram, because the 3-D presentation was substantially less cumbersome than the 2-D representations.

The principal hypotheses therefore are:

1. 3-D subjects will be more accurate.

2. 3-D subjects will answer more quickly.

The dependent variables, interpretation accuracy and speed of decision making, were selected (a) because of the need for the managers to understand the data correctly as a prerequisite to any other possible use, and (b) because faster response time can be viewed as a positive result since any reduction in cognitive effort will facilitate decision making.

## Scattergram Results

## Discussion of Results

In a three-dimensional situation, the scatter-gram led to more accurate and faster decision making. The block diagram, however, did not increase accuracy and slowed decision making. Why was 3-D apparently successful in the scattergram presentation but not in the block diagram presentation?

The 3-D scattergram depicted a condition where three axes were required to portray data, each point varying along three continua. Portraying that information using 2-D techniques was less efficient and required three separate scattergrams. In the case of the block diagram situation, however, the data was collapsed into nominal categories on two axes and only the third axis (houses sold) was a continuum. Thus, it was possible to represent that data in a single tabular exhibit and both presentations were efficient. It may also be that the novelty effect of seeing and interpreting 3-D data slowed subjects somewhat.

In a situation where data must be depicted on x, y and z continua, and the data cannot be conveniently collapsed into categories, then the 3-D scattergram provides a far more convenient and comprehensive method for presenting the data. All data can then be comprehended from a single exhibit, regardless of non-linearities and interactive effects which may be present. Furthermore, the graphic nature of 3-D scattergrams facilitates visualization of the information. Further research is needed to determine the number of data points, and the complexity of the configurations which they represent, that can be meaningfully presented in this manner before the decision maker becomes confused.

In situations where two of three axes can be represented in non-continuous fashion, then the appropriate 3-D representation is a block diagram. However, in this experiment the block diagram was found to have no advantage over tabular presentation of data which is also a possibility when data is at this level of complexity. It appears, therefore, that the forte of 3-D graphics will be in the presentation of data of higher levels of complexity. When x, y and z axes are continua then there is no efficient alternative method for presenting the information using 2-D representations. The best that can be done in 2-D is to create many x-y plots representing each level of the z variable.

## Concluding Remarks

The objective of this study was to assess the utility of stereoscopic images in presenting data for managerial decision-making. As the technology becomes available managers will be pressured to add 3-D portrayal to their present capabilities. We found that a continuous 3-D effect could be produced with relatively inexpensive and readily available technology. This technology, incorporating an IBM PCjr, was then used to test scatter plots and block model presentations of data in both 2-D and 3-D. Our experimental results showed that the 3-D scatter plots provided better decision support than their 2-D counterparts. However, the 3-D presentations of the block diagrams did not increase accuracy and, in fact, slowed decision making.

It would be reasonable for software developers to create programs which would convert spreadsheet data to 3-D scattergrams. This could assist researchers in determining patterns and interactions, and facilitate optimization decisions such as the advertising/promotion example mentioned earlier. This could be done most efficiently with the anaglyph method because the only accessory required to make viewing possible with a color monitor-equipped PC is the red/green glasses. However, it may be necessary to develop red/green filters of more precise spectral characteristics than those available when the pre-test reported here was conducted (there was some ghosting due to one eye seeing a faint version of the image intended for the other eye). A more desirable solution would be a single monitor which would create a 3-D image in color. As mentioned earlier, there are technical reasons to believe this will be possible.

Our work, as well as a great deal of research on computer graphics, has yet to result in prescriptions useful to those designing and implementing management information systems. We can offer some guidance with respect to stereoscopic images. If the potential user performs data analyses where the variables are continuous and interactive, a 3-D capability will facilitate interpretation of the relationships among the variables. If the sense of depth, similar to that which our eyes and brain generate, is needed in graphic presentations, stereoscopic images are needed — and will become commercially available.

In this exploratory study we focused on rudimentary tasks and used laboratory experimentation as our research methodology. A logical extension is to develop exercises that contain more complex tasks and have practitioners as subjects. However, a more fundamental question is the interrelationship between the mode of presentation, 3-D vs 2-D, and the cognitive aspects of human information processing. As technology attempts to mimic the way humans process data and make decisions, we need to continue to address the issue of “why” as well as “how well.”

The research demonstrates that even with present technology it is possible to generate stereoscopic images using a personal computer, and that in situations involving complex data, faster and more accurate managerial decisions could result. However, we fervently hope that this new technology will not join “the stockpile of information miracle drugs” [6], but instead will serve as an additional capability in our striving to match the technology with the decision situation, in order to truly provide decision support.
