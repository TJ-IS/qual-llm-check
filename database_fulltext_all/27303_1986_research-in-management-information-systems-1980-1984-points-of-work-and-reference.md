## Abstract

This article examines the progress of MIS as a scholarly field of study since 1980. In this examination, MIS is identified as emerging from a supporting base of three foundational fields: computer science, management science, and organization science. Hypotheses related to this emergence are tested by an analysis of data on 271 MIS articles published during the period 1980-84 in six academic journals and one conference proceedings. Each article is described by its work point (the field of study represented by the publication in which the article appears) and its reference point (as represented by the distribution of the article's publication citations). Results of the analysis support the proposition that MIS is emerging as a distinct field of study, with its own cumulative tradition.

Keywords: MIS, management science, computer science, organization science, bibliometrics

ACM Categories: H.0, H.1.0, H.4.0, J.0, K.6.0

## Introduction

The present article seeks to assess the emergence of management information systems (MIS) as an academic field, and to report on progress toward the development of a cumulative tradition, as called for by Keen [15] at the First International Conference on Information Systems.

It does so by examining the emergence of MIS from three related foundational fields of study — computer science, management science, and organization science — using a conceptual framework suggested by Swanson [21]. The most recent five years of MIS publications (1980-1984) provide the basis for this examination.

A comprehensive review of the development of the MIS field is beyond the scope of the present paper. For excellent treatments of the full history, the reader is referred to Dickson [9] and Davis [7].

## MIS and Its Foundation

Most scholars would agree that MIS is, at present, an interdisciplinary field of endeavor. Davis [5] suggests, for example, that MIS represents the intersection of six fields of knowledge: computer science, behavioral science, decision science, organization and management, organizational function and management accounting. These fields draw upon diverse disciplinary bases in mathematics, engineering, and the social sciences.

More recently, Swanson [21] has argued that three fields of study constitute necessary foundations for MIS: computer science, management science, and organization science. Each focuses upon foundational elements held to be fundamental to the study of MIS [1, 8, 11, 12, 16].

Computer science focuses upon data, hardware, and software as basic elements of study. Data provide means by which the basic facts of any organization are established and employed. Software incorporates various important decisions made by the organization with respect to its data. In effect, absorbing the uncertainty associated with their processing. Hardware, in turn, establishes architectural parameters for data and software and provides the vehicle for organizational employment of both.

Management science attends to problems, models, and solvers. Information is classically understood to be of relevance only in a problem context. Models constitute the formal means of problem structuring and representation, thereby expressing relationships asserted to hold among the data of the organization. Solvers provide the computational technology by means of which models are addressed and explored.

Organization science studies individuals, organizations, and institutions. The study of individuals' behaviors and attitudes, in particular those of information systems users, are important to understanding a system's overall performance and acceptance. Organizational study is basic in that systems are ostensibly designed to serve organizational purposes or, in other words, to provide organizational functionality. Finally, institutional study is necessary in that no information system exists apart from the larger social context in which it is understood and accepted, and ultimately, because systems themselves are institutionalized.

From the point of view of this article, MIS has evolved, and continues to evolve, by addressing the integration of the foundational elements. Hamilton and Ives [12] reached a similar conclusion based on their study of knowledge utilization by MIS researchers in publications over the years 1970-1979.

While these elements, and the three fields with which they are associated, are held here to be necessary to the development of the MIS field, it is recognized that other fields and disciplines have contributed directly, as well as indirectly, to MIS development. For example, the field of economics has made important contributions $[18]$ . Similarly, political science has also been drawn upon in studies of computing in local governments $[4, 17]$ .

A useful view of MIS is to conceive of its growth from its foundational base as shown in Figure 1. The three foundational fields form a triangular base. Work wholly in one or the other of the three fields takes place at one of the vertices of the base. Work spanning two of the fields takes place along an edge of the triangle. Work spanning all three takes place at some internal point of the triangle.

MIS, as a distinct field of study, is seen as evolving from work contained within the foundational base, to work defining a new point lying above the plane of the base. That is, work that comes to be recognized as “uniquely MIS,” as opposed to simply crossing existing foundational fields. MIS thus “arrives” as a unique field of study to the extent it differentiates itself from its base by bringing a new dimension to the total pool of knowledge.

This research explores the extent to which MIS research is emerging as a distinct field of study. Bibliographic citation analysis of MIS research publications forms the basis of this exploration.

## MIS Work and Reference Points

Scholarly journals and conference proceedings constitute obvious vehicles for the location of MIS research. Both MIS and the three foundational fields are associated with publications of central importance to each, all of which disseminate MIS research. For present purposes, these publications may be regarded as representing the four work points defined in Figure 1.

Any published MIS article may thus be characterized by its work point (the field represented by the journal in which the article appears), and by its reference point (the distribution of the article's bibliographical references to journals at the same and other work points). As a consequence, the relative frequency with which MIS articles are published at different work points, and the corresponding distributions of reference points for these same articles, provide a useful basis for the location of the MIS field. This conceptualization of MIS research in terms of work and reference points distinguishes the present research from prior research [11, 12].

In choosing publications to represent the four work points in the present research, the authors employed several criteria. First, the publication was to be unambiguously associated, preferably by means of formal professional association, with one of the four fields of study. Second, the publication was to feature academic research. Third, the publication was to possess academic prestige within its field.

No attempt was made to be inclusive on these criteria, however. For this reason, the reader is cautioned that the choices were to some extent arbitrary, and reflect the biases of the authors.

## MIS

The MIS field is currently centrally represented by the MIS Quarterly (MISQ), and by the Proceedings of the International Conference on Information Systems (ICIS). The former, a joint publication of the Society for Information Management (SIM) and the Management Information Systems Research Center of the School of Management at the University of Minnesota, has been published since 1977. It is identified by Davis [7] as the leading journal in the MIS field. The ICIS Proceedings have been published annually since the first ICIS meeting in 1980 under the sponsorship of SIM, ACM and TIMS. Its growing prestige as the leading conference publication for MIS academics is documented by Couger [3]. Because conference proceedings do not generally suffer the publication lags common to journals, its inclusion also contributes to a more timely portrayal of research at the MIS work point.

## Computer Science

The field of computer science is centrally represented by the journal Communications of the ACM (CACM). This publication is distributed to the entire membership of the Association for Computing Machinery (ACM), and has been published monthly since 1958. It is ranked third among journals by MIS academics expressing their article submission preferences [11].

## Management Science

Management science is centrally represented by the journal of the same name, Management Science. It has been published monthly since 1955 by The Institute of Management Sciences (TIMS). As a reflection of its prestige, it has been ranked first among journals by MIS academics expressing their article submission preferences [11].

## Organization Science

The field of organization science is centrally represented by the Academy of Management Journal (AMJ), the Academy of Management Review (AMR), and by the Administrative Science Quarterly (ASQ). The two former have been published by the Academy of Management since 1958 and 1976, respectively. The latter is published by the Graduate School of Management at Cornell University, and has appeared regularly since 1956. None of the three is considered a major source of MIS articles [4, 11]; however, the academic prestige of all three journals is well recognized [2].

It is noteworthy that the three foundational fields are all represented by journals begun in the late 1950's. The central scholarly publications of MIS are more recent in origin, having been launched in the late 1970's.

The above publications are by no means exhaustive of the journals publishing MIS research. For example, Hamilton and Ives [11] include 37 major journals in their earlier published analysis of MIS journal stratification. However, their list includes a number of journals (e.g. Datamation, EDP Analyzer) which are not primary sources of research publications, as well as research journals (e.g. Accounting Review) from fields other than the four addressed here. For our purposes, we have taken a more exclusive view, focusing instead on a small number of academically-oriented journals and one conference proceedings which are unambiguously associated with the four work points represented, and which are top-rated by academics in terms of submission preferences [13]. We believe these journals to be representative of the most influential MIS research published at the four work points. Thus, while some may be concerned over the exclusion of certain publications from the present exploratory analysis, there should be little disagreement on those included.

## Analysis of Bibliographic Citations

Bibliographic citation analysis is one form of bibliometrics or quantitative bibliography [19]. Bibliometrics generally involves counting citations to other publications in a body of literature and using these counts to develop statistical distributions. The utility of bibliometrics as a research methodology is predicated on the assumption that bibliographic citations are an acceptable surrogate for the actual influence of prior works. In fact, much that is read is not cited, and citation behavior can be biased by the accessibility or inaccessibility of a particular item. Nonetheless, citations are a measure of scholarly dependence upon previous work.

As a form of measurement, bibliometrics is attractive because it is unobtrusive; analysis always occurs after a paper has been published and without any direct contact with the author. Further, because a scholarly publication is not merely a piece of work, but rather an expression of the state of research in a field at a point in time, bibliographic citations serve as surrogate measures of scholarly communication between authors, reflecting both the research published previously and the reference disciplines underlying the work [4]. Bibliometrics thus provides a relatively objective means for documenting interactions between MIS and its foundational fields.

Prior investigators of scholarly communication and knowledge utilization within the MIS field have employed three types of bibliometric techniques: journal inventories and citation counts [11, 12, 13, 22] and co-citation analysis [4]. For a more complete treatment of citation analysis, the reader is referred to Smith [20] or Garfield [10].

## Research Hypotheses

In exploring the question of whether MIS research is emerging as a distinct field of study four hypotheses were advanced.

H1: The reference point for any work point will lie nearer that work point than will the reference points associated with the other work points. In other words, references to a field of work are most likely to occur at the point of work. For example, references to the computer science literature are most likely to be found in a computer science publication. This concept is basic. Should the evidence not be consistent with the hypothesis, it would suggest that the work points in the present research are not empirically well represented.

H2: The reference distance for the MIS work point will be greater than the reference distances associated with the foundation work points. That is, work published at the MIS work point is more likely to draw from the foundation work points, than work published at a foundation work point is likely to draw from the MIS or other foundation work points. Substantiation of this hypothesis provides support for the proposition that MIS is less established than are the foundational fields.

H3: The reference distance for the MIS work point is gradually decreasing. MIS is emerging from its foundation as an established field of study.

H4: The reference points for the MIS and foundational work points are converging (while moving in the direction of the MIS work point). There is a growing consensus as to the body of MIS work held to be integral to the field.

## The Research Sample

A bibliographic citation analysis was thus conducted, the purpose of which was to locate the MIS field with respect to its foundations, employing the hypotheses advanced above. Recently (1980-84) published MIS articles from the six journals and one conference proceedings described above were selected, and their references to these same journals and proceedings were documented and tabulated.

In the selection of articles published at the MIS work point all articles published in the MIS Quarterly were included, as were all articles published in full in the ICIS Proceedings. Articles published in extended abstract form in the ICIS Proceedings were classified according to the journal in which they were ultimately published in full. (Selected articles accepted for ICIS are forwarded to MISQ, CACM, and Management Science for publication consideration, with the author's permission. The author thus exercises choice over the work point to be represented by the article.)

In the case of articles published in CACM, articles published as research contributions to the departments of Management of Computing; Human Aspects of Computing; Social Impacts of Computing; and Applications to Operations and Management were included and classified as representing MIS work at the computer science work point. Articles published in CACM under Reports and Articles were also included, if they were described (by means of subject descriptors, general terms, keywords and descriptors) as “management” related by their authors.

In representing MIS work at the management science work point, articles published in Management Science were included if they were published in the editorial department of Information Systems and Accounting, or in the editorial department of Organization Analysis, Performance and Design. In the case of the latter, specific references to information systems or information technology were required in the abstract or keyword classification to confirm the inclusion.

Articles published in the three organization science publications — ASQ, AMJ and AMR — are not published within editorial departments, nor are they self-indexed by their authors. It was thus necessary to review them individually for inclusion by subjective judgement. The present authors chose to review the articles independently, and to include an article only where there was agreement between the two judgements. The selected articles were considered to represent MIS work at the organization science work point. Clearly, a significant number of judgements were thus required of the authors in selecting the research sample. These must be borne in mind in interpreting the results described next.

## Results

Table 1 presents a tabulation of the articles selected as described above, including the counts of references to these same journals and proceedings. It is seen that a total of 271 MIS articles were selected, of which 172 (63.5%) were published in the two MIS publications, 51 (18.8%) appeared in CACM, 24 (8.9%) in Management Science, and 24 (8.9%) in the three organization science journals.

The selected articles produced a total of 1249 references to these same “core” publications. Of these, 296 (23.7%) were to the MIS publications; 150 (12.0%) were to CACM, 391 (31.3%) were to Management Science, and 412 (33.0%) were to the three organization science journals. This distribution is in sharp contrast to the distribution of publication frequencies described above (Chi-square statistic = 208.5; 3 d.f.; significant at the 0.001 level). The comparison is discussed further below.

The selected articles contained a mean of 27.9 references overall (s.d. = 19.1). References to the seven core publications averaged 4.6, 16.5% of the total.

Table 2 summarizes the reference points for the four journal groups, in terms of the average number of journal references to each of the same four groups. These four numbers thus identify the (average) reference point for each of the four work points. These reference points differ significantly across work points, as confirmed by the reported analysis of variance. Substantial differences in the average number of total core references (the sum of the references to the four publication groups) across the groups are also identified.

Hypothesis 1 is seen to be generally supported by the data of Table 2. The average number of references to a work point is greatest at that same work point, without exception. Further, all but two post hoc tests of relevant differences prove to be of statistical significance. Thus, evidence is provided consistent with the assumption that the work points selected are empirically well represented.

Hypothesis 2, central to the thesis of this paper, is also seen to be generally supported by the data of Table 2. Articles appearing in the MIS publications refer to these same publications for 32.0% of their core references. Articles published in CACM refer to CACM for 32.5% of their core references, articles published in Management Science refer to Management Science for 47.0% of their core references, and articles published in organization science journals refer to these same journals for 78.5% of their core references. This data is consistent with the proposition that MIS is less established than the foundational fields from which it is emerging (with the exception of computer science), though striking differences between the three foundational fields are themselves noteworthy.

A further examination of Hypothesis 2 is enabled by means of a formal discriminant analysis, whereby linear combinations of references to the four groups of publications are used to discriminate between publications in the same four groups. In short, reference points are employed to discriminate between work points. Results are shown in Table 3.

Two discriminant functions, both statistically significant, account for almost 97% of the explained variance in the discrimination exercise. Canonical correlations are moderately high in both cases, supporting the discriminatory utility of the two functions. The third function, which accounts for little more than 3% of the explained variance, is found not to be of substantial importance.

## Discussion

This paper examines the emergence of MIS as a scholarly field of study in terms of its relationship to three foundational fields: computer science, management science, and organization science. The results allow us to draw some tentative conclusions about the current state of the field, and to speculate on how the field may continue to evolve in the future.

The reader is cautioned that the present results may be strongly conditioned on the data selection procedures of the study, and in particular on the publications presented. It is possible that a broader pool of selected journals and proceedings would yield different findings.

It remains to be seen, too, whether MIS articles published at the foundation work points will similarly reflect this growing consensus, in terms of increased references to literature published at the MIS work point. As indicated by the data of Tables 4A-C, it is by no means clear that a convergence of reference points overall will occur. In fact, the opposite may prevail. As MIS becomes more established, it may instead tend to fractionate from its foundational base.

The difficulty is perhaps illustrated by MIS articles published at the organization science work point. Core references are most numerous at this work point (averaging over nine), but these consist substantially of references to the same point (averaging over seven). As a consequence, while the proportion of MIS articles at this work point is small (8.9%), citations to the literature at this same point are disproportionately large (41.0%). Thus, if one reads an MIS article in the organization science literature, one obtains particularly good access to this same literature. However, those already familiar with this literature obtain relatively poor access to the growing MIS literature.

In contrast, articles currently published at the MIS work point provide a relatively balanced access to the literature of the three foundational fields, in addition to the MIS field itself (a finding which differs from that of earlier studies [4, 12] which reported that MIS citations to the organizational science literature were disproportionately small). There is presently, however, little depth in this balanced access. Core references by the two MIS publications average only slightly more than four, and less than two of these are to the MIS work point itself.

In conclusion, both benefits and costs are associated with the establishment of a scholarly field of study. Indeed, it is in this sense that the emergence and establishment of any field may be understood to be problematic. On the one hand, the potential benefits are substantial; the emergence of a unique work point and the gradual shortening of its associated reference distance signal the intellectual unity of the field, and offer significant economies of communication among members of the research community. On the other hand, potential costs must also be considered; among these are the risks of premature independence, and eventual stagnation resulting from intellectual in-breeding and isolation.

In our estimation, the continued emergence of MIS over the longer term will probably prove to be no exception to this rule of cost-benefit trade-offs. In evaluating whether the benefits of emergence and establishment ultimately exceed the costs, we therefore suggest one guideline; we may look to see whether other fields, old or new, come to rely themselves, in part, upon MIS foundations.
