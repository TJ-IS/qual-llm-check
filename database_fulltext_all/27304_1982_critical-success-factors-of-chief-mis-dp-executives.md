# Critical Success Factors of Chief MIS/DP Executives

By: E.W. Martin

## Abstract

A critical success factor is a key area where things must go right if an organization is to be successful. In order to better understand the management of the MIS area, we explore the critical success factors of a group of chief MIS managers in successful organizations. Knowledge of these critical success factors is useful to top management in understanding how to cope with and evaluate the MIS organization. Critical success factors are also helpful to MIS managers in improving their own performance, in communicating with their boss and with those reporting to them, and in defining their personal information needs.

Keywords: Critical success factors, management of MIS

ACM Categories: 2.40, 3.50

## Introduction

Computers have been in use for about twenty-five years. They have been used by a number of organizations to significantly improve their performance. Yet, it is generally recognized that the typical organization is far from realizing the full potential benefits of the computer. One reason for this relative lack of success in using the computer is that management has not yet coped with the problems of managing the company's information processing activities. In order to provide a better understanding of how the information processing area might be better managed, this article explores the critical success factors of a group of chief Management Information Systems or Data Processing (MIS/DP) executives of sizable organizations from business and government.

The concept of a “Critical Success Factor” (CSF) was proposed by John F. Rockart of MIT as an approach to defining the information needs of top level managers [1]. According to Rockart:

Critical success facors...are...the limited number of areas in which results, if they are satisfactory, will insure successful competitive performance for the organization. They are the few key areas where "things must go right" for the business to flourish. If results in these areas are not adequate, the organization's efforts for the period will be less than desired.

As a result, the critical success factors are areas of activity that should receive constant and careful attention from management. The current status of performance in each area should be continually measured, and that information should be made available [1].

Although the concept of a CSF as presented above is associated with the chief executive of a company, it is equally applicable to any organization and to any managerial level within an organization in which multiple functions report to the manager.

It is obvious that an understanding of the areas that are critical to the success of an MIS/DP manager and of an MIS/DP organization can be most helpful to MIS/DP managers. What may not be quite so obvious is that an understanding of

MIS Quarterly/June 1982 these areas is equally important to top company management, for several of these critical areas are not under the control of the MIS/DP manager, but depend heavily upon top management of the company. Similarly, communication with and development of MIS subordinates would both be improved by more widespread knowledge and pursuit of critical success factors.

## Description of This Study

The sample in this study was composed of fifteen chief Management Information System or Data Processing (MIS/DP) executives of sizable business or governmental organizations. The chief MIS/DP executive was defined as the highest level executive in the organization that was directly responsible for the development and operation of the organization's computerized information or data processing services. Most of these MIS/DP executives were members of an informal advisory and support group for the MIS concentration in the Indiana University School of Business Graduate program. Thus, while many of the organizations had some involvement in the Midwest area of the U.S., several were international in scope, and most were national.

In Rockart's approach critical success factors and associated information measures for an executive are defined in a series of two or three interviews between the executive and an analyst [1]:

The actual CSF interviews are usually conducted in two or three separate sessions. In the first, the executive's goals are initially recorded and the CSF's that underlie the goals are discussed. The interrelationships of the CSF's and the goals are then talked about for further classification and for determination of which recorded CSF's should be combined, eliminated, or restated. An initial cut at measures is also taken in this first interview.

The second session is used to review the results of the first, after the analyst has had a chance to think about them and to suggest “sharpening up” some factors. In addition, measures and possible reports are discussed in depth. Sometimes, a third session may be necessary to obtain final agreement on the CSF measures-and-reporting sequence.

Because of the intensive participation by the interviewer in the above approach it appeared that the biases of the researcher might influence the outcome, and that the results of the first interviews might subconsciously influence the outcomes of the later ones. Since less obtrusive measures appeared to be desirable, this study used a questionnaire approach that asked each manager to write out a “first cut” set of CSF’s. Then these results for the group were summarized and returned to the managers, giving each manager an opportunity to review the initial CSF’s in light of the group response. Eight of the fifteen managers submitted a revised set of CSF’s, and five of these managers made significant revisions to their CSF’s.

Two questionnaires were used in this study. The first had three parts. Part one had forty-two questions exploring the characteristics of the MIS/DP managers, their MIS/DP organizations, and the parent organizations. Part 2 was intended to establish a context for consideration of the CSF's, and asked each manager to list the objectives of the MIS/DP organization. Part 3 concisely explained the concept of a critical success factor and asked the managers to briefly describe each of their CSF's. It was indicated that the number of CSF's to list was up to the manager and space was provided for eight responses. The second questionnaire asked the MIS/DP managers to list their revised CSF's.

In the following paragraphs the characteristics of the parent organizations, the MIS/DP organizations, and the MIS/DP managers are discussed based upon analysis of the data obtained from the first questionnaire.

## The parent organizations

The term “parent organization” refers to the organization served by the MIS/DP organization. In some cases this was the entire corporation, but in others it was a division or a plant. There was a wide variety of industries represented in this study, as indicated in Table 1.

The number of employees of the parent organization ranged from 2,100 to 95,000, including five relatively small (no more than 5000 employees) and ten relatively large (at least 18,000 employees) organizations.

Total yearly revenues of the parent organizations ranged from about 150 million to around 9.4 billion dollars. The smaller organizations averaged revenues of around \$290 million, while the larger ones averaged revenues over three billion dollars per year.

## The MIS/DP organizations

On the basis of the questionnaire responses, the MIS/DP organizations in this study appear to range from moderate to quite successful. They all appear to be in stage 3 (Formalization) or stage 4 (Maturity) according to Gibson and Nolan's stages of growth model [2]. All fifteen reported the use of Database Management software, but two-thirds of them were using database technology on less than 20% of their applications.

These MIS/DP organizations ranged in size from fifty-two to 770 employees. Their total budgets ranged from about 2.8 to 49.4 million dollars per year. In terms of their major computers, these organizations were predominately IBM equipped: eleven of them had at least one IBM mainframe, two had UNIVAC mainframes, and one each had CDC and Burroughs mainframes.

## The MIS/DP managers

The average age of the managers in the study was forty-eight, with the youngest thirty-five and the oldest sixty-four. Typically they had worked for the parent organization for about thirteen years, but the range was from one to forty years. The typical manager had occupied the present position for less than five years, with a range from two months to fifteen years. Only one had been hired directly into this position from outside the parent organization.

The educational backgrounds of these managers varied widely, from a two-year associate degree through five at bachelors, and six at the masters level with three doctoral degrees. Two managers reported additional bachelors degrees in Business, and two reported additional bachelors degrees in Engineering.

Their job histories were also quite varied. Seven managers had come up through the professional MIS/DP organization, four had most of their experience outside of the MIS/DP area, and four had had a mixture of significant work experience both inside and outside the MIS/DP area.

The titles of the positions held by these top MIS managers were remarkably consistent. There were three with the title of vice-president (of MIS, Management Information, and Operations). Seven held some variation of the title Director of Management Information Systems, two held the title of Director (or manager) of Management Systems, and there was one Director of the Data Processing Division. In the remainder of this article this position will be referred to as Director of MIS.

The three MIS/DP managers who had the title of vice president reported directly to the chief executive. However, the Director of MIS typically reported to a vice president, four reported to the Vice President for Administration, two reported to the Vice President for Finance, and two reported to other vice presidents.

## Analysis of the Critical Success Factors

The following analysis is based upon seven initial sets of CSF's obtained from the first questionnaire and eight revised sets of CSF's obtained from the second questionnaire. The number of CSF's reported by the managers ranged from three to nine, with an average of 5.9.

The objective of this analysis was to deduce the critical success factors that are characteristic of MIS/DP organizations from the personal CSF's reported by the fifteen managers in this study. A critical success factor is defined as an area in which things must go right for the manager to be successful. When analysis of the questionnaire responses began, it quickly became evident that areas can be broad and general, or narrow and specific. For example, different managers reported the following personal CSF's: "prioritization of development effort," "management of projects," and "develop quality application systems that meet user needs in a timely and cost effective manner." We concluded that each of these managers believe that the organization must be effective in the general area of system development, but that each of them reported different aspects of system development as the key to success in this general area. Thus, we have grouped the specific success factors as stated by the managers into more general areas of concern.

The general CSF's reported below were developed by a subjective trial and error process. Several colleagues participated in this process, but the final grouping of the managers' stated CSF's into broader organizational CSF's was done by the author on a judgmental basis. Also, the names given to the resulting areas reflect the judgment of the author.

This analysis leads the author to the conclusion that the first six of the following critical success factors characterize most MIS/DP organizations, and that the seventh is important to many. In other words, if the typical MIS/DP organization is to be successful, things must go well in the following areas:

1. system development

2. data processing operations

3. human resource development

4. management control of the MIS/DP organization

5. relationships with the management of the parent organization

6. support of the objectives and priorities of the parent organization

7. management of change

## System Development

The management of system development projects has often been quite a problem, with large cost and time overruns being frequently reported. Thus it is not surprising that fourteen out of fifteen managers indicated that this area is crucial to success. The specific concerns expressed in this area included project selection, effective project management, ability to respond effectively to user needs within a reasonable time frame, and the development of reliable, timely, and cost effective application systems.

## Data Processing Operations

It is obvious that success of the MIS/DP organization depends upon successfully delivering data processing services. There is nothing more debilitating than continual errors, late reports, or online systems that are “always down when you need to use them.” Since thirteen of the fifteen managers indicated critical success factors in the area of effective data processing services, it may be that only a few of the managers currently feel completely secure in this area.

The transition from a batch-oriented, off-line data processing system to an online, communications oriented system produces a host of new problems and challenges in the area of data processing operations. Many of the specific CSF's reported concern for system availability and response time. It may also be of interest that four of the managers indicated that the area of security — protection of the data and data processing resources of the organization — is crucial to their success.

## Human Resource Development

This area includes the recruitment, career development, and retention of the technical and managerial personnel in the MIS/DP organization. It is not surprising that eleven of the fifteen managers indicated this as a critical area, for there is a significant shortage of qualified MIS/DP personnel. For example, in late 1979 it was reported that there was a 17% to 27% shortage in many MIS/DP specialities, and that this is resulting in a turnover rate of up to 34% per year [3]. Thus the typical application programmer only stays with the organization for three years, and since it takes several months for a person to become effective in a new organization, this rate of turnover causes significant productivity problems.

The importance of a highly qualified professional staff was mentioned frequently, and the importance of training in maintaining quality staff was emphasized. Two managers emphasized the importance of the MIS/DP department being a source of outstanding people for other areas within the company. There were also several mentions of the importance of MIS/DP staff morale, and one manager felt that it was crucial to him to meet his affirmative action goals.

## Management Control of the MIS/DP Organization

This is an area that overlaps with others, for both Systems Development and Data Processing Operations include areas of management control. However, eight of the fifteen managers included CSF's that were oriented toward management control of the entire MIS/DP organization, and several other managers expressed management control concerns separately in both systems development and data processing operations. This represents a substantial majority that consider management control as critical to success.

Among the specifics cited were concerns with planning, adherence to budgetary controls, standardization of policies and procedures, and control of costs. Again, it is worth noting that MIS/DP activities have been difficult to manage and control in the past, and apparently this continues to be a problem.

## Relationships with the Management of the Parent Organization

There is wide agreement among the participants in this study on the importance of good relationships with the management of the parent organization. Nine of the managers expressed a concern for relationships with users, including the importance of user satisfaction and of user involvement in system development. Five of the managers expressed the importance of good relationships with top management, usually in the form of the need for support from the active involvement of top management. (Three managers mentioned both the users and top management.)

Anyone who has visited a number of organizations has likely encountered instances of poor relationships between the MIS/DP organization and the parent organization. Given the natural sources of conflict between the MIS/DP organization and the parent organization, even good relationships may require constant maintenance. Thus the Director of MIS may devote considerable effort to the resolution of problems between the MIS/DP organization and parent organization managers.

## Support of the Objectives and Priorities of the Parent Organization

One-third of the managers mentioned the importance of aligning the MIS/DP priorities so that they support the priorities of the parent organization. As mentioned in the discussion of the System Development CSF, other managers were concerned with development of application systems that serve the parent organization's needs and that project selection be determined by needs of the parent organization. Other managers indicated that the MIS/DP organization must be aware of and responsive to user priorities. Thus, there is a solid majority who indicated that support of the objectives and priorities of the parent organization is crucial to MIS/DP success.

Problems in this area are not surprising when one considers that the MIS/DP organization is composed of individuals that may have stronger loyalties to their profession than they do to their employer, especially in light of the high turnover and short tenure with a given company. Nevertheless, it is clearly the responsibility of the Director of MIS to ensure that the MIS/DP organization supports the objectives and priorities of the parent organization. What may not be so obvious, however, is that support of the parent organization's priorities involves more than the will or intention to do so. It also requires mechanisms for determining and expressing these objectives and priorities.

The allocation of resources to major system development projects is an area where it is important that decisions be made in accordance with the priorities of the parent organization. The organizations in this study used a variety of mechanisms for allocating system development resources, most of which involve high level management in these decisions. Five organizations used top level steering committees, three used the budgeting process, and three used the business planning process. Thus eleven of the fifteen organizations involved high level management in these decisions via steering committees, the budgetary process, or the business planning process. Only one-third of the organizations in this study charged system development costs to the users.

In addition to the above critical success factors that were mentioned by a heavy majority, this study revealed the following area that is viewed as critical to success by a substantial minority of the managers.

## Management of Change

Forty percent of the managers mentioned this area which involves long range technological planning in order to take advantage of technological change in such a way as to make it as non-disruptive as possible. This has overlaps with several of the other CSF's.

In addition to the above seven factors that appear to be broadly applicable to MIS/DP organizations, three areas appeared critical to more than one manager: data handled as a corporate resource, increased usage of common systems, and sensitivity to people's needs.

## Implications

The above critical success factors are not surprising. By their nature, a manager's personal CSF's are likely to be immediate rather than long range concerns. Like most managers, MIS/DP managers are probably more involved with "fighting off the alligators than with draining the swamp," which probably explains why "management of change" was mentioned less frequently than the others.

Some areas that were not emphasized should perhaps be noted, given the attention that they have received in the literature. For example, little concern for organizational matters was expressed, with only one manager indicating that this was critical to his success. It appears that the location of MIS/DP within these organizations is satisfactory.

Only two managers indicated that “Data Treated as a Corporate Resource” was a CSF, despite the attention in the literature to the importance of this matter when employing the database approach. Since only one-third of the companies in the sample are using database technology for more than 20% of their applications, this may only indicate that these companies have not yet realized the importance of this area, or are in a transition period.

Several years ago, there was great emphasis on the importance of top management support to MIS/DP success, but only one-third of the managers mentioned this in the study. “Top management support” was roughly complementary to “support of the priorities of the parent organization” in that all but one of the managers mentioned one or the other of them, but only two mentioned both. Perhaps the majority recognize that the primary consideration is the MIS/DP organization’s support of the priorities of the parent organization, for an MIS/DP organization that favorably affects the performance of the parent organization will have the support of top management, while an ineffective MIS/DP organization is not likely to have top management support for long! It appears that two-thirds of the MIS Directors in this study have sufficiently strong support from top management so they are not concerned with this area.

## Value to top management

Top management has experienced some difficulty coping with the MIS/DP area. These critical success factors of the MIS/DP organization provide a framework for concentrating top management attention on the things that really matter in MIS/DP. For example, the sixth CSF reveals that top management must provide an effective means for expressing organizational priorities so that MIS/DP decisions can support them. CSF number 7 indicates the importance of long range planning in the management of change. CSF number 3 emphasizes the importance of acquiring and keeping outstanding MIS/DP people in an exceptionally competitive market. CSF number 4 points out the importance of good management as well as good technical people in the MIS/DP area.

Furthermore, these CSF's provide a framework for top management to evaluate the performance of the MIS/DP area and the Director of MIS. Each CSF provides an area where evaluative questions should be asked. For example, in the area of support of the objectives and priorities of the parent organization, one might ask:

Do your present systems really contribute to the profitability of your company?

Is the MIS/DP organization responsive to the needs of its users?

Do you decide which new systems to develop on the basis of their contribution to company performance?

How do you assure that MIS/DP resources are allocated in accordance with overall company priorities?

Similar sets of questions could be asked in each of the CSF areas.

## Value to the Director of MIS

Knowledge of these general CSF's is quite useful to the Directors of MIS by providing them with a framework for validating or testing their own personal CSF's. They may also be helpful in developing the Directors' own sets of CSF's, for the MIS/DP manager may reverse the process of development of these general factors by asking for each of them: "What are the key areas that insure my success in this general area?" For example, Directors of MIS might ask "What are the keys to success in system development for our organization?"

Being aware of personal critical success factors can be important to the Directors of MIS, for this focuses their attention on those areas that are important to their success. Each manager has so many demands on time and attention that some mechanism may be needed that reminds the manager to tend to the things that are important.

The Director of MIS's CSF's may also be used as a framework for planning and communication within the MIS/DP organization. Several of the managers who participated in this study have indicated that they are using their CSF's to communicate with the managers who report to them, and that they then plan together for the attainment of satisfactory performance in each CSF area.

Similarly, these CSF's can serve as a framework for communication between top management of the company and the Director of MIS. Not only can the Director of MIS obtain a valuable critique of the adequacy of the department's CSF's from the viewpoint of top management, but top management can also thereby obtain a better understanding of the MIS/DP area.

Finally, as was mentioned in the introduction, the concept of a critical success factor can be used to define the information needs of a manager. Thus the CSF's of the Director of MIS can be used to define personal information needs. One might surmise that some MIS/DP managers have been so busy satisfying the information needs of others that they may not have given much thought to their own information needs. Furthermore, if MIS/DP managers want to use the CSF approach to defining the information needs of their top management, using this approach on themselves is a good way to gain experience with it before trying it out on their bosses!

## Summary

This study of the critical success factors of the chief MIS/DP executives of fifteen sizable organizations reveals the following six areas that are crucial to the success of most MIS/DP organizations.

1. system development,

2. data processing operations,

3. human resource development,

4. management control of the MIS/DP organization,

5. relationships with the management of the parent organization, and

6. support of the objectives and priorities of the parent organization.

A seventh area, management of change, was reported to be crucial to success by a substantial proportion of MIS/DP managers.

These CSF's are useful to top management in understanding how to cope with and in evaluating the MIS/DP organization. The Directors of MIS's CSF's are also helpful in improving their own performance, in communicating with their bosses and with those who report to them, and in defining their personal information needs.
