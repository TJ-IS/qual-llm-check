# Information Technology in the 1990s: A Long Range Planning Scenario

By: Robert I. Benjamin

## Abstract

This article describes a reasonable scenario for information technology, and its use within a major organization in the year 1990. The scenario is based upon a model used at Xerox to portray the use of Information Systems (IS) internally within the corporation in the year 1990. Assumptions are made about the technology and economics, and by coupling these with observed trend lines from historical data, predictions about the 1990 IS organization, technology, investment requirements, and support structure are drawn. Estimates are developed for the extent of distributed processing at four levels of the organizational hierarchy. Finally, a number of conclusions which deal with the changing nature of the IS technology and role of IS management are described.

Keywords: Management information systems, information systems technology, information systems strategic planning, cost performance, technology trends, operational computing, end user computing, functional architecture, office systems, applications software, distributed processing

ACM Categories: 2, 2.4, 3.5, 4.0, 8.1

## Introduction

The genesis for this article was a futures planning study of the internal information systems environment within Xerox Corporation. The study was done to provide a better understanding of the relationship between centralized and distributed processing so as to set down some policy guidelines for internal systems development and operation. The study, when completed, provided some strong insights as to the potential degree of distributed processing that would be experienced. It also provided some useful insights into the need for integrated networks, and of the likelihood of Information Systems increasing its share of the corporate expense base.

## Methodology and Overview

The dramatic improvements in technology cost/performance coupled with rising salary inflation have produced an Information Systems (IS) environment in major organizations that is in a considerable state of flux. Senior management and the IS manager will encounter many pitfalls in planning and managing the IS function during the '80s. An end point scenario for the IS environment for the year 1990, which contains a number of business related models of the future technology, provides some key landmarks to focus major planning strategies.

This article will present a methodology for developing an environmental strategic plan for the Information Systems (IS) function. The primary objective of the methodology is to understand the effect of long term technology trends on Information Systems (IS) Management and on the organization. It does this by making assumptions about the technology and key business factors, and by coupling these with observed trend lines from historical data, some conclusions about the IS organization, technology, and support structure are drawn. Some examples from a 1990 IS Strategic Planning Technology Forecast done by Xerox Corporate Information Management staff will be used to clarify the methodology and the resulting scenario. The Xerox data has been generalized into that of a typical large manufacturing corporation for the purpose of this article.

The major segments of the article are as follows:

1. Basic technology assumptions are described.

2. Predictions are derived for the use of IS technology in the organization.

3. Projections, economic and descriptive, are made for a number of the key segments of the IS environment.

4. Conclusions are drawn about the changing nature of IS technology, and the management of IS at the end of the decade.

## Technology Assumptions

First Assumption — The cost/performance of the hard technologies in support of Information Systems, (processing logic and storage devices) will follow the 30% to 40% annual improvement rate of the '60s and '70s in the decade of the '80s [6]. This trend coupled with the anticipated high salary inflation rate of 10% for the '80s will produce a radically different set of economics for information processing by 1990.

Several examples will illustrate the nature of what lies ahead.

1. Where today a typical large commercial processing system (IBM's 3033 for example) requires extensive justification for installation, the equivalent processing power in 1990 will cost dramatically less to purchase and will be a minor justification burden. This is shown in Figure 1. It shows processor capacity (using the IBM 3033 as the base line, and using millions of instructions per second (MIPS) as the measure of capacity), processor cost, and “payout ratio” for 1970, 1980, and 1990. “Payout ratio” is an index used to relate the effect of salary inflation and technology cost performance. It is calculated by dividing the cost of a particular technology (in this case processors) by the cost of a person's salary. The index then represents the amount of people that would have to be replaced in one year, or not hired, to pay for that piece of technology in the one year period. The payout ratios for the three decade points are 4800, 210, and 2-8 respectively.

It should be noted that the processor cost described in Figure 1 is for what is typically considered a fully configured system. Thus the growth in capacity (MIPS) is accompanied by growth in speed and size of direct and mass storage, and by channel capacity.

2. Where today's technology revolution has been fueled by the power of 8 bit microprocessors, essentially equivalent to an IBM 1401 computer of 1962 on a chip, the technology revolution of this decade will be powered by 32 bit microprocessors, which will be essentially equivalent to something between an IBM 4341 and an IBM 3033 on a chip.

3. Where today's terminal in wide usage at best approaches the functionality of an Apple personal computer, the terminal of 1990 will be powered by a 32 bit microprocessor and will have many functions not considered affordable, and some not thought of, in today's low priced terminals. The cost of today's terminal will be little different than that of the 1990's work horse terminal despite all the increased functionality and power. Figure 2 shows a composite cost for the supported terminal in 1980 and 1990. The elements of cost include communications network, large processor (including operations cost), as well as the cost of the terminal. The assumptions behind this data are presented in the appendix to this article.

The above examples vividly describe the changing IS economics. It should be noted that with today's economics the demand for terminals is massive. At 1990 economics the cost of the terminal approaches that of the telephone when measured in terms of relative cost to salary. Given its increased functionality at that time, it is reasonable to assume that terminals will become as common as telephones in the 1990 office.

Second Assumption — Data communication costs in the U.S. will decline on a unit cost basis, in the 1980s at a rate about equal to inflation. This will be true over the decade with some rapid price rise early in the '80s as the data communications industry restructures itself. Outside of the U.S. data communications costs will rise more rapidly, as the national telephone and telegraph monopolies tend to subsidize other services, mainly speech related, through their newer data communications offerings.

Given the vastly increased traffic that can be foreseen as online terminal services spread through organizations, data communications will become a dominant factor in the IS cost structure.

Third Assumption — Steady improvements in all aspects of software will continue to be made. However the demand for software is so great as to appear infinite and the improvements will be perceived as having little effect on the problem. Improvements in software production will come from working many approaches rather than any one miracle cure. The major approaches to software improvement that are anticipated to make a significant impact during the decade are:

## Derived Systems Predictions

First Prediction — The installed processing base within the organization will continue to expand at least at the historical rate of the '70s. Historical records were available for one major component of the Xerox business. They indicated that the growth in installed MIPS was "20 times" for the decade of the '70s. This growth can be analyzed in terms of two planning components:

\- Operational Computing — the basic business transaction processes of the business.

\- End User Computing — a difficult term to define with great clarity but heuristically definable in terms of financial modeling, file retrieval and analysis, engineering modeling, use of outside databases, etc., all with the user in direct interactive control of the computer session.

Growth of each of these components is shown in Figure 3. Additionally Figure 3 shows the derivation of actual cost/performance for 1970 to 1980. This is done by converting the MIPS for the beginning and ending years to an equivalent number of processors of a popular sales configuration. Purchase values are established from 1970 and 1980 pricing, and growth rates are determined for both processing capacity and cost, giving a resultant ten times improvement, or cost effectiveness. This provides reinforcement for the prediction for cost performance for 1990 in Figure 1. It would be reasonable to assume the cost performance to at least continue at the ten times rate for the '80s as the large element of software cost which is included in the '70s cost and pricing is gradually being unbundled and priced separately.

It is particularly significant to observe the growth in end user computing from an insignificant amount of the processor demand to where it consumed 40% of the cycles in 1980. $^{1}$

Second Prediction — Operational Computing will expand at least at the rate of growth of the '70s. It can be argued that the basic operational systems have all been implemented, and that therefore we should not be significantly expanding the processing to support them. However, the author believes a more plausible argument is that the operational systems will have to be reimplemented at least once during the '80s to maintain the cost/competitive postures of the organizations they support. These reimplementations will be database, online oriented, and will be carefully human engineered to support and improve the quality and productivity of the operational processes through added functionality. These requirements will consume a great number of computer cycles and will cause the continued rate of expansion of the operational computing load.

Third Prediction — The location of databases and processing power within the organization will spread from today's relatively centralized construct to one that is much more distributed. The model suggested for 1990 is that processing and databases will be distributed to the lowest level of the organization possible, subject to two constraints. They are:

1. Data will be stored at a higher level of the organization only if the need to integrate that data above the lower organizational hierarchy levels exists. This is described in Figure 4. It shows a partial set of applications in support of the product development and engineering side of the organization (the relationship to the marketing organization is excluded to keep the discussion relatively simple), and attempts to demonstrate a positioning of databases and processing in terms of the organization levels and need for integration. Following the schema, it shows a need for integration of data from the three major organizational components, which can only be resolved at an organizational level above them, (or by delegation of the database responsibility to one of the organizations). The organization in charge of worldwide manufacturing must similarly integrate a database of elements from the units below it. This slotting of the applications, processing, and databases in terms of organizational levels is referred to in this article as a “functional architecture.”

2. The applications at the lower levels of the organization must be designed so that they will be free of any data center operations content. An example of what not to do would be to require the people working at a sales branch to perform complex set up and backup/recovery procedures. This is because the economics of distributed processing will evaporate if high labor content is required to operate computers at the lower levels, and data center operations is a foreign culture for the people at the lower levels of the organization.

To summarize, the economics that drive the distributed processing prediction are as follows:

\- processing power will be equivalently cheap wherever it is put,

\- communications cost will represent the only significant cost variable and will always favor distribution of processing and databases, and

\- lower level applications will contain a minimum, if any, of DP Operations labor cost.

The “Functional Architecture” methodology just described permits a description and quantification of the lowest possible level of distribution of computing in the organization. In reality, several factors will constrain the ideal Functional Architecture — organizational politics and culture [4], integration with existing systems, and the time frame when the economics become self evident. By describing each application in terms of its place in the functional architecture and projecting volumes of growth related to business long range plan data, estimates can be made of the amount of processing and database required for a particular functional architecture. Figure 5 describes the metrics of the Functional Architecture for one business unit for the year 1990. Of particular interest is the large number of processors, and the size of the database at the lower two levels of the organizational hierarchy. Because the number of MIPS required to support operational computing is so large, they prove difficult to relate to. Thus the metric used to describe processing requirements in the rest of this article is “number of equivalent IBM 3033s at 4.5 MIPS each.

Fourth Prediction — The end user will dominate the use of computing cycles. Figure 3, discussed earlier in the article, showed that the growth of end user computing went from insignificant to nearly 40% of the cycles in the '70s. This growth rate, particularly with the effect of office systems not yet felt, will continue for some time. (For the purpose of extrapolating the amount of computing required in 1990 later in the article the author makes the assumption that end user computing will require 75% of the available cycles in 1990. This would represent some flattening out of the end user computing growth curve by the end of the decade).

Fifth Prediction — The distinction between office systems and end user computing will disappear by the end of the decade. The terminal will be ubiquitous in the office, and will be multi functional — able to support data processing, end user and office system tasks. This is shown in Figure 6 where a list of generic functions that can be performed on a terminal are associated with the major groups of workers in the organization.

Sixth Prediction — The terminal will be as common as the telephone in the office. Thus networks connecting as many as 100,000 terminals within a large organization to many computers will have to be constructed. $^{2}$

## The characteristics of applications

Figure 7 describes changes in the nature of applications that have occurred or can be reasonably anticipated for the three decades.

During the early '70s significant effort was put into cleaning up the basic operational systems for the subfunctions of the business such as invoicing, payroll, and general ledger accounting. By the middle of the decade the emphasis had shifted in many organizations to the development of fully integrated systems for the function. An example of a set of fully integrated systems for a function is the payroll, personnel record keeping, and benefits management systems that comprise an integrated set of personnel systems. During the '80s the integration of the functional systems will generally be completed and emphasis will be turned to the integration of systems across functions [3], as was shown in Figure 4 previously for the product development functions.

The scope of the applications has moved since the early days of computing from the most basic transaction systems to the complete set of operational systems, to decision systems, and finally some emphasis is being given to the strategic planning systems. This movement both reflects a continued ability to deal with less structured problems over time and an emphasis on systems that apparently coincides with a movement upward in Anthony's description of levels of management planning and control systems [1].

## Terminals

Figure 8 projects the number of terminals in the organization as a function of the number of telephones in the organization, noting the rather high proportion of terminals to telephones today, when the economics of the new technology is only beginning to be felt. It also shows the investment needed at the end of the decade to support the terminal population. Several points need to be made with respect to Figure 7. First, there have been a number of predictions in the trade press that the telephone and the terminal will be integrated as essentially one personal support device. This prediction will be realized at some level within the decade, however, the number of terminals, whether they are integrated with telephones or not, will still be very high, and will require substantial investments. Second, there will be more terminals than telephones in a large organization in 1990, since many professionals will use portable terminals as well as terminals at home.

## Application development

A number of experts have concluded that the largest constraint on the success of the IS function for this decade will be the inability to deliver the software required for the new applications. To dramatically improve application development great strides will have to be made in software productivity. This will be accomplished not through any panacea such as a miraculous new language, but rather through the applications of many methodologies to solve the problem. ## Information systems function costs

A cost profile for 1990 was established making use of the capital requirements described in the preceding sections. Figure 11 shows the change in cost structure of IS from 1980 to 1990 and the relative cost increase. $^{3}$ It is interesting to note the increasing importance of data communications in the cost structure. The model assumed that communications volumes and costs would rise in proportion to the growth in the number of terminals. A more recent study [5] indicates that the effect of adding electronic messaging, document distribution, and other new services, will nearly double the communications costs expressed in this scenario. It is also significant to note that both DP Operations and Telecommunications reflect technology cost performance improvements, whereas Systems and Programming are essentially labor intensive and show little cost performance improvement in this model.

Figure 11 also allocates the costs to end user/office systems, and operational computing, showing that by the end of the decade they will be 1/3 of the cost base, or a 50% increase over traditional percent to revenue IS measures, which have been almost entirely composed of operational computing costs. If the model is correct, then a company which historically has bounded its computing at the 2% of revenue rate, would be operating in the range of 3% by 1990.

## Conclusions

The scenario suggests several conclusions that appear to be applicable to large organizations. They are described below.

Both the functional architecture described, and the rapid growth in number of terminals illustrate this dynamic. To accommodate this without losing control there will have to be greater emphasis on centralized control of IS policy and standards, technical and functional architectures, and network management. ## Conclusion 2

The combination of office/end user systems will substantially increase IS spending as a percent of revenue. The growth will be difficult if not impossible to contain due to the economics of the new technology. However it will be very difficult to justify to senior managements this increment in costs, and to assure them that it reflects greater productivity or improved effectiveness. Figure 13 shows how operational computing was essentially equivalent to the historical IS cost line during the

1970s. The significant growth in IS expense in the 1980s is in the component above the operational computing line which is made up of end user and office information systems costs.

It also shows a “payout ratio” which is the total cost of IS divided by the cost of an employee. In essence, the “payout ratio” says that the IS function would at a minimum be paying for itself in any year if it had replaced, or avoided hiring the number of people described in the ratio, over the number of years IS has been in existence. From 1975 to 1980 the payout ratio indicates that IS needed to replace or cost avoid 1700 employees to pay for itself, and from 1980 to 1990 would have to replace or avoid hiring almost 5000 people. It should be emphasized that this simplification of the IS benefits rationale does not take into account the many other tangible and intangible benefits that should be achieved in this time frame. It does however provide a minimum benefits target which demonstrates that IS is at least paying for itself.

## Conclusion 3

The rapid increase in computers (40% of processing capacity today and predicted to be 75% in 1990) devoted to end user computing will cause IS management to develop policy and control mechanisms for the management of the demand for its services, rather than the traditional policy and controls which have been concerned with managing the supply of computing services. Although it is clear this movement is taking place it is not at all understood what models should be followed for managing IS in the demand mode.

## Conclusion 4

The primary added value of centralized IS utility services will be in providing interconnectability to the organization. This will be reflected in the movement of emphasis for the large DP utilities within corporations from managing computer cycles to managing networks and the interconnection of the corporation.

## Conclusion 5

Organizational frameworks will have to be developed to encourage the development of applications that provide the necessary integration across the business functions. Today it is very difficult to either find an executive responsible, or with an inclination to pursue inter-functionally integrated applications.

## Conclusion 6

A major mission for the IS function in this decade will be to increase productivity of application development. Its inherent inefficiency has been tolerated in the past decade, but the demand pressures, and the cost and time frames for new applications will so severely impact organizational objectives, that major efforts will be initiated to improve productivity.
