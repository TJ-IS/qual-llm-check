# DSS Series Editor's Note

Stimulated by the development of low cost powerful micro computers, personal computing promises to be an important part of the Decision Support Systems movement. The authors of this article identify several potential dangers, however, and suggest organizational policies for avoiding them.

Ralph H. Sprague, Jr.

# Organizational Strategies For Personal Computing In Decision Support Systems\*

By: Richard D. Hackathorn
Peter G. W. Keen

## Abstract

This article suggests a strategy for using Personal Computing to provide decision support to individuals within an organizational context. The growing phenomenon of personal computing is recognized as a valid technological base for providing decision support to individuals. However, the challenge is the exploitation of this capability within an organizational context. In particular, personal computing raises many management control issues. The article suggests a useful distinction between personal decision support and organizational decision support. This distinction is critical in formulating a strategy for personal support within the organizational context.

Keywords: Decision support systems, personal computing, organizational theory

ACM Categories: 3.50

## Phenomenon of Personal Computing

Decision Support Systems (DSS) are interactive computer based aids designed to assist managers in complex tasks requiring human judgment. The aim of such systems is to support and improve a decision process.

Early implementations of DSS were constructed using interactive timesharing on large-scale computer systems $[13]$ . These developments were largely stimulated by MIT's Project MAC, which provided the individual user access to computing power for the first time. The technique of timesharing pointed toward a human-machine symbiosis that promised vast increases in our ability to handle complex problems $[10]$ .

More recently, small-scale “personal computer systems,” such as the APPLE II computer, have been used directly by both technical and nontechnical individuals for a wide variety of information processing activities. With these small-scale systems, the user can exercise direct, personal control over all aspects of the technology [5]. These aspects include: equipment selection, software selection, software development, customization of applications, and data management. The emphasis is on the end user of the technology, acting as programmer, analyst, etc. — without role differentiation.

With the advent of personal computing, a different relationship is forming between the user and the technology. These differences occur both in the system usage and in the system development process. The growing body of conceptual and empirical work in the DSS area is relevant to understanding the impacts caused by personal computing. This article explores these differences by starting with the concepts of Decision Support that address issues of how individuals can make effective use of computer technology.

## Using Personal Computing for Decision Support

A coherent conceptual framework for Decision Support is emerging [8, 14]. Practitioners are extending the conceptual work of researchers by building innovative systems to support a range of managerial tasks. There are many case studies of DSS usage in organizations [1, 9].

Most of the conceptual literature on DSS has emphasized the individual manager. Its theoretical base comes from cognitive psychology; its focus is on human problem solving rather than organizational decision making. Common phrases in DSS literature tend to be: cognitive style, individual differences, information overload, heuristic problem solving, etc. Terms such as information flows and organizational environments are usually absent. On the other hand, it is clear that practitioners are far more concerned with organizational issues. The systems that they need are built around activities that involve planning and coordination. Therefore, while DSS theory prescribes a system to support a manager's budget decision, the DSS practitioner is building a system to support the organization's budget process.

Another issue is the requirement for a detailed understanding of the manager's decision making process prior to building the DSS. Unlike Operations Research, Management Science, and most of traditional Management Information Systems, Decision Support is based on descriptive paradigms of decision making, rather than prescriptive or rationalistic ones. An axiom of Decision Support has been that a DSS builder cannot improve the decision making of the manager unless the builder has a detailed understanding of that decision making. However, Personal Computing provides the opportunity for a manager to directly use the technology for Decision Support in a natural way, not dependent on another's notions of the decision making process.

## The Challenge of Personal Computing

At its simplest level, Personal Computing can be viewed as a hobby [7, 12]. It is true that a significant percentage of sales of small-scale computers are to individuals rather than corporations, for entertainment rather than to perform some utilitarian function. Another portion is also to individuals but is used for performing a specific task, much in the same mode as a pocket calculator. These individuals could be performing tasks within the home or within a self-employed situation. In all of these cases, the use of Personal Computing has been very successful, especially when analyzed from a cost/performance standpoint.

Personal Computing has been driven by technological developments, and hence, has lacked a clear direction in its evolution. Decision Support can make several contributions to strengthening the directions of Personal Computing:

1. Application — What to do with the technology? Decision Support research contributes a clear distinction between automation and support that eliminates much of the users' resistance to the idea of using computer technology.

2. Development — How to apply the technology? Decision Support research contributes an adaptive or evolutionary approach to system development, emphasis on dialogue as the interface between the user and system, and attention to the learning processes of the user about the problem domain.

3. Integration — How to combine with other kinds of support technology? Decision Support research has made progress in merging concepts from MS/OR and traditional MIS, such as the use of data plus model management.

The challenge of using Personal Computing comes when it is used within an organizational context. On the positive side, Personal Computing can produce increases in productivity. It is an effective technology to aid thinking and communication. There are commercially available software packages, such as VisiCalc from Personal Software, Inc., that can be used as very effective DSS tools. Personal Computing has started to integrate and enhance many existing technologies to aid thinking — such as the telephone for communications, adding machine for calculation, dictionary for standards, and copier for memory.

On the negative side of using Personal Computing in organizations are several management control issues within the organizational context. First, there is the data integrity of the “corporate” data. Secondly, there is the certification of reports produced by personal systems as to whether they are based on valid corporate data. Thirdly, there is the coordination of information sources and uses.

## Dependencies Among Tasks

To understand the role of Personal Computing, let us extend a frequently cited framework for DSS described by Gorry and Scott Morton [4]. This framework combines Simon's dimension of task structuredness (i.e., structured, semi-structured, and unstructured) with Anthony's classification of managerial activities (i.e., operational control, management control, and strategic planning). The framework does not accommodate notions of interactions among persons within an organizational context.

This article suggests that a third dimension — task interdependency — be added to the Gorry and Scott Morton framework. This new framework is illustrated in Figure 1. The dimension of task interdependency is best described by J. D. Thompson [15] in which he differentiates among three types of interdependencies:

1. Independent — A task is independent if a person can perform the task without interaction with other persons.

2. Pooled — A task is pooled if two or more people must interact in order to perform their respective tasks.

Figure 1. DSS Framework with Task Interdependency

3. Sequential — A task is sequential if several people can only perform their respective tasks in a certain sequence in which the outputs of one task must be used as the inputs to another task.

Note that this classification forms three levels of increasing task interdependence in terms of flexibility of interaction. In other words, independent tasks have more flexibility than pooled tasks, and pooled tasks have more flexibility than sequential tasks.

Using the Thompson dimension, the nature of Decision Support can be separated into three distinct, but interrelated, components:

1. Personal Support focuses on a user or class of users in a discrete task or decision (e.g., setting a price, selecting a stock) that is relatively independent of other tasks.

2. Group Support focuses on a group of individuals, each of whom are engaged in separate, but highly interrelated, tasks (e.g., office activities).

3. Organizational Support focuses on an organizational task or activity involving a sequence of operations and actors (e.g., developing a divisional marketing plan, capital budgeting).

When the task is independent, the problem solving activities do not involve interdependencies and the manager can reach a decision through private analysis, though when the choice has been made, it must be communicated to others. In this situation, Personal Support (PS) may improve the quality of the decision by a system designed for direct and individual use by the manager.

Pooled interdependency requires face-to-face negotiations and discussions. No single unit can carry out its work without interaction with others. This suggests an additional type of support — Group Support (GS). This allows users to develop joint plans through a system that provides fast response, easy exploration of alternatives, and explanation of analysis.

When the task involves sequential interdependencies, each individual's activities must mesh closely with other people's. Any computer based aid will be used as much for a vehicle for communication and coordination as for problem solving. For example, Organizational Support (OS) for budgeting will need to provide facilities for storing disaggregated, detailed budgets at the department level, then allowing these to be integrated first by division and finally for the company as a whole.

The benefit of the above distinctions among Personal Support, Group Support, and Organizational Support is the focus on interactions among persons based on tasks. The point is that these interactions generate dependencies or constraints on outcomes of the decision making process. Thus, the dimension of task interdependency has important implications for the application and development of DSS, as discussed in the next section.

## Implications for Personal Support

Task interdependency has several implications for using technology to support decision making. There are many successful implementations of DSS that have assisted managers as effective tools for personal problem solving. An obvious characteristic of these implementations is that the user was able to make the complete decision without much interdependencies with other actors or units. Examples of this situation are the cases of BRANDAID [11] and PROJECTOR as described in Keen and Scott Morton [9]. By contrast, several DSS have been recently developed to support the planning activities of large organizations. For example, the EIS by Berger and Edelman [2] dealt with decision support of personnel information across an entire division of RCA.

Personal Support may be used within an Organizational Support context. For example, each manager may use a small-scale system to help set the department's marketing budget, and the divisional staff can then coordinate and integrate these budgets using Organizational Support. In both cases the aim of the system is to support the user's decision making; the key difference is that Personal Support focuses on a person, while the emphasis of Organizational Support is on an organizational process.

While the conceptual literature on DSS emphasizes Personal Support, several of the most successful applications of Decision Support involve Group Support, and more recently, Organizational Support. For example, the DSS built by Scott Morton [13] which facilitated marketing and production planning provides Group Support. It allowed managers from two departments to come together and use the system as a means of making tradeoffs, sharing ideas, and reaching a consensus.

There are at least a few DSS, designed for Personal Support, that have been difficult to institutionalize because the broader task actually involved sequential interdependency. For example, BRANDAID is a system that in one company substantially facilitated the development of marketing plans by individual brand managers [9]. Their plans are integrated at the next level of the organization. This requires a different process of analysis. In particular, the problem of “cannibalization,” i.e., the plan for an individual brand results in sales being gained at the expense of another of the company’s products, had to be addressed. In addition, the managers responsible for integrating the plans required brand decisions to be presented in a format that was not directly compatible with the outputs from BRANDAID. This whole process needs Organizational Support in addition to, or in place of, this Personal Support. Clearly, current DSS research needs a much stronger exploration of organizational, rather than cognitive, issues [6].

This concept of different support types seems important, and not only in relation to DSS. For instance, Office Automation (OA) is a packaging of some differentiated technical building blocks similar to DSS. The conceptual literature on OA emphasizes Personal Support, such as text-editing, document preparation, and electronic mail. There are tools that assist and appeal to professional “knowledge workers.” Practitioners are, however, far more concerned with Organizational Support. They wish to use OA to streamline order entry operations, coordinate decentralized activities, and improve the efficiency of large-scale administrative functions. The literature on OA does not seem to acknowledge the differences in priority and focus.

In summary, a DSS relies on a careful selection of application, descriptive understanding of use, and definition of improved effectiveness. The type of DSS to be built depends on the clear identification of the task interdependencies involved. A successful DSS is a personal tool for stimulating creativity. It may not, however, lead to “more effective” decisions if these decisions are determined through a process that goes beyond the individual, and if the DSS is designed to provide only Personal Support.

## Organizational Strategies

Personal Support, in and of itself, is only valid if the tasks involved are discrete and independent of other tasks. In other words, Personal Support may be inappropriate as a component of organizational tasks because of the issues of managerial control and data integrity. The mixture of Personal Support and Organizational Support is possible if an explicit management policy of the acquisition and usage of personal computing technology is adopted.

First, standardization is needed for the personal use of data. For instance, direct update to the corporate database by personal systems should not be allowed. The usual organizational transaction processing should maintain the corporate database. Also, personal databases should remain personal. Corporate reports should be certified as being valid. Extracts from the corporate database should be controlled on a “need to know” basis. See Clemons [3] for elaboration on data management for decision support.

Secondly, Personal Support tools should be facilitated in a systematic manner. In particular, the economic aspects of personal support tools, such as microcomputers versus timesharing, should be analyzed. Important and generic tasks that are oriented toward individuals and are discrete should be identified. Further, the purchasing of the Personal Support technology should be controlled uniformly based on appropriate consideration of duplication, servicing, etc.

Thirdly, good “end user” software packages should be disseminated throughout the organization. An example is the VisiCalc package from

Personal Software, Inc. for simple financial calculations. Other software packages should include application generators and flexible database managers. Leverage for the individual manager should be achieved through the technical staff.

## Conclusions

This article presents the challenge that Personal Computing poses to developing Decision Support Systems in organizations. The potential for using Personal Computing for Personal Support within organizations is great, but serious problems with managerial control may result if Personal Computing becomes involved in Organizational Support activities.

This difficulty is best understood in terms of task interdependency that makes clear distinctions among Personal Support, Group Support, and Organizational Support. Organizational Support, if it is to be consistent and effective to the organization, depends on managerial control in ways that Personal Support does not. These concerns should not impede efforts to apply Personal Computing to Personal Support as long as the boundaries of this Personal Support are explicitly delimited.
