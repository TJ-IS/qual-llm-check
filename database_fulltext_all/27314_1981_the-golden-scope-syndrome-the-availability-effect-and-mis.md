## Abstract

Some underlying causes of increasing costs in the health care field and some of their proposed remedies are examined for relevance to the practice of MIS. Conflicting values between specialists and administrators have been described and illustrated in health care by behavior patterns called “The Golden Scope Syndrome” and “The Availability Effect.” These are seen by the author as working in similar ways in computer using organizations. Underlying organizational factors and ways to bring specialist goals and organizational objectives into alignment are discussed.

Keywords: MIS cost control, MIS management, professional values, MIS and organizational behavior, computer specialist's behavior
ACM Categories: 2.11, 2.2, 2.40, 2.41

## Background

Recent studies of American hospitals describe phenomena causing increasing costs that appear to be similar in the area of computerized management information systems (MIS). Two aspects of increasing costs in health care stem from professional values and behavior patterns. These have been called the “Golden Scope Syndrome” [4] and the “Availability Effect” [3]. A third factor is that the organization of health care has been mainly geared to the treatment of illness rather than maintaining health [7]. Each of these factors and their counterparts in the information systems field are discussed briefly below.

## The “Golden Scope Syndrome”

The “Golden Scope Syndrome” can be described by an anecdote in which a physician demands that a hospital administrator immediately purchase a new special diagnostic scope made of solid gold. When the administrator demurs, the physician responds with the ultimate professional trump card. The physician asks, “Will you take responsibility for all of the patients who will die next week because I don’t have a golden scope?” The principle underlying the efficacy of such a ploy, as Friedson points out [4], is that power is directly proportional to the ability to justify that a critical emergency exists. The physician alone can make this judgment and cannot be authoritatively contradicted by a non-medical professional.

General managers sometimes perceive analogous behavior on the part of some computer specialists. While computer specialists seldom can raise the spectre of life or death responsibility, the possibility of causing bills and paychecks to be delayed, production schedules to be disrupted, stockholder and tax reports to be in serious error, or shipping of goods to come to a halt, can sufficiently frighten a manager to enable the specialist to obtain the latest computer version of the Golden Scope.

The analogy between computing resources and medical diagnostic equipment is, of course, inaccurate from another point of view: that of relative costs. While new medical apparatus often is highly expensive, the cost per unit of data processing or computing continues to go down. On the other hand, expanding demand and the proliferation of applications and users of computers continue to rise, and the total cost of computing also rises albeit more efficient on some “per unit” bases. Administrators, therefore, continue to question whether or not the latest computer equipment is really needed or only desired as the newest toy for computer specialists or some users. In this and in other ways, the computer specialist and general administrator dialogue may be similar to the dialogue between medical doctors and hospital administrators.

## Varying Values

By means of the “Golden Scope Syndrome,” physicians and computer specialists often seem to triumph over cost conscious administrators, but why would these professionals want their Golden Scopes anyway? Is it just because they are “turned on” by the latest technological toys? This may be the case, but a somewhat different explanation lies in a comparison of professional values and the values of administrators. While each group may feel that it serves the objectives of the system they are part of, they may interpret those objectives differently. Professional values tend to equate higher quality service with greater professionalism. The professional, even when employed by an organization, serves a client or user group. If, as seems typical, the professional interprets higher quality as providing users with better service, cost is seen merely as a constraint on quality and service.

The concept of professionalism includes both the idea of service to a client and the notion of maintaining one's depth and range of knowledgeability and skills. Because the latest technology usually provides some enhancement of an aspect of quality and has some innovative features, it will appeal to both professional values of quality service for clients and the maintenance of up-to-date experience and knowledgeability.

Because of these values, the threshold of unaffordability will be approached by the professional and the administrator from different directions. The professional tries to maximize benefits up to the limit of the highest administratively acceptable cost. The administrator tries to minimize cost down to some professionally acceptable lower limit of benefits. If one postulates “pure” roles, the professional/administrator interaction is a negotiated exchange transaction between a buyer and a seller. The “deal,” not any objective measure of costs versus benefits, determines where the cost/benefit constraint should be placed.

Power plays a part in such a negotiation. Before considering this aspect further, the “Availability Effect” should be discussed.

## The “Availability Effect”

The “Availability Effect” in health care was described by Detsky [3]. It holds that in the health care market — unlike classic supply/demand relationships in free, competitive markets — demand for services is a function of supply. In other words, the more capability offered to the professional, or sometimes to the users themselves, the more that will be used.

This occurs because professionals, and some other users, are free to define their own requirements for usage without paying for usage directly. In most health care set-ups, third party insurers directly pay the bills, thus paving the way for the “Availability Effect” to operate.

Requirements will usually expand up to the capacity of the system as long as the system is highly accessible and offers any benefits at all. Costs are not weighed against benefits as long as they are below the limit of any perceived administrative acceptability. This is the case unless the professionals are themselves paying as they go in either real cash or in perceived tradeoffs of other benefits, such as time, prestige, status, etc., which is not the case in most existing health care arrangements.

The same phenomenon has often been noted by MIS specialists regarding user demand for internal MIS services. Various user charge systems have sometimes been applied to try to counter this kind of perceived “free goods” effect. Some such charge systems fail because they continue to be seen as dealing in “funny money,” instead of real currency or funds tradeable against other operating budget categories.

However, MIS specialists are likely to be equally as susceptible to the temptation and convenience of nearly free goods as either physicians or managers. Expensive CAT scanners have been used for relatively routine hospital admission procedures if they are accessible. Likewise, one can suppose that an expensive three dimensional color graphics terminals system may be used to display the course schedules in a university computer science, or corporate training, department if they would be so fortunate as to acquire such a device. Outside the realm of speculation, programmers already are often accused of rapidly using up as many resources as they are given. An obvious example is disk storage space.

The “Golden Scope Syndrome” and the “Availability Effect” work in tandem. Once a Golden Scope is acquired, it will usually become highly, if not well, utilized by virtue of the “Availability Effect.” High utilization will in turn help justify acquisition, if such help be needed, of the even newer, more improved Platinum Scopes (laser printers). . .and so on.

## Organizational Conflicts and the Control of Knowledge

Both computer people and physicians are in organizational situations where there are often structurally determined conflicts of economic interest. On the one hand, they may realize they have a responsibility to serve their organizations' economic objectives as well as to fulfill their own professional goals. But a physician's personal income often increases with the number of procedures he performs. Computer specialists also may see potential gains in their ability to justify their own higher economic value as utilization and apparent need for more sophisticated equipment increase. This conflict — coupled with the fact that these professional suppliers often act as agents for the consumers of their services in determining both the quantity and quality of their needs — tends to drive up usage.

If professional and organizational objectives are in conflict, power maneuvering will occur. Reiff [6] has described the professional's basis for power as the control of knowledge. Power can be obtained not only from possessing professional knowledge, but from attaining this knowledge without a professional's control. The development of jargon and its continual change, almost as if one is changing telecommunications security passwords, can be seen as a strategem to keep others from trespassing on a professional's turf. This kind of accusation has been made against physicians, educators, behavioral scientists, economists, and others, as well as against computer specialists. Even so-called hard scientists such as physicists and chemists, when they step outside their laboratories and find themselves in conflict with citizen environmentalists and politicians, have been found to use “technological jargon. . . at esoteric conferences to defend the arbitrary and belabor the obvious” [5].

In addition to excluding others from knowledge, Reiff [6] points out in his own jargon, that professionals will also engage in “knowledge expropriation and aggrandizement.” By this he means that, starting from a base of professional knowledge, they attempt to extend their power by laying claim to expertise in non-professional matters in which they become involved. Professionals thereby “aggrandize intuition, common sense, cultural and moral values in giving it the trappings of professional knowledge” [6]. Through this procedure the “Golden Scope Syndrome” is extended into new areas in which the “Availability Effect” can then come into play. The former power of the purse that resides with administrators is thereby often countered and overwhelmed by the control of knowledge, knowledge being the factor that Daniel Bell has called “the strategic resource” of the post-Industrial Age [1]. Thus, the power of medical doctors, computer specialists and other professionals has tended to grow.

## Redefined Objectives and Other Proposals

In addition to these professional values and behavioral factors, problems in the health care field often have been attributed to improperly defined objectives. Inordinately high costs, it is claimed, are largely due to the structure of the hospital-based system. This structure in turn is based on the wrong objective — that of treating rather than preventing illness. An alternate structure can be set up with the objective of maintaining health. It would obtain revenue on a prepaid basis and would automatically pay an economic penalty when it is less effective and has to treat illnesses among its subscribers. This would eliminate the insurers and incremental third-party payment aspect of the hospital system.

Such organizations have been set up, some of them having been in operation for many years, under the name of Health Maintenance Organizations (HMO's). Without entering into the advantages and disadvantages of such organizations compared to hospitals, the principle is clear: If some of the internal conflicts and causes of inefficiency are inherent in the structure of the organization, that structure should be changed or replaced with a more appropriate one. This is an attainable remedy — even though it is extreme — when the existing structure can be seen as not serving the main purposes agreed upon by everyone concerned.

The typical American business organization does not find itself in an economic environment similar to that of hospitals. If a business organization can continue to meet the needs of its customers and thereby earn a profit, it probably needs no basic restructuring. Within the organization, however, we can ask whether or not the rewards of information system professionals are well integrated with those of the organization.

It is easier, according to the author's experience, to establish integrating incentives within computer service companies, than to do so for internal computer service departments of other businesses. The link between company profits and cost-effective computer services is easier to establish in the computer service company. Since its profits directly depend on them, they tend to be more efficient data processors.

In contrast, as potential client organizations' costs of internal computer operations rise, the existence of more cost-effective outside alternatives may serve as a continuing competitive force to make internal suppliers more cost conscious. Organizations should, therefore, encourage users to use outside services whenever they are found to be cost effective. This competitive threat, however, does not eliminate the need for positive incentives for internal information system specialists.

Direct performance incentives could be geared to measures of user satisfaction, meeting planned target dates, low error rates, savings due to innovations and operating efficiencies, etc. As methods of managing systems development projects become more formalized and measurable, such incentives become more feasible. The use of self-set goals and provision of information feedback on performance, even without associated tangible rewards, has been shown to be effective in a variety of work situations. A group ethic of cost consciousness can be established by means of “systems productivity circles,” a la Japanese “quality circles,” coupled with a generous group share in the first year's savings [2].

More important than specific incentive plans is the establishment of a cost conscious managerial orientation among the systems specialists. The existence of such a culture among specialists who identify with a profession depends on its existence in the organization in general and also the extent to which such an orientation is consistent with the values of the professional establishment outside the organization. In the MIS field, the latter requirement seems largely to have happened, based on the expressions of professional associations through their journals and meetings. In addition, as more MIS specialists receive formal training and acculturation in good business schools, the managerial orientation of these MIS specialists can be expected to increase.

However, as we all know, the demand for MIS people continues to outstrip the supply, and this tends to drive down the level of professionalism attainable in the average organization. This places a greater burden on general management to establish and enforce suitable organizational structures and incentives in MIS operations.

Many observers have concluded that health care systems need restructuring in order to prevent even the best medical professionals from falling into the “Golden Scope Syndrome” and the “Availability Effect.” In the MIS field, we may claim that our best professionals already do not operate according to these strategems. Our problem — which is also management's problem — is to find ways of restructuring and managing, such as the use of the type of incentives we have suggested, so that even average MIS practitioners do not follow the computer based versions of the "Golden Scope Syndrome" and the "Availability Effect."

## About the Author

Lawrence F. Young received BIE and MIE degrees from New York University and a D.Sc. in IE and behavioral science from the Technion, Israel Institute of Technology. Dr. Young lived in Israel from 1972 and 1980 where he taught and did research at the Technion and consulted in MIS. Prior to that, he was vice president and director of management sciences at a division of the Interpublic Group of Companies in New York City, where he developed computer based models and information systems for advertising planning. After returning to the U.S. in 1980, he was an internal consultant with the DuPont Company until recently joining the faculty of Drexel University. Dr. Young has published several journal articles and monographs on management science and MIS topics.
