# Firm Size and the Characteristics of Computer Use

By: William H. DeLone

## Abstract

This article studies the proposition that firms of different sizes manage their computer operations differently, and it seeks to determine whether small firms face special circumstances in their uses of computers. The author presents several hypotheses which associate computer related variables with differences in firm size. These hypotheses are tested using data collected from seventy-four Los Angeles manufacturing firms of various sizes. The results indicate that selected computer-use characteristics vary with firm size. These research findings and their implications are discussed.

Keywords: Firm Size, small vs. large, computer use, data processing management, small business computer systems

ACM Categories: 2.11, 2.40, 3.50

## Introduction

Although many researchers have described the differences between managing small and large businesses, few studies have explored these differences in terms of managing the use of computers. This research study examines a sample of manufacturing firms of various sizes to determine whether firm size is associated with differences in the management of computers.

Many Management Information Systems (MIS) articles suggest that small businesses face unique problems in the management of their computer resources, but thus far the evidence cited is anecdotal. Few research efforts have studied this issue scientifically. This research study investigates the opinion that small businesses use computers differently by developing specific hypotheses, by collecting sample data, and by testing the hypotheses using statistical methods.

## Previous Research

Management theory acknowledges that differences exist between small businesses and large businesses. Several research studies have compared the advantages and limitations of small and large firms. Cohn and Lindberg surveyed executives in both small business and large business and found many differences in the problems which these managers face [3]. For instance, small businesses must deal with a shortage of management personnel, with personnel recruitment disadvantages, with financial limitations, and with insufficient external information. On the positive side, small firms implement decisions more quickly, and they offer their managers a broader experience and more immediate job satisfaction.

In his research, McFeely discovered that, since small businesses have limited staff, their managers must rely more heavily on personal experience than on information contained in staff reports $[14]$ . Deeks studied the differences in specialized knowledge and in the training of personnel $[5]$ . He learned that small businesses must frequently look outside the business for specialized expertise.

In the field of MIS research, Ein-Dor and Segev conducted a thorough survey of MIS literature, and proposed a framework for studying MIS success in an organizational context [6]. In their framework, the authors suggest organization size as one of the critical variables affecting the success or failure of management information systems.

Whisler conducted an indepth study of the data processing systems at nineteen insurance companies [20]. He reports that firm size was (1) directly related to the time interval required to introduce computer applications; (2) inversely related to the relative level of expenditures on computer equipment; and (3) inversely related to the involvement of top management in computerization. In a more recent empirical study, Ein-Dor and Segev tested the relationship between company size and the structure of the company's MIS department [7]. They found that firm size is inversely related to the degree of MIS centralization, and that firm size is inversely related to the rank of the MIS director.

## Hypotheses

The previously cited research work and this author's own experience in MIS have suggested the following global hypothesis:

Hypothesis G: Computer usage characteristics are different in firms of different sizes.

These characteristics include, but are not limited to: (1) the dependence on external programming support, (2) the type and number of computer applications undertaken, (3) the type of computer installation (inhouse, service bureau, timesharing, etc.), (4) the structure of the MIS department, (5) the level of experience with computers, (6) the technical expertise of the computer personnel, (7) the time it takes to install a computer system, and (8) the relative emphasis on hardware vis-a-vis software and personnel. This hypothesis is consistent with small business management theory, and it is supported by the research work of Ein-Dor and Segev [7] and by Whisler's study [20].

The specific hypotheses that are tested in this study are as follows:

Hypothesis H1: Smaller firms have been using computers for a shorter period of time than have larger firms.

In the 1960's and the early 1970's, small businesses could not afford the cost of computers and their associated expenses. Technology has reduced the cost of modern computers. Within the last five years, mini-computers and microcomputers have offered low cost computer opportunities to small businesses. Thus, it is only recently that small businesses could pay the price of a complete computer system. On the other hand, larger businesses found it feasible to invest in computers at earlier dates.

Hypothesis H2: Smaller firms are more highly dependent on external programming services.

Deek's research shows that “...the small firm, with its more limited managerial resources, must either look outside the company for specialist knowledge or must attempt to develop less sophisticated techniques internally,” [5]. Cohn and Lindberg found that small firms lack specialization and are at a disadvantage when recruiting personnel [3].

The improbability of hiring only a part of a programmer places a greater burden on smaller firms $[8]$ . Qualified programmers are in short supply. Small businesses which must compete with large firms have difficulty in hiring programmers $[2]$ , and programmer salaries are high relative to the low price of minicomputers $[9]$ . These factors force many smaller firms to purchase software rather than hire a programmer and develop programs “inhouse” $[9, 18]$ .

Hypothesis H3: Smaller firms allocate a smaller portion of their revenues to total electronic data processing expenditures.

Small businesses are faced with tight financial limitations [1, 3, 12, 13]. Consequently, small business managers are more concerned with cash flow than with return on investment [3]. As a result, the computer budget is often constrained. Furthermore, these managers lack the technical sophistication and knowledge to plan a comprehensive electronic data processing (EDP) operation [15]. Significant requirements are left out of the EDP budget [2, 10]. Common omissions are: backup systems, documentation, program maintenance, training, supplies, and computer security. Therefore, smaller firms, by omitting key items, allocate insufficient funds for computer use.

Hypothesis 4: Within the EDP budget, smaller firms spend a larger portion of their computer expenditures on computer hardware alone.

The lack of knowledge about computers causes small business managers to focus their attention on the purchase of the equipment $[2]$ while overlooking the key issues of personnel and software. Many managers become highly dependent on the hardware vendor for guidance $[4, 9]$ , and this reliance exacerbates the problem. These managers never consider all the requirements for a successful EDP operation. Thus small firms overcommit on hardware $[20]$ while undercommitting on personnel and software.

## Hypothesis 5: Small businesses can implement a decision to invest in computers faster than larger businesses.

The first four hypotheses deal with the problems associated with small business computer use. In contrast, smallness can provide advantages and opportunities. In large firms, complex organization structures and communication networks delay decision making and the implementation of decisions $[8]$ . “The less complex, small organization can reorient quickly and can realize computer based management information systems benefit within two years of the decision to conduct a feasibility study,” $[4]$ . In his study, Whisler found small insurance companies took less time to install their computer systems than did the larger companies $[20]$ .

## Research Methodology

The survey research method was chosen in order to test these five hypotheses. A systematic, random sample of 240 was selected from the approximately 2,400 Los Angeles manufacturing firms which are listed in the California Manufacturing Register. As ten percent of the population, a sample of 240 firms is representative of the population, and was large enough to include fifty or more firms which use computers. Each manufacturing firm in the sample was contacted to determine whether it uses computers (inhouse, service bureau, timesharing, etc.) for business data processing. Eighty-four firms were found to be using computers and were the focus of this survey.

At each of these firms, the person responsible for EDP was identified. Each of them received a letter which explained that he or she would be contacted by telephone and asked to respond to specific questions. The telephone interview items were listed on an attached sheet so that each respondent would be prepared to answer each item during the telephone interview (see Appendix). The interview items covered data relating to firm size, to the five research hypotheses, and for classification and general purposes. For the final analyses, seventy-five firms provided sufficient data to be useful in the study. (Five respondents did not know enough about their computer operation to respond to the interview items, two firms declined to participate, and two have not responded.)

The key variable, firm size, was measured by the number of employees (EMP). This measure of size is consistent with the current Small Business Administration (SBA) classification scheme for manufacturing firms $[11, 19]$ . At present, manufacturing businesses with less than 250 employees qualify for SBA assistance (this cut-off point varies from 250 to 1000 employees depending on the specific manufacturing category).

The computer usage variables which were collected to test the five hypotheses are:

Maturity - the number of months that the firm has been using computers for data processing.

External - the percentage of total programming costs which are paid to individuals or organizations external to the respondent firm.

Total EDP - the percentage of annual sales revenues which is used for total electronic data processing expenditures.

Hardware - the percentage of annual EDP expenses which is spent on computer hardware alone.

Installation - the number of months which passed from the initiation of a computer feasibility study until the firm's first computer application was operating.

Three of these variables, External, Total EDP, and Hardware, are likely to be associated with the age of the EDP operation, or Maturity, as well as with firm size $[16]$ . This makes Maturity a probable control variable. The relationships might be pictured as follows:

Thus it is important to determine whether the computer usage variables are related to firm size independent of the intervening effects of the maturity variable.

In addition to the variables already described, the firms were asked the following (1) what type of computer configuration they use (inhouse, service bureau, etc.), (2) what is their most important business application processed by computer, (3) what benefits they are deriving from computers, and (4) what has been their major problem in the use of computers.

Review of the data collected from the seventy-five firms revealed one extraordinary case. One firm reported 30,000 employees while the next largest firm had 3000 employees, and the mean firm size (without the largest firm) is 305 employees. This makes one firm ten times larger than the second largest firm and one hundred times larger than the average respondent firm. This one firm was dropped from the analysis lest it overwhelm the responses of the other seventy-four firms. It is worth noting that this firm's responses were consistent with the various hypotheses: as the largest firm, it had the longest EDP experience (25 years), it uses no external programming services, its relative expenditures on EDP (.75%) were higher than the mean level, and its relative allocation for hardware (32%) was less than the average. This firm had no estimate on how long it took to install its first computer application.

The independent variable, firm size, and each of the hypothesis variables have ratio measurement scales so Pearson correlation coefficients were computed to measure the strength of association between firm size and the other variables. Pearson correlation coefficients between age of EDP operation (Maturity) and the other hypothesis variables were computed to determine whether Maturity acts as an intervening variable. Where indicated, partial correlation coefficients were computed between firm size and the hypothesis variables while controlling for the effects of EDP Maturity.

## Other Findings

A few categorical variables were included in this study so that more could be learned about the relationship between firm size and computer use. These variables covered the following issues: (1)

the nature of the firm's computer use (inhouse, service bureau, etc.), (2) the firm's major computer application, (3) the benefits being derived from the use of computers, and (4) the firm's main problem with computer use. The findings in these four areas are summarized below.

Each respondent firm was placed in one of four computer use categories; namely 1) computers onsite, 2) using a service bureau, 3) using timesharing, or 4) using parent company EDP services. No firm claimed to be using a combination of these services. Multivariate discriminant analysis was used to determine whether firm size and/or age of EDP operation affect the nature of a firm's computer use.

The univariate analysis of variance results (Table 5) show that the age of the firm's EDP operation is associated with the nature of the firm's computer use, but that firm size showed no significant association.

The multivariate discriminant analysis shows that, in the presence of EDP maturity, firm size does improve the ability to discriminate between configuration classes. A summary of the multivariate results are:

Firm size improved the discriminant function by helping to distinguish between a firm's use of a service bureau as opposed to its use of one of the other three configuration categories. The results indicate that smaller firms tend to use service bureaus.

The list of major application responses was divided into two groups or categories. The popular beginning applications (payroll, accounts receivable, general ledger, accounts payable, and inventory control) were assigned to group one, which was labeled “basic” applications. All other applications were assigned to a second group termed “advanced” applications. The advanced category included sales analysis, order entry, material requirements planning, job costing, consolidation of financial statements, and strategic planning. Once again, multivariate discriminant analysis was applied in order to test the association of firm size and EDP maturity with the firm’s most important application.

The age of the computer operation (Maturity) was found to have a significant association with application category, while firm size showed no relationship. The results of the univariate analysis of variance are summarized in Table 6.

Furthermore, firm size did not improve the discriminant function when added to the effects of Maturity (Wilks Lambda was only reduced from .852 to .845). The results indicate that firms which have used computers longer (eight years, five months on average), consider applications in the “advanced” group to be their most important, while firms which have been using computers for less time (mean of four years, three months) report that applications in the “basic” group are their most important.

## Implications

The major finding of this study is that firm size does make a difference in how businesses manage their computer resources. Small businesses face some different computer related opportunities and problems than large businesses do. Most of the MIS research to date has been conducted in large firms with established computer operations, but much of this research may be of little use to the smaller enterprise.

This study found that small manufacturers have been using their computers for less time than larger manufacturers. Inexperience can cause problems regardless of firm size. This survey also shows that smaller, less experienced manufacturing firms are more likely to be using service bureaus and emphasizing applications such as payroll, A/R, general ledger, A/P, and inventory control. These “basic” applications may not have as high a payoff for manufacturers as the applications which are considered important by the more experienced firms, applications which include materials requirements planning, job costing, and order entry.

This study has also found that, regardless of how experienced a small firm is in its use of computers, the firm will: (1) be highly dependent on external software support, (2) spend relatively less on its EDP department, and (3) will put more of its EDP dollars into hardware. Dealing with outside software vendors may be good or bad depending on the quality of the software and on the ability of the small firm to make a knowledgeable choice of software vendor [17]. Experience indicates that many small businessmen are ill-equipped to make this decision which is likely to be critical to the success of the overall EDP effort [1, 2].

The low level of EDP expenditures may be the result of poor planning, and may imply that the small firm is taking some ill-advised risks. For example, it may be the case that no provisions are made for backup of computer systems and for computer security. This oversight leaves the firm vulnerable to events that could have disastrous effects on the firm's computer use and even on the firm's financial viability. Finally, the high hardware costs relative to total EDP expenditures may be indicative of a misguided emphasis on equipment. Small firms may be shortchanging software, personnel acquisition and development, and other important factors.

This survey has also found that small manufacturing firms are concerned about different computer-related problems than large manufacturers are. Small firms have more difficulty acquiring acceptable software. This problem may be caused by budgeting insufficient funds for software or by small firm dependence on outside programmers. This study also shows that many small firms use service bureaus for their electronic data processing. These firms are experiencing problems with turnaround time, with non-responsiveness, and with inaccurate reports.

As small clients, they are powerless to rectify the situation.

One favorable result was that nearly 20% of the small firm respondents were experiencing no major problems in their use of computers. However, this may reflect the application of computers to insignificant tasks, or it may reflect the ignorance of problems which exist but have not yet surfaced.

More research is needed in the area of computer usage by small businesses. This study has provided some preliminary results which substantiate the need to isolate those EDP variables which are dependent on size. Further studies should confirm the results of this study and expand the list of variables which differ according to firm size. The relationship between these variables and the success or failure of computer based information systems should be identified. A more complete list of small firm computer problems should be collected, and a preliminary set of solutions developed and tested in various small business settings. ## Limitations of the Study

This survey was restricted to firms in the single, broad industry category of manufacturing. The results, therefore, apply only to manufacturing businesses. However, Ein-Dor and Segev studied firms across different industries and found that firm size was associated with differences in MIS centralization and with differences in the rank of the MIS director [7]. Nevertheless, further research in other industries is necessary in order to substantiate the value of organization size as an MIS control variable. This study was also limited to a single geographical location (Los Angeles) and this further limits the applicability of these results.

Finally, this research focused on but a few of the EDP variables which are likely to vary with firm size. More such variables need to be identified and tested in order to better understand how computer use differs between small businesses and large businesses. Other variables worthy of study are: 1) the EDP knowledge and experience of the firm's management, 2) top management participation in computer use, 3) the technical competency of the firm's personnel, 4) personnel training in computers, and 5) turnover of MIS personnel.

## Summary

This research study investigated the general hypothesis that the characteristics of computer use vary according to firm size. This hypothesis and five more specific hypotheses were tested on the basis of responses from seventy-four Los Angeles manufacturing firms. The study determined that firm size is: (1) directly related to the age of the firm's computer operations, (2) inversely related to the amount of external programming that is used, (3) directly related to the portion of revenues allocated to electronic data processing (EDP), and (4) inversely related to the percentage of EDP costs that are used for computer equipment. No relationship was found between firm size and the amount of time required to implement the firm's initial decision to use computers.

This survey produced four additional findings about the relationship between firm size and computer use. Firm size does not determine whether a firm uses “inhouse” computers, service bureaus, timesharing, or parent company EDP services. Secondly, the age of the EDP operation, but not firm size, is associated with a firm’s most important computer application. Thirdly, it could not be determined whether firms of different size derive different benefits from their use of computers. Finally, firms of different size do experience different computer related problems. Small firms are concerned with poor quality software and with poor service from service bureaus, while larger firms complain of problems with poor user understanding and with information system complexity.

The above mentioned findings have important implications. More computer based management information systems research must be conducted in small business settings. Small businesses need to evaluate the payoff of their “most important” applications. Small businesses need qualified assistance in making difficult software decisions. Small businesses are probably not allocating their EDP dollars effectively, spending too much money on equipment and not enough on software and personnel. Finally, organization size is an important control variable to consider in the design of management information systems research projects.
