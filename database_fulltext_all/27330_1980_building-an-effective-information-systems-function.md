# BUILDING AN EFFECTIVE INFORMATION SYSTEMS FUNCTION

By: William B. Miller

## Abstract

The small number of truly successful Information Systems departments is well known. Part of the problem clearly lies in the management and direction of the Information Systems department in an organization.

The organizations with successful Information Systems departments appear to have some elements in common. These elements include defining the department's role and responsibilities, structuring its basic approach, establishing management direction, developing a plan, and development of good people.

This article describes these common elements in detail, with emphasis on case studies. Alternative means of achieving objectives are described. In summary, a conceptual framework for building an effective Information Systems department is established.

Keywords: Information systems, MIS management, MIS planning

ACM Categories: 2.4, 3.5

Information Systems (IS) departments are becoming more difficult to manage, not easier. The accelerating pace of technological change, complex new software such as database systems, new concepts such as distributed data processing, and the scarcity and cost of qualified personnel are a few of the factors that contribute to the problem of managing the Information Systems function. Nevertheless, many companies have developed effective IS functions. (In this article the terms "Information Systems" and "company" will be used, although the function could be called "EDP" or something else, and the overall organizational entity might be something other than a company.) What are the secrets of their success?

It appears that successful companies have some things in common in their approaches to Information Systems. If this impression is accurate, understanding of the commonalities would move us all one step closer to a better world. Therefore, this article, has as its central theme a description of the recurring elements in successful Information Systems departments.

There are five key steps for a company to take regarding its IS function:

1. Define the role and responsibilities of the IS function.

2. Define the basic parameters affecting the IS function's approach to fulfilling its responsibilities.

3. Establish proper management guidance of the IS function.

4. Develop an IS plan and a way to measure performance against the plan.

5. Obtain and support good people in the IS function.

The remainder of this article describes why these steps appear to be important, and how they have been implemented in various companies.

## Defining the Role and Responsibilities of the IS Function

What could be clearer than what the Information Systems department is supposed to do? It's obvious—run the computer, and develop and maintain the company's computerized systems. Well, it is not quite that simple.

First, there is the question of the role of the IS function. Is it intended to be a technical service group that implements decisions made by the rest of the company, or is it intended to be a leader in developing solutions to current and future business problems? How should it operate vis-a-vis other company functions and, in a company with several operating units, in relation to other Information Systems departments?

Second, what are its specific responsibilities in fulfilling the role? The responsibilities could include:

\- Application of systems analysis and other problem solving methods to business problems.

\- Designing and programming of computerized systems, and development of operating procedures for users of the systems.

\- Development of forms, methods, and procedures for systems that do not involve a computer.

\- Maintenance and distribution of policy and procedures manuals.

\- Training users for implementation of new systems and proper operation of existing systems.

\- Selection and operation of computer equipment.

\- Coordination and direction of use of outside data processing services, such as time sharing facilities and service bureaus.

\- Management of word processing and other office services functions.

\- Selection and operation of telecommunications equipment and services, including voice and data communications.

Typically, senior management would define the role and responsibilities with assistance from the senior Information Systems executive and other functional heads as appropriate. Only top management is in a position to determine unequivocally what is expected of Information Systems, and to make the major decisions that are required, such as those regarding company organization.

Frequently, companies that take this step end up with significant departures from traditional approaches to data processing. For example, Macy's of California has an extensive point-of-sale (POS) terminal network, and therefore has a great deal of communications expertise in their data processing department. They have taken advantage of the expertise by making the data processing department responsible for voice communications (the telephone system), as well as data communications. On the other hand, Deere and Co., the farm equipment manufacturer, opted to reduce the scope of their business systems department's responsibility. The company decided that local user department control of the systems development process, including computer programming, was appropriate in many cases. Different companies have different environments and needs which will require different approaches to data processing. The only way to determine which approach is best for a given company is serious examination and thought by company management.

The two examples which follow illustrate what can happen when this step is not performed correctly.

## Example 1

A California electronics subcontractor established a new subsidiary to produce printed circuit boards (pcb's). It was their first foray into pcb's specifically, and into process manufacturing in general. As might be expected, engineers and other technicians dominated the subsidiary. The parent company allowed the subsidiary to exist autonomously. The subsidiary established an information systems department, and selected a young engineer who had worked a lot with computers to manage the department. The new manager purchased a technically elegant computer which was well known in the field of scientific and engineering computing. Unfortunately, he overlooked some business considerations.

Over the next nine months, several problems arose. The computer broke down frequently, and there was no local service capability. It was impossible to locate business-oriented systems personnel who were knowledgeable about the equipment. Virtually all application systems had to be developed from scratch since there was not a library of business systems available from the manufacturer and other users.

Word of the problems eventually drifted back to the parent company when the subsidiary was unable to meet its cost targets because of inadequate IS support. The parent's information systems department was able to fix the situation in six months. However much damage had been done during the nine months of chaos. With hindsight, it was obvious that the parent's IS function should have had a strong role from the beginning.

## Example 2

The second example is a tale of two Material Requirements Planning (MRP) systems. $^{1}$ MRP is a computerized production and inventory control technique. It is based on “exploding” production schedules into time-phased requirements for constituent components and material by using computerized bills of material and predetermined decision rules for scheduling and inventory replenishment.

Company A, an aerospace subcontractor, had a written policy that both IS and the user departments had to approve the non-technical design of a new system, such as output reports and information to be stored by the system for later retrieval by the users.

Company B, a medical equipment manufacturer, had an equally clear policy that IS had to develop a system to meet whatever requirements the users imposed. In other words, Company A's IS department was viewed as an ally to users in problem solving, whereas Company B's IS department was a technical service group. Both companies attempted to develop an MRP system.

MRP, is one of the last frontiers of data processing. It is quite possible, as Company B found out, to establish design requirements that prohibit development of a cost effective system. Company B's user requirements were such that the system would literally have required a computer twice as large as the company had, solely for MRP processing. No data processing department in the world could have designed a system to meet the users' needs cost effectively! The company finally had to redesign the system at a cost of an additional million dollars and one more year. Company A avoided the problem, because IS participated in the definition of the user requirements and was able to point out the pitfalls of asking for certain features in the system.

Many such problems have occurred and documenting these could fill volumes, including tales of inefficient coordination of communication networks and technically brilliant system designs that failed to solve the business problems at hand. Fortunately, as indicated by the companies cited earlier and others, there are many success stories to be told. Successes almost always include proper definition of Information Systems' role and responsibilities, but other issues are involved as well.

## Determining the Basic Parameters Affecting Information Systems' Approach to Fulfilling its Responsibilities

Information Systems' methods have to work within the corporate environment. In any company, a few key parameters describe the company's approach to surviving and prospering. The task is to determine what these parameters are and which ones affect Information Systems. The following four examples of basic parameters may help in recognizing them.

## Risk aversion

As with engineering projects or new product introductions, there can be a certain risk of failure in systems development or other IS projects. The risk is influenced by matters such as ambitiousness of schedules and whether proven equipment or software is used. Some companies are comfortable with a certain level of risk in business matters, whereas others adopt a policy of risk aversion. The IS approach should be consistent with top management's approach to risk taking.

Hewlett-Packard Company (HP) is accustomed to taking chances and moving aggressively in the information processing industry. By doing so, it has become one of the most successful companies in an industry where failures and low profitability are legion. HP's IS function reflects the corporate style. It has implemented distributed data processing in a worldwide computer network. "Distributed" refers to the placement of computer power at HP's numerous manufacturing and marketing locations; the computers talk to each other through the communication network. Such an approach would still be like science fiction to more conservative companies.

On a different note, I recently had the opportunity to work with a state-of-the-art semiconductor manufacturer in which the chairman and founder believed in keeping IS expenses to the bare minimum. The result was an IS function with marginally qualified people, old equipment, and outdated systems. The bottom line impact appeared to be favorable, since a good ROI is possible on a small investment as well as on a big one. Unfortunately the department was so clearly out of step with the rest of the company that it was a time bomb waiting to go off. It finally went off in the form of a middle management revolt against the conservative approach that limited IS failures, but also limited successes. The company hired a young aggressive Manager of Information Systems, and she is now transforming the department into one that will fit with the style of the company.

## Implications of company financial performance

Current and projected financial performance affects all operations of a company, including IS. The effect may be simply budgetary in nature, permitting more or fewer funds for IS, perhaps lowering or raising the hurdle rate of return for proposed systems projects.

For some companies, however, strategic issues may be involved. For instance, it is no secret that commercial banking is becoming a contest to see who can offer the most electronic services, from automated teller machines (ATM's) for retail customers, to sophisticated account analysis systems for corporate customers. Low cost provision of bank services is a key contributor to profitability, and often demands displacement of high personnel costs by lower equipment costs. In this environment, IS decisions have been made, and will continue to be made, on the basis of achieving satisfactory corporate financial performance.

## Flexibility

Flexibility is the ability to recover successfully from events that were not forecasted. If it is important, IS should attempt to remain flexible. The company should also be ready to suffer the consequences. It has become fashionable to desire flexibility, but the plain fact is that true flexibility costs money, often lots of it. In the case of IS, it can mean costly short term equipment leases, use of contract programmers, etc. There is nothing wrong with flexibility, but as the ancient proverb suggests, moderation may be appropriate.

## The company's maturity regarding IS

Several authors, chief among them Richard Nolan, have described the concept that many companies appear to develop their use of IS in phases or stages $[1, 7, 8]$ . Each stage has certain characteristics regarding the kinds of systems that can be developed successfully — user awareness and attitudes, and IS organization and management approaches. The stages generally are related to maturity and experience with IS, i.e., each stage builds on the knowledge and capabilities gained in previous stages.

One implication of the stage theory is that the IS department and user departments should grow up together. If one accepts the theory, IS should not attempt to do more than can be absorbed by the rest of the company. In one specialty apparel retailer, the chief executive officer (CEO) caused a problem by deciding that he should have point-of-sale (POS) terminals, and by hiring a Director of IS who had implemented a POS system in a much larger chain. The new director hired many people and bought a great deal of equipment. Unfortunately, the merchandising and store operations people had no idea of how to operate in a POS environment, and the POS project never got off the ground. The Director of IS resigned and the company was back where it started, with a smaller bank balance.

The four parameters just described have been dealt with by various companies in the process of developing effective IS functions. Additional parameters can probably be identified for any company. However, by now it should be obvious that providing guidance to IS is a big job that demands serious attention.

## Establishing Proper Management Guidance of the Information Systems Function

Who should oversee the IS function? Traditionally, the IS function has reported directly to a financial executive. Surveys $[3, 4]$ have continually indicated a decline in the prevalence of this reporting relationship, to a point that today fewer than 50% of the IS functions report to an executive with a purely financial title such as Controller or Vice President of Finance. Over 50%, and the percentage increases with the size of the company, report to an executive with broader responsibilities. In about one out of five companies, the president, CEO, or chairman, is the reporting point. In divisionalized companies, the trend for division Information Systems departments is the same—just replace the corporate positions mentioned above with their divisional counterparts.

In addition, in divisionalized companies there has been a movement toward the development of strong division IS capabilities. The divisions' Information Systems groups have often become the doing functions, and the corporate groups are now the planning and coordinating functions. The situation is already evident at some companies. CBS, Inc., for example, has Data Base Administration functions at division and corporate levels. The corporate function has the last word on database definition and control. However, the division functions provide all user support such as accessing the database.

Changes probably have come about because of rising IS budgets, the proliferation of nonfinancial systems, and the increased use of computerized systems, such as MRP systems, that can have significant impact on the bottom line. These factors point toward control by top management and management with profit and loss responsibility, such as division heads. I would submit that IS will end up as one of the few key company functions that will universally report to a top management individual. Whether the reporting position is the CEO, Executive Vice President, a functional Vice President, or a division head, is a matter of a specific company's organization and its specific individuals.

A steering committee is another form of management guidance. Some companies use them heavily, and some do not; there is a fair amount of controversy about their usefulness. A closer examination of the situation reveals that the role of steering committees is linked to the role of IS. Not surprisingly, where IS is a technical service group, steering committees have been very successful. Where Information Systems is supposed to be the leader, steering committees may be redundant and potential sources of conflict.

If a steering committee is right for a company, it should be designed to meet the company's specific needs. The needs may demand a unique approach. Supermarkets General, for example, established two steering committees—one for systems enhancements and one for major new systems, with different management personnel on each committee. Although this approach is unusual, it works for them, which in the final analysis is the best reason for doing anything.

The Information Systems overseers—reporting point and steering committee—would typically bear the primary responsibility for deciding or recommending basic policy decisions affecting IS. Would they also be involved in the implementation of policies? The evidence says yes. In fact, they are responsible for ensuring that Information Systems knows where it is going and how to get there. Specifically, they ensure that the next step is carried out.

## Developing an Information Systems Plan and a Way to Measure Performance Against the Plan

Information Systems should have a plan. In fact, people frequently think that the great majority of companies, and certainly all the well managed companies, have such plans. They are partially correct. Most companies do have a plan for Information Systems, but the plans are frequently deficient in some respects. When we discuss the contents of an IS plan this will be clear, particularly based upon the following review of IS activities.

Broadly speaking, Information Systems activities can be categorized as either project oriented or production oriented. The project oriented activities include systems development, of course, but they also include other activities that are expected to produce a certain result at the end of a period of time. Other project oriented activities are, for example, selection and installation of a new computer, and personnel recruiting.

Production oriented activities are those of a routine nature from which the output is the same, time after time. The day-to-day value of the IS function lies not in the project oriented activities, such as new systems being developed, but in the production oriented activities, such as the effective and timely operation of existing systems. In this respect, the production side of IS is like a factory that must deliver current products while engineering (the project oriented side) develops new products.

An IS plan should deal with all areas of the function's responsibility. The following topics might be included:

\- Systems development and maintenance

• Equipment acquisition

• Equipment and systems operation

• Facilities requirements

\- Organization and personnel

\- Standards and controls (for instance, as required by the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act).

A case in point is Xerox Corporation. The company's IS plans include a seven year long range plan, and a two year operating plan. Both plans are comprehensive, covering all facets of IS. The plans differ, of course, in scope of issues covered, level of detail, and degree of commitment to specific results.

The plan should be consistent with the basic parameters described previously and with the company's business strategy. Discussion of how to do this is beyond the scope of this article, but other authors have addressed the issue [6].

Some companies fail to include one or more key topics in their IS plan. Equally common, however, is a failure to deal with the topics as required by the project production distinction. For project oriented activities, the appropriate contents of the plan would be the project output, schedule, activities, responsibilities, key milestones, budgets, and other financial analyses such as cost/benefit analyses. This constitutes the standard type of planning. For production oriented activities, a service level approach is more appropriate, in the same way that a manufacturing plant is measured on schedule performance, cost control, and the like. The IS plan for production oriented activities would then describe the production objectives and the plan for achieving the objectives. Examples of production concerns for which objectives could be set include the following:

\- Performance against schedules for processing and distribution of output.

\- Number of computer jobs requiring reruns and otherwise not processed as planned.

\- Availability to the users and response times for online systems.

\- Service levels (e.g., turnaround times) for processing jobs on request.

\- Computer equipment availability and utilization levels.

Specific project and production plans and objectives are not the sole contents of a good IS plan. A plan containing such information would be a vast improvement for many companies. Another section should deal with a strategic awareness of how IS ought to affect the company in the future.

Figures 1 and 2 are graphic illustrations of the technological advances in computers made over the past fifteen years. The world has changed from one in which personnel costs were overshadowed by the cost and scarcity of computing resources that had to be used efficiently, to one in which personnel costs are highly significant and efficiency of computer use is a consideration only in special cases (such as MRP). Many people are familiar with this phenomenon, but they may not know that the rate of advances shows no signs of slowing down. In fact, some recent price announcements have been at levels below the extensions of trend lines in Figures 1 and 2.

In addition, as indicated by Figure 2, the difference in cost effectiveness between different sizes of computers has narrowed, and in the minds of some observers has disappeared forever. Among other things, the advent of distributed data processing was made possible by this trend. Discussion of all the implications of this and other data processing trends is beyond the scope of this article. A key point to bear in mind is that the next twenty years will hold as many advances as the last twenty. The company that does not prepare strategically for the advances will face some serious problems.

The ease and effectiveness of measurement of IS is directly related to the plan. Measurements of project related activities is easy if the Information Systems plan included milestones and financial targets. Measurement of performance on production oriented activities similarly becomes a matter of comparing actual performance to service objectives.

Companies generally approach IS planning and progress measurement in a manner consistent with their approaches to Information Systems. Aggressive IS functions usually take the lead in their own planning and measurement, whereas the opposite is true where Information Systems efforts are controlled by other company functions.

According to a study by the American Federation of Information Processing Societies, the insurance industry is more heavily dependent on data processing than any other private industry. In total, it follows only the federal government. As data processing veterans, insurance companies have done several interesting things in Information Systems management.

Aetna Life and Casualty has an advanced computerized database to monitor and record computing equipment reliability. Equipment problems, causes, and corrective action taken are documented daily. The data processing department of the Equitable Life Assurance Society establishes quantitative service objectives for timely delivery of output reports, availability of online systems to users, and percentage of time the computers are used for productive work. Performance against the objectives is reported routinely and also included in an “annual report” published by the data processing department that covers all the department’s activities and plans. I suspect that many companies could benefit by implementing a similar system.

One other aspect of measurement deserves attention. This is the notion of comparing the Information Systems department to the “average” Information Systems department as defined by surveys. One of the most popular pieces of data for comparisons is IS expense as a percent of sales, assets, operating expenses, etc. Such comparisons may be meaningful if they are put in the proper perspective. The fact is, definite patterns do exist in the Information Systems world, including the percentage data mentioned above and additional data, such as allocation of Information Systems expenses (e.g., equipment, software, people, and facilities). Ignoring the patterns cannot help a company. It is appropriate to learn what the patterns are for its industry, computer size, etc., and then use the information as it would any other data to do a better job with Information Systems.

Planning and progress measurement is the fourth area of commonality among companies with effective IS functions. The fifth area is one without which the other four are meaningless.

## Acquiring and Supporting Good People in Information Systems

There are more pitfalls in implementing this idea than companies may imagine. Consider the situation of the top Information Systems executive, whom we will continue to call the Director of Information Systems. Some companies, acutely aware of the salaries of such people, look for a bargain. They usually get what they pay for. Other companies have paid the going rate for a highly qualified Director, only to discover later that the individual was overqualified for their expectations from IS. In addition, many companies have hired a Director of Information Systems with personality or style problems, on the grounds that the individual was a technical genius. History has demonstrated that to obtain an effective Director of Information Systems, a company should not let the Information Systems mystique overpower its common sense in hiring.

There are similar problems with the other people employed by the IS function. People productivity is the single greatest problem facing many Information Systems functions. For example, ten to twenty lines of computer program code (e.g., COBOL) per day per person is about all that can be expected on the average on a systems development project [5]. On complex projects, productivity is even lower! It has been observed by Fred Brooks, project manager for many aspects of IBM 360 development, in his classic book, The Mythical Man-Month, that the productivity ratio between a good Information Systems technician and a poor one is ten or higher, not the two or three that might be expected. In addition, of course, the good people become more difficult to find and keep. On top of these depressing facts, Information Systems salaries just keep on going up. Companies are paying more and getting less.

The implications of the productivity problem in IS are important. If a company has high expectations for IS, the management of the function must be capable of identifying personnel needs, filling the requirements with qualified individuals, and getting the maximum work out of them. The Personnel or Industrial Relations department may have to be re-educated. The IS function will have to take advantage of the decreasing costs of technology that we discussed earlier to improve people productivity.

Hills Bros. Coffee, along with many other companies, has moved strongly into online program development in order to use the computer more effectively as a tool for programmers. Rohr Industries has done this, and has also implemented improved systems development methodologies, some of which use the computer and some of which are procedural improvements. These methodologies include structured design and programming, and HIPO (Hierarchy plus Input, Output, and Processing). Surveys indicate that familiarity with improved systems development techniques is widespread, but use of them is not. The companies that are using advanced techniques are still pioneers; there is not yet a “cookbook” of techniques from which a selection can be made to solve a company’s particular productivity problem. To summarize, the task of acquiring and supporting good Information Systems people is bigger than company management might think, and deserves to be taken seriously.

## Closing Comments

We have reviewed some ways by which companies have increased the effectiveness of their Information Systems departments. I hope some of the ideas were interesting and useful. I also hope that the conceptual framework which I established is beneficial in the quest for a practical theory on how to develop effective Information Systems functions.
