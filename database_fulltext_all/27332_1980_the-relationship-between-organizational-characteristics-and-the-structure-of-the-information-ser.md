# THE RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN ORGANIZATIONAL CHARACTERISTICS AND THE STRUCTURE OF THE INFORMATION SERVICES FUNCTION

By: Margrethe H. Olson
Norman L. Chervany

## Abstract

Although much has been written about the centralization versus decentralization of information services, relatively little is known about how the structure of an organization influences the level of centralization or decentralization. This paper presents the results of a study which examined the organizational context of the information services function. The authors attempted to identify those organizational characteristics that are associated with the structure of the information services function. It was found that the organizational environment does have an influence upon the level of decentralization of information services. Few organizational characteristics, however, were found to influence the structure of information services consistently across all organizations. Further research relating organizational form to its impact on information services is suggested.

Keywords: Centralization/decentralization, information services organization, organization structure, organization contingencies

ACM Categories: 2.4, 3.5

## Introduction

How should information services be organized to best meet the information needs of an organization? This question has been debated for many years as continuous technological changes have provided top management with new opportunities for the centralization or decentralization of information system resources. In the late 1960's, developments in the use of large scale computers permitted, and even required, more centralization of information services than had previously been feasible. Today, developments in mini and microcomputer technology and distributed processing are causing renewed interest in the feasibility of decentralizing information systems.

However, the real centralization/decentralization issue facing organizations is much broader than the choice between alternative computer hardware configurations. What degree of control over the information services function should be exercised by its users, and how much control should be retained in a centralized department?

There are three separate elements of the information services function that can be decentralized [15, 21]:

1. System Operations

2. System Development

3. System Management

The decision regarding how to organize each of these three separate elements is based on a different set of criteria. Furthermore, each decision can be made relatively independently of the others. Even within each separate element there are a variety of ways to increase or decrease the degree of centralization. Therefore, the challenge to the information services practitioner is to achieve the “best” combination of centralized and decentralized services for the particular organization.

The study presented in this article is based on the premise that most information processing literature that examines centralization/decentralization issues is focused too narrowly. The primary weakness of most discussions is that they assume no organizational constraints on decisions regarding the organizational form of the information services function [6]. Those discussions that do focus on the organizational context of information services tend to treat the centralization/decentralization question as one-dimensional rather than multi-faceted [4, 12]. What is needed is a set of guidelines that simultaneously takes into account the organizational context of information services and the alternative mechanisms for accomplishing centralization or decentralization.

In this article the results of a study which examined the organizational context of the information services function are presented. Based on our findings, we summarize what is known about the existence of, or lack of organizational influences on information services. Some suggestions for future research to further progress toward providing a set of context-dependent guidelines for the organization of information services also are made.

## Previous Research

Although several researchers have studied the effects of computerization on organizational structure [5, 8, 10, 22, 24, 25], few have examined how organizational structure influences the information services function. In the 1960's, when centralization became feasible, several studies attempted to identify the circumstances under which a move to centralization was appropriate for an organization.

Garrity [12] compared 211 companies rated as either “successful” or “unsuccessful” in their use of computers. He found that in the “successful” firms the computer function was organized along divisional lines. In most “unsuccessful” companies the computer function was directed centrally. His study showed an even more important “success factor” to be whether the organization of the computer function corresponded to the general organizational pattern (i.e., was the computer function more decentralized in companies that were decentralized and vice versa). In a study of 100 business firms, Dean [7] found that of those companies with decentralized organizational forms, 91% had decentralized operations and 81% had decentralized systems analysts. In a study of 16 large firms with “successful” computer functions, Reichenbach and Tasso [20] concluded that “...it is the underlying characteristic management style of the company that is the significant factor in the determination of how centralized EDP operations should be...”

In organizational theory research, there have been a few studies dealing with the relationship between organizational structure and automation of “information technology” [14]. A particularly relevant study by Blau, et al. [4], revealed that manufacturing plants having their own decentralized computers had more levels in their hierarchies, less dependence on hierarchical communication channels, and greater specialization of support personnel than either manufacturing plants that utilized a centralized computer or manufacturing plants with no computer utilization. Blau also concluded that plants with their own in-house computer tended to be more decentralized than the others. They hypothesized that this was because of the increased autonomy of the plant manager.

A few studies currently in progress are addressing the centralization/decentralization question. Rockart, et al. [21], have developed a framework of organizational factors influencing the organization of information services. This framework is currently being tested in the field. Ein-Dor and Segev [9] have presented and are now validating a set of propositions regarding the effects of organizational context on the success of management information systems. These studies, as well as the research presented here, will contribute to the goal of providing a set of guidelines for centralizing or decentralizing information services along multiple dimensions.

## Centralization/ Decentralization Strategies

A single continuum between complete centralization and decentralization of the information services function does not exist. As has been noted, there are several dimensions to the issue, each of which may be centralized or decentralized. In this article, we use three classes of activity suggested by Norton [15] to represent these dimensions—system operations, system development, and system management.

## Strategies for system operations

Norton defines system operations to include physical computer hardware and the operations personnel associated with it. The most common argument for centralizing computer operations is the efficiency advantage resulting from economies of scale of equipment. Current trends in technology, however, tend to reduce the need to have large computers to benefit from economies of scale. Minicomputers and distributed computing provide possibilities for high-speed, high-capacity decentralized operations at relatively low cost.

A common argument supporting decentralization of system operations is that users can take full responsibility for handling their own operations. A reduction in data errors should result from having data entry at the source of transactions. Regardless of the physical location of computer hardware, some aspects of control over operations such as data entry and run scheduling can be decentralized to users.

## Strategies for system development

Norton defines system development activities to include the analysis, design, and programming of new applications, and the maintenance of existing ones. The key issue is how much users should participate in, and be responsible for, the system development process. Generally speaking, decentralization of the system development function should lead to increased user involvement in the development process [5]. Another organizational consideration is the degree to which applications need to be tailored to fit particular user areas rather than centralizing system development to facilitate the design of standard applications, while decentralization may not be more effective for unique user requirements [5].

At the extreme of centralized system development, all development personnel are retained in a centralized “pool” for assignment to any user application as needed. At the extreme of decentralization, systems analysts are physically located in, and report to, user departments. Two other alternatives can provide a compromise of improving service to users while maintaining a centralized development staff. The first alternative is to assign permanently each analyst to a “team” responsible for all projects for a particular user department. This increases the analyst's ability to specialize in one type of system requirements. The second alternative is to create a “user liaison” position in the information services area with the responsibility of maintaining an effective communications link between the users and the central development staff.

## Strategies for system management

Norton defines system management to include the administrative aspects of planning, developing, and controlling information system resources. The major activities included under the system management function are:

\- selecting projects for development,

\- negotiating user budgets for information services,

\- charging for information services,

• controlling project development costs,

\- auditing the system,

\- overall planning of equipment, data, and people resources, and

• overall policy setting.

System management refers to the location of executive responsibility for information services (e.g., information services management—centralized, corporate management—steering committees, or user management—decentralized).

Any of these three dimensions of information services may be centralized or decentralized relatively independently of the others. For instance, in an organization with highly centralized operations and development, the user may exert a great deal of control over the use of information services through decentralized control over management activities. In contrast, an organization may require highly decentralized operations and development but still maintain centralized control by centralizing management activities.

## Research Method

The research study reported here was a field survey in which interviews were conducted with corporate executives and information processing managers in 43 business organizations in a medium-sized metropolitan area. Companies were selected for possible participation if they were functionally autonomous from a parent organization, if they had a minimum of 500 employees, and if they had in-house information processing. The companies represented five industry classes—financial, merchandising, transportation and utilities, food processing, and manufacturing. Companies were classified based upon their major Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) code. Food processing, which is normally included within manufacturing, was counted as a separate class because of its predominance in the region under study. Companies were also classified in three size categories—500-2000 employees, 2001-4000 employees, and 4001+ employees.

Out of an original population list of 86 companies, 43 participated in the study. Although the researchers attempted to choose a random sample stratified by size and industry, the decision about whether an organization should be included in the study was made partly by the organization's management. Therefore, the decision was not completely controlled by the researchers. However, each of the fifteen size/industry categories was represented in the final sample.

All data were collected through structured interviews. Three members of each company were interviewed for approximately one hour each—the information processing manager, a corporate executive, and the personnel director. Questionnaires also were completed by the heads of four departments which heavily utilized information services.

## Characteristics of the organization

There is a large body of theory and research in organizational behavior that examines the characteristics of organizational structure and their interrelationships [3, 11, 13, 18]. In addition, their relationship to the organization's production/operations technology has been examined [17, 23, 26]. These studies focus on the type of technology (e.g., job shop versus batch versus continuous process), rather than types of products. In the same way, in this study we examined the organization's "information technology” [4]—how the information processing function in the organization is structured, rather than the use of particular information systems. Our goal was to understand the relationship between organizational structure and the structure of the information services function.

In the study, six organizational characteristics were measured through the interviews with the corporate executive and the personnel director, and through questionnaires of user department heads. The definition and measurement of these characteristics are taken from an extensive set of studies of organizational structure that are summarized in Pugh and Hickson's work [19]. It should be emphasized that these characteristics refer to the overall organization, not to the information services function. The six variables measured are listed below.

Centralization of Authority (AUTH). This refers to the level in the organization where most decisions of an operational control nature are made [2]. In a highly centralized company, most decisions are made at the top of the management hierarchy. In a decentralized company, many decisions are delegated to lower management levels.

Standardization (STAN). This refers to the use of rules and procedures in the organization. In a company which is highly standardized, there are many organization-wide rules and procedures for guiding activities. In a company that is low in standardization, there are few rules and procedures. In the latter case, each division of the company usually establishes its own methods of operating rather than having to conform to organization-wide standard procedures.

Formalization (FORM). This refers to the degree to which rules, procedures, and organizational activities are documented with formal paperwork. A highly formalized organization records organizational activity extensively, while an unformalized organization does not.

Line Control of the Workflow (CONT). This refers to the control procedures applied in the operations processes in the organization. Line control implies direct supervisory-subordinate interaction for control purposes. At the other extreme, a company may employ impersonal control mechanisms (e.g., performance standards and budget variance reports) and minimize the use of direct communication for control purposes.

Functional Specialization (SPEC). This refers to the number of organizational functions that are performed by specialists. A highly specialized organization has many specialists who do one job and only that job. In a company which is low in specialization, employees will commonly do a variety of different jobs rather than specialize in a particular activity.

Perceived Power of the Information Services Function (POWER). This refers to the influence, as perceived by users, of the information services department relative to user departments. Users were asked about the department's influence in important decisions regarding system operations, system development, and system management. High perceived power implies the users see the information services department as having more influence than themselves over these decisions. Low perceived power implies users see themselves as having as much or more influence over these decisions as the information services department.

These organizational characteristics were chosen as representative of an organization's structure. It was felt that these characteristics would influence the decision to centralize or decentralize information services in order to achieve effective information processing. In addition, it was expected that different structural characteristics would influence different dimensions of information services — system operations, system development, and system management.

## Characteristics of information services

For each of the dimensions — system operations, system development, and system management—several characteristics were considered. Based upon these characteristics, an organization was rated highly centralized, highly decentralized, or at a middle level between the two extremes. A more complete description of the model used to relate these characteristics to the degree of centralization or decentralization of information services overall is presented in Olson [16].

## System Operations (S.O.)

Four aspects of system operations were considered:

Number and Location of Computer Facilities (S.O.1). This refers to the location of machines for general information processing. Special purpose or process control computers are not considered. An organization may have a centralized facility only, several remote facilities as well as a central site, or completely decentralized facilities.

Range of Work Performed at Each Facility (S.O.2). This characteristic refers to the extent of operations at local sites. It differentiates between use of local processing only for data entry versus having most operational processing performed locally. The extent of local processing is not necessarily dependent on the location of machines. Even with a central computer, some local processing may take place through online terminals. On the other hand, minicomputers in user departments may be only employed for data entry, while most processing is done centrally.

Location of Control Over Operations (S.O.3). Given some decentralization of hardware or processing, this characteristic measures the degree of local autonomy over scheduling of processing. It also evaluates the degree of technical expertise in computer operations supplied by personnel at the local site.

Location of Data (S.O.4). This characteristic measures the amount of data physically stored at local sites.

## System Development (S.D.)

Three characteristics of system development were considered:

Location of Systems Analysts (S.D.1). This characteristic differentiates between an organization with a central design staff, an organization with a few decentralized analysts as well as a central staff, and an organization with all analysts decentralized to user departments.

Method of Assignment to Projects (S.D.2). This characteristic applies to centralized design groups and differentiates between staffs where analysts are assigned to projects from a “pool” as they become available and staffs where analysts are permanently assigned to particular user departments.

Existence of Other Communications Mechanisms (S.D.3). This characteristic applies to centralized design staffs. If some type of a formal liaison with users is established, the central staff is considered “more decentralized” than if no liaison exists. This may be a formal position within the central development department, or it may be a position within user departments.

## System Management (S.M.)

Three characteristics of system management were considered:

Method of Project Selection (S.M.1). In most organizations, there is some relatively formalized method for establishing priorities among systems projects. It is usually a process of negotiation between users and the information services department, and is often done in conjunction with the budgeting process. The information services department (usually the data processing manager) may make the final determination of the list of projects. In contrast, the responsibility may lie with corporate management, often in the form of an information systems steering committee. A third alternative is a mechanism by which user departments set their own priorities, through either controlling their own decentralized resources or having strong budgetary control over centralized resources.

Method of Charging for Services (S.M.2). Although methods of charging for information services can be quite complex, the focus of this characteristic is on a gross determination of the degree to which users have cost responsibility for the information systems that they used. The rationale is that the more this cost responsibility is distributed to users, the more control users will have over information services. At the extreme of centralized control, users are allocated a portion of the data processing budget in rough proportion to their use, or not at all. At the other extreme, users are charged for actual use, both for operations and development. Both examples imply centralized operations or development for which users are being charged. When operations or development are decentralized, the costs for those services usually exist in the user's budget.

Degree of User Control Over Project Costs and Project Management (S.M.3). This characteristic represents the degree to which users exert control over the ongoing activity of managing project development, regardless of how the project was selected. At the extreme of centralized control, a user department may have little involvement in project management or evaluation. At the other extreme, a user department may be directly involved in project management through approval of system specifications and evaluation of a project's benefits.

## Findings from the Study

Table 1 summarizes the results of the study, showing all cases where there was a significant association (using the $x^{2}$ contingency coefficient) between organizational characteristics and characteristics of information services. The results are discussed below.

## System operations

The results of the study show that the degree of autonomy of local sites (S.O.3) was related to several organizational characteristics. Organizations where user departments scheduled their own work and had their own computer expertise were characterized by high functional specialization (SPEC), high standardization of procedures (STAN), and high line control of their workflow (CONT). Of the other characteristics of system operations, decentralization of operating data (S.O.4) was found in organizations with higher specialization (SPEC); more decentralization of operations to local sites (S.O.2) was found in organizations with high line control (CONT). The degree of decentralization of physical hardware (S.O.1) was not found to be related to any of the organizational characteristics measured.

## System development

In those companies with centralized system development, the results revealed that the use of mechanisms to increase user involvement in the development process was influenced by organizational characteristics. Organizations with a high degree of functional specialization (SPEC) tended to employ permanent project teams assigned to particular user functions (S.D.2), thus permitting more specialization of systems analysis activities. Organizations where decision making authority was decentralized (AUTH) tended to provide user liaisons (S.D.3) to help improve communications between users and the central development staff. Although not specific to any factors of system development, the degree of formalization (FORM) was related to the degree of decentralization overall (S.D.). We speculate that high formalization of procedures facilitates decentralization of system development regardless of the mechanism used to decentralize. Those few organizations where system development was completely decentralized showed no distinctly consistent organizational style, but the very small number of companies in this class made it difficult to identify similarities.

## System management

Organizational characteristics were found to be related to certain aspects of system management in fairly complex ways. Companies that were highly formalized (FORM) tended to utilize steering committees for project selection (S.M.1) and to have some facilities for user control over project management (S.M.3).

There was a complex relationship between centralization of decision making authority (AUTH) and the method of charging for services (S.M.2). Companies that charged back fully for computer services tended to have centralized decision making authority. This was contrary to expectations. Full chargeback was expected to be found in more decentralized companies. In companies that instituted full chargeback for information services, however, this did not necessarily imply that the users of those services had decision making authority over costs.

Companies using more impersonal mechanisms for control purposes (CONT) also had greater user control over project management (S.M.3). Those companies which perceived the power of the information services function to be low relative to their own power (POWER) employed mechanisms that ensured decentralized control. Low perceived power of information services was related to both the use of steering committees for project selection (S.M.1) and user control over project management (S.M.3).

## Relationships Expected but Not Found

Based upon previous research, some relationships between information services and organizational characteristics were expected, but were not found in this study. These are discussed below.

First, contrary to expectations, few characteristics of information system services were found to be related to centralization of decision making authority within the organization (AUTH). Decentralized organizations did not tend to decentralize control over operations any more than centralized organizations. Of the system management functions, only charging for services (S.M.2) was related to organizational centralization, and that in a way that was complex and not predicted. The only relationship found between decision making and system development was that decentralized organizations tended to employ user liaisons to improve communications between the data processing department and users.

Second, under system operations, one unexpected result was that the location of hardware (S.O.1) was not related to organizational characteristics. It appears, for example, that moving a minicomputer into a user department does not by itself affect the degree of user control over applications run on that machine. Thus, the decision regarding where to locate machines physically does not have to be related to organizational structure, but appears to be determined more by physical constraints such as the geographical location of user sites and economies of scale.

Finally, no differences were found in the organization of information services across industry or size classifications. This means, for example, that large organizations were not more likely to decentralize information services than small organizations, and that banks were not more likely to have centralized information services than manufacturers.

## Questions Raised by the Study

A number of questions were raised by the study that could not be answered due to limitations in the design. These questions suggest important areas for further research and are discussed below.

First, even where the study showed a relationship between information services and organizational characteristics, cause and effect could not be determined. For example, organizational standardization (STAN) was found to be related to the autonomy of local sites (S.O.3). We could not determine, however, if organizational standardization is necessary for organizations to successfully decentralize control over local operations. Alternatively, it may be that decentralizing system operations to users causes the need for increased standardization as a means of control over information services.

Second, regarding the relative power of the information services function (POWER), we could not determine whether centralized information services departments tended to accumulate a high degree of power. We hypothesized that centralized information services departments with a high degree of power would tend to resist decentralization in order to retain that power. In the study, we could not determine if highly centralized information services departments were also perceived by users to have a high degree of power.

Finally, from the study we could not conclude when or why control over information services was decentralized to users. First, there were no mechanisms for determining the degree of user sophistication regarding information services. Second, it was not known what organizational processes affected the need for a change in the organization of information services. It may be that the need for change motivates a reorganization — from centralization to decentralization or vice versa — regardless of the current general organization structure.

## Conclusions

The goal of this study was to provide meaningful guidelines to practitioners for organizing information services. In a small study of implementation strategies [1], it was found that managers want organization-specific guidelines for answering questions of implementation. Analogously, research is needed to provide answers to the questions raised in the previous section, so that a manager can, in any given organization, determine an appropriate degree of decentralization of information services and choose the appropriate means of accomplishing it. Toward this end, some conclusions can be drawn from this study that begin to provide these necessary guidelines.

## Conclusions about system operations

1. Location of machines is not affected by organizational factors and is probably primarily an economic decision.

2. It is possible to increase the degree of user control over their own operations regardless of the location of hardware.

3. The degree of decentralization of operations is related to standardization, functional specialization, and line control within the organization.

The practitioner who wishes to decentralize control over system operations may provide user divisions with more control over scheduling and more local systems expertise to troubleshoot operations problems. This can be accomplished regardless of the location of hardware. The study indicated users had more control over operations in companies that were highly specialized, highly standardized, and used direct supervisory control rather than impersonal control mechanisms. These organizational characteristics may be necessary in order to decentralize control over operations successfully.

## Conclusions about system development

The study showed that there are several ways to decentralize control over system development without physically decentralizing systems analysts. The most appropriate way appears to depend in part on organizational characteristics.

In companies where there is high functional specialization, there is a greater need for specialization of systems analysis activities than in companies with less functional specialization. This can be accomplished through permanent assignment of analysts to all systems for a particular functional user area.

In companies which are organizationally decentralized, it is especially important that a centralized staff have good user liaisons to facilitate communications with users to promote user involvement in project development.

Of course, these two organizational characteristics are not mutually exclusive. A company may want to employ both permanent project teams and user liaisons in order to have more effective system development and successful use of systems.

## Conclusions about system management

Highly formalized companies tend to use steering committees to select projects and to decentralize control over project management. Although steering committees were seen as a mechanism to increase user control over information services, few companies using steering committees were found in the survey. Many companies had previously had steering committees but had dissolved them, claiming they were ineffectual.

The greatest decentralization of control was found where users controlled both project selection and project management. Where users were charged for services, but had no involvement in project selection or project management, control generally remained centralized.

If a manager feels that control over system management functions should be decentralized, there are several possible combinations of mechanisms to accomplish it. Project selection may be done by a steering committee made up of corporate management or it may be completely decentralized to user management. Control over project management may be decentralized to users; however, this may be difficult without the institution of formal procedures for executing project management. The use of chargeback for services may not increase user control over services unless it is used in combination with one or both of the other mechanisms.

## Closing Remarks

This study examined several alternative mechanisms for decentralization of information services, indicating when they may be appropriate and how they affect user control over information services. More research on the centralization/decentralization issue is needed. Three important questions that should be addressed in further study are suggested below.

1. What organizational pressures and constraints cause a need for change from centralized to decentralized control over information services and vice versa?

2. How do different combinations of mechanisms for decentralizing control over information services influence the effectiveness of the information system function?

3. How does the degree of user control over, and responsibility for, information services influence the effectiveness of the information system function?

## About the Authors

Margrethe Olson is an Assistant Professor in the Computer Applications and Information Systems Department of the Graduate School of Business at New York University. She holds a B.S. in Computer Science from the University of Michigan and an MBA and Ph.D. in Management Information Systems from the University of Minnesota. Professor Olson's research interests focus on the organization of information services and the impact of computer systems on individuals and organizations. She is currently conducting research on user involvement in information system development and on the impacts of office automation on organizations.

Norman L. Chervany is a Professor in and Chairman of the Management Sciences Department in the College of Business Administration at the University of Minnesota. He is currently President-elect of the American Institute for

Decision Sciences (AIDS), a national professional organization for applied behavioral and quantitative scientists working in Business Administration. He has been at Minnesota since 1967. During that time, he has been an active teacher, researcher, and consultant in the area of implementation of management systems. The management of the implementation change process is his specialty. He has published articles in Management Science, Decision Sciences, Interfaces and the American Statistican.
