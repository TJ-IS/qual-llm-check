# Reliability of Information Systems Based on the Critical Success Factors—Formulation

By: Fatemeh Zahedi
Management Sciences Department
University of Massachusetts/Boston
Boston, Massachusetts

## Abstract

This article develops reliability as a measure of information systems (IS) success based on the critical success factors (CSF). This paper constructs the hierarchical configuration of CSFs using Rockart's data on CSFs of IS executives, and checks the applicability of this configuration by using Martin's data. The article then derives IS reliability from the CSF configuration.

Reliability is a quantifiable measure useful in the control and management of IS. It provides an early warning about the quality of the IS, and identifies the areas where the trouble lies. Reliability can be used to compare various information systems, and as the numerical example in this article demonstrates, this measure could be used extensively in cost/benefit analyses of competing IS investment projects.

Key Words: Information systems reliability, critical success factors, reliability measure.

ACM categories: C.4, K.6.4

## Introduction

This article presents a method for quantifying the reliability of information systems based on their critical success factors (CSFs). To this end, it discusses the hierarchical configuration of the CSFs of information systems. Then, using the reliability theory, it develops the overall reliability of IS from the CSF configuration. The resulting reliability measure combines performance and standard measures of various components of the IS, and could serve as the system's overall measure of success.

## A review of MIS literature—why an overall measure?

An IS is a complicated system consisting of a hierarchy of interconnected resources, objectives, perceptions, and outcomes $[14, 15]$ . To guarantee the successful working of such a system, one needs (i) to identify the components of the system that are critical to the system's performance $[1, 22]$ and (ii) to measure the combined effectiveness and performance of these components in accomplishing the system's intended objectives. Such a measure aids management in managing, controlling, comparing, and maintaining IS, as well as in evaluating IS investment projects.

The first stage—identification of the crucial components of IS—has been investigated for some time. Rockart [26] introduced the concept of critical success factors (CSFs), key areas where “things must go right” for a business to succeed. Martin [20, 21] and Rockart [27] have carried out field studies to identify the CSFs of IS executives. Munro [23] compared the results obtained by Rockart and Martin and finds them complementary. However, Davis [5, 6] cautions against the use of CSFs because they rely on managers’ responses which may be “incomplete, incorrect, or insufficient.” Davis recommends further research on the use of implicit and explicit models to elicit information requirements.

A number of studies attempt to offer structural designs which overcome the weaknesses attributed to CSFs [4], and apply the concept to a number of areas, such as information requirements and planning [4], crisis communications [7], the board of directors' strategic guidelines [11], MIS planning [29], managerial control [2], and the management decision process [28].

Munro and Wheeler [24] present a general approach for identifying CSFs within the context of corporate planning, and raise the issue of performance measures and standards.

Measuring performance and effectiveness follows naturally from the identification of CSFs. Hamilton and Chervany [14] reported the need for a performance measure for IS. The MIS field has enthusiastically taken up the task of responding to this need with an impressive array of studies and recommendations on the measures of system success (as surveyed by Zmud [35], Hamilton and Chervany [14, 15], Ginzberg [13], and more critically by Ives and Olson [17]).

More recent work on system evaluation is critical of previous recommendations regarding measures of system success. “Evaluating system effectiveness in meaningful terms has been the most difficult aspect of the MIS implementation process” [14, p. 61]. Srinivasan [31] shows that behavioral and perceived measures of IS effectiveness are not always positively correlated, as suggested by the MIS empirical literature. Ives and Olson [17] reviewed 22 studies and concluded that “the measures of user involvement and system success . . . are generally weak. This is due both at the lack of a conceptual foundation to guide measurement development, and to the absence of a rigorous program of measurement validation,” [17, p. 599].

This article attempts to provide a theoretical framework for measuring system success. It uses the results of CSF literature to identify crucial components of the IS and to build an hierarchical configuration of these components from which the reliability of the IS could be derived. In other words, the paper suggests reliability as the measure of system success. The foundation for this undertaking includes a brief review of the system reliability concept.

## System Reliability

The concept of reliability is hardly new. The Bell Telephone Laboratories pioneered it in 1924 by developing the philosophy and practice of user satisfaction [25, Chapter 3]. It has risen from the practical experience of system variability. "It is important to recognize that some variability is inevitable and that there is no such thing under practical conditions as perfect precision. This variability arises in a number of ways: man, materials and machines are all liable to vary from time to time, or from place to place. It is no good shutting your eyes to this variability or treating it as a nuisance which should not exist; if we are to get good results we must appreciate it, measure it and control it," [12, pp. 24–25]. Translating this concept for an IS system, one may interpret the IS reliability as the probability that the system remains successful (does not fail) in achieving its intended objectives within a given period of time and under a given set of conditions.

In major products and manufacturing systems, reliability constitutes the most important feature, determining the price difference among products and services and monitoring the performance of manufacturing and operating systems. As IS becomes one of the major systems within an organization, it seems only natural to employ some reliability techniques to measure the performance of IS. (One may add that IS has helped assure the success of manufacturing products and services by increased reliability [9, p. 7]; it is now time for reliability to contribute to the success of IS.)

## Reliability Configuration

To compute the reliability of an IS from its CSFs, one needs to establish how the critical factors are interconnected, i.e., the configuration of IS CSFs. To explain the CSF configuration, let us consider the example of the probability of success—the reliability—of a desert expedition. Assume that interviews with the adventurers have produced the following collective list of critical items essential to the success of the expedition: bread, cereal, boiled egg, water can, tea can, gun, ax, and matches. From the nature of the items, one can establish the following hierarchical structure for the items (see following diagram).

Furthermore, although the combined list shows the critical items, they may not possess equal roles in the survivability of the expedition. To investigate the “configuration” of items, one may review the individual list of every adventurer. Assume the following lists: {bread, water can, ax, matches}, {cereal, tea can, gun, ax}, {boiled egg, water can, gun, match}, . . . From such lists one may conclude that since each adventurer has listed items related to food, fluid, and safety categories, then for the mission to survive, all three categories should remain available throughout the expedition. That is, if one becomes unavailable, the mission fails. (This configuration is called a series type.)

![](/api/attachments/WXXUJSWQ/fulltext/images/8118c6df11c42bbf45931e9fdd88c990db6466b3ab96d5bd81cfa06822777478.jpg)

At the lower level, items under each category are not uniformly listed by all adventurers. Under the food category, only one food item is mentioned by each adventurer, leading to the conclusion that the availability of one of the three food items will suffice. The other two food items constitute the back-up items. (This configuration is called parallel.) The same parallel configuration exists for fluids as well, because only one of the two fluid items is listed by each adventurer. In the case of safety items, however, each mentions two of the three items (a 2 out of 3 configuration.)

Once the configuration of the items are determined, one can use the probability of the survivability of items to compute probability of the success of the mission.

This paper uses the above approach to model the reliability of IS. The CSFs of IS executives replaces the critical items of the adventurers in the example. To reduce the degree of abstraction and to emphasize the applicability of the approach, the presentation of the method is based on Rockart's data. The paper utilizes the CSF list of nine executives in a study by Rockart [27] to develop the hierarchy of CSFs and to arrive at the CSF configuration. This structure is checked against Martin's [20] data on the CSFs of 15 IS executives to observe the applicability of the IS configuration to other data sets.

The configuration of CSFs, in turn, facilitates the derivation of a system's reliability measure from the reliability of its CSF. (Another paper [34] discusses the methods of estimating CSF reliability.) This measure may be used as a general performance measure of IS to manage, control and compare ISs, as well as a cost/benefit measure for evaluating IS investment projects.

This paper develops a CSF hierarchy and configuration based on Rockart's data and tests the results against Martin's data set. $^{1}$ It presents the use of IS reliability measure in management decisions, and discusses cases lacking quantifiable performance measures. Appendix A contains the mathematical derivation of the IS reliability measure based on critical success factors.

## CSF Configuration of Information Systems

As in any complex system, the dimensionality of an IS constitutes one of the most serious impediments in the analysis of a system's success [1]. Of the available methods, the critical success factors approach directly addresses the issue of IS dimensionality.

Rockart [26] defines “critical success factors” (CSFs) as “. . . the limited number of areas in which results, if they are satisfactory, will ensure successful competitive performance for the organization. They are the few key areas where ‘things must go right’ for the business to flourish. . . . The current status of performance in each area should be continually measured, and that information should be made available."

Based on the above definition, Rockart [27] and Martin [20, 21] have collected data on the CSFs of information systems executives. Their data show that IS executives share a similar and limited number of CSFs. Each executive in these two studies lists some, but not all, of the CSFs gathered from the sample as a whole. The lack of a uniform listing of CSFs has been attributed to organizational differences and pressing problems facing the manager at the time of data collection [27]. We believe that this lack of uniformity has an important informational value which may be used to develop both a configuration of IS and a measure to quantify its reliability. This is accomplished in three steps:

## 1. Establish an hierarchy of CSFs,

2. Build a CSF configuration within the above hierarchy from the observed CSFs, and

3. Derive a reliability measure from the CSF configuration.

## Step 2—A configuration of CSFs

According to the definition of CSFs, these factors comprise the vital components of the system. In other words, if they fail, the system fails. The critical success factors, therefore, are also the “critical failure factors.” The question, then, is how many of these factors must fail for the information system to fail.

A system with multiple vital components may have one of the three configurations: series, parallel, k out of n.

A system (or subsystem) has a series configuration when each one of the components is necessary for the system to remain operational. In this case, the system fails if any one of the components fails. A system (or subsystem) with a series configuration looks like a system (subsystem) with no back-up, a system which depends on every one of its components. Hence, its components have equally vital and critical roles in the successful functioning of the system.

A system (or subsystem) has a parallel configuration if it continues to successfully work as long as one vital component is working. This system type fails only when all the components fail. In this system (or subsystem) the components support each other as back-ups, and have a far lesser role in the successful functioning of the system than those in the series configuration.

The series and parallel structures are two extreme modes of system configuration. An intermediate structure between the two extremes is the k out of n configuration in which the system continues to work as long as k of its n vital components work $[16, 19, 30]$ .

In the hierarchy of Figure 1, for any component which has a set of subcomponents, one may ask which of the above three configurations is present. For example, at the highest level of the hierarchy, the question is whether both internal (I) and external (E) factors must work, or whether the information system will remain successful as long as one of the two factors is working successfully. Again, we rely on Rockart's data to demonstrate how the system configuration may be derived from observations of CSFs. (In future independent studies of MIS reliability, questions revealing the configuration of CSFs may be included directly in the CSF questionnaire.)

If the CSFs of an executive are categorized under both I and E at the first level of the hierarchy, then the executive has implied that both I and E are needed for IS to succeed, i.e., an observation for the series configuration of I and E factors. If, on the other hand, the CSFs of an executive are categorized under one of the two components, say I only, then the executive has implied a parallel configuration. Similarly, in cases with more than two components, if an executive's CSFs are categorized under, say, 2 of 3 possible factors, then the executive has implied the 2 out of 3 configuration.

Table 2 summarizes the configurations implied by the majority of the nine executives in Rockart's study. All of the executives had both I and E for the first level, leading to a series configuration at the first level of the hierarchy in Figure 1. At the second level, on the internal side, IM (management), IR (resources), and ID (delivery) have a series configuration according to 67% of the executives. (That is, 67% of respondents have listed all three factors as their CSF.) The same is true for the external side with EA (alignment) and EU (user satisfaction). In other words, at the first two levels of the hierarchy, every one of the components must work successfully for an IS to be successful.

At the third level, however, the majority of the executives imply less stringent configuration for the components. As Table 2 reports, 67% of the executives imply a 2 out of 3 configuration for IMP (planning), IMC (control), IMT (communications). At the same level, IRH (human resources) and IRO (other resources) have a parallel configuration according to 56% of the executives. On the external side of this level, 89% of the executives have either EAF (formal alignment) or EAI (informal alignment) for their CSF, implying a parallel structure. The flowlines of Figure 1 show CSF configuration of IS based on data from Table 2. This completes the construction of CSF configuration from the observed data.

## Test results with a second data set

Although the use of Rockart's data is intended to demonstrate the methodology rather than establish a general configuration of CSFs, it may be of interest to check the configuration against another set of collected data on the CSFs of IS executives—Martin's work [20].

Martin's reporting method for CSFs is different from Rockart's. Table 3 shows the results of interpreting Martin's categories according to the hierarchy in Figure 1. The two studies also match on the majority choice of configuration on the internal side of the hierarchy—series for IM, IR & ID, 2 out of 3 for IMP, IMC & IMT, and parallel for IRH & IRO. On the external side of the hierarchy, however, the two studies diverge. In Martin's data, the majority of 15 executives imply a parallel configuration for EU & EA, while Rockart's data supports a series configuration. On the other hand, Rockart's data strongly supports a parallel configuration for EAF & EAI, while in Martin's data parallel and series have close votes with series having a slight edge. Obviously, the results obtained here depend on the way Rockart's and Martin's data on CSFs are fitted into the CSF hierarchy. In all cases, categorizing CSFs involves a value judgement. This requirement, however, does not reduce the usefulness of the approach. As Davis [6] has commented, the analyst is not a "blank slate;" he/she is an active participant in collecting and organizing CSF data. The present approach provides the analyst with an analytical model to organize the data, as called for by Davis [5, 6].

## Reliability of factors lacking quantifiable performance measures

CSFs vary in nature and quantifiability. For those critical factors which have quantifiable performance and standard measures, one can combine such measures to arrive at the reliability of the factor. However, some factors lack a quantifiable performance measure, as observed by Munro and Wheeler: "A difficulty exists with respect to the problem of generating performance measures in 'soft' areas" [24, p. 36]. In such instances the decision maker must evaluate, subjectively, the probability (or chance) of success of the factor. In other words, the analyst attempts to elicit the preferences or perceptions of managers, users, or IS workers, depending on the critical factor under consideration.

There exist several methods for eliciting subjective probabilities and preferences. The utility evaluation methods are among the most widely used techniques $[10, 18]$ . To use such evaluation methods, one should identify the most ideal and the worst possible conditions regarding a factor. Then, the respondent is presented the following gambling choices:

Choice 1: take the present condition with certainty

Choice 2: take a gamble of having the most ideal condition with probability of p, and having the worst condition with probability (1-p).

The value of p at which the respondent is indifferent between the above two choices constitutes the utility of the component's present condition, and may be used as the respondent's subjective evaluation of the factor reliability.

For example, let us consider the User Satisfaction factor. We intend to estimate the reliability of this factor, i.e. (p9). One may offer the following two choices to the respondent:

Choice 1: take the present condition of the system with certainty

Choice 2: take a gamble in which the system succeeds, in the ideal condition, to satisfy the users' needs with probability of .60 and fails to satisfy the users' needs, in the worst condition, with probability of .40.

If the respondent is indifferent between the two choices, one concludes that the subjective probability of success for the User Satisfaction factor, i.e., its reliability $p_{9}$ , is .60. If, on the other hand, the respondent selects Choice 1, then .60 and .40 values in Choice 2 should be changed and the question be repeated till the respondent becomes indifferent between the two choices. The first probability value of the second choice constitutes the subjective estimation of the reliability of the User Satisfaction factor. Similar questions could be designed for factors such as Communication, Planning, CEO Link, and any factor for which a quantifiable measure of performance is unavailable.

In some cases, the individual evaluator of the factor may feel uncertain about the probability of the factor's success. One way to accommodate this uncertainty is to ask the evaluator to evaluate the factor once pessimistically, once optimistically, and once as the most likely case. The three evaluations may be combined, similar to that of the PERT analysis, as:

$$
p _ {i} = \left(a _ {i} + b _ {i} + 4 m _ {i}\right) / 6\tag{2}
$$

where $a_{i}$ , $b_{i}$ , and $m_{i}$ are the pessimistic, optimistic and most likely responses, respectively. For example, the respondent may evaluate the probability of success of the User Satisfaction factor as .80 at best, .60 at worst, and .75 as most likely. In this case, the probability of success of the User Satisfaction factor, according to (2), is equal to .73.

Using a single respondent to evaluate the reliability of a factor raises the question of who should assign the subjective probability—an insider who best knows the details of the factor, or an outsider who has an overall knowledge of its impact.

An alternative to the determination of a single evaluator is to ask a group of decision makers to evaluate the success probability of the factor. (Using more than one rater for evaluation is not uncommon. For instance, Edstrom [8] uses multiple raters to evaluate the perceived measure of system success and Vanlommel and DeBrabander [32] and Boland [3] have evaluators both from user and systems groups.)

Employing multiple raters may resolve the problem of choosing a single evaluator, and counter some of the personal biases which may exist in the case of a single evaluator. A group evaluation, however, raises the issue of the method of combining the group members' responses.

One method of combining the subjective probability evaluations is to find the weighted average of evaluations, where the weight of each response represents the respondent's importance to the evaluation [18]. Hence, we have

$$
\begin{array}{r c l} p _ {i} & = & \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {n} w _ {k} \bullet p _ {i k}, \\ n & & \\ \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} w _ {k} & = 1, \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

where $w_{k}$ is the weight assigned to member k of the group, and $p_{ik}$ is the subjective probability expressed by member k in evaluating component i of the system. $w_{k}$ 's may be assigned by the CEO, by the group members, or be derived from the observed data, as discussed in [33].

For example, assume that the reliability of Communication is subjectively estimated by the MIS executive as .85, and by a CEO as .65. The question is how to combine these two estimations to arrive at a single reliability value for the factor. Assume that through consultation, the MIS executive has received a weight of 40% and the CEO a weight of 60%. Applying (3) to these values leads to an estimation for the Communication reliability, $p_{3}$ , as:

$$
p _ {3} = . 4 0 (. 8 5) +. 6 0 (. 6 5) = . 7 3 \text { or } 73 \%
$$

In cases with more respondents and with more variations in evaluations, more sophisticated statistical methods are available to estimate the group consensus point [33].

## Concluding Remarks

This article attempts to present a model for formalizing the reliability measure of the information system by synthesizing the critical success factor and system reliability concepts. It was argued that CSFs may have double significance. That is, if these factors are crucial for the success of the system, then their failure would lead to the failure of the information system. Given the role of CSFs, this article uses them to derive IS reliability.

Data in Rockart's study [27] was utilized to demonstrate how an hierarchy and configuration of CSFs might be constructed from CSF responses. Next, data from Martin's study [20, 21] was used to check the generality of the configuration derived from Rockart's data. This check indicated that the configuration could be adapted to an outside sample data with some minor modifications.

Based on the configuration derived from the CSF data, this article derives a reliability measure for IS, presents numerical examples, and demonstrates how this measure can be employed in the IS project evaluation process. The performance and standard measures could be combined to compute the reliability of a factor. In cases lacking such measures, one can apply the decision analysis and utility theory estimation methods to elicit the subjective probability and preferences of managers, users, and IS workers, depending on the type of the factor under consideration.

A number of areas remain unexplored. The data utilized for this study were not originally intended for such usage. Studies exclusively designed to collect and analyze failure data should be carried out to check the configuration developed here. Furthermore, the categorization of CSFs within an hierarchy requires a more careful and detailed analysis.

The reliability measure derived in this paper contains the simplifying assumption of probability independence of components. The relaxation of this assumption would lead to a more realistic, though more complicated, reliability measure for the system. Finally, the dynamic nature of IS has remained unexplored here, and constitutes another interesting avenue for further research.

# Appendix A

This appendix derives the reliability measure of IS based on the system configuration of Figure 1 and the reliability of critical factors.

In Figure 1, since the first level has a series configuration, then the reliability of the information system is

$$
R = P \{I = 1, E = 1 \},\tag{A1}
$$

where I and E represent internal and external factors, respectively. These variables take binary values 0 and 1, with 0 representing the lack of the factor (the component not working, or a failed component), and 1 representing the existence of the factor in working condition.

In its general form, (A1) may be written as:

$$
R = P \{I = 1 \mid E = 1 \} \times P \{E = 1 \},\tag{A2}
$$

where | stands for the conditional probability. If the probability of the success of I is independent of the probability of the success of E, then $P\{I=1 \mid E=1\}$ reduces to $P\{I=1\}$ . In many cases, the probabilities of success may be interdependent, especially in cases where the successes (or failures) of components have common causes. While recognizing the possibility and importance of such interdependencies, we assume that the probabilities are independent for the sake of simplifying the analysis and as a first approximation. Hence, (A2) may be written as:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \mathsf {R} & = \mathsf {P} \{\mathsf {I} = \mathsf {1} \} \times \mathsf {P} \{\mathsf {E} = \mathsf {1} \} \\ & = \mathsf {P} _ {\mathsf {I}} \times \mathsf {P} _ {\mathsf {E}}, \end{array}\tag{A3}
$$

where $P_{I}$ and $P_{E}$ are the probabilities that internal and external factors are in a working condition. For example, if the probability (or the chance) that the internal factors work successfully is 80% and that of the external factors is also 80%, the series configuration of these factors, according to (A3), implies only a 64% probability (or chance) that the whole IS will work successfully in the period of time where the probabilities remain unchanged.

The configuration of the internal factors also is a series one. That is, for the internal factors to be working, all three components: IM, IR, and ID should be in a working condition. From the independence assumption we have:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} P _ {I} & = P \{I M = 1, I R = 1, I D = 1 \} \\ & = P \{I M = 1 \} \times P \{I R = 1 \} \times P \{I D = 1 \} \\ & = P _ {I M} \times P _ {I R} \times P _ {I D}. \end{array}\tag{A4}
$$

Again, if each of IM, IR, and ID factors works successfully with a probability of 80% (or works successfully 80% of the time), according to (A4), the probability that the internal factors will work successfully is only 51% which, in turn, has a serious consequence for the reliability of the IS system, as shown in (A3). This immediately highlights the importance of CSF configuration, especially for the series type. In this configuration type, where success of the system depends on every one of the factors, the probability of successful working of each component should be quite high. In other words, one sub-standard factor jeopardizes the whole system.

The working of IM—Management—depends on the third-level factors: Plan (IMP), Control (IMC), and Communication (IMT). Two out of these three factors must be successful for IM to work, which leads to

$$
P _ {I M} = P \{I M P = 1, I M C = 1 \} \cup P \{I M P = 1, I M T = 1 \} \cup P \{I M C = 1, I M T = 1 \}.\tag{A5}
$$

Using the independence assumption, we have

$$
\begin{array}{r l} P _ {I M} & = p _ {1} p _ {2} q _ {3} + p _ {2} p _ {3} q _ {1} + p _ {1} p _ {3} q _ {2} + p _ {1} p _ {2} p _ {3} \\ & = p _ {1} p _ {2} + p _ {2} p _ {3} + p _ {1} p _ {3} - 2 p _ {1} p _ {2} p _ {3}, \end{array}\tag{A6}
$$

where $p_{1}$ , $p_{2}$ , and $p_{3}$ are the probability of success of IMP, IMC, and IMT, respectively, and $q_{i} \equiv 1 - p_{1}$ for all i. For example, if each of the three factors work at the 80% level, the probability of success of IM, according to (A6), is .90. This value is radically different from the .51 result of a similar example for I, using (A4), where the number of factors and their probability are the same as the present example, with the only difference that the configuration of factors leading to I is a series one, while the configuration in the present example is 2 out of 3. This major difference underscores the importance of factor configuration in measuring the probability of system success.

Similarly, one may develop the probability of success for the resources factor (IR) by utilizing the configuration of this factor as:

$$
P _ {I R} = p _ {4} q _ {5} + p _ {5} q _ {4} + p _ {4} p _ {5} = p _ {4} + p _ {5} - p _ {4} p _ {5},\tag{A7}
$$

where $p_{4}$ and $p_{5}$ are the probability of success of IRH and IRO, respectively. Again, the parallel nature of these two factors renders the measure less sensitive to the performance of each factor. For example, if each of these two factors operate at 80%, the probability of success for IR, using (A7), is 96% (far better than 64% in case the configuration was a series one.)

Defining $P_{\mathrm{ID}} \equiv p_6$ , and using (A6) and (A7) in (A5) leads to the probability of success of the internal factor as:

$$
P _ {1} = \left(p _ {1} p _ {2} + p _ {2} p _ {3} + p _ {1} p _ {3} - 2 p _ {1} p _ {2} p _ {3}\right) \left(p _ {4} + p _ {5} - p _ {4} p _ {5}\right) p _ {6}.\tag{A8}
$$

For the external factor to be working, a series configuration at the second level should be in a working condition:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} P _ {E} & = P \{E A = 1, E U = 1 \} \\ & = P \{E A = 1 \} \times P \{E U = 1 \}. \end{array}\tag{A9}
$$

The external side at the third level has a parallel structure, hence

$$
P \{E A = 1 \} = p _ {7} q _ {8} + p _ {8} q _ {7} + p _ {7} p _ {8} = p _ {7} + p _ {8} - p _ {7} p _ {8},\tag{A10}
$$

where $p_{7}$ and $p_{8}$ are the probability of success of the EAI and EAF components. Defining $p_{9} \equiv P\{E \cup =1\}$ and using (A10) in (A9) result in

$$
P _ {E} = \left(p _ {7} + p _ {8} - p _ {7} p _ {8}\right) p _ {9}.\tag{A11}
$$

Finally, incorporating (A8) and (A11) in (A3) results in the measure of the system reliability as:

$$
R = (p _ {1} p _ {2} + p _ {1} p _ {3} + p _ {2} p _ {3} - 2 p _ {1} p _ {2} p _ {3}) (p _ {4} + p _ {5} - p _ {4} p _ {5}) p _ {6} (p _ {7} + p _ {8} - p _ {7} p _ {8}) p _ {9}.\tag{A12}
$$

To measure the reliability of IS at the most aggregate level, therefore, it would be sufficient to estimate $p_{1}$ through $p_{9}$ and use them in (A12) to arrive at a single measure for the system. This measure is the probability of success of the system for the period of time in which the estimates of $p_{1}$ through $p_{9}$ hold true. The first 9 cases of Table 5 demonstrate how the reliability of a system changes when each of $p_i$ to $p_9$ increases from .50 to .70, one at a time. The increases in R are not uniform.
