# Forging a Partnership to Achieve Competitive Advantage: The CIM Challenge

By: William J. Doll, D.B.A.
Professor of MIS and Strategic Management
The University of Toledo
Toledo, Ohio

By: Mark A. Vonderembse, Ph.D.
Associate Professor of Information Systems & Operations Management
The University of Toledo
Toledo, Ohio

## Abstract

Computer-integrated manufacturing (CIM) could well become the most important application of information technology for achieving competitive advantage. Recent advances in manufacturing and information technologies present promising new strategic alternatives for designing business systems. CIM enables firms to respond quickly to market changes, achieve economies of scope, and manage the complexity of a multi-product environment. Attaining the strategic benefits of CIM requires decision support to integrate marketing with design, design with manufacturing, and manufacturing with strategic positioning. This emerging technology requires a partnership of executives in engineering, manufacturing, marketing, and MIS who share a common vision of how CIM makes possible new approaches to designing business systems.

Keywords: Management, strategic planning, computer integrated manufacturing

ACM Categories: K.6.0, K.6.4

Rapid changes in information technology and manufacturing technology make possible new approaches for designing business systems. Firms are increasingly interested in managing the strategy-technology connection to develop new ways of achieving competitive advantage $[1, 19, 50]$ . Firms are attempting to link manufacturing strategy with business strategy $[59, 62]$ , to examine the strategic impact of rapidly changing manufacturing and information technology $[24, 38, 41]$ , and to find new ways of viewing manufacturing as a competitive weapon $[31, 39, 59]$ . Information technology is a key ingredient in this emerging recipe for competitive advantage through manufacturing $[40]$ .

Today's emphasis on competing through manufacturing may stimulate firms to reassess their alignment of information systems (IS) strategy with business strategy. New ideas concerning how information technology can change the way firms compete are being explored [3, 37, 44, 48]. Cyert [13] contends that U.S. manufacturers who compete against foreign firms with substantially lower labor costs have three alternatives—automate, migrate or evaporate. Computer integrated manufacturing (CIM) is the automation alternative which blends recent developments in manufacturing and information technology to achieve competitive advantage.

As firms develop CIM capabilities, change in strategic thinking will occur. CIM enables firms to build economies of scope through flexible manufacturing systems. Goldhar and Jelinek [24] describe economies of scope as economies of scale across products or markets. Computer controls, programmed production sequences, and electronic memory make feasible the application of leading-edge processing techniques to small production runs.

CIM enables firms to efficiently produce multiple products, respond to rapid market changes, adapt to shorter product life cycles and develop high quality custom designs. It helps firms decrease inventory while improving delivery performance. By enhancing decision support capabilities, CIM enables firms to manage an increasingly complex manufacturing environment $[12,49]$ . Making full use of these new capabilities requires a re-examination of the assumptions underlying business strategy.

Top management's lack of understanding of CIM and its strategic impact is a major barrier to implementing this technology [12, 23, 39, 52]. Management has approached this emerging technology cautiously—focusing on operational rather than strategic benefits [6]. Most firms lack CIM experience and are concerned about the technical problems of integrating manufacturing components into a system. Present CIM efforts consist largely of stand-alone applications such as CAD or MRP II and attempts to retrofit existing facilities. A few pilot operations focusing on low market risk projects to replace or add capacity within a product line have been initiated [21, 22, 52].

In present CIM development efforts, MIS is typically charged with an integrating role, working with process engineering and manufacturing to define database and communication linkages. Achieving full strategic benefits from CIM will require a broadened partnership of top management as well as engineering, manufacturing, marketing and MIS executives who share a common vision of how CIM makes possible new approaches to designing business systems. Developing this common vision requires a broad conceptual framework which integrates CIM, environmental analysis and business strategy.

This article provides a framework for defining CIM and its component technologies in a business rather than a technical context. A content rather than a process-oriented conceptual framework for CIM strategic planning is described. This framework identifies environmental threats and opportunities, CIM capabilities, and related marketing, manufacturing, organizational design, and information systems development strategies. Broad strategic directions are identified to formulate a vision of how CIM can be used as a competitive weapon. Finally, the implications of these changes for the MIS executive are discussed.

## Computer Integrated Manufacturing

Past factory automation efforts have focused on “stand-alone” applications of CIM component technologies. Examples include: computer numerical control (CNC) machines; robots to replace people in hard, dirty or dangerous jobs; computerized bill-of-material processing for ordering raw materials; and turnkey CAD systems that improve engineering design productivity. As a result, current manufacturing systems are often characterized as “islands of automation.” Table 1 defines these CIM component technologies and identifies stand-alone project objectives.

CIM seeks to integrate these stand-alone engineering, manufacturing and business applications into the factory of the future. Figure 1 illustrates how information technologies blend emerging manufacturing technologies, making possible new approaches to designing tomorrow's factory. A computer integrated manufacturing system provides a shared database, a database management capability, and a communications network to link engineering, flexible manufacturing and business information systems.

CIM implies movement towards integration of automated design capabilities, flexible manufacturing capabilities, and a production planning and control system $[12, 43]$ . This integration provides enhanced business capabilities which are not fully described by the standalone project objectives in Table 1.

Successful CIM development enhances the firm's capabilities to: mold separate manufacturing components into an integrated system; reduce the cost of coordinating complex systems (i.e., reduce indirect labor); improve decision making and control; and increase flexibility through soft automation [64]. The degree of implementation and integration of these component technologies depends upon the environmental threats and opportunities facing a particular firm, the firm's business strategy, its CIM resources, and technical limitations. The text which follows describes how these CIM components might be integrated to enhance business capabilities.

## Flexible manufacturing

Flexible manufacturing systems (FMS) produce a family of products. FMS uses information technology to integrate materials handling, robotics, and computer-aided process planning (CAPP) with cellular manufacturing [12, 60]. When linked with automated design capabilities, manufacturing can quickly and easily change to produce a different product within a family to respond to customer demands for new designs.

The technological window of each FMS is the range of products the system can produce. This window is determined by technical decisions (e.g., types and characteristics of machines in the manufacturing cell, the capability and flexibility of the automated guided vehicle system, and robotic components). Advances in FMS technology are likely to broaden the variety of products which can be produced [28].

The flexibility to quickly and easily change to produce different products enables firms to match production with precisely timed customer requirements-cutting lot size and inventory. The key is identifying and retrieving the appropriate tools and fixtures, quickly changing machining instructions, and ensuring the timely delivery of materials for machining the new part. As a consequence, increased emphasis on business systems for ordering and managing material flow has emerged.

## Cellular Manufacturing

Cellular manufacturing is the physical layout of the factory floor into compact groups of machines that are responsible for producing a family of parts $[25]$ . Within this compact cell, materials handling costs are reduced. Scheduling families of parts with similar machining requirements reduces set-up time. Batch scheduling of parts within a family enables machine operators to build their expertise by handling a number of parts with similar requirements $[7, 35]$ . As a result, throughput time, in-process inventory and expediting needs are reduced $[4]$ . Storing the tools necessary to produce a family of parts at the cell simplifies tool storage, transportation and control procedures. Thus, cellular manufacturing simplifies routing and materials handling.

Group technology (GT) identifies families of parts that require similar machining operations [36], thus simplifying the problems of organizing both product (part) designs and the manufacturing floor (i.e., cellular manufacturing) [11, 34]. As a result, it is argued that group technology provides the missing link in achieving the integration of design and manufacturing [18]. Group technology also plays a critical role in the design and implementation of process plans (CAPP).

Computer-aided manufacturing (CAM) involves the application of computer hardware and software. CAM provides the same advantage of a stand-alone CNC machine, but goes beyond to provide monitoring and automatic feedback to improve control of manufacturing operations. CAM implies communication links between CNC components which enable them to function as a coordinated system (i.e., a cellular manufacturing system) [23, 38]. Software that translates CAD designs into machine tooling instructions is being developed. By establishing linkages between CAD and CAM, engineers are able to specify machining instructions to pass to manufacturing.

## Computer Aided Process Planning

Computer-aided process planning (CAPP) generates routings and machining instructions. In a complex, multiple product facility, process planning requires a tremendous amount of detailed information on cell layouts, the machining requirements for each part and the capabilities of each machine. A quality CAPP database captures the special expertise that has developed through years of working with a particular product. Although still experimental, knowledge-based CAPP may be critical in determining the best processes to use in manufacturing a particular design.

CAPP is essential if the manufacturing system is to be adaptable to changes in the products, the mix of products or the volumes of particular products requested by the marketplace. For example, CAPP enables management to explore alternative routings through manufacturing cells to relieve capacity shortfalls created by shifts in product mix [9].

## Automated Materials Handling

Pressures to cut inventory and materials handling costs have increased interest in low cost and flexible means of transporting materials to and from manufacturing cells. Automated guided vehicle systems (AGVS) are more flexible than conveyors and considerably more efficient than driver-operated transport systems. Under computer control, AGVS can interface with automated storage and retrieval systems (AS/RS) to coordinate the flow of tools and fixtures or parts from storage to the manufacturing cell $[28, 46]$ . Within cells, robots play an important role in handling materials, tools and fixtures.

## Robotics

In the fully automated factory, robots play a different role than they do in stand-alone applications. As firms move towards CIM, manufacturing components will be linked by both information flows and physical flows. Under computer control, robots physically link steps in the manufacturing process (e.g., loading and unloading automated guided vehicles, changing fixtures and tools) [17, 26].

## Production planning and control systems

## Just-in-Time

Just-in-time (JIT) and manufacturing resource planning (MRP II) are two business systems that can be combined in a complementary manner. JIT can be used for short-term scheduling according to precisely timed customer delivery requirements. In contrast, MRP II is useful for longer term planning of labor availability, material procurements and capacity requirements [55]. JIT works best in an environment where these longer term requirements are known and short-term production schedules are frozen.

Although JIT is a scheduling technique, it might be more properly viewed as a business strategy for designing manufacturing systems that are responsive to customer requirements. Applying JIT forces a reexamination of operating philosophy. The JIT philosophy focuses on reducing lead times, reducing setup times and improving product quality to minimize raw material, work-in-process and finished goods inventory. Minimizing inventory uncovers hidden work flow problems, reduces working capital requirements, reduces floor space requirements and requires a more reliable manufacturing system $[27, 45, 54]$ .

## Manufacturing Resource Planning

Manufacturing resource planning (MRP II) provides an integrated decision support system that ties together departments such as engineering, finance, personnel, manufacturing and marketing via a computer-based, dynamic simulation model $[18]$ . Based upon an order forecast from marketing and the bill of materials specified by engineering designs, MRP II uses lead times and capacity constraints to create schedules for material procurement, manpower planning, cash flow requirements, production and shipping $[56, 63]$ . MRP II provides the software to monitor actual shop floor performance (i.e., costs, target volumes, quality and inventory) and adjust schedules. When necessary, this database can be used to reevaluate product and process designs in light of manufacturing problems.

Projects to implement CIM components can often be justified by their stand-alone objectives. But as a number of these islands of automation are implemented, the focus of CIM planning efforts shifts away from stand-alone project objectives toward the enhanced business capabilities provided by integrated manufacturing systems. It is this capability to integrate engineering, manufacturing and business databases into a decision support system which distinguishes CIM from flexible manufacturing.

As a firm develops its CIM capabilities, top management should forge a partnership of engineering, management, MIS and marketing executives to explore the strategic impact of this technology. Such planning efforts, which integrate CIM and business strategy, will require a vision of how CIM capabilities might be used to achieve competitive advantage.

## Integrating CIM & Business Strategy

The challenge of CIM is taking advantage of rapid changes in information and manufacturing technology to design new business strategies. Business strategy focuses on competitive advantage and emphasizes creating synergy among major functional policy decisions such as manufacturing system design and product line policies $[32]$ . Figure 2 illustrates a conceptual framework for integrating CIM with business strategy. In this framework, business strategy is viewed as the way in which a firm attempts to accomplish its organizational and manufacturing performance objectives by applying its resources to cope with the environment. Although many resources must be considered in formulating business strategy, Figure 2 focuses on the technological resources necessary to achieve CIM development success.

Strategic planning for CIM involves many of the problems inherent in MIS planning. These problems include aligning project objectives with strategic business objectives and managing a portfolio of projects which must be integrated into an overall strategic plan. Strategic planning for CIM is further complicated by the need to integrate stand-alone CIM projects to achieve substantial synergistic effects (i.e., enhanced business capabilities). In CIM planning, it is useful to distinguish between the specific objectives of particular CIM projects, the enhanced business level capabilities provided by CIM development success (i.e., CIM planning objectives) and the strategic objectives of the business [15, 16].

In discussing CIM planning objectives, Mackulak [43] highlights the need for integration. Success in achieving these objectives provides a firm with new capabilities for coping with its environment. These new capabilities can be used by top management to devise and implement new competitive strategies which turn environmental threats into opportunities.

## Environment

In formulating a vision of how CIM makes new approaches to designing business systems possible, several interrelated environmental factors must be considered. These factors include market trends (i.e., rapid market changes and increasing demand for high quality products), competitive threats (i.e., low cost foreign competition), and industry characteristics (i.e., increasing complexity and the presence of economies of scope and scale).

Increasing domestic as well as foreign competition have led to rising consumer expectations for new and better products. The result has been rapid market changes with shorter product life cycles $[47]$ . At the same time, an underlying diversity of customer needs has created pressures for high quality specialized designs. Together, these pressures have led to smaller and more finely tuned market segments.

Rapid market changes in this multi-product business environment have increased cost and complexity as firms have built large multiproduct factories. With smaller market segments, it has become more difficult to achieve economies of scale within a single product design. More recently, the literature has begun to focus on the opportunities provided by economies of scope rather than scale [24, 38].

Economies of scope exist where the same equipment can produce multiple products more cheaply in combination than separately $[24]$ . Flexible manufacturing systems enable firms to quickly and easily change to produce different products with the same equipment. The integration of CAD and flexible manufacturing systems further enhance opportunities to achieve economies of scope. Decision support systems for production planning and control enable firms to manage the complexity of such multi-product facilities.

Thus, CIM capabilities enable firms to turn environmental threats such as rapid market changes, increasing complexity, and declining possibilities to achieve economies of scale into opportunities to achieve competitive advantage. Also, CIM improvements in cost and quality should enable firms to mitigate the threats posed by low cost international competition $[5, 13]$ and increasing consumer demand for high quality products.

## Business strategies

The authors contend that five general and interrelated changes in strategic thinking are necessary if CIM capabilities are to be successfully integrated with business strategy. These changes in strategic thinking are illustrated in the business strategy component of Figure 2. Achieving this integration requires an organizational level system's perspective [57] and an appreciation of the interrelationships between marketing, manufacturing, organizational and IS development strategies.

## Marketing Strategy

In the past, the underlying diversity of customer needs had been masked by the need to develop a few standard product designs (i.e., dominant product designs) to achieve manufacturing economies of scale. CIM capability relaxes manufacturing and engineering constraints on marketing effectiveness. A variety of low volume products can be produced concurrently with efficiency that approximates a continuous flow process. Thus, CIM makes it economically feasible to develop and manufacture specialized designs for more finely tuned market segmentation and product positioning strategies. Firms will seek manufacturing economies by building volume across product lines and market segments to achieve economies of scope rather than scale.

CIM capability may enable firms to attack well established competitors who continue to rely on traditional approaches to manufacturing (e.g., job shop or transfer line technologies). By building economies of scope, job shop competitors might be attacked by providing custom designs at lower costs. Transfer line competitors might be attacked by modifying dominant product designs to satisfy customer needs or processing requirements. By emphasizing application engineering as well as low cost, the CIM competitor may be able to slice up large markets dominated by transfer line technology.

With reduced economies of scale, marketing related barriers to entry will become more important. The key to success will be building distinctive competencies to capture and maintain sales in smaller and more finely tuned market segments. Firms will employ "first to market" strategies and seek to maintain position by developing specialized knowledge and experience in customer applications. Firms will seek to differentiate themselves by offering better service (i.e., application engineering, delivery time, product customization). Competitive strategies that emphasize service, specialized designs and economies of scope may allow domestic competitors to slice up markets dominated by foreign competitors competing solely on cost.

## Manufacturing Strategy

A central theme of the manufacturing strategy literature highlights the distinction between the conventional or “complex” factory and the “focused” factory [31, 53, 58]. A factory’s focus is defined in terms of four dimensions—products, quality, lead-time and process technology.

Driven by the short-term profit motive, U.S. factories became increasingly complex in the 1960's as management attempted to spread fixed costs by adding new products and process technologies with differing quality and lead-time requirements to their existing facilities. Since the mid-seventies, U.S. manufacturing executives have become increasingly concerned about their ability to cope with this complexity.

Focused factories with consistent manufacturing policies have been advocated as a means of simplifying operations. Focused factories create smaller self-contained units which focus on a limited range of products, quality, lead-times and process technologies. The focused factory copes with complexity and uncertainty by narrowing the factory's mission and reducing its need to process information.

CIM technology has created a new view of manufacturing strategy as a competitive weapon by creating the “flexible factory” alternative. The flexible factory is more closely related to the focused factory than the complex factory. The flexible factory limits two dimensions by setting uniform high-quality standards and providing a consistently short lead-time for all products. Flexible manufacturing relaxes process technology constraints and extends the product dimension of a factory’s focus. CIM combines the flexible factory with information processing capabilities (e.g., MRP II, CAD, etc.) to manage (rather than merely cope with) the complexity of a multi-product facility.

Achieving economies of scope in this flexible factory will require an integration of the traditionally separate functions of marketing and manufacturing. Organizational design and IS support strategies must be changed to facilitate cross functional decision making.

## Linking Marketing and Manufacturing Strategies

CIM development success changes the nature of manufacturing economies in multi-product facilities by increasing the importance of economies of scope and reducing or eliminating the traditional trade-off between efficiency and flexibility. Despite these changes, marketing has had a limited role in planning CIM installations. As advances in CIM technology widen the technological window (i.e., the range of products a CIM installation can produce), marketing executives must play an increasingly important role in CIM planning.

Marketing executives must be included in a partnership of top level executives from engineering, MIS and manufacturing. This partnership should rethink how CIM capabilities can change the way in which their firm coordinates marketing and manufacturing strategies. The traditional assumptions $[29, 30, 62]$ which link marketing and manufacturing strategies are:

1) Product entry/exit decisions are made infrequently.

2) Process life cycles evolve as volume increases and products pass through stages of the product life cycle.

3) Process evolution (i.e. from job shop to batch, to assembly line, to continuous flow) requires a dominant product design and involves a continuing trade-off of flexibility for efficiency.

4) Low volume products cannot be produced with continuous flow processes.

5) Once the process life cycle reaches the continuous flow stage, few significant process improvements are made because of the large capital investment in hard automation.

6) High quality products cost more.

CIM may change these traditional assumptions. Competitors may use CIM capabilities to design custom products for finely tuned market segments, quicken the pace of market changes and further shorten the product life cycle. Industries characterized by small market segments and short product life cycles will make frequent product entry/exit decisions.

CIM makes process selection less dependent on the product life cycle. Many new products may be produced initially on a flexible manufacturing system and skip the job shop stage altogether. CIM technology enables firms to concurrently produce a variety of low volume designs with efficiency that approximates a continuous flow process. In many industries, the result will be the demise of the dominant product design. Even in industries with a dominant product design, the efficiency of CIM technology and the risk of market changes may make the investment in a dedicated transfer line unattractive.

With CAD and flexible manufacturing systems, firms can easily make product design or manufacturing process changes which improve product quality without increasing costs. This flexibility enhances opportunities for collaboration between marketing and manufacturing. Product design conflicts can be resolved by exploring alternatives that meet the needs of both marketing and manufacturing. Also, MRP II provides shop floor feedback necessary to identify continuing opportunities for product and process changes which enhance quality and efficiency. Achieving collaboration between marketing and manufacturing will require an innovative approach to organizational design.

## Organizational Design

The traditional trade-off between flexibility and efficiency had encouraged management to seek ways of buffering their technological core (i.e., production) from market uncertainty and changes $[61]$ . Buffers absorb uncertainty from the environment in order to make the technical core as efficient as possible. Examples of buffering mechanism include raw material and finished good inventories and buffer departments such as warehouse, sales, scheduling, industrial relations, etc. $[14]$ .

This traditional approach to organizational design must be reexamined. With CIM, firms may be able to achieve competitive advantage by turning environmental threats (i.e., rapid market changes and increasing complexity) into opportunities. Firms that have achieved CIM development success will have the CAD capability to rapidly respond to demands for custom designs, the flexible manufacturing capability to quote short lead times, and the production planning and control system to guide the order through the complexity of a multiproduct manufacturing facility.

The key organizational design issue is how to build an adaptive technological core which can respond to rapid market changes, cope with uncertainty, and deliver a variety of high quality custom designs with short lead times. The factory of the future will be an organic organization which has the integrated information processing capability to manage complexity and adapt quickly to environmental changes. The organization will be characterized as smaller and less mechanistic with more decentralized operations, reduced formalization, and flexible job descriptions [10]. The factory of the future will be a multi-product facility without the functional separation which characterizes today's complex factory. The functional departmentalization (receiving, warehousing, shipping, purchasing, etc.) which buffered the technological core will be replaced by a multi-product mission team with greatly enhanced information processing capabilities.

## Information System Development Strategy

Organizational design will focus on increasing the capacity to process information through the creation of lateral relations and the development of decision support systems $[20, 33]$ . Past information systems development efforts focused on monitoring and exception reporting systems $[2]$ . The emphasis was on operational level applications of limited scope which reduced clerical $[51]$ or direct labor costs and improved decision making within functional departments. This development pattern focused on achieving economies of scale by automating highly structured repetitive tasks. Development efforts have been constrained by limited data communications capabilities, little mutual understanding between analysts and user management, and a lack of systems development methodologies for designing decision support systems for semi-structured tasks. This traditional approach to developing information systems has resulted in “islands of automation” and has, as yet, had little impact on the size and productivity of middle management.

Achieving competitive advantage through CIM will require a reexamination of these patterns of information system development and their underlying assumptions. Past automation efforts have substantially reduced direct labor. Today, the cost of goods sold is approximately 55% material cost, 35% overhead, and only 10% direct labor [8]. Flexible manufacturing system are almost entirely fixed cost facilities with even less direct labor. Future IS development strategies should focus on reducing overhead and material costs.

This focus on overhead and material costs will require the development of cross-functional decision support systems $[42]$ which use an integrated engineering, manufacturing and business database to build a smaller and more adaptive technological core. Applications will focus on economies of scope rather than scale. The key is using information technology to manage the complexity of a multi-product facility.

Key applications might include supporting more frequent product entry/exit decisions, integrating product design and process selection decisions, and coordinating the revision of complex production plans and schedules in a frequently changing manufacturing mix. Without such enhanced information processing capabilities, the “factory of the future” can not be implemented.

## Summary and Conclusions

Changes in strategic thinking will occur as firms attempt to develop competitive applications of CIM technology. Rapidly changing manufacturing and information technologies will change fundamental assumptions underlying the design of competitive business systems. Economies of scope, the demise of the dominant product, the decreasing importance of market share, and the decoupling of product and process life cycles suggest a need to rethink traditional business strategies and explore innovative ways of using CIM as a competitive weapon.

Although these changes in strategic thinking may occur slowly over the next decade, they will have a subtle yet profound impact on the MIS function.

1. Manufacturing firms will increasingly realize that survival requires systems integration. The MIS function will need to greatly enhance its data communication capabilities and resources. Increasingly, application development will focus on integrating “islands of automation” within the factory and between functional areas.

2. Aligning IS development strategies with business strategy will require a substantial shift in the application portfolio mix from monitoring and exception reporting to decision support applications.

3. MIS directors will encourage the design of knowledge based systems which enable managers to cope with complexity of a multiproduct environment. The application portfolio will no longer emphasize economies of scale by automating highly structured repetitive tasks. Increasingly, the emphasis will be on economies of scope applications.

4. To achieve economies of scope, new decision support applications will have to be developed. Product entry/exit, quality management, and product design/process selection decisions will become more frequent and complex in a flexible factory.

Gaining commitment to these changes will require a top management team which understands the strategic importance of information technology.

The framework described in Figure 2 can guide efforts to design new business systems. Designed for economies of scope rather than scale, these businesses will employ focus strategies which emphasize low cost specialized designs for finely tuned market segments. As organizations attempt to build small adaptable technological cores, IS applications must enable a shrinking middle management staff to cope with the increasing complexity of a multiproduct flexible factory.
