## Abstract

This paper investigates the impact of IT investments and worker composition on the productivity of life insurance companies. The majority of previous IT productivity studies follow a technological imperative, hypothesizing a direct relationship between higher IT investments and increased productivity. This paper shifts the focus toward the organizational imperative, which views returns on IT investments as a result of the alignment between technology and other critical management choices. Specifically, the study focuses on the alignment between IT investments and worker composition, measured in terms of relative numbers of clerical, managerial, and professional positions to the total number of employees.

Hypotheses are tested using a data set compiled over a 10-year period for 52 life insurance companies. With respect to prior research, the study is novel in its adoption of a model of productivity that accounts for both separate and combined effects of IT investments and worker composition. Premium income per employee and total operating expense to premium income are used as indicators of productivity.

Study findings show that increases in IT expenses are associated with productivity benefits when accompanied by changes in worker composition. Life insurance companies that have decreased their proportion of clericals and professionals while at the same time investing in IT have experienced productivity improvements. On the other hand, companies decreasing their proportion of managers while investing in IT are found to have reduced productivity.

Keywords: Organizational productivity, information economics, IT-organizational alignment

## Introduction

Research questions on the relationship between information technology and productivity have been studied extensively since the initial application of computers in business. However, recent surveys concur that, despite extraordinary efforts, this area of research still offers conflicting findings with evidence to both confirm and disconfirm IT's productivity benefits (cf. reviews by Brynjolfsson 1993; Smith and McKeen 1993; Willcocks 1992; Wilson 1995). A positive relationship between IT and productivity is suggested by some researchers (Barua et al. 1995; Brynjolfsson and Hitt 1993, 1996; Krueger 1993; Mahmood and Mann 1993; Siegel and Griliches 1991), while other researchers propose a negative contribution from greater investments in technology (Loveman 1988; Powell and Dent-Micallef 1997; Strassman 1990; Weill 1992).

With few exceptions, these studies assume a direct causal relationship between higher IT investments and productivity improvements. Evidence supporting this causal model is appealing to establish IT as a critical determinant of productivity, but is also subject to theoretical criticism. For over 20 years, researchers have discussed how technology alone is an insufficient predictor of variance in productivity (Robey 1977). The exclusion of other variables in productivity models may distort the measurable influence of technology, thus providing results with lower explanatory value (Caves 1980). The direct causal relationship between IT investments and productivity has been referred to as the technological imperative and contrasted against the organizational imperative (Markus and Robey 1988), which views productivity gains as a result of a deliberate alignment between managerial and technological choices (Figure 1).

The organizational imperative is supported in different streams of literature. The contingency perspective points to classes of variables—organizational, human, and technological—that companies have to combine in order to compete successfully in different industries (Lawrence and Lorsch 1967; Woodward 1965). Compared to the external competitive environment, firm-specific integration of different resources, such as technology, skills, and organizational culture, underlies corporate performance (cf. Rumelt 1986). The strategy literature has welcomed these considerations and formulated the strategic necessity hypothesis asserting that IT, although a necessary factor, cannot in and of itself generate sustainable performance advantages (Clemons and Row 1991). It has been explicitly stated that performance variance across companies originates in a management difference and not simply a technology difference (Keen 1993).

This paper adopts the organizational imperative view to analyze the productivity benefits of aligning IT investments and worker composition in the life insurance industry. Focusing on a single industry is beneficial in order to gain a precise understanding of IT's contribution to productivity, by controlling for differences in competitive environment and use of IT (Wilson

## IT and Productivity in the Life Insurance Industry

Insurance companies and, more broadly, the financial services industry have historically been among the largest investors in information technology (Franke 1987). IT capital investments in the life insurance industry have exceeded 50% of total capital outlays throughout the 1980s (LOMA 1995; LOMA and Arthur Andersen Report 1984, LOMA and Tillinghast Report 1988). Insurance companies have traditionally been leaders in the application of information technology, both as a productivity tool and to stimulate new, more diversified products (Black and Skipper 1994; Huggins and Land 1992; Teal 1991). Despite IT's prevalence, few empirical works have investigated the IT-productivity link in the insurance industry.

Data spanning 36 years between 1948 and 1983 has been examined for the financial industry, including banking and insurance (Franke 1987). This analysis relates productivity of capital to capital intensity, assuming the latter to be “largely from investment in computers and accessories” (Franke 1987). The growth rate of capital intensity is found to rise from 2.7% to 9.8% a year after 1958, coincident with a sheer expansion of computer use. In contrast, capital productivity starts a steady decline from 1958 onward, from a level of 28% of its peak value in 1957. This low productivity is attributed to the limited experience of companies with information technology, and the study anticipates a long learning curve before benefits from technology introduction become apparent (Franke 1987).

A later study presents the most recent large-scale analysis of life insurance companies, based on 40 companies from the Life Office Management Association (LOMA) $^{2}$ database in the time frame 1983–1986 (Harris and Katz 1991a). That study reports that companies in the top performance quartile show a higher ratio of IT expense to total operating expense and a lower growth of total operating expense than low performers. A follow-up study shows that smaller insurance companies tend to have a higher ratio of IT expense to total operating expense, while spending less on information technology (Harris and Katz 1991b). Overall, higher IT expenses are not found to be related to greater performance, unless companies are able to “substitute IT expenses for other operating expenses” (Wilson 1995, p. 245).

Over the past 10 years, the insurance industry has experienced significant restructuring driven by an intensified competition (Black and Skipper 1994; Roach 1991). At the same time, an exponential growth of information technology capital per white-collar worker in the service sector, which, in total constitutes over 85% of the installed IT base in the U.S. has been documented (Roach 1991). Have the recent restructuring initiatives in conjunction with growing IT investments contributed to productivity from an organizational imperative perspective? If so, what are the organizational variables that interact with IT in determining productivity?

Three studies found IT expense not to be directly related to increased productivity (Harris and Katz 1991a, 1991b; and Franke 1987). However, by taking the technological imperative perspective, previous studies may have underestimated the significance of managerial action. One study considers capital and labor as the primary inputs to the production of insurance services (Franke 1987), and the other two suggest a technology substitution effect on labor in higher performing companies (Harris and Katz 1991a, and 1991b). This study argues that IT's substitution effect is not homogeneous across different classes of workers and the extent of substitution for each class is the consequence of a conscious managerial action. The paper contends that IT investments and worker composition are important managerial levers in the insurance industry and that their joint consideration may uncover a positive influence on productivity.

## Research Hypotheses

In contrast with previous studies mostly rooted in a technological determinism, this study hypothesizes that IT investments should be accompanied by corresponding changes in worker composition in order to affect productivity positively. Worker composition is defined in terms of the percent distribution of workers into three broad categories: managers, defined as workers with supervisory or coordination roles; professionals, defined as workers in charge of specialized production activities; and clericals, defined as workers in charge of administrative support activities. These conceptual definitions are consistent with Mintzberg (1993) and reflect labor division in typical life insurance companies (Black and Skipper 1994; Huggins and Land 1992).

The decentralization of decision making to lower classes of workers in the hierarchy of authority is a key objective of current restructurings in the insurance industry (Jarvenpaa and Stoddard 1994; Roach 1991; Teal 1991) and a prescription common among recent paradigms of management (Drumm 1995), such as process reengineering (Hammer 1990), virtual organization (Mowshowitz 1997), and networked organization (Nohria and Eccles 1992). Information processing theory, transaction cost economics, and agency theory discuss the role of information technology in the decentralization process (Galbraith 1973, 1977; Malone and Crowston 1994; Williamson 1975). At the same time, micro-organizational behavior research analyzes the relationship between IT and worker composition, albeit without considering their combined impact on productivity (Drumm 1995; Sulek and Marucheck 1994). Hypotheses in this paper consider IT and worker composition as intermediary predic tors of productivity whose alignment is driven by the trend toward decentralization in the insurance industry.

Information processing theory explains how environmental uncertainty related to industry changes increases the information processing requirements of an organization to operate successfully (Galbraith 1973, 1977). To accommodate higher information processing needs, organizations tend to decentralize decision making, either by creating self-sufficient units or by increasing lateral coordination across different functional responsibilities (Galbraith 1973, 1977). Transaction cost economics and agency theory observe that, while providing higher information processing capacity, decentralization causes additional costs related to the inherently opportunistic behavior of individuals (Gurbaxani and Whang 1991; Jensen and Meckling 1992; Malone and Crowston 1994; Williamson 1975). Reducing the costs associated with opportunistic behavior, agency costs, can be beneficial to company productivity in that it allows a higher degree of decentralization of decision making and, in turn, greater information processing capacity. IT can reduce the occasions for opportunistic behavior by facilitating control and, hence, decrease agency costs, favor decentralization of decision making, and ultimately increase productivity (Gurbaxani and Whang 1991; Malone 1987).

The managerial component of the workforce is central to this debate. Information processing theory characterizes decentralization as a response to increased information processing requirements (Arrow 1974; Galbraith 1973, 1977). Obtaining a higher information processing capacity involves faster, more frequent, and less structured decision making (Carley and Svoboda 1996). As the primary agents of decision making, managers can be expected to experience an expansion of their role with respect to other classes of workers as decision-making requirements and decentralization increase. According to agency theory, IT can mitigate the additional agency costs that organizations incur as decision making is decentralized, by both extending and supporting management control activities. Hence, it is expected that IT investments would accompany the growth of the managerial component of worker composition as companies decentralize decision making and augment supervision:

H1: Increases in IT investments in conjunction with increases in the proportion of managerial workers are associated with positive increases in productivity.

Research studying the impact of IT on managerial work seems to converge on similar conclusions. Both a reduction and an increase in the number of middle managers have been theorized as a result of IT introduction (Child 1984; Drucker 1988; Ellis 1984; Leavitt and Whisler 1958; Wildavsky 1983; Wynne and Otway 1983) and empirical results are mixed and often contradictory (Pinsonneault and Kraemer 1993). The main controversy is on whether IT replaces or complements managerial work, since on one hand it seems to substitute for some of the more routine decision-making activities, while on the other hand it opens new opportunities for more accurate analyses. A review of these studies leads to the proposal of a reconciling framework based on the degree of decentralization of decision making (Pinsonneault and Kraemer 1993). The argument is that, in decentralized organizations, managerial decision making tends to be more unstructured and creative and technology is used to improve and extend rather than supplant management responsibilities. Although focusing on the direct relationship between IT and managerial work as opposed to their combined contribution to productivity, these considerations seem to reinforce a heavier weight of managers as decentralization increases and technology support is extended.

Similar to managers, professionals are also attributed additional decision-making responsibilities as a consequence of greater information processing requirements and decentralization (Drucker 1991; Drumm 1995; Malone 1997). However, additional decision making may not directly translate into an increase of the professional component of worker composition. The empowerment of professional workers in decentralized organizations should combine broader professional competence with accountability over one's own output and adaptability to a broader variety of job assignments (Drucker 1991). However, professionals cannot be expected to take the risk associated with broader responsibility unless they are given the authority to make autonomous decisions and be compensated accordingly (Malone 1997). Rewards for professional workers who succeed in extending their capabilities have been found to involve higher wages and, often, the promotion to managerial positions (Bowen and Lowler 1992, 1995). At the same time, unempowered professionals tend to be assigned, or left with, increasingly standardized tasks (Rolfe 1990; Sulek and Marucheck 1994). A routinization of professional work has been witnessed in the life insurance industry, partly due to the limited innovation in life insurance products since the late 1980s, but mostly related to a conscious effort to modularize and standardize production activities (Brown 1997; Taylor and Arthur 1996). The second hypothesis summarizes these considerations and posits that simultaneous increases in IT expenses and decreases in the professional component of worker composition are associated with productivity gains.

## H2: Increases in IT investments in conjunction with decreases in the proportion of professional workers are associated with positive increases in productivity.

Research on the relationship between IT and professional work would reinforce the hypothesized correlation. IT has been hypothesized to have a twofold impact on professionals, just like on managers. On one hand, IT has a substitution effect on routine professional activities, while on the other hand IT enhances information processing capabilities that complement and extend professional work (Sassone 1992; Sulek and Marucheck 1994; Zuboff 1988). The IT-substitution effect on professional work is referred to as the deskilling effect, which leads to a reduction of intellectual content and consequent routinization of professional work (Sulek and Marucheck 1994). Deskilling, however, seems to be more limited for professional positions with greater decision making responsibility (Chia 1995; Sulek and Marucheck 1994). In a recent study, job experience and seniority were found to be positively related to specialization and to contribute to limiting deskilling (Sulek and Marucheck 1994).

Clericals represent the only class of workers for which IT has been unanimously attributed a substitution effect, as their tasks are mostly programmable, repetitive, and thus automatable (Foster and Flynn 1984; George 1986; Klatzky 1970, Sassone 1992). It must be noted that, despite this clear theoretical orientation, the only large-scale study on insurance companies notes that the proportion of clerical workers has continued to increase long after the first introduction of computers (Roessner 1985). However, early negative findings may be caused by a longer learning curve for the effective implementation of office automation (Franke 1987). Consistent with accepted theory, the third hypothesis states that higher IT investments should be accompanied by a reduction of the clerical component of worker composition.

H3: Increases in IT investments in conjunction with decreases in the proportion of clerical workers are associated with positive increases in productivity.

Overall, testing hypotheses H1 through H3 helps verify that in the insurance industry, by taking the organizational imperative perspective rather than the technological imperative perspective, IT shows a positive contribution to productivity; and IT's contribution to productivity is predicated upon the appropriate alignment with managerial choices on worker composition.

## Variable Definition and Operationalization

IT expenses, worker composition, and productivity variables are operationalized in terms of ratios as opposed to absolute values. $^{3}$ Ratios are recommended in cross-sectional studies since they provide measures which can be more easily compared across different companies (Afifi and Clark 1990).

## IT expense

IT expense is operationalized as the ratio of firm-level IT expenses to total premium income. IT expense for a company at a given year t is calculated from the amortization quotas of IT investments over a five-year period, according to the following expression:

$$

where $Inv(t)$ indicates IT investments at year t. The amortization function above assumes that the effects of IT investments peak after two years and are proportionately lower in the preceding and following years. A five-year period for amortization is common practice among companies capitalizing IT investments (Nolan 1973). Amortized investments have been preferred to lag variables to maximize the available degrees of freedom in regression models, the usable number of observations and, in turn, model significance (Afifi and Clark 1990).

## Worker composition

The conceptual definition of worker composition presented earlier has been translated into the checklists for data collection reported in Table 1. The ratios of managers, professionals, and clericals to the total number of workers are referred to as managerial intensity, professional intensity, and clerical intensity, respectively.

The different items in the checklists comply with the most commonly recognized categories of workers in the industry and include workers in both line and staff functions (Huggins and Land 1992). However, while the definition of clericals can be unambiguously understood by companies, lower middle managers and professionals may in practice overlap. This potential ambiguity has been resolved by classifying as professionals any workers employed in line activities for over 50% of their work time. Note that the sales force of insurance companies is often comprised of external agents (Black and Skipper 1994), which are excluded from the above classes of workers. However, marketing and sales employees in charge of managing a company's relationship with its external sales agents have been classified as managers.

## Productivity

An in-depth review of productivity measurement in organizations defines productivity as a "family of ratios of output quantity to input quantity" (Siegel 1986). The family of eligible productivity measures is very large, with different categories of output and input quantities (BLS 1982; Kendrick 1984; Thor 1982). A primary distinction is between currency terms (e.g., revenues) and quantities (e.g., number of items, employees), and corresponding financial and non-financial productivity measures (Lehrer 1983, Siegel 1986). While the first provide a direct dollar-figure evaluation of bottom-line productivity, the second are less subject to accounting practices and valuation techniques (Farbey et al. 1995; Siegel 1986). Analyses employing multiple productivity indicators are advisable to increase the reliability of findings (Siegel 1986; Thor 1982).

In this study, premium income per employee and total operating expenses to premium income are used as operating measures of productivity. The first, defined as total premium income divided by the total number of employees, is adopted as a non-financial measure of labor productivity of broad use in practice. The second, known as the operating cost efficiency ratio, is a standard A. M. Best indicator to assess the financial performance of insurance companies (see also Harris and Katz 1991a). Total premium income is considered as opposed to total revenue, in order to exclude returns on financial investments from the measure of labor productivity.

## Methodology and Results

The sample is comprised of data from 52 U.S. life insurance companies over a 10-year period, between 1986 and 1995. This data set has been selected from a larger sample including 250 companies over the 14-year period from 1982 to 1995. The time period for analysis has been set to 10 years starting with 1986 to calculate amortizations of IT investments over a four-year period (see the previous section on IT expense). The original sample has been reduced to include only stock companies, thus excluding mutuals and fraternals for which public sources of finance data are not always available. Companies with less than 10 years of available data have also been eliminated from the sample, in order to adequately correct for temporal effects of technology adoption (Brynjolfsson 1993; Vitalari 1985).

Data have been checked for consistency by triangulating multiple sources. The original data set was obtained primarily from a database maintained by the LOMA. Data on worker composition were then complemented and validated with information from both annual and 10k reports available on all publicly traded companies. Additional performance data were also obtained from A. M. Best insurance reports and COMPUSTAT, a database of financial reports on publicly traded companies.

## Statistical approach and methodology

Independent and combined effects of IT and worker composition were assessed with multivariate linear regression by measuring independent and interaction effects on productivity of changes in the explanatory variables (Draper and Smith 1981). Hypotheses H1, H2, and H3 are tested by the statistical significance and sign of the coefficients of the interaction variables between IT expense and clerical, managerial, and professional intensity. The coefficient of an interaction variable indicates the direction of the combined effect of the interacting variables. For example, a negative sign of the coefficient suggests that increasing or decreasing both variables together affects productivity negatively.

The generalized estimating extension (GEE) of the GLM random estimator (Liang and Zeger 1986) has been chosen for analysis as an advanced regression technique to correct for heteroscedasticity. Data is in the form of cross-sectional time series. Data across units were pooled to increase the efficiency of the estimation and obtain an adequate sample size (Ostrom 1978). The pooled sample was then corrected to deal with potential heteroscedasticity and serial correlation problems. A first-order autocorrelation has been specified within units and validated by the pooled Durbin-Watson statistic for the corrected sample. Heteroscedasticity was dealt with by applying the Huber-White sandwich estimator of variance in place of the traditional variance calculation (Huber 1967; White 1982). This alternative estimator produces consistent standard errors even if the residuals across groups are not identically distributed.

## Discussion

The interaction variables between IT expense and worker composition are all found to be statistically significant in models M1 through M4 (Table 2). The significance of these variables confirms that there is a combined productivity effect of IT and worker composition. From a statistical perspective, models that include significant interaction variables provide more accurate estimates than those assuming null interactions between explanatory variables (Afifi and Clark 1990), thus supporting the organizational imperative against the technological imperative perspective.

## Findings

M1 and M2 show negative signs for $\Delta IT$ Expense \* $\Delta$ Clerical Intensity and $\Delta IT$ Expense \* $\Delta$ Professional Intensity, and a positive sign for $\Delta IT$ Expense \* $\Delta$ Managerial Intensity. This means that simultaneous increases of IT expenses and reductions in the proportion of clericals and professionals have a positive contribution to revenue per employee. On the other hand, increases of IT expenses in conjunction with increases in the proportion of managers positively impacts revenue per employee. Overall, these results support hypotheses H1 through H3.

M3 and M4 show the impact of the same independent variables on an alternative measure of productivity, total operating expense to premium income. Interaction variables have opposite signs as compared to M1 and M2. While increases in revenue per employee indicate improved productivity, total operating expense to premium income is inversely proportional to productivity. Accordingly, positive signs for $\Delta IT$ Expense \* $\Delta Clerical$ Intensity and $\Delta IT$ Expense \* $\Delta Professional$ Intensity, and a negative sign for $\Delta IT$ Expense \* $\Delta Managerial$ Intensity in M3 and M4 also support hypotheses H1 through H3.

The negative sign of the coefficient for $\Delta IT$ Expense across all models indicates that increases in IT investments alone have a negative impact on productivity. That is, companies that increase their IT expenses without simultaneously altering their workforce composition are likely to obtain a negative impact on productivity. The main effects of worker composition variables indicate that increases in the proportion of mangers alone would contribute to productivity positively, while increases in the proportion of clericals and professionals would have a negative impact. Unlike IT expense, main effects of worker composition would reinforce the overall positive contribution to productivity of the hypothesized direction for combined changes in IT expenses and worker composition.

## Limitations

Workers have been classified as clericals, professionals, and managers, following an agreed-upon distinction in the organizational literature (Mintzberg 1993). It must be noted that this definition of worker composition is high level and may disregard the differential interaction between technology and subclasses of workers. High-creativity professional activities, such as research or design, may be enhanced and extended by information technology, as theorized by some authors and contrary to what is proposed by H2 (Sulek and Marucheck 1994; Zuboff 1988). Conversely, routine managerial activities, such as monitoring and reporting, may be replaced by technology. A finer classification of work activities would help highlight this substitution effect in addition to the hypothesized growth of the managerial component (Pinsonneault and Kraemer 1993). Furthermore, the operationalization of the theoretical definition for worker composition may be subject to discrepancies in how different companies classify their employment subcategories as clericals, professionals, and managers. As a complement to the statistical methodology adopted in this paper, case-based analysis may facilitate the collection of indepth information on more specific work activities.

Hypotheses H1 through H3 have been justified and tested for the life insurance industry. A fundamental issue is the generalizability of findings to other areas in insurance and, more broadly, to other industry sectors. The organizational imperative perspective views technology's benefits as a consequence of the alignment among multiple organizational variables (Markus and Robey 1988). Variables interacting with technology are highly industry dependent, especially if benefits are measured at an aggregate organizational level. IT and worker composition have been found to be significant predictors of productivity for the life insurance industry. Generalizing the analysis to other industries may require reconsideration of the proposed explanatory model of productivity and careful operationalization of variables.

## Conclusions and Implications

Results demonstrate that positive and negative effects on productivity may both be related to higher IT expenses and suggest a possible view to reconcile previous conflicting findings in the IT productivity literature. The negative correlation between IT expense and company productivity is consistent with previous empirical analyses of insurance companies (Harris and Katz 1991a, 1991b; Franke 1987) and with numerous studies adopting the technological imperative perspective (Loveman 1988; Powell and Dent-Micallef 1997; Strassmann 1990; Venkatraman and Zaheer 1990). The positive effect of aligning IT expense and worker composition demonstrates that the benefits of investing in technology can be uncovered from an organizational imperative perspective even when technology alone has a negative contribution to productivity.

It is important to note that worker composition is likely to be only one of several organizational variables that interact with IT in explaining variance in productivity. In the practice of business, managers make decisions on IT investments in conjunction with decisions on other organizational variables and measuring combined effects has a greater potential for obtaining usable prescriptions. However, as additional organizational variables are included in models of productivity, a strong theoretical basis becomes indispensable to support interaction hypotheses and build coherent, cumulative results (Bakos and Kemerer 1992; Wilson 1995).

Initial findings reported in this paper support the alignment between IT expense and worker composition as hypothesized. Their alignment is regarded as a consequence of greater decentralization needed to withstand an intensified competition in the insurance industry (Malone and Crowston 1994). Worker composition resulting from greater decentralization is found to grow in the proportion of managers and decrease in that of clericals and professionals. This seems to be a more costly workforce, with higher ranks growing with respect to lower ones. According to agency theory, higher costs implied by a greater proportion of managers can be interpreted as agency costs that companies incur as they decentralize decision making and intensify control over employees to prevent opportunistic behavior (Jensen and Meckling 1992). However, changes in worker composition are found to have an overall positive effect on productivity when combined with investments in IT. As the- orized in transaction cost economics and agency theory, technology appears to help compensate for higher agency costs and allow increased productivity as decision making is decentralized (Gurbaxani and Whang 1991; Malone 1987).

IT and worker composition are shown to be relevant managerial levers for decentralization and managers who are aware of their interaction are more likely to implement coherent changes. The reported shifts in worker composition indicate that as new IT applications are deployed, responsibilities should be redesigned not only at an individual, but also at an aggregate firm level. Findings show that, with a decentralization strategy, IT's effects on work result in a heavier managerial component out of the total number of employees. Insurance companies should realize that management skills will represent a more critical resource in the future and that technology will be indispensable to make the most productive use of this growing component of worker composition.
