## Abstract

This paper presents a model of the IS applications portfolio and illustrates how its “health” can be assessed through an example from process manufacturing. The health assessment is based on an evaluation by senior managers of a business unit’s portfolio of information systems. This assessment is made on five separate, but related, attributes of each system in the portfolio: importance, investment, technical quality, use, and management value.

The "Health Grid" is introduced as a way of representing the IS applications portfolio in order to facilitate the assessment and interpretation of its health. One of the advantages of using the grid is to make such an assessment explicit, transparent, and discussible. In an example, the Health Grid is used to assess and interpret the IS applications portfolio of the most profitable business unit in a large process manufacturing firm. The applications portfolio, consisting of 18 systems, was generally assessed as requiring attention. For example, there was no evidence of any relationship between the investment in a given system and its management value. The paper includes a suggested approach for using the Health Grid and an analysis of the strengths and weaknesses of the approach. A description of the changes made to the IS portfolio in the example firm after the initial data collection completes the paper.

Keywords: Applications portfolio, value of IS, IS assessment

ISRL Categories: EFO7, EI01, EI02, EL03

Why is the Health of the Information Systems Applications Portfolio Important?

The debate over the value of investments in information systems (IS) has been conducted fiercely in the popular press, the academic literature, and corporate offices. Studies have assessed the relationship between investment in IS and performance at the level of the firm, industry sector, and the economy as a whole (for literature reviews, see Brynjolfsson 1993; Brynjolfsson and Yang 1996; Kauffman and Weill 1989). The results of these studies have been mixed.

Some studies have demonstrated a positive relationship between IS investment and performance (e.g., Bender 1986; Brynjolfsson and Hitt 1994; Harris and Katz 1988). Others studies have found little or no relationship (e.g., Strassmann 1990, 1999; Turner 1985), while still others have concluded that IS investment has actually been detrimental (e.g., Roach 1988). These studies generally do not address the context of the firm, and in particular often assume that IS is used equally well in each firm. Other studies found relationships that were dependent on firm context (Barua et al. 1995; Bender 1986; Brynjolfsson 1995; Cron and Sobol 1983; Soh and Markus 1993; Weill 1992), such as how well the IS investment was targeted or whether IS was well managed. Understanding the health of an organization's IS applications portfolio may well help explain these mixed findings.

The overall aim of this paper is to present a model for, and demonstrate an approach to, assessing the health of the IS applications portfolio. The use of the model is illustrated in assessing the health of the IS applications portfolio (referred to hereafter simply as the Portfolio) of the most profitable business unit in a large process manufacturing firm. The state of the IS applications portfolio is represented on a "Health Grid" to assist in interpretation. The paper includes a discussion of how the Health Grid technique could be applied to other firms and an analysis of its strengths and weaknesses, as well as a discussion of the study's limitations. A description is also provided of the changes the example firm made in the two years following the initial assessment of the health of its Portfolio.

The paper has four specific objectives:

1. To provide a model for assessing and interpreting the health of the Portfolio.

2. To illustrate, through an example, a way to represent the health of the Portfolio.

3. To describe how the key attributes of the health of the Portfolio relate to each other in the firm studied.

4. To suggest a general approach to assessing the health of the Portfolio.

The health of the Portfolio is defined as a combination of the assessments made by individual senior managers, based on the management value, technical quality, investment, importance, and use of each of the systems in the manager's domain. Different managers are likely to judge the health of the Portfolio differently, based on their individual goals and experiences. Such differences in interpretation are inevitable, due to varying managerial perspectives and responsibilities. Thus, the initial outcome of a health assessment is intended to be informed conversation about the Portfolio, rather than a definitive and statistically defensible quantitative evaluation. In deciding how much data to gather, a choice was made to speak with top managers, who are required to make decisions about IS investments, rather than to gather data from a very large number of managers.

Assessing the health of the Portfolio is important for a number of reasons. Understanding and evaluating the current Portfolio is an essential part of the IS planning process (Earl 1989, p. 73). The health assessment provides a starting point for identifying problem areas and for locating opportunities for better coverage of business needs. These problem areas and opportunities can then be translated into plans for investments in system building or renewal.

Assessing the health of the Portfolio also provides a vehicle for dialogue between IS managers and line managers, a dialogue that has been described as all too often consisting of “two independent monologues” (Keen 1991, p. 213). The Health Grid displays evidence from a quantitative perspective that often appeals to both business and technical managers and can form the basis for discussions on where the Portfolio is (or is not) delivering value. These discussions can lead to the development of greater shared understanding and purpose between IS and its business unit customers.

## A Model of the IS Applications Portfolio and Its Health

As noted above, studies of the relationship between IS investment and firm performance have not always addressed firm context. However, the evaluation of the “success” of systems has been a major topic in IS research and has focused specifically on the context of the firm (DeLone and McLean 1992; Ein-Dor and Segev 1978; Ginzberg 1978; Hamilton and Chervany 1981a, 1981b; Ives and Olson 1984; Seddon 1997; Seddon et al. 1998; Zmud 1979). $^{2}$ The literature contains many different measures of “success.” DeLone and McLean identified six “interrelated and interdependent” categories of measures: system quality, information quality, use, user satisfaction, individual impact, and organizational impact. The model used here for the health of the Portfolio is derived, in part, from the DeLone and McLean “IS success model” and covers each of their categories. The health model expands the emphasis from one particular system to the entire Portfolio (McFarlan 1981) within a business unit.

The description of the health of the Portfolio (see Figure 1) is based on value judgements by senior business managers regarding their unit's set of systems (systems 1 to n). In our example, senior managers were chosen from various functional groups, because the business unit was organized functionally and the structure of its IS unit reflected that functional orientation. The senior managers were asked by the heads of their functional units to participate in the study, and all agreed to do so. In other situations, senior managers might have been chosen from various departments, processes, or regions, depending on the orientation of the business unit and its systems. Commitment and participation from senior managers is very important for the success of the Health Grid approach.

Many system attributes could affect a manager's evaluations of an information system. As the discussion immediately following will indicate, the literature suggests five interrelated attributes as being key:

1. Importance of the system to the business unit

2. Investment in the system

3. Technical quality of the system

4. Level of use of the system

5. Perceived management value of the system

Each system in the Portfolio has a perceived importance, a cost, a technical quality, a frequency of use, and a perceived management value. The health of the Portfolio is based on a summary across all systems of a combination of management value and the other four key attributes for each system. The motivation and evidence for identifying the key attributes is presented below.

The importance of a system for assisting the business unit in meeting its goals is a key attribute of any system. A system is more likely to be perceived as important if it is aligned (Broadbent and Weill 1993; Earl 1989; Floyd and Wooldridge 1990; Henderson and Venkatraman 1993) with the business goals of the business unit. How important a system is to the business has been suggested to be one of the factors that determine how much will be invested in the system and how often it is used.

Investment in a system is the total financial cost of the system. This cost may be paid directly by the business unit through the expenditures of its own IS unit, paid to the corporate IS function, paid to a third party provider, or some combination of these. Investment in a system has three major components: provision, operations, and maintenance. Provision is the up-front cost of building or purchasing, and then installing, the system. Provision cost also includes major modifications made during the life of the system. Operations is the annual cost of running the system, while maintenance refers to the annual cost of making minor modifications.

Figure 1. A Model of IS Applications Portfolio Health

Investment produces a system that has a certain technical quality. The technical quality of a system has a number of characteristics including data accuracy and reliability, understandability of reports and screens, code structure and modularity, response time, and down-time (Davis and Olson 1985, p. 604). Technical quality has long been suggested as an important factor that influences information systems use and performance (DeLone and McLean 1992; Lucas 1973).

Much of the previous work on system success focused either on system usage or on perceived system effectiveness (DeLone and McLean 1992; Seddon 1997; Srinivasan 1985). System usage (e.g., connect time) is a behavioral indicator often used as a surrogate for effectiveness, while system effectiveness is based on perceptions of managers or users. $^{3}$ Use is often a behavioral indicator of the value of an information system (DeLone and McLean 1992; Ives et al. 1983; Srinivasan 1985). Ives et al. suggest that both behavioral (usage) and perceived value (effectiveness) indicators should be used in evaluating the Portfolio. In evaluating the health of the Portfolio, measures of both usage and perceived value are proposed.

The management value of a system is defined for this study as senior management's perception of the outcome of the investment in the system.

Management value indicates how useful a system is to a senior manager in the performance of his or her job. Since a senior manager's job typically includes responsibility for a group, function, or process, the view of a senior manager about the value of a system includes not only its direct value to the manager personally, but also implicitly the indirect value gained from use of the system by employees within the manager's domain.

Intuitively, in a healthy Portfolio, all of the important systems will have good technical quality and be used regularly. The investment in each system will represent a sensible resource allocation, with relatively little funding going to unimportant systems or those having low management value.

The health of the Portfolio is thus basically an assessment by the senior managers as to whether investments in IS are paying off. Rather than trying to reduce five complex attributes (importance, cost, technical quality, use, and management value) down to one construct, it is recognized that each manager will weigh each attribute differently in reaching a judgement about Portfolio health. The IS applications portfolio health grid (referred to hereafter as the Health Grid) is intended to provide a vehicle to enable the assessment and interpretation of the Portfolio to be both more transparent and open to discussion.

## The Company

An example illustrates the assessment approach. The example is based in a single business unit of a multinational process-manufacturing firm with eight autonomous business units and annual sales in excess of US \$2 billion. The company manufactures a wide variety of products and operates in a number of markets. The main principles of its strategic intent (Hamel and Prahalad 1989) are to:

\- Minimize costs in all areas

\- Drive economies of scale through shared best practice

\- Achieve world class product quality

The organizational structure is designed to "promote communication and autonomy so as to achieve quality standards and operational efficiency." The relatively flat structure is divided into autonomous Business Groups, each led by a General Manager responsible for performance. The business unit studied had annual sales of over US \$300 million and more than 1,000 staff. The unit was highly sophisticated in its manufacturing operations, with large investments in manufacturing plant and equipment. The business unit was, and historically had been, the most profitable of all the company's business units. The charismatic CEO of the business unit had a marketing background and was an enthusiastic supporter of using IS as a management tool.

The Business Groups were supported by four functional Business Support Groups: Finance, Corporate Advisory, Engineering, and Business Services. The Business Support Groups provided support functions to the Business Groups and aimed to capture economies of scale and scope. The Business Services Business Support Group was responsible for personnel, administrative services, insurance, business presentations, and the information systems division (ISD).

The IS function in the firm was centralized, with ISD servicing all Business Groups. At the time of the study, ISD operated predominantly an IBM mainframe environment with more than 20 major systems, some of which were first commissioned in the early 1970s. The early mainframe systems were predominantly for transaction processing, while later systems were added to provide management information. The firm's IS environment was very complex, with large volumes of data and a relatively heavy processing load. Several of the recently developed or purchased systems, including the Office Support System, were based on personal computers.

The CIO reported to the director of Finance. Starting in the late 1980s, IT managers were appointed in the Business Groups. The IT manager in each Business Group reported to the general manager of that Group, who then reported to the Board via an assigned executive director.

Costs incurred by ISD were 100% charged back to the business units on a complex basis that included the number of connections, telecommunications lines, CPU time, and storage. All development costs were charged to business units at cost plus a loading for overhead recovery. Business units were free to use ISD, to manage their own IS, or to go outside the company. At the time of the study, one business unit was spending a substantial amount of its IS budget with external suppliers; other business units were also considering this possibility. The business unit studied had no significant IS investment outside of ISD at the time the initial research was carried out.

## Measurement Methods Used for the Study

This section describes the methods used in the study including the operationalization of the variables, the unit of analysis, the sampling approach, and the questionnaires. Descriptive statistics for all variables are available from the authors. The instruments for data collection are presented in Appendices B and C.

## Unit of Analysis and Sampling

The business unit studied was organized into seven functional areas, all heavy IS users: Technical, Bulk Product, Packaged Product, Customized Product, Materials, Finance, and Operations. In total, these seven areas used 18 different systems, although not all systems were used by all areas. The names of the 18 systems are presented in the first column of Appendix A and provide an overview of the business areas that are supported by IS.

The most senior manager within each of the seven functional areas was sent a questionnaire (see Appendix B for the items used) asking about the use, importance, and management value of each system in the area. All scales and questionnaires were piloted during interviews with firm managers prior to data collection. All questionnaires were returned completed (100% response rate) resulting in 66 independent observations. An observation $^{4}$ (i.e., the unit of analysis) is a system-functional area pair (e.g., the budgeting system used by Operations is one observation, while the pricing system used by Bulk Product is another).

The data on investment and technical quality was collected at the same time from the senior management of ISD. A questionnaire (Appendix C) was distributed by ISD to the developer or maintainer of each of the 18 systems. All questionnaires were completed (100% response rate) and a meeting was then held between a senior ISD manager and one of the researchers. Each system was evaluated for technical quality by the researcher, based on the questionnaires and the views of the ISD manager. The presence of the researcher, who could ask questions and seek clarification as necessary, provided consistency in interpretation of the measuring instruments and definitions of terms. On several occasions, respondents were recontacted for additional clarification. The systems studied included purchased packages used with minor or no changes, purchased packages that had been extensively modified, and systems developed completely in-house.

## Operationalizing the Variables

The importance of each system was evaluated using a single item (five-point scale) with choices ranging from unimportant to crucial. Each choice was associated with a behavioral anchor. For example, choice 3, “moderate importance” was described as “the system would have a noticeable impact if abandoned.”

The investment in each system included the annual costs of system provision, operation, and maintenance. The data for operations and maintenance were taken directly from ISD's charge-back reports for one year; as noted above, operations and maintenance expenditures were 100% charged back to the business units. Development and major upgrade costs were also provided by ISD, which amortized them over five years. Thus one-fifth of a system's development and upgrade costs was included in calculating one year's system cost. If a development or upgrade had occurred more than five years ago, no cost for it was included in the investment data, as the cost had already been written off in the firm's accounting system. This approach meant that the figures for investment used in this study were consistent with those reported by the firm's accounting system and familiar to the firm's managers. The average cost of each system to the business unit was \$119,000 per year.

The technical quality of each system is a multifaceted construct designed to capture how the system performs from a technical perspective. Six aspects of technical quality were identified for this study.

1. Source Code Quality. How easily the source code can be understood and maintained (Sprauge and McNurlin 1986, p. 217) is fundamental to a system's technical quality. The current state of the code was perceptually evaluated for desirable characteristics including modularity (Zmud 1983, p. 239), structure (Senn 1992, p. 417), documentation, and integration of modifications over time (Martin 1982, p. 9). A five-point scale was used, with each choice anchored by a description. For example, choice three was described as "code can be understood with difficulty by experienced programmers."

2. Data Quality and Reliability. The degree to which its data is accurate (Ives et al. 1980), reliable (Davis and Olson 1985, p. 604), and current (Kim 1988) is fundamental to a system's technical quality. A five-point scale was used, anchored at each choice with descriptions ranging from "data is not trusted" to "data could not be improved."

3. System Reliability. How well a system performs its expected function is partly dependent on its reliability (Perry 1992). Reliability, an important component of technical quality (Kim 1988), encompasses response time, uptime, and availability (Alter 1992, p. 66). A five-point scale was used asking how often the system was not able to perform its expected function. Choices ranged from “very rarely” to “always.”

4. Ease of Use. Ease of learning and use of a system is a fundamental aspect of its technical quality (Davis and Olson 1985). How easy it is for a new user to become proficient depends on a number of design issues including the help facilities, menus, user documentation, screen prompts (Burch and Grudnitski 1989, p. 262] and the general system complexity (Alter 1992, p. 67). Respondents were asked to consider these issues and respond on a five-point scale anchored at each choice with descriptions of ease of learning. For example, choice one describes a system that could only be "learnt and used by an IS professional," while choice five describes a system that can be "learnt easily without training."

5. Output Quality. Screen based and printed output is often the major product of an information system, and its quality and understandability (Burch 1992, p. 279; Srinivasan 1985) are vital. Respondents were asked to consider screen and report design, readability, and layout on a five-point scale anchored at each option with descriptions ranging from "very poor— unreadable" to "excellent— could not be improved."

6. Portability. Lack of compatibility with other systems is a major cause of problems in the technical quality of information systems (Alter 1992, p. 69). The extent to which the systems were portable (Perry 1992) was assessed during this study. However, given that all of the systems were developed on the same platform, there was almost no variance in portability, and this variable was therefore dropped from the analysis.

The score for technical quality was calculated as the equally weighted sum of the five aspects (excluding portability).

Use of an information system was evaluated by the perceived number of accesses (Srinivasan 1985) to the system by the manager's department. A five-point scale was used, with choices ranging from "never" to "several times a day."

The management value of a system was measured in terms of its usefulness to executives in performing their jobs as managers of their areas. Each manager was asked to evaluate each system according to how much management value it provided to the functions performed by the manager and the manager's group. The list of functions that managers perform was taken from earlier work by Mahoney et al. (1965). $^{4}$ Following a large empirical study of managers, these researchers identified eight tasks that “included all performance activities and ... are mutually exclusive”: planning, investigating, coordinating, evaluating, supervising, staffing, negotiating, and representing. $^{6}$

For each of the eight managerial tasks, managers rated each system using a five-point scale ranging from "system is not useful" to "system provides all information needed." A sentence describing each management task in more detail was provided for clarification. At the end of this process, the manager was requested to identify and rank which of the three tasks he or she spent the most time doing. The score on management value for each system was calculated as the weighted sum of the manager's scores for the eight management functions. $^{7}$

## Process of the Study Within the Firm

The following steps were followed:

1. One of the researchers met with the firm's CIO to explain the study and the required commitment of the firm, to understand the IS structure, and to gather background information.

2. The researcher and CIO identified the business unit that was most suitable for the study. The selected business unit

• was very profitable and sizeable,

\- was perceived as less than happy with its current systems, although little concrete information was available about which aspects were unsatisfactory,

• had a good relationship with ISD, and

\- could potentially benefit from an analysis of the health of its Portfolio using an analytical tool.

3. The CEO felt that the assessment of the health of the Portfolio could provide information on both technical quality and management value that could be used in an upcoming review of IS. One of the researchers then

\- tailored the questionnaires to suit the firm's systems,

\- distributed the questionnaires, collected the completed questionnaires, and conducted interviews,

\- analyzed the survey and interview data, and - provided feedback via a presentation and discussion.

4. Finally, the same researcher conducted a follow-up interview to help interpret the results.

Steps 1 to 3 took six weeks. Four days of interviews were conducted, and ISD managers spent several days collecting information. The most difficult data to collect was costs associated with the development and modification of systems, even though this firm kept excellent records.

## IS Portfolio Health Grids

Once the data on each system in a Portfolio has been gathered and analyzed, a means is needed to convey the results clearly and consistently. The Health Grid was developed as a communications vehicle that would be easier to use than a table of data. The Health Grid is motivated by Earl's (1989, p. 74) suggestion that systems can be represented on a grid with technical quality on one axis and business value on the other.

The Health Grid displays information on four aspects of each system: management value, technical quality, importance, and investment. (The reason for omitting use is given below.) The power of the Health Grid is to portray this information in ways that managers can then use to assess and interpret the Portfolio's overall health.

To aid this process, the grid is divided into four quadrants. $^{8}$ Systems in each of these quadrants have different characteristics, and different management action is suggested for each (Earl 1989, pp. 74–75):

Nurture: These systems are the lifeblood of the business unit. They provide high management value and have good technical quality. They must be carefully nurtured as they form a basis for the business to operate. The business unit studied had few systems in this quadrant.

Upgrade: These systems are rated highly by users on management value but are poor technically. These systems are potential business risks and require immediate attention. The business unit studied had no systems in this quadrant.

Consolidate or Eliminate: These systems have neither high management value nor good technical quality. These systems may have been of value once but are no longer useful and also are deteriorating technically. These systems need to be carefully examined for potential consolidation or elimination. The business unit studied had very few systems in this quadrant.

Question: These systems are technically sound but have questionable management value as rated by senior managers. Systems can be in this quadrant for a number of reasons. For example, the system may be a technology looking for a business application that did not quite fit. Or, it could be that users did not help to specify the system or have failed to use or support the system. Or, it could be that the system once served a business need that has now changed. These systems need to be seriously questioned. Perhaps they can be modified to add more management value or perhaps training is required to increase the use of a fundamentally good system. Alternatively they may be candidates for elimination. The general importance rating can be used as an indicator of the urgency and importance of corrective action. The business unit studied had the majority of its systems in this quadrant.

Health grids were created for each of the functional areas of the business units, based on information from ISD and the senior manager in that functional area. Figures 2 and 3 are Health Grids for the Materials and Operations functions, respectively. These two grids depict a system's management value and technical quality (on the axes), annual cost (circle diameter—one cm represents A \$50,000), and importance (shading). Use is not represented in the Health Grids. Trials in which use was represented by a number in the circles met with consistent cries of "information overload" from managers. Therefore, another useful form of the Health Grid was developed, in which importance is replaced by use.

In this example, the Health Grid for the Materials area shows six systems. Five of the systems, and most of the investment, fall into the Question quadrant (see Figure 2). The system importance legend identifies each of the systems as being of crucial, high, or moderate importance. While pricing is technically good, it is also relatively costly, and provides only limited management value. However, senior Materials management view pricing and sales forecasting as crucial to the business and, therefore, action to update or replace these systems is urgent. Overall, the health of the Portfolio was assessed by senior line management as quite poor. IS management felt that health was reasonable given the good technical quality. The Health Grid was very useful in highlighting this difference of interpretation about the same Portfolio.

The systems in Operations fell into both the Question and the Nurture quadrants (see Figure 3). Operations was one of only two functions to report high management value from some of their information systems. The general ledger, budgeting, plant accounting, and capital projects systems were all seen to provide significant management value. Furthermore, they were perceived as being of crucial or high importance to the business and are candidates for careful nurturing. The purchasing system was perceived as delivering little management value despite its importance, cost, and use (not shown on the grid). However, purchasing is crucial to the operation of the business and urgent attention was seen as necessary to rectify this situation.

Figure 2. IS Health Grid of the Materials Area

A number of other systems in Operations fell into the Question quadrant. These systems ranged in importance (and thus in urgency for attention) from inventory, which was viewed as having solid business value as well as high importance, to pricing, which was evaluated as having low business value and low importance. Despite having low business value and importance, the pricing system was relatively expensive and a candidate for elimination. There was closer agreement as to the health of the Portfolio by line management and IS management in the Operations area than in the Materials area, including the mutual identification of pricing as a system in need of attention.

In interpreting the Health Grids, managers in the sample firm found that the position of the system on the grid and its cost were primary indicators of the health of the Portfolio. Systems importance was used as an indicator of the urgency and importance of action to improve or replace the system.

## Overview of the Health of the Portfolio of the Business Unit

The great majority of the sample business unit's systems fell into the Question quadrant on the Health Grid. This pattern indicated that action was required to establish a migration path toward a Portfolio of systems that provide more management value.

This situation was probably caused by an aging Portfolio of mainframe systems that were designed primarily for transaction processing. As is typical of systems of this vintage, those in the Portfolio are not well integrated, cumbersome to use, and not flexible to meet the changing needs of the business. Some quotes from interviews with senior managers support this view:

"the systems provide no analysis ... just the raw data"

"the systems are not business oriented"

![](/api/attachments/HACPWA74/fulltext/images/04b78b1fd3487f0270b8de7d34b71192c9632e16c739f30b1135fe9736e81a02.jpg)  
Figure 3. IS Health Grid of the Operations Area

"the systems don't help me do the important managerial tasks"

The average management value of all systems across all areas was 2.0 out of a possible 5. This relatively low management value applied equally to high cost and low cost systems. Systems were generally seen as more useful for investigating, planning, and coordinating than for negotiating, staffing, and representing. Finance and Operations generally perceived more management value from the systems than the other functions, particularly Packaged Product and Technical. By contrast, the average importance of all systems was 3.9 out of 5. This difference indicates a mismatch between the perceived importance of the information systems to running the business and the management value actually delivered by the Portfolio.

The different functional areas have quite different information systems needs, and, therefore, the same system provided different levels of management value to the different areas. For example, the Operations manager rated the pricing system as having lower management value than the Materials manager. The Operations manager also rated the pricing system as having low importance, while the Materials manager thought it was of crucial importance. Therefore, effort may be required to tailor the major systems to the particular needs of each functional area.

These different perceptions from managers of different functional areas may also be symptoms of a “stovepipe” view of the organization, with each manager optimizing local performance and ignoring “cross-functional” processes (Hammer and Champy 1993). Another useful way to assess the health of the Portfolio might be to draw a Health Grid for each of the major business processes rather than for each functional area.

The perceived technical quality of the systems was rated as sound across almost the entire Portfolio, with an average technical quality of 3.8 out of 5. Systems were generally very reliable with high data quality. Ease of use was rated the lowest of the aspects of technical quality. Almost no systems appeared in the Consolidate or Eliminate or the Upgrade quadrants, indicating a strong technical base and focus in ISD.

In summary, although ISD believed the current Portfolio to be technically sound, senior business managers perceived that it delivered only limited management value. The Portfolio was fundamentally designed for transaction processing and was very reliable, with good data quality. However, the systems provided managers with limited information that was directly useful to them.

One possible cause of this situation is a lack of alignment (Henderson and Venkatraman 1993) in the firm's planning processes between business strategy and IT investment (Broadbent and Weill 1993). A lack of high-level alignment could lead to the development of systems that did not provide the information needed by managers trying to attain strategic goals. To investigate this possible lack of alignment and other characteristics of the firm, the relationships between the key attributes of the Portfolio can be analyzed further. The details of the analysis carried out for the sample firm are included as Appendix D.

## Changes Since Assessing the Portfolio

Approximately two years after collecting the initial data, follow-up interviews were conducted with the firm's CIO and other senior managers. The purpose of this follow-up was to determine the impact, if any, of the initial assessment of Portfolio health. The following changes had occurred in the business unit studied.

1. An extensive PC and LAN infrastructure had been implemented across the business unit.

2. Lotus Notes had been implemented on a dedicated server. Lotus Notes was being used for messaging, text, discussion databases, and some applications (e.g., customer complaints).

3. SAP R3 was being implemented with a dedicated server using the PC and LAN infrastructure (see Appendix A for details). The CIO explained, "Basically we are using SAP for transaction processing and Lotus Notes for information and knowledge management."

4. The management of the new systems (client and server hardware, software, training, etc.) had changed. ISD performed a facilities management role, while IT staff in the business unit managed the systems.

5. The firm had established an advisory board, with representatives from each of the Business Groups and chaired by the CIO, to oversee the data center and telecommunications activities of the central IT function. An IT Council was also established to address broader IT issues at a higher level. The IT Council was made responsible for guiding the development of IT from a corporate perspective to minimize waste and maximize opportunities.

The decision of the firm to adopt new technology was an attempt to address many of the issues identified by the patterns in the data gathered during the health assessment. For example, the new IT structure of splitting facilities management from system selection and management was a way to have line management take more responsibility for identifying the importance of a system, which was found to be a strong predictor of management value. It was anticipated that the new technology infrastructure, integrated applications package (SAP), and groupware technology (Lotus Notes) would increase management value as well as technical quality. The new hardware and software were also expected to address the lack of correlation between system investment and importance, because importance could be explicitly taken into account when deciding which systems to replace.

The creation of the advisory board and IT Council was also directly related to the health assessment, particularly with respect to the lack of connection between investment and importance. Thus, the business unit studied had, in the two years following the health assessment, responded both technically and organizationally to the findings of the study and the discussion that followed the presentation of those findings to senior managers.

## How to Use the Health Grids: Strengths, Weaknesses and Practical Advice

The suggested approach to assessing the health of the IT portfolio is summarized in Figure 4 as a four-step process. Taking into account the feedback from the firm's managers and the observations of the researchers, $^{9}$ the strengths of the Health Grids may be summarized as follows.

\- The Health Grids provided a good vehicle for measuring the components of the health of the Portfolio and for presenting them in a format that enabled both line and IT management to make an assessment of Portfolio health. The combination of the position of the systems in four quadrants and the circle size (representing system cost) was very effective in quickly assessing health.

\- The Health Grids were helpful in clearly presenting the health of the Portfolio. Many of the senior IT managers, and some of the line managers, already intuitively understood the health of the Portfolio, but Health Grids provided a clear case for action.

\- The Health Grids highlighted which systems were in need of attention from both a management value and a technical perspective. The highest priority for attention became those systems that were low in actual management value but whose intended function was rated as crucial or important.

\- The Health Grids were seen as being objective as they had been prepared by a third party.

\- The Health Grids highlighted a general trend of high technical quality and low business value which gave clues to answering step 3 in Figure 4: how did the firm get this way?

The firm's investment in producing grids was viewed by senior management of the firm as having produced a solid return given the time and cost expended to produce them.

The Health Grids also have some known weaknesses, which are summarized below. Suggestions on how to improve the process, based on discussion with business unit managers, are included in italics.

\- "Management value" as represented on the Health Grids emphasizes informational aspects of the systems and gives less weight to transaction processing.

Add questions to the management value questionnaire that directly address the value produced by the system from transaction processing (e.g., reducing costs, increasing throughput, coping with the increased transaction volume without headcount)

• Health Grids are a snapshot in time.

Perform an analysis of the Portfolio at significant transition points in an IS organization (e.g., new CEO, new CIO, major technology changes, or the commencement of a strategic IS planning cycle)

\- Health Grids do not assess the health (or flexibility) of the underlying infrastructure. Add several questions that identify the time and cost taken to make changes to existing systems or to build new applications on the existing infrastructure.

\- There is often a lot of discussion about whether the placement of the systems seems intuitively right, and in some cases people never do agree.

Before collecting the data, explain to the CIO and other senior managers the way the major variables are calculated so as to set expectations and possibly make changes.

(Note also that disagreement can be a strong benefit of the Health Grid as it generates discussion on the issues.)

Some further practical advice in using the Health Grids based on our experience in the example firm and other firms is given in Appendix E.

## Limitations of the Study

There are a number of limitations inherent in this study and to the approach described:

1. The patterns identified are unique to the firm studied. The firm was predominantly a mainframe environment and was organized by business units that were functionally structured. The same analysis could equally be applied by business process, geographical region, or to any other focus that is important to the organization. Useful insight may also be gained from applying the approach to assess the health of the same applications from several perspectives, for example business processes, functions, and geographic coverage.

2. The five-point scales used to gather managerial perceptions about system importance, various aspects of system quality, and so on, were not validated. The scales were, as described, behaviorally anchored, and validating them was not the point of the study. The introduction of these scales lays the groundwork for subsequent, more formal research approaches in the future.

3. It is recognized that many other factors that were not addressed may influence the health of the Portfolio, including political influences (Markus 1983) and the relationship between the CEO and the CIO (Feeny et al. 1992).

4. The data collected on costs was not the total business unit cost, but the IS supply costs in the form of chargeback costs to ISD. This cost did not include consumption costs by personnel in the business units. However, in this case, the consumption costs are limited to user time as the business unit had no IS investment outside ISD.

5. The study focused on the perspective of the senior manager of each functional area in the business unit. The senior manager was asked to consider his or her area as a whole when responding. However, to further understand the health of the Portfolio it would be useful to ask middle level and operational level personnel, construct Health Grids from their responses, and compare the health from the different perspectives.

## Conclusion

This paper introduces the notion of the health of the information systems portfolio and provides a model to help understand its attributes. The Health Grid is offered as a way to portray the IS Portfolio in a form that allows senior management readily to assess and interpret Portfolio health from a common base of information. By way of illustration, the health of the Portfolio of a business unit of a large manufacturing firm is assessed using a set of Health Grids to display the Portfolio's managerially important attributes. An illustrative statistical analysis is also provided.

One of the primary objectives of the approach is to make the assessment of the health of the Portfolio explicit, transparent, and open for discussion. Each senior manager will assess the Portfolio health differently based on his or her needs, responsibilities, and perspectives, and the Health Grid is useful in making this clear. Line managers may look first at management value and ignore the importance of stable, reliable systems with good technical quality, while IS managers may look first at technical quality and equate that with management value. The essence of a healthy Portfolio is an appropriate balance, in the eyes of senior management, between importance, investment, technical quality, use, and management value of the systems in a business unit. Thus, it is not advisable—and perhaps not even possible—to specify, in advance, the ideally healthy Portfolio.

The approach illustrated here assesses Portfolio health from the perspective of the customer of information systems, and thus focuses on applications. Once this has been completed, the next step might be to assess health from the perspective of the provider, that is, the IS group. Health from this perspective would include the capability and costs of the IT infrastructure, the suitability of the IT architecture for current and future business needs, and a comparison of costs to those of similar companies or external providers. In all of these comparisons, the dialog begun by the use of the Health Grid should prove useful both to the IS group and to its customers.

## APPENDIX D

## How Key Attributes of the Health of the Portfolio Relate in this Firm

With no causal model posited, an exploratory data analysis was pursued. To investigate generally how the key attributes of the Portfolio relate in this firm, first order Pearson correlations and partial correlations $^{10}$ were calculated. In addition, principal components analysis and stepwise regression were conducted to assess the overlap of the variables. The objective was to provide diagnostic information to the firm that could be used in discussions about whether to make changes, and if so how. In many cases, there are multiple plausible explanations for the patterns found. The statistical analysis is intended to be used as an adjunct to the graphical information provided in the Health Grid as the IS group and the business jointly set out to determine the way forward.

Some interesting patterns emerge that are statistically significant for this firm, supported by both first order (r) and partial correlations $(\mathbf{r}^{*})$ :

1. Important systems tended to deliver higher management value (r = 0.56, r\* = 0.51, p < 0.01).

2. Important systems tended to be used more (r = 0.4, p < 0.01), ( $r^{*} = 0.23$ , p < 0.1).

3. Systems that were used more tended to provide higher management value (r = 0.34, p < 0.01). $^{11}$

4. Systems with lower technical quality tended to be used more (r = -0.2, $r^{*} = -0.22$ , p < 0.1).

This pattern indicates that use is negatively related to technical quality. Apparently systems are used extensively despite their poor technical quality. Alternatively, extensive use of a system may reveal defects that are repaired hastily, leading to a decline in technical quality.

Two other patterns emerged that were both interesting and of concern to the firm, although there were no significant correlations with adequate statistical power for the test (Cohen and Cohen 1993).

5. In this firm, there was no relationship between investment in a system and its technical quality (r = 0.07, r\* = -0.12).

The lack of relationship is cause for concern in the firm and it is important to understand for the future management of IT projects. One possible explanation is that the complexity of the systems varied, and that complexity moderates the relationship between cost and technical quality. Complexity was not measured in this study. Another possibility is that investment produces larger systems, but not better ones; technical quality did not vary greatly across the Portfolio.

6. In this firm, there was no relationship between a system's importance and the investment in the system $(r = 0.12, r^{*} = 0.01)$ .

This is the most concerning of all the findings, because it suggests poor Portfolio health as well as a lack of alignment between investment in IT and what is considered important by the senior managers of the firm. As noted above, this pattern may indicate the need for a more effective IS planning process to better align IT investment with business needs (Earl 1989, p. 69).

The principal component analysis was suggestive but not conclusive. Two factors emerged. Factor 1 had management value, importance, and use loading with values over 0.7. Factor 2 had technical quality loading at 0.91. Cost loaded equally over both factors at 0.41. Details of the regression equation and the principal component analysis are available from the authors.

Stepwise regression analysis was used to further understand how the variables interacted. Of particular interest was how management value related to the other variables. No causal ordering of the variables was assumed. The total model of the regression equation explains 36% of the variance in management value. The only variable that explains a significant amount of variance in management value is system importance, which explained 31%. Technical quality, cost, and use were not significant factors in explaining management value.

Dropping management value from the analysis and performing the regressions with use as the dependent variable revealed some additional findings. Both importance and cost explained significant amounts of variance in use. Technical quality was not significant in explaining use.

Importance thus turned out to be related to both management value and use. Managers and their areas used those systems that they regarded as important more regularly and perceived more business value from important systems regardless of their technical quality. Systems that cost more were used more often, but were no more likely to provide management value. Regression analysis and principal components analysis confirmed the results of the Pearson correlations but revealed little new insight.

## In summary, for the business unit studied:

\- The most important predictor of management value was the perceived importance of the system. Importance dominated all other factors, including cost, technical quality, and use. Thus a critical aspect of successfully producing management value from this IS department is to determine what is important to the user group.

\- Technical quality was not a factor in explaining either management value or use. ISD ensured all systems were of reasonable technical quality. Given this acceptable quality level, marginal improvements in technical quality provided no additional management value. Thus, technical excellence does not "pay off" in this organization, and funds may be better directed to establishing what users think is important.

\- There was no evidence of any relationship between how much was spent on a particular system and its management value.

\- In this business unit, importance and cost affected use, but only importance affected management value. Managers used more expensive systems more often, but these did not provide more management value. One explanation is the lack of relationship between system importance and investment. In this business unit, systems of higher management value are apparently more likely to be produced by ensuring that IS investment is made in systems considered important by the user group.

## APPENDIX E

## Practical Advice on Using Health Grids

1. Use both the Health Grids and statistical techniques for analysis, as they highlight patterns in different ways.

2. It is not necessary to assess all systems in a business unit. Focus on the systems that are the most costly, because experience shows that small circles on the Health Grids tend to be ignored. However, Health Grids with only a handful of systems tend to be of less value, because their comparative power is reduced.

3. The Health Grids are focused on the systems that the business unit already has, not those that it wishes it had. In particular, managerial needs that are not being addressed by the existing Portfolio are not explicitly considered. However, information about those needs generally emerges naturally during data gathering and should be recorded for later integration with the outcomes of the health assessment.

4. Technical quality is more difficult to assess across different technology platforms. For the first application of the Health Grids in a given firm, try to select a Business Unit with a single platform and multiple systems.

5. Use the most senior managers of a department, process, or function to complete the questionnaire on management value. Their credibility is critical and they represent the most important internal customers of the IS department. However, because the view of a single manager is neither representative nor complete, ideally a triangulation of the views of ISD, senior managers, and line managers would be used.

6. Assess technical quality via interviews rather than with questionnaires. Some technical people may tend to overrate the technical quality of systems they are very familiar with, and it is useful to have an objective person ask probing questions.

7. Show the Health Grids to a number of both line and IS managers to get a broad perspective on the health of the Portfolio.
