## Abstract

The consequences of information system failure become more acute as organizations continue to invest in information technology and application development. Being able to better predict IS failure before implementation of a system could facilitate changes in the information system that can lead to implementation success. The realism of user expectations has been suggested as one possible means of assessing the eventual success or failure of an IS. Cognitive dissonance theory was used to hypothesize the behavior and attitudes of end users having certain expectations of a system. This experiment investigates the association between unrealistic expectations with both users' perceptions (i.e., user satisfaction) and their performance with the IS (i.e., decision performance). A longitudinal experiment was performed in which the expectations of the subjects were manipulated to be unrealistically high, realistically moderate, or unrealistically low. The results suggest an association between realism of users' expectations and their perceptions but not their actual performance. Future research should be directed toward the development of an instrument to measure user expectations, as well as toward understanding the causes of unrealistic user expectations.

Keywords: Information systems development, realism of user expectations, cognitive dissonance theory, implementation expectations

## Introduction

The lack of success of approximately 30 percent of new information systems (IS) by either failing to improve organizational processes or by being underutilized continues to be a major concern for organizations (McDermott, 1987). To lessen the risk of a failure of an information system, organizations need to be able to more accurately predict the outcome of their IS development efforts. The earlier this prediction can be made in the development stages of an IS project, the more likely it is that changes needed to facilitate a successful system can be made. A number of researchers have advocated the early evaluation of an information system in its developmental stages (Alavi, 1984; Gould and Lewis, 1985), while others have suggested approaches for this evaluation (Davis, et al., 1989; Ginzberg, 1981; Jones and Kydd, 1988).

When an information system fails, one cause may be its inability to meet the expectations of its stakeholder groups (i.e., system analysts, end users, sponsors, and customers). Lyytinen (1988) focused on one stakeholder group, the systems analysts, and found their IS expectations could be largely accounted for by their individual interests in the project and in their structural and professional positions in the development process. In a field study conducted in the trust department of a large United States bank, Ginzberg (1981) focused on another stakeholder group, the users. He examined the realism of user expectations and found that they could provide early warning indicators of IS implementation outcomes.

Definitions of expectations have been offered by researchers in such fields as social psychology and organizational behavior (Shackle, 1952; Vroom, 1964). In reviewing these definitions, two primary components of expectations become apparent: (1) a future time perspective and (2) a degree of uncertainty. On these grounds, the following working definition of user expectations for an information system is proposed: a set of beliefs held by the targeted users of an information system associated with the eventual performance of the IS and with their performance using the system.

Understanding the role of realistic user expectations in IS implementation is an area of potential interest to both IS researchers and practitioners. The purpose of this research is to investigate effects of the realism of users' expectations via a laboratory experiment using cognitive dissonance theory (Festinger, 1957) as a theoretical basis.

The paper proceeds as follows. The next section describes the research model and reviews literature on expectations from the fields of social psychology, marketing, and information systems. This is followed by a discussion of the hypotheses, the experimental framework, and the data analysis. Finally, the last section offers some concluding remarks that highlight the implications of the results from both a research and managerial point of view.

## Research Model and Related Literature

Figure 1 is a model of user expectations in the development and implementation of an information system. User expectations are positioned between pre-implementation factors and two indicators of IS success (Sanders, 1984). Pre-implementation factors are those variables that could have an impact on the realism of user expectations (e.g., developmental, organizational, individual, and environmental variables). A number of these pre-implementation factors (e.g., user involvement, management support, user training) were used to manipulate user expectations to be relatively high, moderate, and relatively low. The research reported in this paper addresses the association between user expectations and two measures of IS success, user perceptions (i.e., satisfaction) and user performance (i.e., decision quality), as indicated by the dotted-line box in Figure 1. Using definitions from Ives and Olson (1983), satisfaction is “the extent to which users believe the information system available to them meets their information requirements” and decision quality is an important goal of an IS in support of decision making. $^{1}$

A number of causes of unrealistic user expectations have been proposed: poor user/analyst interactions (Ginzberg, 1981), too much being promised based on the capabilities available (Doll and Ahmed, 1983), lack of top management involvement, analysts who do not understand the business function of the system (Anderson, 1978), unsuccessful past experiences with information systems, inexperience of the users, or lack of user education (Lyytinen, 1988). Whatever the actual causes of unrealistic versus realistic user expectations, the existence of unrealistic user expectations prompts an investigation of the association of expectations with perceptions and performance.

The association of perceptions and performance with the realism of expectations has been investigated in a number of behavioral disciplines, including social psychology (Carlsmith and Aronson, 1963), consumer behavior (Anderson, 1973) and information systems (Ginzberg, 1981; Lyytinen, 1988). The remainder of this section discusses cognitive dissonance theory and relevant social psychology, marketing, and information systems literature.

## Cognitive dissonance theory

Festinger (1957) proposed a theory of cognitive dissonance based on the assumption that individuals have a need for cognitive consistency. The theory states that when an individual maintains two cognitive structures (ideas) that are inconsistent with one another, a psychological state of dissonance will occur. The individual will attempt to attain a state of consonance by changing one of the two cognitive structures.

The point on the graph where the realistic expectations line and the actual performance line intersect represents a confirmation of expectations (see A in Figure 2). Disconfirmation occurs when expected performance and actual performance differ from one another (see shaded areas B and C in Figure 2). Negative disconfirmation exists when actual performance turns out to be less than expected performance (shaded area B). On the other hand, positive disconfirmation occurs when actual performance exceeds expected performance (shaded area C). Cognitive dissonance theory predicts that when an individual experiences negative disconfirmation of expectations, perceptions will be assimilated toward expectations, exceeding the perceptions of those with realistic expectations (see arrow D in Figure 2). It also predicts that those who have experienced positive disconfirmation of their expectations will have perceptions that will be assimilated toward their expectations and will be less than those with realistic expectations (see arrow E in Figure 2).

## Social psychology literature

Cognitive dissonance theory has been utilized to predict the effects of expectations upon both performance (social psychology) and perceptions (marketing). Aronson and Carlsmith (1962) suggested that performance on a task is influenced by performance expectations. They reasoned that an individual who had performed differently on a task than his or her expectations would be in a state of cognitive inconsistency. In order to reduce this inconsistency, Aronson and Carlsmith predicted that the individual would alter performance to be more in line with expectations.

While Aronson and Carlsmith provide empirical support to this reasoning (Aronson and Carlsmith, 1962; Carlsmith and Aronson, 1963), other researchers found it intuitively difficult to understand why an individual would seek cognitive consistency in the low expectations-high performance group by actually attempting to do more poorly on subsequent tasks, decreasing their performance to meet their low expectations. A number of replications of the Aronson and Carlsmith (1962) study followed. One found some support for Aronson and Carlsmith's results (Brock, et al., 1965), while most did not (Bruehl and Solar, 1974; Cottrell, 1965; Kornreich, 1968; Lowin and Epstein, 1965; Moran and Klockars, 1967).

For the user of an IS, cognitive dissonance theory suggests that those with high expectations of their performance using the IS should perform better than those with low expectations of their performance. But a series of empirical studies has found, especially for those with low expectations, that performance is not associated with expectations. Therefore, the expectations of the user of an IS do not necessarily predict the performance of the user.

## Marketing literature

The marketing literature on consumer expectations is particularly relevant to the study of information system user expectations because an information system can be viewed as a product or service provided to the user (consumer) by the IS department (Cash, et al., 1988; McLeod and Fuerst, 1982; Schewe, et al., 1977). Thus, it is important that the IS department develop an understanding of its customers before considering the products and the technology it offers them (Panko, 1987).

Marketing literature tests cognitive dissonance theory in relation to consumers' expectations of their perceptions about a product. Cognitive dissonance theory predicts that if a consumer has unrealistically high expectations about a product that are disconfirmed with the use of that product, perceptions about the product will be higher than for those consumers whose expectations were moderate or realistic. In like manner, consumers with unrealistically low expectations will have lower perceptions about the product than those consumers with realistic expectations. Cognitive dissonance theory, as applied to consumer perceptions, has substantial support in the marketing literature (Cohen and Goldberg, 1970; Olshavsky and Miller, 1972; Ross and Kraft, 1983) and is a generally accepted explanation of consumer behavior (Engel, et al., 1990).

For the user of an IS, cognitive dissonance theory implies that an individual with unrealistically high expectations that have been disconfirmed will experience higher perceptions about the information system than the users whose expectations are more moderate (realistic). On the other hand, an individual who initially has unrealistically low expectations of the IS that have been disconfirmed will experience lower perceptions about the IS than will a user with more moderate, realistic expectations.

It is important to note that cognitive dissonance theory does not predict which of the two inconsistent cognitive structures (i.e., expectations or perceptions) an individual will change in order to achieve cognitive consistency. In order to satisfy the predictions of cognitive dissonance theory, perceptions might change, expectations might change, or both perceptions and expectations might change to achieve cognitive consistency. Therefore, in this study, changes in expectations are measured to examine the effects of disconfirmation on user expectations (see feedback loop in Figure 1). Further, in order to test the effects of repeated disconfirmation on expectations (Olson and Dover, 1979), the dependent variables are measured in each of three decision periods.

## Information systems literature

In a survey of 33 information systems managers, user expectations were ranked first in a list of major problems facing IS departments and in the importance to the success of the department (Doll and Ahmed, 1983). In a test of expectancy theory (Vroom, 1964), DeSanctis (1983) measured users' expectancies and found them related to system use. In addition, Rushinek and Rushinek (1986) discovered that user expectations exhibit a strong association with overall satisfaction. Recently, expectations about the outcomes (i.e., changes in task effectiveness, changes in media use) of different communication technologies have begun to play a role in media use research. For example, in studies of electronic messaging systems, expectations about the outcomes of EMS have been found to endure and influence later evaluations of that medium (Hiltz, 1983; Rice and Case, 1983; Rice, et al., 1990; Rogers, 1983; Torobin, 1987).

While user expectations are important, some clarify this notion by stressing that it is the realism of user expectations that is potentially a factor in IS success or failure. Gibson (1977) asserts that user involvement in the design of an information system brings about realistic expectations of system capabilities. The key is that the interaction of user and analyst during the development process generates realistic user expectations (Doll and Ahmed, 1983).

The consequences of the realism of user expectations have been the subject of one empirical study (Ginzberg, 1981). Ginzberg conducted a field study where the realism of user expectations was defined and measured as the difference between user expectations for the information system and the systems analysts' expectations. Ginzberg found that the realism of user expectations: (1) was significantly correlated with user attitudes and usage of the information system and (2) explained the success or failure of an information system better than several other pre-implementation factors (i.e., user involvement and management support).

While his investigation did not test any theory, Ginzberg's results indicate that unrealistic expectations could lead to users exhibiting symptoms of system failure (i.e., low satisfaction and usage). Ginzberg's main emphasis is on unrealistic expectations and not on the direction of the unrealistic expectations.

The research reported in this paper builds upon the work of Ginzberg (1981) by utilizing a laboratory experiment design based on cognitive dissonance theory. The experimental setting provides the controlled environment needed for theory testing, while cognitive dissonance theory predicts the relationship of expectations with performance and perceptions. An additional component of user expectations, the direction of unrealistic expectations, is reflected in the independent variable, which involves three levels of user expectations: unrealistically high, realistically moderate, and unrealistically low.

## Hypotheses

Two main hypotheses are proposed. Hypothesis 1 tests the predictions of cognitive dissonance theory by investigating differences in the dependent variables across the three user expectations groups for each of the three decision periods. Hypothesis 2 examines the effects of repeated disconfirmation on the dependent variables across the three decision periods for each of the three user expectations groups.

## Association with user satisfaction

H1a. Within each decision period, subjects with unrealistically high expectations will have higher user satisfaction scores than subjects with moderate (realistic) expectations.

Within each decision period, subjects with unrealistically low expectations will have lower user satisfaction scores than subjects with moderate (realistic) expectations.

In the same way that consumer expectations have been shown to influence an individual's perceptions of product performance, so are IS user expectations proposed to influence user satisfaction. Cognitive dissonance theory predicts, and it has been empirically supported, that those consumers with unrealistically high expectations of a product's performance experience higher perceptions of the product's performance after use of the product than those individuals with more moderate, realistic expectations. Likewise, those individuals with unrealistically low expectations have been shown to experience perceptions of a product after use that are lower than those individuals with more moderate, realistic expectations for the product. The same predictions have been applied to users of an information system in Hypothesis 1a.

Association with decision performance

H1b. There will be no difference in decision performance across the three expectations groups.

While consumer expectations research deals with the perceptions of product performance, the social psychology literature addresses the consequences of unrealistic expectations on actual performance. After a decade of empirical studies in social psychology, no association has been found between performance and expectations. Therefore, no difference in decision performance across the three levels of expectations is to be expected.

## Effects on user satisfaction over time

H2a: Over time, subjects with unrealistically high expectations will have lower user satisfaction scores than they did in preceding decision periods.

Over time, subjects with unrealistically low expectations will have higher user satisfaction scores than they did in preceding decision periods.

Over time, subjects with moderate expectations will experience the same user satisfaction scores as they did in the preceding decision periods.

In both the social psychology literature (Atkinson, 1964) and consumer behavior literature (Olson and Dover, 1979) there have been calls for investigating the effects of repeated disconfirmation. Therefore, while cognitive dissonance theory predicts higher perceptions (i.e., user satisfaction) of the IS for those with unrealistically high expectations relative to those with moderate expectations, it is expected that repeated use of the IS will result in a decrease in those perceptions as the effects of expectations “wear off.” Likewise, cognitive dissonance theory would predict that those with unrealistically low expectations experiencing lower perceptions of the IS than those with moderate expectations will increase those perceptions (user satisfaction) after repeated uses of the IS. The moderate expectations group is not expected to experience any change in perceptions since their expectations are regarded as realistic.

## Effects on decision performance over time

H2b. There will be no difference in any change in decision performance for the expectations groups across the three decision periods.

As discussed in Hypothesis 1b, it is expected that cognitive dissonance theory will not predict performance. Therefore, the subjects in all three expectations groups should experience the same differences in decision performance (if any) across the three decision periods. In other words, if there is an increase in decision performance due to learning effects, the three expectations groups should each experience a comparable increase.

## Effects on expectations over time

H2c. Over time, subjects with unrealistically high expectations will have lower expectations than they did in preceding decision periods.

Over time, subjects with unrealistically low expectations will have higher expectations than they did in preceding decision periods.

Over time, subjects with moderate expectations will have the same expectations as they did in preceding decision periods.

With repeated disconfirmation of expectations, the expectations of the unrealistically high expectations group are predicted to decrease and approach the level of the moderate group, whose expectations are considered to be realistic. In other words, repeated disconfirmation of expectations should bring about more realistic expectations, which would result in a decrease of expectations in the unrealistically high expectations group and an increase in expectations in the unrealistically low expectations group. The subjects with initially realistic, moderate expectations are predicted to experience no change in their expectations over the three decision periods because their initial expectations were realistic.

## Methods

In order to investigate the association of expectations with user perceptions and performance, a laboratory experiment was employed. A behavioral manipulation of the subjects was used as the mechanism to establish three expectations groups. The dependent variables include user satisfaction, decision performance, and changes in expectations.

## Decision-making task

The task used in this experiment is the “brand manager’s allocation problem” (Benbasat, et al., 1986; McIntyre, 1982), where an assigned promotional budget must be allocated to three sales areas. Each of the three sales areas had an independent response function that related promotional dollars to profit and took the form:

$$
\begin{array}{l l} \text {PROFIT} = t _ {\min} + t _ {s} (a x ^ {b} / (1 + a x ^ {b})) - x \\ \text {where:} \\ x & = \text {promotional dollars allocated to the sales area} \\ t _ {\min} & = \text {sales in the area when x = 0} \\ t _ {s} & = \text {saturation sales minus t} _ {\min} \text {in the area} \\ a, b & = \text {parameters} \end{array}
$$

Three separate decision-making scenarios were used, one for each of the three decision periods. Each scenario had different promotional budget and response function parameters, causing each to have a different optimal solution.

The subjects played the role of regional sales managers for a national manufacturing firm, each of whom was responsible for allocating an assigned promotional budget to three sales areas in an effort to maximize the profit for his or her sales region. The information services department of the firm implemented a spreadsheet-based information system to assist them in this task by allowing both the display of data and the entry of the allocation decisions.

The subjects were able to choose from two sets of data in two presentation formats: tabular and graphic. One set, the “historical” data set, depicted historical promotional dollars, sales, and profit for the previous five decision periods, while the other set, referred to as the “functional” data set, contained the expected profit in each of the three sales areas based on the number of promotional dollars allocated to that area. The purpose of the second data set was to help the regional sales manager determine the optimal allocation of promotional dollars to each of the three sales areas to achieve a maximum total profit for the sales region. While the graphical format provided a picture suggesting the heuristic involved in arriving at the optimal solution, the tabular format permitted the subjects to determine the precise allocation necessary to maximize total regional profit.

## Independent variable

The independent variable was the realism of user expectations. User expectations were manipulated to be relatively high, moderate, and relatively low through the use of three different 13-page written case presentations. $^{2}$ Each case described the organization, functional department (i.e., marketing), information services department, and the information system to be used. The cases also included a communication from the user (a regional sales manager named Joe; see Appendix A) who represented the targeted users (i.e., the other regional sales managers) in the development of the system. A variety of pre-implementation factors was collectively described in each case to manipulate user expectations. These factors were those found or suggested in the IS literature to influence information systems success. Table 1 outlines the principal factors and briefly illustrates how each was operationalized for the high, moderate, and low expectations cases.

For example, consider user involvement, a pre-implementation factor believed to influence the realism of users' expectations (Gibson, 1977; Ginzberg, 1981). Ives and Olson (1984) suggest six degrees of user involvement (Table 2). Schonberger (1980) also suggested degrees of user involvement in an IS development project that took the form of three levels. Schonberger's three levels correspond readily to Ives and Olson's six degrees, as depicted in Table 2. The last column in Table 2 indicates how Schonberger's classification of the levels of user involvement was used in the three case presentations for the manipulation of user expectations. For example, in the case for the low expectations group, the representative user, Joe, assigned to be involved in the development of this project, had very little real involvement, whereas in the case for the high expectations group, Joe was described as having control over the project (see Appendix A for excerpts from each of the cases for the user involvement pre-implementation factor).

Thus, the assumptions were that (1) the relatively high expectations group would experience negative disconfirmation of their expectations, (2) the moderate expectations group would have their expectations confirmed, and (3) the low expectations group would experience positive disconfirmation of their expectations. In short, both the high and low expectations groups were assumed to have unrealistic expectations, while the moderate expectations group was assumed to have realistic expectations.

## Sample

The sample consisted of 159 undergraduate business students enrolled in an introductory MIS course, with each subject randomly assigned to one of the three expectations groups. Each subject in an expectations group received the written case presentation prepared for that group.

## Results

To simplify discussion of the results, the subjects in the relatively high expectations group will be referred to as group $E_{h}$ , those in the moderate expectations group will be group $E_{m}$ , and those in the relatively low expectations group will be group $E_{i}$ . Table 4 outlines the characteristics of the subjects in the sample. It also includes the mean score on the 15-item case knowledge test for the entire sample (87.55 percent). $^{3}$

An analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare means across the three expectations groups for each of the specific hypotheses in Hypothesis 1. When the ANOVA yielded a significant result, pairwise comparisons of the expectations groups using the least significant difference (LSD) method were employed in order to investigate the differences among expectations groups on a particular dependent variable (Cramer and Bock, 1966; Spector, 1977). For Hypotheses 2a and 2c, a repeated measures ANOVA was used to compare means across the three decision periods for satisfaction and expectations. When this ANOVA yielded a significant result, contrasts were used to analyze pairs of decision periods. ANOVA based on differences scores with respect to decision performance was used in conjunction with Hypothesis 2b.

A manipulation check was performed prior to decision periods 1, 2, and 3. The results of an analysis of variance to verify the difference in manipulated expectations among the three groups appear in Table 5. For expectations prior to all three decision periods, a significance level less than 0.001 indicates that the three expectations groups were significantly different from one another. Since one of the purposes of this experiment was to determine differences among those with realistic expectations (group $E_{m}$ ) and unrealistic expectations (groups $E_{h}$ and $E_{l}$ ), differences that are relevant are those between groups $E_{h}$ and $E_{m}$ and between $E_{m}$ and $E_{l}$ .

The ranges for the manipulation check for each expectations group were: $E_{h}$ (15-28), $E_{m}$ (12-26), and $E_{l}$ (4-26). As might be expected from the relatively large standard deviation in group $E_{l}$ , the range for group $E_{l}$ was also relatively large. It is believed that the students used as subjects in this experiment may be responsible for the relatively large variation in $E_{l}$ . Even though the case presentation given to the subjects in group $E_{l}$ described a poor information systems situation, some of the subjects had little experience with information systems. Consequently, they had few experiences with which to compare their case presentation other than those acquired in the classroom. For example, there were eight subjects in group $E_{l}$ who had manipulation check scores above the mean of group $E_{m}$ (19.22), with 2 subjects having a score above 24. However, of most relevance in an experiment employing a behavioral manipulation is that the mean scores of the groups be significantly different from one another on the manipulation check. Table 5 shows this to be the case. In addition, further data analyses were performed to measure the effects of eliminating those subjects from further consideration who had initial expectations scores that overlapped the mean expectations score of the adjacent groups. The results from these analyses were not different from those presented later in this section.

The reliability scores (Cronbach's alpha) of the user satisfaction and expectations questionnaires used throughout this experiment were all above Nunnally's (1978) recommended lower limit of 0.80. ## Hypothesis 1: Across groups (within decision period)

Hypothesis 1a predicted that perceptions would be influenced by the level of users' expectations, with group $E_h$ having higher user satisfaction than group $E_m$ and group $E_i$ having lower user satisfaction than group $E_m$ . These predictions were based on cognitive dissonance theory. For decision period 1, the F-statistic (27.14) from the ANOVA of user satisfaction (see Table 6) is significant at a level less than 0.001. The paired comparisons indicate that the user satisfaction score of group $E_{h}$ is significantly higher than that of group $E_{m}$ and that the user satisfaction score of group $E_{l}$ is significantly lower than that of group $E_{m}$ , thus supporting Hypothesis 1a for decision period 1.

These results provide support for cognitive dissonance theory. Perceptions (user satisfaction) of the information system by those in the unrealistically high expectations group are higher than those of the moderate expectations group, and perceptions of the information system by those in the unrealistically low expectations group are lower than those experienced by the subjects in the moderate expectations group. This supplements Ginzberg's (1981) findings in his field study of user expectations. Ginzberg did not set out to investigate the direction of unrealistic expectations; rather, his emphasis was on unrealistic expectations, whether they were negatively or positively disconfirmed. In a post hoc data analysis, Ginzberg dichotomized subjects with unrealistically high expectations as those whose expectations were higher than the referent realistic group (i.e., systems developers) and those with unrealistically low expectations as those whose expectations were lower than the referent realistic group. Since he could not get a measure of satisfaction or usage from the realistic group (because they were systems developers and not system users), there could be no comparison in satisfaction or usage levels between the unrealistic groups and the realistic group. Therefore, it is difficult to compare Ginzberg's results to the ones here except to agree that unrealistic expectations appear to influence the satisfaction levels of users.

## Hypothesis 2: Across decision periods (within groups)

Cognitive dissonance theory suggests that it is not only possible to change a user's perceptions of an information system but that it is also possible for individuals to change their expectations for future use of that information system to attain a state of consonance. Hypothesis 2 investigates the possibility of changes in user satisfaction, decision performance, and expectations over the three decision periods for each of the expectations groups.

Hypothesis 2a stated that the user satisfaction scores of (1) the unrealistically high group would decrease, (2) the unrealistically low group would increase, and (3) the realistic expectations group would remain the same across the three decision periods. The repeated measures ANOVA yielded insignificant F-statistics for each of the three expectations groups for user satisfaction (see Table 7). Therefore, Hypothesis 2a for user satisfaction is not supported for groups $E_h$ and $E_l$ but is supported for group $E_m$ . Researchers have suggested that user satisfaction may not be so much a point-in-time measurement but, rather, an historical measurement of user satisfaction with an information system (Bailey and Pearson, 1983). In other words, a user satisfaction score may be a cumulation of user satisfaction over time rather than a measurement of a user's satisfaction at the particular time the satisfaction instrument is administered. This notion may be reflected in these results because user satisfaction would tend to change very slowly over time, showing no significant changes, if it measures the user's cumulative perceptions of an IS rather than their perceptions at a particular point in time.

Hypothesis 2b stated that there would be no difference in any change in decision performance for the expectations groups across the three decision periods. By using the difference in performance scores obtained between decision periods 1 and 2 and decision periods 2 and 3, it is possible to investigate any disparities in the differences of decision performance across the groups, over and above any learning effects that might have occurred from using a new system. The results of the ANOVAs for differences in decision performance across the three expectations groups indicate that there are no disparities in the mean differences of decision performance between either decision periods 1 and 2 or decision periods 2 and 3 (see Table 8). Therefore, Hypothesis 2b is supported.

Hypothesis 2c deals with the changes in expectations over the three decision periods, with group $E_h$ 's expectations projected to decrease, group $E_l$ 's expectations projected to increase, and group $E_m$ 's expectations projected to remain the same. For group $E_h$ , an F-statistic of 14.35, with a significance level less than 0.001, resulted from the repeated measures ANOVA (see Table 9). The contrast analysis revealed a significant decrease in expectations from decision period 1 to decision period 2 and no difference between decision periods 2 and 3. For group $E_{l}$ , the repeated measures ANOVA yielded a significant F-statistic of 22.05, with a significance level less than 0.001. Again, the contrast analysis showed that the most significant difference, an increase, occurred between decision periods 1 and 2 and not between decision periods 2 and 3. For group $E_{m}$ , there is a small significant difference across the decision periods for expectations. The contrast analysis revealed that a significant difference exists between decision periods 1 and 3. This increase in expectations was minimally significant and only after three decision periods. Hypothesis 2c is supported for groups $E_{h}$ and $E_{l}$ but not for group $E_{m}$ .

## Concluding Remarks

The objective of this experiment was to determine if there might be a relationship between users' expectations and their perceptions or performance. The results associated with Hypothesis 1, summarized in Table 10, show that users with unrealistic expectations had perceptions of the information system that were significantly different from those with realistic expectations. Specifically, subjects with relatively low expectations had user satisfaction scores lower than those with moderate expectations, and subjects with relatively high expectations had user satisfaction scores higher than those with moderate expectations. On the other hand, decision performance exhibited little relationship to the level of expectations.

At first glance it may appear more desirable for users' expectations to be unrealistically high rather than realistic. However, this experiment dealt with only the direction of the disconfirmation of expectations and not with the degree of disconfirmation. If an individual experiences disconfirmation (actual performance different from expected), cognitive dissonance theory predicts that perceived performance will differ from actual. However, if the degree of disconfirmation is included in the analysis, it may be that perceptions will be affected differently dependent upon the degree of disconfirmation. Most studies of expectations/perceptions have been concerned with the direction rather than the degree of disconfirmation. Studies that have involved the degree of disconfirmation (e.g., Anderson, 1973) have indicated that it, too, may be important for predicting perceptions.

The results associated with Hypothesis 2 are summarized in Table 11. Overall, the most important outcome is that unrealistic user expectations experience a substantial change. Unrealistic expectations appear to “wear off” and become more realistic with the use of the information system. Figure 3 is a graphical comparison of the changes in expectations across the three decision periods.

## Implications for researchers and practitioners

There may be a risk associated with “overselling” an information system. As long as the degree of disconfirmation cannot be determined, it is best that expectations be kept at a realistic level. The degree of disconfirmation provides another avenue for studying user expectations, with particular emphasis on the determination of a potential expectations threshold where cognitive dissonance theory may no longer predict perceptions.

The field study conducted by Ginzberg (1981) demonstrated that disconfirmed expectations are associated with user dissatisfaction, while this controlled laboratory experiment showed that (1) unrealistically low user expectations are associated with user dissatisfaction and (2) decision quality is not affected by unrealistic expectations. Does this mean that those responsible for systems development and implementation need not be concerned about unrealistic expectations of the eventual users? When users are dissatisfied with an information system and the use of that system is voluntary, the users may discontinue their use of the system. This has important implications for systems that consume substantial organizational resources. The significance of these findings for researchers is that it may not be enough to look only at the objective performance of an IS in determining its effectiveness. It is also important to consider the perceptions of the users for the IS in much the same way that marketing professionals study both the performance of a product and the consumers' perceptions of the product's performance.

## Limitations and future research

In other research on user expectations, it may not be possible to manipulate user expectations as was done here. It will, therefore, be necessary to measure the construct of user expectations. A promising area for future research is the comprehensive psychometric development and validation of an instrument to measure user expectations and determine the construct's dimensionality. Such an instrument might ultimately provide IS professionals with a simple, cost-effective means of assessing user expectations during a development project.

One of the limitations of this study is the use of undergraduate business students as subjects. For example, as previously discussed, students typically have had little experience with information systems, a fact that must be taken into account when generalizing their expectations of an IS. Therefore, a next step in this line of research would be additional field studies to look at the consequences of unrealistically high and low expectations in organizations. A field study would also be better able to introduce other measures of system success, such as user acceptance/adoption of an information system and usage of the information system.

Although a limitation of this experiment is an inability to generalize the findings to an actual business situation, one of its strengths lies in its ability to test hypotheses developed from theory in a controlled environment. Ginzberg's (1981) research concluded that unrealistic expectations cause unfavorable user attitudes and decreased system usage. The research reported here attempts to go one step further to indicate any differences that might exist among those with unrealistically high and low expectations in comparison to those with realistic expectations. In order to address the full research model depicted in Figure 1, future research needs to be directed toward increasing an understanding of the causes of unrealistic expectations. From a practical point of view, the focus should be on development of suitable approaches for IS professionals to avoid unrealistic expectations.

# Appendix A

# Excerpts from Case Presentations Concerning User Involvement

## High Expectations Case

"The key to their [IS department] success lies in well-planned systems that involve the people who will be using the system. John Williams [vice president of information] realizes that it is the users who 'own' the system, and that the ISD [Information Services Department] is merely responsible for addressing and servicing their needs. Therefore, in any endeavor, the user is the 'boss.' The user defines the needs for the system and has control over what goes into it, how it works, and what comes out in the form of information and reports. . . . At each step of the way, it was Joe's [user representative] responsibility to 'sign off' that phase of the project as it met the requirements of the regional sales managers. He was definitely in control of the project at all times and had authority over continuation to the next phase."

## Moderate Expectations Case

“John Williams [vice president of information] believes that the key to success in information systems lies in well-planned systems that involve the people who will be using the system. He realized that it is beneficial to consult the eventual users for their advice in developing the information system. . . . [U]ers are asked to define their needs and the system developers are encouraged to attempt to meet these needs. Since the eventual users of an information system are now consulted as to their needs since John has been vice president, they are gradually becoming familiar with the systems development process. . . . The users of the information systems feel, though, that if they had more control of the development process itself with more authority over the information system, that even better information systems would be possible. . . . Gloria [a systems analyst] talked to Joe at length about his suggestions for the information system. She made no promises but said the ISD would do all they could to accommodate the regional sales managers’ requests. Several other times over the next few months while the information system was being developed, Gloria would call Joe about specific advice on things like the types of graphs they would like, the format of the tables, etc. . . . he [Joe] did feel like he at least had had some input even if he didn’t have the ‘last word’ on the information system development process.”

## Low Expectations Case

"The success rate of the ISD has been poor when attempting to implement new technology or new systems. One of the reasons for this is that the users of the information system being developed are seldom contacted as to what their needs might be. Whenever a new information system is being developed, a 'user representative' is appointed to assist on the project, but most of these 'representatives' have found that rarely are their . . . suggestions attended to by the system developers. . . . Joe submitted a report to Gloria of the requirements of the regional sales managers for this information system. He received a polite letter of thanks and did not hear another thing about the information system until [six months later]. At that time, Gloria felt that the information system was ready to be installed. She called Joe to take a look at it. Joe was disappointed with what he saw. It seemed as if Gloria had ignored a good number of the suggestions made by the regional sales managers as far as their needs for such a system."
