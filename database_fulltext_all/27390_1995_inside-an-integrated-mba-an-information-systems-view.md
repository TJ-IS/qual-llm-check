## Abstract

This paper chronicles the evolution and content of an integrated MBA (IMBA) at the University of Denver and relates an IS-oriented view of the curriculum integration and teaching experience. The study emphasizes IS faculty efforts to integrate curricula from the traditional MIS course into three of the seven team-taught, interdisciplinary IMBA courses. Their teaching experiences illustrate pedagogy, and ensuing sections summarize student feedback and provide insights on what appears to work and what does not, as well as what remains to be done.

A process is recommended to MIS faculty for infusing IS topics into an integrated curriculum. Gaining respect from other faculty for teaching the use and management of IT beyond its use for personal productivity, and incorporating IS into multiple class sections with scarce IS faculty resources are major problems. What appears to work best for MIS faculty is to attend curriculum discussions with (1) an appreciation of what IS/IT knowledge is essential for an MBA to (a) understand IS contributions to the solution of business problems and (b) participate in systems development activities to obtain (and later manage) IT applications; and (2) a willingness to look for opportunities to position IS/IT topics to complement other curricula.

Keywords: IS education, IS curriculum, integrated MBA education

ISRL Categories: IA, IA01

Stakeholders both internal and external to the academic community express concerns about the MBA and urge systemic transformation in curriculum content and course delivery. Critics assail the relevance of the subject matter taught and often claim that MBAs are technically brilliant but exhibit minimum social skills (Reeve, 1992). Further, MBAs are portrayed as opportunistic in pursuit of personal financial gain.

MBA faculty are not off the hook either. Pundits accuse business faculty of being “methodologically obsessed” (Smith, 1989) and call for more emphasis on teaching in the teaching/research/service triumvirate as well as for more practical utility in research endeavors (Boyer, 1990; Edgerton, 1993; Main, 1989).

Universities need to become more responsive to the perceived concerns in order to better serve students and business (Porter and McKibbin, 1988). Mounting evidence suggests that MBA graduates are experiencing more difficulty obtaining desired jobs, and if they are successful, they often command less-than-expected salaries (Miller, 1992; O'Reilly, 1994). At the same time, demographics signal a declining pool of potential students (Boyd and Halford, 1992-1993; O'Reilly, 1994).

Boyd and Halford succinctly capture the often-cited flaws in graduate management education and suggest visions for change. They consider professors as esoteric and detached from the business community rather than responsive to corporate concerns. Additionally, they see B-schools

MIS Quarterly/September 1995 391 as (1) too insular in presenting subject matter as opposed to solving problems by considering strategic interactions, (2) too standardized and reluctant to consider a differentiated curriculum, and (3) too grounded in recognition by academic peers rather than by students and the business community. "Those institutions that abandon their traditional aloofness are more likely to survive," claim Boyd and Halford (1992-1993, p. 10).

Training people to become knowledge workers requires less splintering and more holistic views on business issues (Drucker, 1992). Many universities are embarking on an integrated MBA curriculum to increase the relevance of their programs and to remain competitive. Mirroring what is happening in business, a new paradigm is emerging where students (1) work and present in teams, and (2) solve business problems experientially through a cross-functional approach. Cross-disciplinary faculty teams replace the individual instructor.

The purpose of this paper is to portray a realistic IS-oriented view of an integrated MBA experience at the University of Denver (DU). An important focal point is a process-based view of how IS faculty at this school fared in infusing information technology and information systems concepts into an integrated program. In order to set the context for DU's model, and in recognition of others who have an interest in integrated MBA models, we outline trends in integrated education that influenced the DU effort, explain how integration evolved at DU, and define the current requirements for and content of the integrated MBA. Within this context, the paper describes an approach for infusing IS content into an integrated curriculum and provides examples from our personal experiences in teaching three of the seven new integrated MBA classes. Ensuing sections summarize student feedback and provide insights on what appears to work and what does not, as well as what remains to be done. Our aspiration is to encapsulate our experience and suggest paths and caveats for others making this transition.

## Trends in Integrated Education

Two trends initiated the integration effort at DU: (1) cross-functional integration and (2) change in teaching pedagogy. Many schools are undergoing experiences similar to DU's as faculties transform curricula to an integrated model and transition teaching to a more experiential, student-centered approach.

## Cross-functional integration

The meaning of cross-functional integration in education is broader than simply cross-disciplinary teaching within a college of business. Social themes often determine cross-functional curricula, and the cross-functional nature may take the form of cross-college collaboration, or as a closer alignment of the curriculum with business concerns.

## Cross-Functional Integration Around Social Themes

Universities pursuing integrated MBA programs differ on specific course offerings, course-delivery methods, and the extent of integration. Some integrate one course; others encompass the first year of the MBA program; DU's integration spans two years. However, what these universities have in common is a belief that functional topics add student-perceived value when those topics are relevant to basic business themes and activities. With moderate success, some institutions have promulgated underlying social themes for the entire program. Five examples of social themes being used are:

\- Denver University: Values and ethics, communication and leadership, cross-cultural diversity, globalization, and quality.

\- University of Tennessee: Leadership, technology, globalization, organizational reality, and experiential learning.

\- Weatherhead at Case Western Reserve: Managing in the world economy, leadership, innovation and professionalism, and integrity and responsibility.

\- University of Indiana (one course): Professional perspectives on ethics, power, and persuasion.

\- University of Western Ontario (three courses): Technology, entrepreneurship, and quality.

## Cross-College Collaboration

Other schools are differentiating their MBA degree by joining with faculties in fields outside of business. Stanford, Carnegie Mellon, and MIT collaborate with computer science and engineering so that graduates are qualified to fill management slots in the burgeoning technical market (Holusha, 1994; Goldberger, 1991).

## Business and University Curriculum Alignment

Projects, internships, and student-consultant teams are well-known methods of improving B-school presence and interaction with organizations. The practical experiences provided through these methods complement the emphasis on participative, experiential learning. DU's final course, "integrative challenge," allows student teams to act as consultants within area businesses. An interesting student team project at the University of Michigan called the Multi-disciplinary Action Program (MAP) places teams of first-year MBA students in corporations worldwide to work on reengineering projects. While they are working and when they return, students are debriefed by interdisciplinary faculty teams (Dutton, 1994).

The University of Western Ontario's Western Business School brings corporate sponsors to the classroom. An IS faculty member, Sid Huff, is coordinating the MBA-I class around the technology theme (for practical purposes, information technology) (Huff, 1994). The corporate sponsor for this class is Bell-Northern Research, and the corporate/classroom forum will create a full-blown business case for a new BNR product.

Another approach to business/university collaboration is delivering the MBA program in a business setting. The University of Colorado at Denver forged a partnership with StorageTek to deliver the two-year MBA program onsite at the company (Keating, 1994). The goal of this initiative is to spark relevance by fostering deeper faculty knowledge of the company and establishing a critical mass of employees who may apply what they study to their work. In addition to cross-functional integration, DU's integration effort was spurred by the change in teaching pedagogy, which is described below.

## Change in teaching pedagogy

Teaching pedagogy is gradually changing in integrated formats from “faculty informing” to “students discovering” (Davis, 1993; Quinn, et al., 1990a; Boyatzis, et al., 1992). This approach promotes critical thinking skills, active learning and non-threatening practice, acceptance and nurture of student ideas, and a mutual student/faculty learning environment. Student teams are a popular instructional format. The belief is that learning functional knowledge is far more meaningful when a person is first confronted with a problem requiring use of that knowledge. Topics no longer fall in linear sequence, and teachers become resources rather than experts. “Just-in-time learning” often augments case analyses.

Nearly all the schools that have moved to integrated models adhere to “discovery” teaching pedagogy to some degree. There are difficulties in making this change. The Weatherhead School writes of the difficulties in getting faculty to change—it is hard to step out of the limelight, and professors usually need to cut breadth of coverage in order to cover some material in more detail (Boyatzis, et al., 1992).

To summarize, the foundation of DU's integrated MBA (IMBA) was the desire for cross-functional integration and student discovery-based teaching pedagogy. A generous financial gift converged with initial integration efforts and enabled wide-scale changes.

## Development of DU's Integrated MBA

The University of Denver's Daniels College of Business (DCB) began this paradigm shift in the late 1980s. Facilitated by an \$11 million gift in late 1988 by cable entrepreneur Bill Daniels, DU launched a transformation in curriculum and course delivery that led to the inauguration of the Integrated MBA (IMBA) in the Fall quarter of 1993.

Typical of consensus-driven academia, the IMBA evolved through three phases. $^{1}$ The first phase began in 1987, when the DCB created a 20-member business advisory board from the Denver area. The board's charge was to identify important MBA-graduate subject-matter themes. Responses clustered around five themes, and these became drivers for the IMBA:

\- Values and ethics

\- Communication and leadership

• Cross-cultural diversity

\- Globalization

\- Quality

One point to note is that “technology” was not a driving theme (this is discussed in a later section). In 1988 DU addressed these themes by forming stand-alone courses in communication, international business, human resource management, ethics, and negotiation and dispute resolution.

A second phase began in November of 1988 (at the time of Bill Daniels' gift). The gift was to re-structure the MBA to "stress ethics, integrity, and people skills" (Sinisi, 1988). $^{2}$ The MBA Enhancement Series (extra-curricular workshops) became the vehicle for the Daniels' challenge. At this juncture, prevailing thought surmised that the classroom environment was unsuitable for subjects like diversity, community service, and leadership. The Enhancement Series sought to teach these skills by requiring additional activities outside the classroom. Numerous workshops complemented the curriculum. Students worked on community service projects, completed an Outward Bound experience (faculty were urged to participate as well), and engaged in business experiences through various "bootcamps." However, student experiences were not well integrated into classroom instruction. This was consistent with a report from the Weatherhead School at Case Western Reserve that workshop success may require a cultural shift. Currently, student expectations are that value-added activities occur in the classroom; extracurricular workshops lack credibility (Boyatzis, et al., 1992).

A switch in philosophy initiated the third phase. No longer were social themes to be taught outside the core classes, but rather the classes themselves were to be functionally integrated and organized around the key themes: values and ethics, communication and leadership, cross-cultural diversity, globalization, and quality. DU faculty accepted the controversial position that students could learn ethics, social responsibility, and communication and leadership in a classroom setting without relinquishing functional substance. DU's DCB Graduate Programs Committee (1992) stated that the intent of integration was not to dismantle functional disciplines, but rather to give them a context for more effective learning.

The first MBAs entered IMBA core courses in the Fall quarter of 1993. IMBA courses now comprise 69 percent of an MBA student's experience at DU. The old core curriculum had 20 courses; the new curriculum reworked them into seven core courses (some taught over multiple quarters), plus electives. Taught by interdisciplinary teams, each course allows students to examine complex and interrelated decision-making processes through an interdisciplinary lens. The following section details the organization of the IMBA including the requirements for a general IMBA and citing the discipline specializations.

## Organization of the IMBA

The IMBA reflects comprehensive and rigorous curriculum development over an 18-month period by three successive faculty committees for each course (concept, design, and implementation). Input was sought continuously from over 40 business leaders about the structure of the program and the content of the courses.

Each of the seven IMBA courses was developed by a faculty team from selected disciplines under the general direction of a faculty course coordinator. The seven course coordinators, together with the associate dean for graduate studies, met as a committee to be sure there was a proper balance and articulation between all courses.

The traditional core MBA courses at DU required 92 quarter hours. Gradually, the integrated format has reduced the requirement to 72 quarter hours, of which 60 comprise the integrated core. For the remaining 12 quarter hours, one elective course must be selected from a group of cross-disciplinary electives, and the remaining electives must apply either toward the general IMBA or toward a specialization. IMBA specializations are available in accounting, finance, MIS, marketing, real estate & construction management, and entrepreneurship. Specializations require a minimum of 20 additional hours beyond the 60 hours of core courses. The MIS specialization requires the following courses taken in the sequence listed:

\- Database management systems

• Computer systems concepts

\- Client server systems

• Systems analysis and design

• Corporate information systems management

## The Integrated Classroom

In describing our experiences, we emphasize how information technology and information systems play out in the interdisciplinary mix. The general methodology followed by the MIS faculty in introducing IS topics to individual courses was to start with a list of topics that had normally been covered in the introduction to MIS course previously required of all MBAs. Then, as each course team met and topics from other disciplines were introduced, the MIS faculty looked for opportunities to introduce an MIS topic at appropriate places. For example, a session on IS for competitive advantage follows a marketing professor's discussion of Porter's generic strategies and five forces model. A session on EDI follows a discussion of marketing channels. Additional examples are cited in the discussion that follows.

The net effect of this approach was that, for the most part, IS topics were placed in each course in a natural position. Instead of students being exposed to MIS topics in a disconnected way, they were shown the way information technology leverages other functional areas. Critical incidents for each course discussed below illustrate how IS curriculum has effectively meshed with other functional areas.

## High-performance management (HPM)

HPM is a first- and second-quarter course designed to give the entering IMBA student a grounding in basic management concepts. The course revolves conceptually around the idea of "reframing" the way organizations are viewed. Reframing specifies that most management problems can be considered from a wide variety of perspectives, including (1) structure, or formal roles and relationships aimed at getting things done, (2) human resources, (3) consideration of motivation and organizational benefit from individual talents, (4) political activities regarding competition for power and scarce resources, and (5) symbolic aspects that address the need for finding meaning in work.

The course uses topics from organizational communication, organizational behavior, human resource management, management principles, negotiation, and MIS. Unlike many of the other IMBA courses, HPM is taught by a two-person team that is in the classroom at all times. $^{3}$ Table 3 summarizes the content of each HPM session; those sessions with IS content are italicized.

HPM begins with an early discussion of the impact of IT on organizations and management methods in the1990s. This discussion is woven into an overall discussion of the concept of reframing and general management theory. Following this, students are given an assignment to use electronic mail to comment on these initial discussions of theory. The use of electronic mail is built into assignments for the course. Students send their papers and various assignments to the instructors via electronic mail. They also receive comments on their papers, presentations, and tests via electronic mail.

Later on in the quarter, lectures on types of information systems, decision support systems, and expert systems are embedded as a part of discussions on organizational decision making. As a part of this section of the course, students work through several mini-cases on how decision support systems are designed and used. The mini-cases also serve to tie together concepts of alternative decision-making models, including intuitive, rational, and computer-aided methods.

Soon after the discussions on decision-making methods, students are introduced to group processes and group decision making. This opens discussion of how group decision support systems are being used in organizations. Students are led through an exercise on a GDSS in which they examine the problems and issues arising from the team processes they have been going through during the quarter. Since students are responsible for a significant group project during the two-quarter sequence of HPM as well as numerous group assignments, this has proven to be an interesting and provocative session.

HPM covers much of the material traditionally discussed in the human resource management course. So as a part of that topic, a major case study utilizes a personnel database. While engaged in the case study, students are introduced to the concept of a database, given the opportunity to work with queries in ACCESS, and asked to use the information contained in the database to make recommendations on hiring a plant manager for a fictitious manufacturing company.

The planning sessions are structured so that they lead to a discussion of the process of systems development. This includes a discussion of the systems development lifecycle, alternative development methodologies, and the impact of IT on reengineering.

Plans for the future of this course include the introduction of more case studies with MIS concepts included with management concepts. In addition, MIS has been added to the group project that runs throughout the two-quarter sequence. The project requires students to work with an organization to analyze their personnel and management policies. In the forthcoming quarters, they will also be required to describe relevant MIS systems and analyze the effectiveness of those systems.

## Positioning in the competitive environment

PCE is team-taught by faculty from accountancy, finance, management, marketing, POM, and MIS. $^{4}$ The course is intended to provide an integrated framework for examining the key strategic and operational issues faced by modern business. It attempts to do so by immersing students in a modern business environment and exposing them to internal and external issues that affect organizational performance. PCE uses Harvard cases extensively, the first course that students take in the integrated MBA curriculum to do so. Marketing topics comprise about 35 percent of the eight-quarter-hour course. Table 4 outlines PCE class sessions (eight hours each), and again those sessions with IS content are italicized.

Quoting from the instructional philosophy section of the syllabus:

PCE acknowledges that the student must have a thorough understanding of the basic business functions if he/she is to become an effective manager and problem-solver. Although there will be many opportunities to apply what they learn, the emphasis will be on forming a sound foundation that will prove useful during the rest of their MBA experience. The course is a sharp departure from the traditional business function courses. Rather than teaching the discipline as separate business topics, the course will demonstrate how they work together to create positive organizational synergy (Positioning in the Competitive Environment Syllabus, 1994).

One member of the teaching team serves as course coordinator for each section and attends all classes. Individual topics are covered by discipline experts. The course coordinator is well positioned to bring elements from other disciplines into each classroom discussion. The course coordinator also handles classroom logistics and records participation grades, etc.

The class begins with strategy models, industry analysis, and Porter's Five Forces model in the first two sessions. The third session is split between an introduction to marketing and the first IT topic: using information technology for competitive advantage.

A lecture is followed by a classroom discussion of the Frontier Airlines case. The airline industry, of course, is rich in examples of how IT can be used for competitive advantage, most of which are familiar to the students (e.g., reservation systems, frequent flyer programs, yield management systems, etc.). In addition, the strategy colleague can play off the five-forces component in the case, the marketing colleague can play off the frequent flyer, marketing database, and pricing examples, while the production and operations management colleague can integrate the yield-management example into his or her material.

The next MIS topic is titled systems integration. The students read “The Ubiquitous Machine” (The Economist, 1990), an article focusing on information technology. Although published in 1990, it is still quite current. It emphasizes the use of information technology for competitive advantage and the importance of using technology to leverage human talent within organizations. A long article, it introduces such concepts as the learning organization (Senge, 1990), organizational change (“transformation” in the current vernacular), Venkatraman’s five stages (1994), centralization vs. decentralization, dynamic networks (Miles and Snow, 1986), expert systems, neural nets, the waterfall systems development life cycle and the newer variants, packaged software, open systems, process reengineering, productivity payoff concerns, and information value. Moreover, it is an interesting read in the typical Economist style (e.g., “Anyone who tells you that it is easy to change the way groups of people do things is either a liar, a management consultant or both”).

Students then work the Mrs. Fields' Cookies case (Cash and Ostrofsky, 1989). During the discussion of Mrs. Fields' Cookies (which, incidentally, is also mentioned in the Economist article), students come to appreciate the options IT gives in the way companies can organize themselves.

An IT topic is included in the next session. Preceded by a two-hour marketing discussion on segmentation and targeting, the database concept is discussed centered around a marketing database case, Merv Griffin's Resorts (Langbert, 1992). The case is easily team taught.

The final session with an IT topic is again shared with marketing. Marketing's topic is channels, and they work the Lotus Development case (Rangan and Scott, 1986). Following a lecture on EDI, IOS, and IOS process reengineering, together with some speculation on the ultimate shape of electronic commerce and the way IOS process reengineering may evolve in Japan (McCubbrey and Imai, 1994), the class works the Singapore Tradenet case (King and Konsynski, 1990;1993). Thus, the IT portion of the course is concluded by tying together electronic commerce, competitive advantage, and a discussion of project risk assessment and management.

We find that information technology is much better received when students can quickly see the relevance and value of applying it to specific business environments. Using IT for more effective marketing and customer management, creative organizational designs, competitive advantage, and the power of interorganizational systems and IOS process reengineering highlights the value of IT and the understanding that it is the line manager's responsibility to see that this important resource is used effectively in his or her organization. Students are also helped to realize the penalties to be paid if IT is ignored, underutilized, or used in inappropriate ways.

## The quest for quality: the quality panorama and putting quality to work

Faculty from accounting, production and operations management, marketing, management, and MIS teach the two quality courses, "the quality panorama" and "putting quality to work." MIS teaching content spanning the two-quarter sequence includes (1) systems theory and the value chain, (2) systems reengineering, (3) systems integration, (4) spreadsheet design based on an activity-based costing exercise, and (5) organizational change issues and IT. Table 5 outlines the sessions included in both quality courses. Once again, those sessions with IS content are italicized.

The primary objective of IS coverage in the quality courses is to reinforce the systems perspective and organizational challenges involved in integrating information systems and applying IT to quality-based solutions. It is not acceptable for students to blithely report that organizations should integrate their computer systems—they must deal with how to accomplish integration in a defined case setting.

The first course, quality panorama, explores the issues of quality in organizations: objectives, philosophy, principles, language, processes, and measurement. Initially, the MIS team member was very surprised at the differences of opinion among her colleagues on just what "quality" meant. She came to the table with the perception that quality was achievable largely through the effective use of information technology and the close alignment between corporate

IS, human resources, and quality organizations. To the accountant, quality was activity-based costing and the elimination of non-value-added activities; to the marketing person, it was customer satisfaction; to management it meant customer satisfaction and employee well being; and to the production and operations management faculty member, it meant all these things as measured by metrics evaluating continuous improvement. The faculty team discloses their different perspectives on day one of the first class. Subsequently, the differences often animate topic discussions.

MIS is responsible for a class on systems theory and the value chain in the first course. Students read three articles dealing with the value chain from three viewpoints: how to "mine" the value chain through the deployment of IT (Porter and Millar, 1985); how to "mine" the value chain by determining cost drivers and avoiding non-value-added costs (Shank and Govindarajan, 1993); and how to "mine" the value chain by determining an organization's core competency and outsourcing other value-chain components (Quinn, et al., 1990a).

A mini-case pulls the value-chain themes together. The following interaction illustrates how IT issues emerge in case discussions. One student suggested information systems as an activity to outsource. Another student brought up the security issue for outsourced corporate data. Piggy-backing on this, discussion centered on the differences between transaction data (often outsourced), and the development and use of IT for strategic purposes that would not be outsourced. Many students equated IS with accounting information systems. Through dialogue, students connected what they already knew (e.g., advanced communication networks and IT used to expand and track customer data) with a broader scope for the IS function.

Systems reengineering is a second MIS topic in the first quality course. Students examine the differences and similarities between total quality management (TQM) and reengineering. They observe that the primary difference is the pace of change. TQM emphasizes gradual, continuous improvement, whereas reengineering approaches advocate more radical changes in business processes.

The second course, putting quality to work, provides the opportunity to apply total quality knowledge and tools in an organizational environment. The teaching team has written a comprehensive health care case as a vehicle for students to apply quality tools and concepts. Students use the management and statistical tools described in The Memory Jogger + (Brassard, 1989). They also learn survey design techniques as they develop a survey instrument to assess customer satisfaction (with their hospital stay).

A lecture on hospital information systems is woven into the healthcare case by illustrating how disparate systems deter timely and accurate information delivery, thereby contributing to an accounts receivable problem. Students are presented with an argument for an integrated system, and discussion centers around organizational and political problems that may stifle systems integration.

Responding to student course-improvement suggestions, the team added another class session on organizational change issues. Students are aware that approximately 70 percent of TQM and/or business process redesign projects fail, and wanted to know more about change theory. Since change theory is directly applicable to IS implementation and IT innovation issues, this is an appropriate juncture to consider these topics.

On a more technical theme, the IS faculty member presents a spreadsheet design model, and students must use the model to assess cost drivers from three of the hospital's suppliers and recommend measures to cut costs. The assignment builds on two previous classes—the management professor's topic on supplier relationships and the accounting professor's presentation of activity-based costing. As an aside, the team found that although students have done numerous spreadsheets, few are knowledgeable about design principles.

## Student Feedback

The most complete feedback about the IMBA program in general is from student focus groups held late into the first IMBA quarter by an outside consultant (Halterman, 1993). Feedback has led to continuous improvements. At the time, students objected to fragmentation, lack of coordination among faculty teams and between IMBA courses, and instances of sequential team teaching (e.g., three successive weeks featuring, in turn, accounting, finance, and statistics). Students had very favorable comments about examples they remembered where multiple faculty members participated in instruction at the same time. Some faculty teams felt that the first time through they laid bricks all over the ground, not stacking them up and not providing sufficient connections between topics. The faculty noted that very significant intra- and inter-team communication and coordination are necessary to maintain “conceptual integrity” between topics. By Spring quarter 1993, faculty teams perceived that they delivered a more synergistic, integrated product.

One student provides an insight into a problem associated with student experience. Students may have taken classes or attained extensive work experience in particular areas, but cannot eliminate courses in the integrated design. Expressing irritation, the student states (Halterman, 1993):

... I think in a non-integrated program, it's designed that if you have experience in certain areas, you can test out of those certain sections. And I think no one really thought about the fact that by combining everything into one, you're suddenly going to have students who are really specialized in areas who shouldn't be wasting their time ... (p. 10).

More recent feedback was received from a series of five focus groups conducted in early 1995 by a marketing professor. On an overall basis, students continued to like the integrated format and viewed it as being more relevant than traditional MBA programs. There was a feeling, however, that the integrated concept was oversold; i.e., that some courses were still being delivered in sequential mode, with faculty from one discipline following a colleague from another department. They liked outside speakers and asked for more, and requested that faculty re-evaluate the number of team projects in the curriculum. They thought there were too many; that they worked well in some courses, but not so well in others.

On an overall basis, students felt the program could be more rigorous, and suggested that standards be raised, and that they be made consistent between courses. Demanding class formats were favored; cases and projects were preferred over lectures. This recent feedback will guide continuous improvement efforts by the various faculty teaching teams. Caveats form our next theme, based on our collective, cumulative experience with DU's efforts at integrated curriculum development and course delivery.

## Caveats

The first set of insights pertains to IS faculty directly—how do you make the management of IT meaningful to your cohort faculty and to students? The second set of caveats targets the teaching pedagogy, “Are the connections happening?” Finally, the third category of caveats considers processes related to curriculum development and course delivery that need to be addressed. The arguments appear germane to other schools considering integrated programs as well.

## Issues for IS faculty

While IT and information systems content is certainly woven into DU's IMBA, the authors believe that technology could be a greater focal point. In an earlier section, we noted that “technology” was not an expressed DU IMBA theme. Other universities (e.g., the University of Tennessee and the University of Western Ontario) have technology as a basic theme, and IS faculty at these institutions may be better positioned to highlight IT in the curriculum.

Influencing faculty of different persuasions (read "disciplines") is analogous to the struggle of our IS counterparts in business. Garner (1994) warns IS managers:

If you're going to swim with the sharks [business leaders], you must articulate IS issues in a way that's meaningful to those who hold your fate [corporate executives], understand how you can solve the problems of others in the company and motivate your subordinates to work as a team (p. 73).

Transposing this quotation to a cross-functional academic milieu, the quotation might read:

If you're going to swim with the sharks [faculty contending for curriculum content], you must articulate IS issues in a way that's meaningful to those who hold your fate [this same faculty], understand how you can share the problems of others in the college [e.g. understand the contributions of IS to marketing curriculum] and motivate your colleagues to work as a team.

Adequate IS representation on the interdisciplinary teams is an additional problem at DU. Without an undergraduate IS program, our department is small—four full-time faculty and several adjuncts.

Further, judging from our experience, all topics covered in any traditional core course will not be included in an integrated curricula. At DU, two guiding questions lead IS faculty to the curriculum discussions.

\- What IS knowledge is essential for an MBA to (1) understand IT contributions to the solution of business problems and (2) participate in systems development activities to obtain (and later manage) IT applications?

\- How can we position these IT/IS topics to complement other curricula?

Others propose that MBA students need more technical content. For example, Mathieu (1994) recently critiqued the MBA for lacking an appreciation of the difficulty of what they might expect the IS department to accomplish:

For example, a person that merely knows how to create a single-table database in dBase . . . has little or no concept of the issues associated with database design and implementation. The problem . . . is that they often see how "easy" it is to do certain things on the computer, and at the same time have no appreciation for the complexity of the job facing the IS department.

Mathieu advocates greater technical understanding of database technology as the crux of IS curriculum for MBAs. Similar cases may be argued for other technical areas such as telecommunications. Until standards emerge similar to the ACM or DPMA guidelines for IS majors, it is likely that each university will decide integrated curriculum issues independently, and inconsistency will be the norm.

## Teaching pedagogical dilemmas

Student feedback favors cross-functional teaching. The caveat here is that true team teaching is limited by scheduling logistics. Multiple class sections may preclude the entire teaching team from meeting with each class. The model used in PCE seems to work, and the quality courses are also implementing this model. As mentioned, one professor acts as class coordinator for each section and can participate in the team teaching continually. Other faculty members take teaching duties based on the day's topic. One the other hand, HPM faculty favor a two-person model where both professors attend each class. The belief is that this approach provides a tight weave among the ideas in the course.

## Conclusion

Responding to multifarious sources of criticism surrounding the content and delivery of graduate management education, the University of Denver initiated the integrated MBA in the Fall quarter of 1993. This paper describes an IS-oriented view of the curriculum development and course delivery components of DUs IMBA.

Overall, the experiences of the three MIS faculty in integrating curriculum and teaching in IMBA courses have been favorable. Anecdotal comparisons of student reactions to the integrated format compared to student evaluations of the former stand-alone MIS course give us the impression that information technology is much better appreciated when its business benefits are more clearly seen. Integration helps demonstrate business benefits by illustrating how technology can leverage human beings and provide creative solutions to marketing, financial, and operational problems as well as formulate technology-based or technology-leveraged business strategies.

We have had success implementing IS topics by following two guidelines in curriculum discussions:

\- What IS knowledge is essential for an MBA to (1) understand IT contributions to the solution of business problems and (2) participate in systems development activities to obtain (and later manage) IT applications?

\- How can we position these IT/IS topics to complement other curricula?

Inserting IT topics in an integrated curriculum requires that its relevance be demonstrated. An ability to demonstrate its relevance to faculty from other disciplines helps assure that its relevance will be seen by students.

DU has a specialization in MIS for its IMBAs, as well as specializations in several other disciplines. MIS/IMBAs take five quarters of courses in IT topics and thus have a fairly deep exposure to the field in comparison to non-majors.

One trend we have noted since the introduction of the IMBA is an increase in the number of students who choose an MIS specialization over the general IMBA. Why this is happening is a topic for further research. While the trend may mean nothing more than students noting the job market, it could be influenced by the way that IT topics are interwoven with other disciplines in a relevant way. Students may perceive a different view of the contribution an IT professional makes to business than what they had previously believed.
