## Abstract

Despite the significant demographic changes in the work force projected by the year 2000 and beyond, little empirical research has been made on the obstacles faced by women in the field of computing. Since career advancement prospects are especially salient for IS employees, and IS workers are considered a distinct occupational group, it is important to understand the career advancement prospects of IS employees. This study examines the impact of gender on job performance evaluations, job performance attributions, and career advancement prospects. The results show that there are no significant gender differences in job performance ratings; however, women are perceived to have less favorable chances for promotion than men. We found that job performance ratings play an important role in influencing an individual's chances for advancement. We also found that the effect of job performance on attributions is stronger among males than females. Additionally, we found that while the effect of job performance ratings on career advancement prospects is stronger among males, the effect of attributions on career advancement prospects is stronger among females. Suggestions regarding areas for future research are offered, and implications for human resource management are identified.

Keywords: Gender differences, IS career, career advancement, job performance, attributions

## Introduction

Understanding and eliminating barriers to advancement for various subgroups in the information systems (IS) workplace is of increasing concern to organizations. This is critical not only because of the increased interest in better managing IS human resources (Champy, 1992; Niederman, et al., 1991), and the rising personnel costs relative to hardware costs (Lockwood and Sobol, 1989), but even more importantly, because of the significant demographic changes occurring in the work force. The number of women entering the work force has grown considerably in recent years. It is projected that women, minorities, and immigrants will comprise 85 percent of the growth in the general work force and as much as 80 percent of all new hires by the year 2000 (Johnston, 1987). The Hudson Institute, in its Workforce 2000 report for the U.S. Department of Labor (Johnston, 1987), estimates that by the end of the next decade 20 percent of the work force will be U.S.-born non-whites, 42 percent will be U.S.-born white females, and 23 percent will be foreign-born immigrants. Further, the nature of the job market is expected to change with the technological job market growing so rapidly. As a result of these demographic changes, managers will most likely experience increasing difficulty in meeting their staffing needs, especially in expanding fields such as information technology, where women and minorities are already under-represented. $^{1}$ These changes make it imperative that we understand how to manage people with diverse backgrounds—particularly in the technology sector.

Despite the dramatic increase in the number of women and minorities in the work force, they experience more restricted career advancement prospects and fail to advance as quickly as their male and non-minority counterparts (Betts, 1993; Dipboye, 1987; Morrison, et al., 1987). It is suggested that women and minorities encounter a “glass ceiling” that prevents them from reaching the top levels of management. The glass ceiling has been used to “describe a barrier so subtle that it is transparent, yet so strong that it prevents women and minorities from moving up the management hierarchy” (Morrison and Von Glinow, 1990, p. 200). Reports in IS trade journals (Betts, 1993; Johnson, 1990a; Laberis, 1992; Marenghi, 1992) confirm the existence of such a barrier in computing. While women make up about 20 percent of the IS work force, they represent less than seven percent of top IS executives (Betts, 1993; Frenkel, 1990; Goff, 1990; Johnson, 1990a; Marenghi, 1992).

In much of the prior IS research, distinctions of gender were ignored (Baroudi, 1985; Goldstein and Rockart, 1984; Igbaria and Greenhaus, 1992). Issues related to affirmative action, historical patterns of discrimination, and projected demographic changes in the work force have largely been examined only in the professional magazines (Betts, 1993; Fisher, 1992; Johnson, 1990a; 1990b; Laberis, 1992; Liedman, 1992; Marenghi, 1992; Perry, 1992). Most of it, however, is subjective and anecdotal. While these accounts provide useful insights, they do not aid in developing theoretical models that provide a general understanding of this phenomenon. Thus, while considerable research exists on IS careers, we know little about how gender influences IS careers.

Perhaps the most compelling argument for investigating gender differences among IS personnel derives from the identification of IS workers as a distinct occupational group, which impacts their “identity, attitudes, interests, colleagueship, collective action, power, status and work consciousness” (Orlikowski and Baroudi, 1989, p. 23). Given the explosive growth of IS in organizations, IS employees are an increasingly important component of the work force. The design and implementation of new technology by IS workers directly and indirectly impacts the activities of other employees. Additionally, career advancement prospects are especially salient for IS employees whose achievement and growth needs tend to be strong. This research focuses specifically on gender differences in career advancement prospects and the effects of job performance on these prospects.

Many women concentrate in occupations that are predominantly female, mainly in the service industry and small businesses (Kanter, 1977). While women have had more opportunities in certain fields, such as education, the arts, social work, personnel work, public relations, and staff support positions, men dominate engineering and technical positions, particularly, in the private sector and large corporations. Thus, it is possible that the impact of job performance on career advancement prospects among females and males may differ from that found in other occupational groups. Additionally, Pulakos, et al. (1989) conclude that “sex stereotype of the occupation appears to interact with ratee sex such that men receive more favorable evaluations than women in traditionally masculine occupations but that no differences or smaller differences in favor of women occur in traditionally feminine occupations” (p. 779).

These studies reinforce the importance of examining gender differences in the IS work force and the impact of job performance evaluations and attributions on career advancement prospects. Thus, the purpose of this paper is to address this gap in the IS literature. Specifically, the goals of this study are to explore: (1) the effect of gender on IS job performance evaluations and attributions; (2) differences in the career advancement prospects of women and men in IS; and (3) the impact of job performance attributions and evaluations on career advancement prospects.

## Proposed Relationships and Hypotheses

A pervasive theme in the literature is the limited advancement prospects available to women. One potential cause of the “glass ceiling” for women and minorities may be the presence of bias in the performance evaluation process (Greenhaus and Parasuraman, 1993; Igbaria and Wormley, 1992; Stumpf and London, 1981). Some of the bias may occur when the performance level of women and minorities are evaluated more negatively than their actual performance warrants (Greenhaus, et al., 1990; Igbaria and Wormley, 1992; Kraiger and Ford, 1985; Nieva and Gutek, 1980). In addition to the impact that unfair negative job performance evaluations may have, the attributions that supervisors invoke to explain their subordinates’ performance influences their opinions regarding the subordinates’ likelihood of future advancement (Greenhaus and Parasuraman, 1993; Heilman and Gizzo, 1978). For example, while supervisors may attribute the good performance of men to ability and talent, they are more likely to attribute the good performance of women to good luck, ease of task, or extraordinary effort (llgen and Youtz, 1986).

Much of the prior research on gender differences in performance attributions is based on laboratory studies. Although these studies have strong internal validity and provide considerable precision in the manipulation and measurement of the variables, their results may not be generalizable (Kerlinger, 1986). Therefore, this research extends the attribution literature by examining gender differences in performance evaluations and attributions and the impact of attributions on career advancement opportunities in a field research design.

Figure 1 identifies the hypothesized relationships examined in this research. The model, which builds on the work of Green and Mitchell (1979) and Pazy (1986), is comprised of three sets of variables: (1) gender; (2) two job performance evaluation forms: job performance ratings and job performance attributions; and (3) career advancement prospects. It posits that gender influences career advancement prospects through its effects on job performance evaluation ratings and attributions. In other words, job performance evaluations mediate the relationships between gender and career advancement prospects.

## Gender differences in job performance evaluations

Many factors can limit advancement to top management (Morrison and Von Glinow, 1990). These factors are: Women often receive lower job performance ratings than men, especially when the raters are men; Male supervisors use gender rather than work-related cues in assessing individuals' job performance; Raters tend to give higher ratings to ratees of the same gender or race; Furthermore, information selection and use, judgment processes, sex-role stereotypes, social isolation, and work-family conflict can affect management's treatment of women and create potential sources for bias in job performance evaluations (Morrison and Von Glinow, 1990). In addition, rater bias may have an impact on the performance of subordinates (Cabezas, et al., 1989; Larwood, et al., 1984; Maddan, 1985). Therefore, the first hypothesis is stated as follows:

H1: On average, women will receive lower ratings on job performance evaluations than men in the IS work force.

## Gender differences in job performance attributions

This paper also explores the relationship between an employee's gender and the extent to which the supervisor attributes the employee's performance to internal causes. People attribute task performance to one or more of the following causes: ability, effort, the ease of the task, and luck (Weiner, et al., 1971). Two dimensions are often used to characterize these causal attributions—locus of control and stability (Weiner, et al., 1971). An attribution's locus of control distinguishes between causes that are internal (ability or effort) and external (task difficulty, or luck) to the person. An attribution's stability distinguishes relatively enduring (ability or task difficulty) and relatively temporary (effort or luck) causes of behavior. Moreover, ability and task difficulty are viewed as stable factors that are likely to continue in the future, whereas effort and luck are considered unstable and temporary determinants of performance. This study focuses on whether the supervisors' job performance evaluations for male and female IS employees are equivalent.

Weiner's (1974) taxonomy suggests that explanations offered for the success or failure of women and men can differ markedly and are based on the sex-role stereotypes (Weiner, 1974). Supervisors attribute a man's successful performance to ability, skill, and hard work; they attribute a woman's successful performance to luck and ease of accomplishment (Etaugh and Brown, 1975; Feldman-Summers and Kiesler, 1974; Kaufman and Shikiar, 1985). On the other hand, a man's unsuccessful performance is attributed to bad luck, whereas a woman's is attributed to low ability. This suggests that while men are credited with success and excused for failure, women are seen as personally accountable for their failure (O'Leary, 1977).

These attributions can be explained in several ways. Women's success on male-type tasks violates supervisors' sex-role expectations (Nieva and Gutek, 1980); hence the supervisor is unlikely to attribute her performance to ability, but instead, attribute it to luck and other external factors. Further, supervisors who belong to the same group as some of their subordinates (ingroup) may feel psychologically closer to them and are more likely to attribute their success to ability and skills (Green and Mitchell, (1979). Women are typically considered to be part of the outgroup, and this may explain why supervisors are less likely to attribute their success to ability (Kanter, 1979). Therefore, the next hypothesis is stated as follows:

H2: The successful performance of female IS employees is less likely to be attributed to ability and effort than male IS employees.

## Effect of job performance evaluations on attributions

The rating of an employee's job performance is related to the supervisor's use of internal performance attributions, as shown in Figure 1. There is some evidence that people who perform extremely well on a task are most likely to attribute their own performance to ability—an internal factor—and are least likely to attribute their performance to luck—an external factor (Weiner, et al., 1971). Assuming that similar processes are used when making attributions about oneself and others (Weiner, et al., 1971), a supervisor's attributions regarding an individual's job performance will be related to the level of observed performance. Therefore, the third hypothesis is stated as follows:

H3: A positive relationship exists between the job performance ratings and the supervisory attributions of that performance to internal causes.

## Gender differences in career advancement prospects

The widespread perception of a glass ceiling that prevents women from reaching the upper levels of management strongly implies that female employees face more restricted advancement opportunities than male employees. In fact, IS trade journals (Betts, 1993; Johnson, 1990a; Laberis, 1992; Marenghi, 1992) have confirmed the existence of such a barrier in computing. Similar findings were found among other employees. A study of the Fortune 500 companies (Von Glinow and Krzyczkowska-Mercer, 1988) found that only 3.6 percent of board directorships and officerships were held by women. The U.S. government reports that in 1989 women comprised only 8.6 percent of the senior executive service levels (U.S. Office of Personnel Management, 1989), with most female employees clustered in low-paying, non-prestigious levels. The dual labor market theory attempts to explain gender differences in advancement opportunities in terms of a marketplace. It consists of a set of better or primary jobs, including top-level positions, and a set of worse, or secondary, jobs at lower levels, with little mobility between the two (Larwood and Gattiker, 1987; Osajima, 1988). According to this theory, women usually occupy secondary jobs that are typically out of the mainstream of the business and don't lead to top management positions (Jones, 1986; Larwood and Gattiker, 1987; Powell, 1988).

Different sociological and psychological factors may also contribute to the limited advancement of women. For instance, women are assumed to be unable to manage effectively as executives (Auster, 1989; Morrison and Von Glinow, 1990; Ilgen and Youtz, 1986). Such an assumption can lead to isolation, which can block women's access to top management. Since the majority of top executives are male, women who do make it to the top are often perceived as “tokens.” As a result, they feel more isolated, have difficulty gaining the trust of their male peers, have less access to mentors and sponsors, and are excluded from informal networks (Betters-Reed and Moore, 1992; Morrison and Von Glinow, 1990; O'Leary and Ickovics, 1992). These psychological, structural, and organizational barriers that emphasize a woman's lack of appropriateness, opportunity, and power in organizations, as well as the unfavorable sex ratio of groups within organizations, may explain women's lack of managerial success and their limited opportunities for advancement. Therefore, the fourth hypothesis states:

## H4: Women have fewer career opportunities for advancement than men in the IS workforce.

## Effects of attributions on career advancement prospects

Gender differences in career advancement prospects may be a function of gender discrimination, as well as differential job performance evaluations and attributions. Undoubtfully, an employee who performs poorly has little chance for promotion. In addition, the attributions a supervisor uses to explain a subordinate's performance can affect the supervisor's expectations regarding the subordinate's future performance and may, therefore, influence his/her assessment of the subordinate's prospects for advancement within the organization. Expectations regarding subordinates' future performance are generally higher when current performance is attributed to internal or stable causes (e.g., ability) than to external factors (e.g., luck) (Green and Mitchell, 1979; Ilgen and Youtz, 1986). Stable and internal determinants of job performance are likely to project into the future and to assure continued achievement on subsequent tasks.

Supervisors who attribute subordinates' performance to high levels of ability—an internal factor—tend to view the subordinate as deserving of a promotion, since ability should provide the greatest predictability of performance over time (Heilman and Guzzo, 1978). Thus, supervisors' assessments of their subordinates' advancement prospects may be more positive when they attribute performance to internal causes than to external causes. Moreover, it is also possible that employees whose performance has been attributed to external causes may have already experienced restricted advancement opportunities within the organization. Therefore, the fifth hypothesis predicts the following:

## H5: Supervisory attributions to internal causes are positively related to career advancement prospects.

We also predict that job performance evaluations will have a direct effect on career advancement prospects. This prediction is based on the observation that the appraisal of current job performance often plays a significant role in the manager's assessment of an employee's advancement potential (Stumpf and London, 1981). Thus, the next hypothesis predicts the following:

## H6: Supervisory performance evaluations are positively related to career advancement prospects.

In other words, IS employees whose supervisors attribute their performance to internal causes will receive more favorable assessments of their advancement prospects than IS employees whose supervisors attribute their performance to external factors. Further, this study examines the possibility that gender differences in career advancement prospects may be a function of differential job performance evaluations and attributions. Therefore, the final hypothesis predicts the following:

## H7: Gender differences in career advancement prospects will remain significant even after controlling for job performance evaluations and attributions.

In summary, this study contributes to the IS literature in a number of respects. First, it compares the career advancement prospects of female and male IS employees, a comparison that has not been examined in prior research. Second, unlike prior research that examined job performance among non-IS employees and focused on job performance ratings in a laboratory setting, this research examines performance evaluation and gender in an organizational setting. Finally, our study examines the impact of IS employee job performance on career advancement prospects.

## Method

## Overview of the procedure

Participants in this study were IS development staff of a large utility company in the eastern United States. The company agreed to participate in a comprehensive study of IS careers with a special emphasis on the job performance and motivation of IS employees. This company is known to be a leader in affirmative action and minority recruiting as well as in learning to manage and value diversity.

In early 1990, a comprehensive Career Attitude Survey (CAS) was distributed through company mail to all of the 127 IS development personnel. The CAS asked respondents to indicate their agreement and/or assessment of a particular statement concerning their performance and/or motivation. It also included demographic questions. The CAS was accompanied by a cover letter from the vice president of information systems, who strongly encouraged participation in the study, as well as a postage-paid envelope in which IS employees were to return their completed surveys directly to the researchers. Participation was voluntary, and participants were assured that their responses would be treated as confidential; only data aggregated at the group level would be reported. In addition, the supervisor of each IS employee in the sample received a separate Supervisor Survey (SS) for each of his or her subordinates selected to participate in the study. The SS included items related to the supervisors' assessment of their subordinates' performance and career advancement prospects (see the Appendix). $^{2}$ Identical numbers were affixed to the two surveys, CAS and SS, so that IS employees' and supervisors' responses could be matched. Approximately 3-4 weeks after the distribution of the survey all IS employees and supervisors received a letter reminding them of the importance of participation in the study and offering to provide another copy of the survey if the initial copy had been misplaced.

## Sample

Completed surveys were received from 111 of the 127 MIS employees asked to participate in the study (an 87.4 percent response rate). Since our analyses required data from both IS employees and their supervisors, we were interested in the number of employee-supervisor pairs, which was smaller than either the number of IS employees or supervisors who returned their surveys. This requirement of matched employee-supervisor data produced a combined sample of 109 IS employee-supervisor pairs (85.8 percent).

Seventy-seven of the IS employees (70.6 percent) were men and 32 (29.4 percent) were women. The mean age of the IS employees was 35.88 years (SD = 7.41). Tenure in the current job averaged 4.94 (SD = 4.87), and number of years in the IS field averaged 11.64 (SD = 6.56). Almost all of the employees attended college and approximately 80 percent were college graduates. Seventy-five of the IS employees were married (68.8 percent). Of the 109 IS employee-supervisor pairs, 27 (24.8 percent) were systems analysts, and the remaining 82 (75.2 percent) were programmers (including systems programmers and applications programmers). The relationships between the demographic variables and gender were also examined. Analysis of variance showed that age, job tenure, and number of years in the IS field were related to gender (F = 8.47, 8.82, and 9.74, respectively, p ≤ .01); women were younger, had less job tenure, and fewer years in the IS field compared to men. However, no significant relationships were observed between gender and marital status, education, and job function. Table 1 presents a summary of the demographic characteristics of the sample.

Note that in this study, 90 percent of the IS employees were evaluated by male supervisors. An examination of rater bias among male and female supervisors reveals no evidence of cross-gender bias in supervisory ratings of job performance and career advancement prospects.

## Measures

Job Performance Evaluations. The items used to construct the job performance scale were adapted from prior research (Greenhaus, et al., 1990; Touliatos, et al., 1984), with appropriate modifications to make them relevant for IS employees in this company; they were included in the Supervisor Survey (SS). Supervisors were asked to rate each of their IS employees on 21 qualities using a 5-point scale ranging from (1) unsatisfactory to (7) excellent. Examples of characteristics were ability, cooperation, job knowledge, creativity, productivity, and quality of work. A factor analysis with varimax rotation produced two factors with eigenvalues greater than or equal to 1 that accounted for 69.6 percent of the total variance. Factor 1, labeled “Task,” included the following items: ability, job knowledge, productivity, creativity, quality of work, initiative, judgment, planning, accuracy, and responsibility. These items were all associated with the task component of job performance. Factor 2, labeled “Relationship,” included ratings of commitment to the organization, commitment to the job, cooperation, honesty, interpersonal relationships, attitude, loyalty to organization, loyalty to supervisor, dependability, communication skills, and punctuality. These items were all associated with the relationships IS employees had developed with their organization and its members. However, since both factors are highly correlated (r = .70, p ≤ .001) and much of the prior research on job performance used an overall job performance evaluations measure, a second-order factor analysis was used (Nunnally, 1978). The correlation matrix of both factors was submitted to factor analysis. A second-order factor analysis revealed that one global performance factor explained 85 percent of the variance, and the ratings on the entire set of 23 items were averaged to produce a total job performance scale (alpha = .96).

Job Performance Attribution. Since supervisors' attributions represent perceived causes of task performance, their measurement requires the specification of a performance level (normally successful or unsuccessful) as a reference point. Therefore, after completing the 21-item performance rating scale, supervisors indicated whether the employee's overall performance was satisfactory or unsatisfactory in the Supervisor Survey. Those supervisors who indicated that an employee's performance was satisfactory were directed to the following question: "To what extent do each of the following factors contribute to the individual's SATISFACTORY performance?" The four attributions that followed were: "possesses the appropriate skills," "works very hard," "is lucky," and "job is very easy." The supervisor rated the importance of each factor on a 5-point scale with anchors of "not a factor" and "a significant factor" (the items were adopted from Igbaria and Wormley, 1995). (Supervisors who indicated that the employee's performance was unsatisfactory were presented with a parallel set of five failure attributions (e.g., "does not work very hard"). However, since this study's focus was on attributions regarding satisfactory performance, and the performance of 105 of the 109 employees (96.3 percent) was judged to be satisfactory, the attributions for unsatisfactory performance were not analyzed.

It should be noted that we used the “satisfactory” rating of an employee’s performance as a reference point for the supervisor’s attributions in order to include the broadest range of job performance that met or exceeded performance standards. Employees whose performance was judged to be satisfactory had substantially higher overall job performance ratings (mean = 5.31) than employees whose performance was judged to be unsatisfactory (mean = 3.93). Thus, when indicating that an employee’s performance was satisfactory, supervisors did not appear to be focusing on some narrow, barely acceptable level of performance. Further, although specific predictions were made only for ability attributions, other attributions were assessed and analyzed to provide a more thorough understanding of the attribution process.

A factor analysis of the four attributions (ability, effort, luck, and job ease) (with varimax rotation) produced two factors with eigenvalues greater than or equal to 1.0 that accounted for 75.2 percent of the variance. Factor 1 included the following two items: ability and effort. Both items tap the internal component of attributions. The two items were averaged to produce the internal dimension of attributions (reliability = .62). Factor 2 included two items—luck and job ease. The two items were averaged to produce the external dimension of attributions (reliability = .71). Finally, the internal score was divided by the external score to form a ratio such that high scores reflected a supervisor's greater use of internal rather than external attributions to explain an employee's satisfactory performance.

Career Advancement Prospects. In order to assess employees' career advancement prospects, supervisors were asked to respond to the following items on the Supervisor Survey: "How would you rate this individual's chances for promotion to a higher position sometime during his or her career with the company?" (Igbaria and Wormley, 1992). Responses to this item were made on a three-point scale with the following anchors: (1) slight chance for promotion; (2) good chance for promotion; and (3) very good chance for promotion. This item was also used by Greenhaus, et. al. (1990).

## Data analyses

The primary aim of this study was to determine similarities and differences between female and male IS employees on job performance and career advancement prospects. As indicated earlier, there were significant relationships between gender and age, job tenure, and number of years in the IS field among IS employees. Therefore, it was necessary to control for these demographic variables in all analyses so that conclusions regarding gender differences were not confounded by differences in individuals' work/life experiences (i.e., age, job tenure, IS tenure). Because of the strong intercorrelation between the work/life experiences, i.e., age and number of years in the IS field and job tenure (r = .80 and .67, respectively), only age was used as a covariate in all the analyses to eliminate multicollinearity. In each of the analyses conducted, gender was the independent variable, and age was the covariate. Gender differences in the set of variables under investigation were examined in two stages. First, multivariate analyses of covariance (MANCOVA) were performed to examine gender differences in job performance and career advancement prospects. The first MANCOVA tested gender differences in job performance evaluations. In order to assess gender differences in job performance attributions, the second MANCOVA included the job performance attributions as dependent variables. In the third MANCOVA, the dependent variables examined were the two indicators of career advancement prospects—promotability and career plateau.

Hypothesis 1 was tested using analysis of covariance, where job performance evaluation was the dependent variable, gender was the independent variable, and age was the covariate. Note that, for a medium effect size, the statistical power for MANCOVA for our sample exceeded .80 (see Cohen, 1988 for more details).

The second stage of the analyses involved testing the relationships proposed in Figure 1. In order to test Hypotheses 2 and 3, internal attributions were regressed on age in the first step, with gender added to the equation in the second step of the analysis, job performance evaluations in the third step, and the interaction (job performance and gender) added in the fourth step. Hypotheses 4, 5, 6, and 7 were tested by regressing both advancement prospects (promotability) on age (step 1), gender (step 2), job performance evaluations (step 3), job performance attributions (step 4), and both interactions (job performance rating by gender and job performance attributions by gender) (step 5). Note that the statistical power for the regression analyses exceeded .80.

## Discussion

This present study provides evidence that, in the IS field, women experience more restricted career advancement than men. Consistent with our expectations, the results of this paper show that men experience more favorable career opportunities than women. Taken as a whole, these results corroborate reports that women encounter a “glass ceiling” in business. They confirm prior research (Dubnoff and Kraft, 1986) findings that women are rarely promoted beyond a first line supervisory position. Future research should explore the potential barriers to promotability among women who have aspirations to IS upper management and executive careers. We need to look at the reasons for the existence of the barriers and possible ways to overcome them.

In our study women received job performance ratings similar to men. This is inconsistent with our expectations. One possible explanation for

the inconsistent gender effects may be related to the absence of rater bias used in this sample. Prior research suggests that male supervisors used gender rather than work-related cues in assessing the employees' job performance; i.e., raters tend to give higher ratings to same-gender rates. However, in this sample, the company is a leader in affirmative action, and therefore, supervisors may be more aware of diversity. Additionally, Pulakos, et al. (1989) also suggest that gender differences in job performance ratings may vary by occupation, where males may receive much higher evaluations than females in some occupations. It seems to us that no differences were observed in the IS field, particularly in this company. This may contribute to the range of career options within the IS field that are not limited to a particular group. Further research is needed to examine gender differences in the IS work force using different companies and industries.

Consistent with prior research (Stumpf and London, 1981), the results of this study indicate that job performance evaluations are associated with increased chances of promotability. Because the supervisor's assessment of a subordinate's job performance plays such a prominent role in forming opinions about the subordinate's advancement prospects, any bias in the performance appraisal process inevitably will produce bias in promotion decisions over time. This suggests that women and men need systematic and understandable review processes and career guidance related to their performance and career advancement prospects.

Our results suggest that the effect of job performance evaluations on career advancement varies by gender. Thus, female IS employees were seen as having relatively restricted advancement opportunities because their advancement was less affected by their performance than were their male counterparts. This also suggests that career advancement opportunities of women can be resolved if similar criteria are used for both groups. We recommend that organizations monitor the performance evaluation process to include more objective measures of job performance for both women and men IS employees and ensure that the performance appraisal process is administered fairly. Management should seek to connect linkages between job performance evaluations and advancement. Contrary to prediction, however, internal attributions had no impact on career success. But internal attributions, specifically efforts, were correlated with enhanced likelihood for promotion. Nevertheless, it should be recalled that internal attributions played a less critical role in the promotion process than the employee's level of job performance.

In support of Hypothesis 2, supervisors were unlikely to attribute the performance of female IS employees to internal factors, i.e., efforts and skills, a finding consistent with much of the previous research (Cash, et al., 1977; Deaux and Emswiller, 1974; Etaugh and Brown, 1975; Feldman-Summers and Kiesler, 1974). In other words, even when females perform satisfactorily on the job, their performance is less likely to be attributed to internal causes compared to male IS employees. This study also highlights the potential risk of supervisors discounting the successful performance of female IS employees because of their reluctance to attribute the performance of female IS employees to their effort and ability. Moreover, even if the job performance evaluations process itself is unbiased, treatment discrimination that depresses the actual job performance of female IS employees may have a secondary effect on the ability attributions utilized by supervisors. This is likely to have significant implications for promotion, pay increases, and other career outcomes for both groups. However, gender differences in internal attributions disappeared when controlling for age, suggesting that the impact of gender on supervisory attributions is mainly due to age.

Our study also shows that the interaction between job performance ratings and gender is significant. This is consistent with our framework that hypothesized underlying gender differences in internal attributions. The effect of job performance rating on attributions is much stronger for males than females. This may indicate that gender per se does not influence a supervisor's internal attributions, but rather the level of job performance the individual has achieved and their gender. Hence, women are likely to be “penalized” during the performance appraisal process, mainly through low internal attributions that follow from the low level of perceived performance. Explanations offered for the high performance of male and female IS employees differ markedly. Gender biases in attributions are likely to be evoked only when women's high job performance ratings violate gender-role stereotypes and expectations (Heilman, 1983). Results of this study highlight the potential risk of supervisors discounting the high performance of females because of their reluctance to attribute the success of females to their effort and ability.

Consistent with our expectations, women were seen by their supervisors as having somewhat less chances for promotion than men. These gender differences remained significant even after controlling for job ratings and attributions. Women were seen as less promotable than men because their job success was less likely to be attributed to their ability and effort as with men.

Organizations should be responsive to the career development needs of all employees and particularly women and minorities, because of their disparate treatment in the past and underutilized potential. A variety of approaches are being used to eliminate differential treatment and to recognize and manage the diversity that exists in the organization. Managing diversity through increased education and training, for example, can be an important step in this process. For instance, DuPont offers education and training programs such as “Men and Women Working Together” to provide fundamental information on managing diversity and reducing discrimination at all levels of the corporation (Morrison and Von Glinow, 1990).

Future research should identify the determinants of attributional patterns of supervisor-subordinate relationships, mainly among cross-gender supervisor-subordinate pairs. Organizations may be required to intervene to promote a supportive relationship among them. Computerworld (1993) reported that women in management earn 72 cents for every dollar that a male manager makes. These reward allocations by supervisors may be related to their attributions of a woman's performance being the result of external factors.

In summary, this study suggests that organizations need to be sensitive to the potential for differential treatment of male and female employees. Females were perceived as having less favorable career advancement prospects than male IS employees. Additionally, the performance of females was less likely to be attributed to ability and efforts and was more likely to be attributed to help and luck than the performance of male employees. One common element of these differentiations is the employee's immediate supervisor. In order to prepare managers and supervisors for meeting the realities of “Work force 2000,” and to address these differentiation issues, organizations should address the challenge of managing diversity in the workplace. These programs should enable managers to tap the potential of all employees, regardless of gender or race. Managing diversity through increased education and training can be an important step in this process. Future research should examine the impact of such programs on the “glass ceiling” for women and minorities. Additionally, research should examine gender differences for a variety of IS employees. Since the data came from one large utility company—a leader in affirmative action and female recruiting, the reader should be cautioned that our findings may not be generalizable to a broader cross-section of organizations. The generalizability of these findings to other companies and industries awaits confirmation through additional research. Also, our findings may result from the culture of the particular company rather than the culture of the IS occupation and should be considered in this light. The fact that job performance evaluations, job performance attributions, and career advancement prospects were all made by the same person (the supervisor) introduces the possibility that some of the findings were confounded by common method variance. Nevertheless, this study provides important insights into the advancement opportunities of women and men in the IS field. We hope that these findings will stimulate additional research in field settings on the career advancement prospects, job performance ratings and attributions of women and minorities and contribute to a deeper understanding of the dynamics of gender and race in the career development of IS managers and professionals.
