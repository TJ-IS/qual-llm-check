According to much of the prescriptive literature, bridging this gap is the CIO's definitive function. This article addresses these questions by studying five successful CIOs in five divergent industries. The CIOs were studied using the structured observation methodology employed by Mintzberg in his study of CEOs and by Ives and Olson in their study of MIS managers. The findings suggest that the CIO operates as an executive rather than a functional manager. He or she is an active participant in strategy planning and acts as a bridge between the information technology group, the functional areas, and external entities. This study provides a view of how these difficult tasks are accomplished on a day-to-day basis: through scheduled meetings, interaction outside the information technology unit, a skilled reading of situations, and a strategic focus. Factors affecting the CIO's participation in strategy planning meetings include whether he or she has formal resource allocation authority and their level of peer acceptance. Lessons learned from this research pertain to the delegation of day-to-day tasks, expenditure authority, avoiding adversarial relationships, liaison activities, careful use of language, being perceived as a user of information technology, and the need for quiet time.

## Abstract

The role of the chief information officer (CIO) continues to be the subject of much discussion and speculation. Is this just a new name for the MIS manager, or is there truly a new and significantly different function? How has the role of the information systems manager evolved with the changing needs of business? How does the CIO bridge the gap between the organization's strategy and its use of information technology?

## Introduction

Does a new executive function, called the “chief information officer,” actually exist, or is this simply a new name for the “manager of MIS” or, as previously known, the “manager of data processing”? A decade ago, Ives and Olson (1981) asked the question, “How has the role of the information systems manager evolved with the changing needs of business organizations for data processing?” (p. 49). In an MIS Quarterly article entitled, “Manager or Technician? The Nature of the MIS Manager’s Job,” they present a 1958 fictional manager, Ralph, who begins his career as a draftsman and is then transferred to data processing, “where he designed card-based systems, wired boards and occasionally ran the tab card equipment" (p. 49). By 1965, Ralph is manager of data processing, and by 1972, the manager of MIS. In 1975, Ralph is fired when a "major system exceeded time and cost estimates and failed to meet the information requirements of its highest level user" (p. 49).

The answers sought by Ives and Olson can be pursued again today as Ralph's successors seek to use information technology to achieve a competitive advantage for their firms. According to John Donovan, professor of management at MIT and chair of the Cambridge Technology Group, linking information technology to the business is a matter of survival:

If they [information managers] don't move to the strategic end, they will be lost. And so will the companies they serve. I am talking about survival . . . our economic power base is at risk.

Every one of us must understand what is at stake, what we could lose if the gap is not closed between MIS executives and the strategic objectives of the firm (Donovan, 1989, p. 84).

Other researchers define the new partnership paradigm in the 1990s as the “use of information technology” integrated with “the missions and accountabilities of line management” (Dixon and Darwin, 1989, p. 248). They point to the need to “bridge the gap” that currently exists. Although the job of today’s CIO is often described as a critical one, we know very little about the job.

Journalistic profiles, prescriptive literature, and largely undocumented surveys have described this new executive role. Some question whether such a role even exists, suggesting instead that the MIS manager has only been given a new name. Others see a real distinction. For them, the MIS manager is a functional manager with line responsibility for information technology products and services, whereas the CIO is “a senior executive responsible for establishing corporate information policy, standards, and management control over all corporate information resources” (Synnott and Gruber, 1981, p. 66). The CIO has also been described as “the corporate officer who truly understands the interconnection of the information flow to the business” (Benjamin, et al., 1985, p. 180).

If a new information technology role, the CIO, has evolved with the changing needs of business organizations, how does this new role bridge the gap between the organization's strategy and its use of information technology? How does this corporate officer come to truly understand “the interconnection of the information flow to the business”? Further still, what research method is most appropriate for answering these questions? Mintzberg (1975) has asserted that we cannot simply ask a manager what he does. According to Mintzberg,

He will most likely tell you that he plans, organizes, coordinates, and controls. Then watch what he does. Don't be surprised if you can't relate what you see to these four words (p. 49).

Mintzberg watched CEOs at work, spending one week with five of them. Such observations, according to Mintzberg, lead to a realistic description of the manager's job, without which the manager cannot hope to improve the job's effectiveness. Likewise, when Ives and Olson (1981) sought to go beyond the “numerous prescriptions for the MIS manager’s role” (p. 50), they watched the work of MIS managers, replicating and extending the observation methodology employed by Mintzberg.

In 1990, we sought to go beyond the numerous prescriptions for the CIO's role and to provide a realistic description of the job. Empirical research was conducted during 1990-91 as part of a doctoral dissertation (Stephens, 1991). This study further develops the results and conclusions. We observed CIOs at work, spending one week with five of them. Before presenting the results of these observations, this article first provides a brief description of

\- Previous research

• The research methodology for this study

• How the five CIOs were selected

• Individual CIOs and their organizations

## Previous Research

Ives and Olson (1981) found that little empirical research had been conducted on the role of the MIS manager. But in that same year, Synnott and Gruber (1981) coined the term chief information officer. Since then, a significant body of literature has begun to emerge.

One of the few empirical works to date, and certainly the most comprehensive survey on this new role, noted:

The [CIO] research that has been done frequently has not been reported in a scholarly research format. Method is often not clearly delineated, making interpretation of results, analysis of findings, and comparison among studies difficult...

Very few articles provide data to support the inferences which are drawn and the conclusions which are reached. Isolated profiles, although interesting to read, do not paint a picture of an emerging situation, and in fact, may paint the wrong picture (Brumm, 1988, p. 14).

Brumm's survey included 111 CIOs from Fortune 100 service and industrial organizations. A summary of Brumm's survey results follows. Further detail, as well as a comparison with the Stephens (1991) CIO study, is provided in Table 1.

The most frequently reported title was vice president, with only five of the 111 surveyed actually bearing the title of CIO. The chief concern of CIOs was linking information technology to business strategy. This concern also ranked first in a Business Week survey. However, only 45 percent of the CEOs and 43 percent of the CIOs in that survey agreed with the statement: “We do an excellent job of linking our information strategy to our corporate strategy” (Business Week, 1987, p. 4).

Although all CIOs had responsibility for corporate-wide strategy regarding the use of information resources, only 43 percent felt they were part of the management team that sets corporate strategy. Only 20 percent reported they attended all strategy meetings. Similarly, a 1987 Information Week survey found that only 18 percent attended all strategy meetings. Brumm did find that CIOs in service organizations were more involved in strategy formulation, earned more, and reported to a higher level in the organization.

Brown, et al. (1988) interviewed 15 CIOs from small to moderate-sized companies. They sought to answer the question: "How far have organizations other than the largest progressed from considering IS as a function limited to a support role to recognizing IS as a function with strategic significance?" (p. 29). All of the managers interviewed considered themselves as managers rather than "technically oriented professionals." Only four considered themselves to be CIOs: "In many interviews, the respondents replied with statements such as 'I do all of these things, but I don't like the term CIO.' " Nevertheless, only three believed that the CEO viewed them as "intimately involved in the running of the business or in defining the strategic opportunities to be more competitive.... There was little indication that these IS executives were truly members of the top management team" (p. 32).

Two empirical methodologies have been used to study this new executive role: the survey and the interview. CIOs in larger organizations perceive their role as strategic and have been given executive rank. Yet day-to-day participation in strategy formulation is not apparent. The highest ranking information systems managers in smaller organizations did not perceive their role as strategic and, indeed, seemed sensitive to overstepping their boundaries.

## Methodology for Studying Five CIOs

"Hundreds of studies have looked at issues related to leadership and management, but few focus on what managers actually do," observed Ives and Olson (1981, p. 51). They also called attention to the problem of managers' self-reports, a problem with surveys, interviews, the diary method, and the participant observer. Like Mintzberg (1973), they found the method of complete observation to be an expensive method but one that "provides a particularly rich view of the information systems manager's job" (p. 51). They used Mintzberg's structured observation coding scheme for classifying activities and verbal contacts.

Structured observation can provide a richness of understanding while simultaneously providing a quantitative basis for comparison with other studies. Mintzberg (1973) called structured observation “perhaps the only one that enables us to study systematically and comprehensively those parts of managerial work that are not well understood" (p. 228). $^{2}$

Similar to Mintzberg and Ives and Olson, the observer in this study worked with participants to select work weeks that would be a representative sample. $^{3}$ A pilot study was conducted to evaluate coding forms and observer obtrusiveness. $^{4}$ The five CIOs were compared to the profile from Brumm's (1988) survey of 111 CIOs (Table 1) in an effort to assess if they were representative CIOs. The evidence suggests that CIOs and observation weeks were not unrepresentative. $^{5}$

Of the nine elements coded, five were straightforward and required few inferences:

1. Medium employed (phone call, scheduled meeting, unscheduled meeting, tour, desk work)

2. Place (10 coded locations)

3. Area of each participant (external to the firm or the environment, from another functional area (e.g., marketing), from the information technology unit)

4. Participant title (10 coded titles)

5. Initiate (self, other, mutual, or regularly scheduled)

The other four elements were inferential to varying degrees:

1. Attention level (skim, read, study)

2. Purpose (Mintzberg's 69 categories and subcategories) $^{4}$

3. Role (Mintzberg's managerial roles)

4. Responsibility (eight common responsibility areas from a review of the prescriptive literature) $^{7}$

The study extended Ives and Olson's (1981) study of MIS managers and Mintzberg's (1973) study of CEOs. Three elements were added: area of each participant, managerial role, and responsibility. Otherwise, Mintzberg's categories for each element and operational definitions were used.

An activity changed whenever mediums or participants changed. For instance, if a CIO were interrupted by a telephone call while attending a scheduled meeting, the change in medium—from scheduled meeting to telephone call—would cause an activity change. Each activity was timed. Pieces of mail were coded separately, but individual pieces were not timed. Instead, we timed the desk work session.

## Selecting CIOs for Observation

The criteria for selecting five CIOs included: $^{8}$

\- Highest ranking information technology executive

\- Reports no more than two levels from CEO, i.e., either reports to the CEO or reports to one of the CEO's direct reports

\- Areas of responsibility include information systems, computer operations, telecommunications, office automation, end-user computing/information center

\- Responsible for strategic planning of information resources

Preference was given to larger organizations because a study of 15 small to moderate-sized companies' CIOs showed that their involvement in strategy planning was questionable (Brown, et al., 1988). No information technology group smaller than 30 members was to be selected. The actual range was 108 to 1,384 members. Finally, we chose to focus on effective CIOs.

## The Five CIOs

Through a literature search and academic contacts, CIOs in firms representing the following industry types were identified: insurance, university, manufacturing, government agency, and utility. A letter describing the research was mailed to each CIO. The letter was followed up with a phone call. The first five CIOs contacted agreed to participate in the study, which was conducted during the spring and summer of 1990. Each participant proved to be intensely interested in the work life of other CIOs. A brief sketch of the five CIOs and their organizations follows.

## The insurance company CIO

The insurance company CIO, a senior vice president, had been with the organization for five years but had functioned as the CIO for the last two years. He had been in the insurance industry and in information systems for 20 years. His company had recently been named one of the most innovative in America by Forbes magazine and was a member of the Forbes/Yankee Group Information Technology 100. Company officers recently testified in the U.S. Congress about their successful penetration of the Japanese market. In 1989, the company had revenues of over half a billion dollars, assets of over \$6 billion, and an annual information technology budget of over \$16 million. In 1983, a business strategy was formulated to respond to growing competitiveness and demographic shifts. This strategy was to be implemented in three phases. The culmination of the second phase, providing an infrastructure to support product growth, was a new, state-of-the-art data center, implemented in 1988-1990. The information systems division has been at the heart of the organization's successful strategy, but the growing importance of information technology caused some tension among the senior management group. The current CIO assumed the position with some reluctance after the resignation of his predecessor, a resignation attributed to pressure from other senior managers. Both the previous CIO and the current CIO were recruited to aid in the implementation of the

1983 strategy. They had worked together before joining the insurance company.

## The university CIO

The university CIO, the vice provost for information technology, was on the University Advisory Panel for the NeXT Computer Corporation. He also served as an advisor and reviewer for the National Science Foundation, was on an advisory panel for the U.S. Congress' Office of Technology Assessment, and was on the Board of Trustees for EDUCOM (Interuniversity Communications Council). He also served on the editorial board of two journals dealing with computers and education. This well-endowed, private university had an aggressive strategy for gaining national recognition, both for its undergraduate programs and its medical program. One objective was to duplicate Carnegie Mellon's strategy of becoming a computer-intensive institution. As part of this strategic thrust, every student, regardless of major, was to become well-grounded in information technology. The university had recruited the CIO from his position as vice president of computing at another university.

This CIO had over 20 years of experience in information technology at three educational institutions prior to joining this university two years previously. With the new CIO's arrival, administrative and academic computing as well as telecommunications were consolidated. Administrative systems had previously represented a substantial area of responsibility for finance, so this consolidation represented a major change. The transition generated some resistance. The Information Technology Division had an annual personnel budget of \$4.1 million.

## The manufacturing company CIO

The manufacturing company CIO, a senior vice president, had functioned as CIO for eight years but had been with the company for 26 years. His position as a member of the “senior seven” or top management circle was well established. This CIO and the company’s CEO had attended college together and remained friends through long careers with the company. They were recent recipients of the SIM Partners in Excellence Award. MIT’s Center for Information Systems Research had studied the organization’s early use of information technology to dominate its market. Instead of giving priority to what the CIO called “bread and butter” applications or providing systems that automate manual tasks, this company had given priority to systems that affected the critical success factors for their industry. Both the CIO and CEO stated that this deployment of information technology enabled them to not only survive but dominate a highly competitive industry. During the 1980s, a series of mergers and acquisitions reduced the number of competing companies, leaving only one-third the number that had existed in the 1960s. This company had only one close competitor remaining in the over \$500 million in sales category. During the observation week, the CIO spoke to a group of executives at MIT on the topic of using information technology for competitive advantage.

## The government agency CIO

The core business of this federal government agency was to manage science and engineering projects related to space transportation and exploration. This agency was one of 14 field centers with headquarters located in Washington, D.C. The government agency CIO, whose title was director of the information systems office, held a doctorate in industrial engineering. He had been with the agency for 15 years, with the information technology unit for seven years, and functioned as CIO for one and a half years. His experience was primarily in communications, an area in which this field center played a leading role for the agency. In addition to a complex, international network, his office was responsible for agency-wide administrative systems. He managed an annual budget of over \$150 million. His office employed 1,384 agency and contract employees. An academic researcher familiar with the CIO's work had recommended him for the study. This CIO was implementing a Total Quality Assurance program for information technology products and services.

## The utility company CIO

With \$20 billion in total assets and annual revenues of over \$7 billion, the electric utility company was a major player in its industry. The utility is publicly regulated, so geographical markets and rates are restricted. However, the company did not operate in a monopoly environment. The federal government had encouraged independent power producers, and these independents were selling to large industrial customers in the company's markets. Furthermore, the utility anticipated the deregulation experienced by the communications industry. The CIO, whose title was senior vice president and CIO, had been with the company for 27 years. He nevertheless felt that he was under considerable pressure. Commenting on the upcoming year, he told the observer, "If no difference, no job."

He was restructuring the information resource organizations at each of the six operating companies and at the holding company. Several data centers had already been consolidated. This CIO was recommended by academic researchers at three universities and served on the advisory board at two of these universities. He had completed an executive MBA program at one of these universities. A graduate of the U.S. Military Academy, he began his career as a junior engineer, served as a negotiator with regulators and later, with labor unions. In subsequent administrative jobs, he was given responsibility for MIS because, in his words, he was the “user who bitched the loudest.”

## Results and Discussion

The CIOs were each observed for five days, for a total of 215 hours:

• 103 hours during 109 scheduled meetings

\- 60 hours during 122 desk work sessions (processing 464 pieces of mail)

• 30 hours during 176 unscheduled meetings

• 19 hours during 174 telephone calls

\- Three hours during 42 tours or “walk abouts”

They spent half of an average day in scheduled meetings. They worked on 19 pieces of mail for about two hours in half-hour segments. During the day, 14 interruptions occurred: seven unscheduled meetings and seven phone calls. These interruptions consumed about two hours. They walked about or toured their facilities for 10 minutes.

CIOs were involved in many varied activities and had to deal with unplanned interruptions and crises, but their work did not seem fragmented. Each had a clear agenda and juggled other work around that agenda. For example, the utility company CIO sought to restructure the information resource units at the holding company and at all operating companies. The manufacturing company CIO's agenda was to implement an information resource architecture, an architecture that would allow the company's many divisions to present "one face to the market." Despite juggling many activities, often simultaneously, each CIO seemed to be in control of his time. They initiated almost half of their contacts and requested nearly half of the written reports they received. They used different methods to create quiet time: home offices with data communications capability, early morning hours, working through lunch, or "after five" hours. One CIO commented that he enjoyed travel time because he could work on the plane without interruption.

Interestingly, all the CIOs directly benefited from the technology they purveyed. Moreover, all but one believed it important to be seen as benefiting from their day-to-day use of information technology. All five had terminals, four of five used electronic mail at least once per day, four of five used voice mail, and one used an executive support system. The manufacturing company CIO traveled with a portable microcomputer and had used microcomputer software to develop an enterprise model. This model had provided a key insight into the priority for information systems development. The university CIO had two microcomputers in his office, using a NeXT for research and the other as an office automation system terminal. The utility company CIO kept his calendar on an office automation system and used electronic mail to “keep in touch,” issuing a letter to approximately 700 employees each month and receiving an average of 35 responses. His first activity every morning was to work with his executive support system. The hangar for the company plane was equipped with a microcomputer that provided access to electronic mail and the ESS. This CIO believed part of his role was to set an example for other executives by his own use of information technology. Furthermore, two CIOs had home offices equipped with data communications capability; both often worked uninterrupted at home in the evenings, early mornings, or during the weekend.

Twenty-five percent of the CIOs' mail was electronic. One-third of all incoming memos were electronic. Electronic mail was current and timely. It provided “within one day” access to the CIO without requiring the interruption of a telephone call or unscheduled meeting. One CIO commented that electronic mail prevented isolation. Many subordinates or internal customers who would feel uncomfortable dropping by or telephoning would send an E-mail note.

## Is the CIO a New Name for the MIS Manager?

The results of this and two previous observational studies (Ives and Olson, 1981; Mintzberg, 1973) may be used to answer the question posed by Ives and Olson: “How has the role of the information systems manager evolved with the changing needs of business organizations?” Does a new executive function, called the CIO, actually exist, or is this simply a new name for the “manager of MIS,” previously known as the “manager of data processing”? To answer these questions, three work characteristics were examined: the number, duration, and location of activities.

Previous research has shown that lower level managers are involved in more activities per day, each of shorter duration, than managers in positions of greater authority. First level supervisors have been shown to participate in about 200 activities per day, while Mintzberg found that chief executive officers daily engage in nineteen to thirty-two activities (Ives and Olson, 1981, p. 53).

As Table 2 shows, MIS managers did engage in more activities per day than CIOs and CEOs, and these activities were of shorter duration.

The location of activities also varies with management level. Staff managers tend to remain within their own territory (Stewart, 1976). The percent of time spent outside the manager's office increases with rank (McCall, et al., 1978). Unlike MIS managers, both CEOs and CIOs spend significant contact time away from the organization (Table 3). As the table shows, two-thirds of the CIOs' contact time is spent outside the office.

The number, duration, and location of CIO activities suggest that the role of the information systems manager has evolved. In the words of Synnott, “The CIO is not a new name for the same old thing” (1987, p. 47).

## How Does the CIO “Bridge the Gap”?

Factors affecting these CIOs' ability to successfully bridge the gap between the information technology unit and the strategic objectives of their firms are as follows:

\- The nature of the CIOs' scheduled meetings

\- Interaction outside the information technology unit

\- Maintaining a long-term, strategic focus for job responsibilities and purposes

• A skilled reading of situations

• Participation in strategy meetings

Before presenting a short list of lessons learned, each of these factors is discussed.

## Scheduled meetings

On an average day, the CIO spent half of the work day in scheduled meetings. However, these scheduled meetings looked different from those attended by earlier MIS managers. An analysis of the number of participants in oral contacts shows that over half the MIS managers' scheduled meetings were one-on-one contacts. Scheduled meetings for MIS managers tended to be shorter (Figure 1) and to involve fewer people (Table 4). The CIOs, on the other hand, were involved in larger, longer, scheduled meetings, with 45 percent of the 109 scheduled meetings observed involving more than five people. Over half these meetings consisted of people from outside the information technology unit. The primary purpose of scheduled meetings involving participants from outside the information technology group was Mintzberg's strategy purpose, followed by receiving information. Scheduled meetings were a primary means of bridging the gap and coming to understand the interconnection of the information flow to the business.

## Interaction outside the information technology unit

Ives and Olson (1981) conclude that “the contact of information systems managers with functional management at their own level is noticeably absent” (p. 61). However, in this study, 16 percent of the CIOs' contact time was spent with internal peers. Ives and Olson did not use the same activity participant categories as Mintzberg, but grouping categories (immediate subordinates, lower-level subordinates, users, corporate service groups) for MIS managers allows an approximate comparison with the CEO and CIO study. CEOs and CIOs spent about half their oral contact time with organizational subordinates. MIS managers, on the other hand, spent over three quarters of their time with organizational subordinates.

In addition to participant title categories, each activity participant's functional area was coded as being within an information technology unit, from another functional area, or external to the organization. The interaction pattern revealed was almost opposite that of the MIS manager (Figure 2). MIS managers spent a larger percentage of their contact time with subordinates than did either CEOs or CIOs and concentrated their time within their own unit. CIOs, on the other hand, spent more time interacting with those outside the information technology unit or with groups having participants from information technology and other areas. On an occurrence basis, a larger percentage of contacts were within the information technology unit, but more time was spent outside the unit. Even their mail demonstrated the CIOs' heavy interaction with participants outside the information technology unit. Fifty-seven percent of the mail involved participants from outside the information technology unit.

## Strategic focus

The CIO, as an executive, should be operating primarily at the strategic level in a decisional versus an informational role (Mintzberg, 1973). His or her concern should be more toward the long term than the day-to-day functioning of the information technology unit. The study employed two coding elements for a quantitative assessment of the CIO's strategic role:

1. Mintzberg's oral contact purpose categories
2. Areas of responsibility

## Purpose Categories

Mintzberg (1973) employed 13 oral contact purpose categories. These categories may be grouped as decisional versus informational. Contact time devoted to decisional purposes was similar for CEOs (39 percent) and CIOs (42 percent), but lower for MIS managers (29 percent). Sixty percent of all the CIOs' contacts and mail was of a decisional nature. Within the decisional categories, CIOs experienced a higher percentage of contact time devoted to the strategy purpose than either CEOs or MIS managers. Furthermore, the strategy purpose was the most important contact purpose category for CIOs, accounting for a third of all contact time.

## Areas of Responsibility: A Comparison to the Prescriptive Literature

Eight primary areas of responsibility for CIOs were derived from a review of the literature (Figure 3). Six of the eight areas of responsibilities identified were long-term in nature. These areas are given along with the percentage of verbal contact time devoted to each:

1. Policies, procedures, guidelines or standards for information resources (7 percent)

2. Strategic planning for information resources, linked to business planning, to provide improved organization functions and competitive advantage (36 percent)

3. Approval/acceptance of expenditures (10 percent)

4. Coordination of information technology, functional units, environment (21 percent)

5. Education of management, especially top management, on potential uses of technology (4 percent)

## 6. Environmental scanning (2 percent)

These long-term considerations accounted for 80 percent of contact time. They accounted for 84 percent of all mail.

Two areas of responsibility, strategic planning and coordination, consumed almost 60 percent of the CIOs' time. Three areas of responsibility accounted for almost 70 percent of the mail: approval or acceptance of expenditures, coordination, and environmental scanning. Strategic planning, which dominated oral contacts and occurred chiefly in scheduled meetings, accounted for only 9 percent of the mail.

The definitive area of responsibility, strategic planning for the use of information resources, was associated with face-to-face contact. Another strategic area emphasized in the literature, environmental scanning, was associated with desk work, as was expenditure review. Coordinating the information technology unit, external entities, and functional groups was a primary responsibility for both verbal contacts and desk work.

## Skilled reading of situations

The CIO in this study was not isolated, nor did most of his interaction occur within the information technology unit. His interaction pattern was that of a general rather than a functional manager, with coordination being a significant area of responsibility. That the CIO interacted frequently with other functional units and peers tells only half the story, however. Qualitative or anecdotal evidence suggests that these five CIOs in five very different industry types shared skill in reading situations and responding appropriately, and this skill was at the heart of their being able to move in and out of the information technology unit with ease.

All five CIOs operated in a highly charged environment where conflict was the rule, not the exception. Essential to these CIOs' ability to bridge the gap, often defusing conflict, was that each was a skilled reader of situations and people. As illustrated below, each demonstrated a certain detachment in the face of emotional conflicts and/or highly frustrating situations.

The utility CIO was in a particularly volatile situation. Anticipating deregulation, the utility company's strategy was to streamline the operations of five operating companies under the control of the holding company. Instead of each operating company acting independently, as they were required to do by existing regulations, the holding company sought to closely coordinate the five subsidiaries. The information technology unit was the first unit to undergo restructuring. Instead of five information technology units reporting to five operating company presidents—each using the services of the holding company's large information technology unit—all information technology units were to report to the holding company. Some operating company presidents, who were peers of the CIO on the senior management team, viewed this restructuring as a loss of power. Operating company information technology managers were also resisting this change.

During the observation week at this company, many frank and heated discussions took place. One of the CIO's staff members voiced his frustration at continuing opposition from an operating company president. The CIO then presented the situation from this president's point of view so persuasively that the staff member became unsure of his own position. The CIO then explained what the points of persuasion must be and where some compromises might be feasible. He was especially careful in his choice of words in his presentations of this restructuring. The phrase "centralized information systems" was replaced by "common information systems." He explained to a subordinate that none of the descriptive material should contain the word "centralized" because it connoted loss of local control and would evoke resistance. The CIO determined which managers he should personally visit, and inform about the details of this restructuring. He also determined which managers he should avoid, mainly managers with whom communication would amount to "arming the enemy."

The government agency CIO also faced high degrees of frustration working with contractors. His ability to read people and situations was critical. During a video conference with Washington concerning a substantial contract, he noticed that the recording light was on. Immediately following the video conference, he walked to the control room, spoke directly to the operator, who was employed by a contractor, and quietly requested the tape. The contractor employee gave him the tape and commented, "I told them you would ask for it." The CIO called Washington from his office, reported the incident, and stated, "That comment told me all I needed to know." From this incident and other circumstances, he concluded that the contractor's cause for anxiety was that the product in question would not perform as described consistently.

The manufacturing CIO had received complaints from the vice president of marketing about network failures that were forcing a distribution center and warehouse to fax orders to each other. Marketing called the managers who began faxing orders instead of entering them into the SOS (Stock Order System) heroes because “at least they were responding when the system wasn’t working.” The CIO called a meeting of staff members who supported the network and field systems. He began by writing in red on the marker board: “Meeting Topic: Improving Performance.” He quoted the vice president of marketing’s assessment of using fax machines instead of SOS. He listened to several technical explanations of the network problems being experienced. Then he gave the staff his reading of the situation by employing a powerful analogy:

Our problems are very visible. The business depends on our performance. It is the level of expectation that we have created by our own past performance to which we are presently being compared. We're not here to throw blame on anyone but rather to identify what I'll term responsiveness problems that we can control.

We must respond to failure like a parent responds to an electric failure when he has a baby on an iron lung. But maybe kids are like customers. Expendable. Power failure. Throw them away. Have another. File a malfunction report in triplicate. We can't just react to the kid screaming. We must know the power went out before he does. I want you to identify your pulse points, the places to monitor so that problems can be intercepted. Then use measures of these pulse points as a basis for action.

The remainder of the morning was spent identifying “pulse points” and formulating a schedule for the plan of action. When the CIO met with the vice president of marketing that afternoon, the meeting was congenial. The situation had not become an “us versus them” battle or a power struggle. The CIO presented what he perceived marketing needed to know: the schedule for corrective action. This schedule showed that major disruptions would cease within the week. Fine tuning would occur over the next three months. Then, he briefly explained, without using jargon, the network problems experienced.

This CIO believed that hiring personnel, from programmer to vice president of information systems, was a strategic decision. Accordingly, he personally interviewed every new hire. Why strategic? He believed that when systems are mission-critical, life in the information technology unit is life on the front line. The unit requires people who thrive on the resulting intensity and unpredictability of their jobs. Those who seek routine will not remain as alert and responsive, and when that responsiveness lapses, a consequence that is irreversible in the short-term will occur: customers will be lost. His reading of the above situation was that once alertness and responsiveness were restored, technical solutions would follow.

## Participation in strategy meetings

Brumm (1988) found that CIOs neither attended strategy meetings on a regular basis nor felt they were part of the management team that set strategy. The CIOs observed in this study, however, were all participants in strategy meetings and were part of the senior management team that sets strategy. Two factors seemed to affect the participation of these five CIOs:

1. Formal authority to approve or disapprove expenditures for information technology, and

2. Informal authority through personal and political acceptance by other senior managers.

The CIO's authority to approve or disapprove expenditures provided an incentive for other senior managers to accept the CIO as a member of the inner circle rather than as an occasional technical consultant. Nevertheless, the CIOs actively cultivated peer acceptance. They worked to defuse political resistance to the new power base they represented.

The university CIO had proposed and gained acceptance for a student computing fee. He insisted on having authority to approve or disapprove allocation of these new funds. At the same time, he visited academic areas and listened to their plans for using these funds. He exercised during lunch in the faculty gym to “keep up his contacts.” He served as an advocate for enhanced academic computing facilities and established a faculty information technology center in the library. He realized that his existing authority to approve and disapprove expenditures on administrative systems was not accepted by the vice provost of finance, who previously had authority for such expenditures. During the observation week, the vice provost of finance's resentment was expressed by an attempt to sabotage a current administrative system project. The CIO was careful to avoid an adversarial response. During a project meeting, he praised successful projects in other areas, citing the names of those clients who were making contributions. He invited two of these clients to attend the project meeting and act as consultants to this troubled project. Furthermore, he called in an outside consultant to monitor the situation and report to the university president and provost. This action ensured that those to whom he reported were informed but prevented the CIO from becoming involved in “finger pointing.” The CIO actively avoided becoming entangled in conflict and thereby damaging relationships with peers.

The insurance company CIO replaced a CIO who had dealt with these kinds of political conflicts head on. This former CIO had never gained the acceptance of other senior executives. Instead, they resented the sudden power he seemed to have acquired as information technology became an increasingly important factor in the organization's success. This CIO became powerful, heading the strategic planning group and reporting directly to the CEO. The current CIO benefited from the strategic role played by the former CIO but concentrated on becoming personally and politically accepted by his peers. He wrote letters keeping the senior management circle informed on information technology projects. He focused on common goals rather than turf. At the dedication of the new state-of-the-art data center, he transformed Data Center Day into D-Day. His dedication speech humorously compared moving to the new data center without interrupting services to the pivotal World War II battle. He assigned other senior executives the roles of war heroes: Montgomery, Ike, Churchill, and President Roosevelt. The impressive new facility was portrayed as a monument to their joint endeavors, with the enemy being competition from other insurance companies.

The manufacturing company CIO had been with the company for more than 20 years, as had almost all of the senior management team. His acceptance in the inner management circle was secure. His authority was both formal and informal. Still, the CIO reinforced his participation in strategic planning by acting as what he selfdeprecatingly called the “scribe” for these meetings. He summarized the discussion at each meeting and carefully revised his dictated notes to make sure they were accurate. He sent copies of the completed summary to all meeting participants. This CIO commented on the value of putting strategies in written form: “Often we find they don’t hold water.” This CIO believed that his role as “scribe” leveraged his influence over peers and the CEO, giving him an additional, yet subtle, measure of control over strategy.

These CIOs all had resource allocation authority and were therefore a force to be reckoned with. However, they avoided adversarial positions whenever possible, seeking instead the informal acceptance of peers. Empathy, detachment, and careful reading of situations often enabled them to defuse conflict and maintain good relationships with peers. An important aspect of maintaining comfortable peer relationships was the CIOs' use of language. The CIOs spoke to these peers in business language, not technical jargon, and deferred to others when technical expertise was needed. They often pretended ignorance and were self-effacing to encourage others to speak freely. Their participation in strategy planning meetings and frequent interaction with participants outside the information technology unit were both effects of their informal acceptance and authority.

## Caution in Interpreting Results

The limitations of Ives and Olson's (1981) study of MIS managers apply to this study of CIOs as well:

It is based on a small, self-selected sample of managers that may not be representative of the broader population.... We provided some methodological rigor through the structured observation approach.... Nevertheless, this type of study is, almost by definition, non-rigorous. It provides us with a rather fuzzy, big picture of the information systems manager's job (p. 60).

This statement particularly applies to those coding elements that were inferential. However, other coding elements were not inferential: activity changes, mediums, durations, participants' titles, location, and group membership for participants. Observation weeks were found to be representative in terms of scheduled meetings, the dominant medium, and the judgements of CIOs and administrative assistants or secretaries. Profiles for the CIOs observed were similar to the 111 CIOs identified by Brumm (1988). Still, caution must be exercised in generalizing the results. The selection strategy used suggests that these five CIOs may represent leading edge CIOs rather than the larger population of CIOs.

But the strengths of continuous structured observation as an empirical research method must be recognized. Results are not self-reported or delegated to a staff member, as might be the case with a survey. Diaries and logs are plagued by problems of completeness as the executive attempts to maintain a hectic work pace while simultaneously logging activities. The researcher has first-hand exposure to the management function under study, providing in the words of Ives and Olson (1981), a “particularly rich view” (p. 60) of the manager’s job. They noted: “categorization by activities, purpose of contact, or message units tells only part of the story. Through observation we learned some revealing aspects of the nature of the job and the environment” (p. 57) of the manager.

This rich view of the CIO's job did provide some insight into how these five CIOs were able to do their jobs. Clearly, formal authority and status is a necessary but not sufficient condition for this highly political role.

## Lessons Learned

In conclusion, the following is a short list of lessons learned from quantitative and qualitative analysis of observation data. While many lessons were contingent upon a particular situation, these lessons seemed common to CIOs in all five industry types.

\- Delegate day-to-day or line management tasks. Number of activities per day, average duration of activities, location of activities, and size of meetings and whether they tend to be scheduled or unscheduled are all indicants of whether line management tasks are dominant.

\- Seize expenditure authority for information technology. Leverage the resource allocator role.

\- Avoid adversarial positions. Practice detachment and the careful reading of situations.

\- Initiate contacts outside the information technology unit, particularly with internal peers.

\- Use language carefully. Avoid jargon, exploit the connotative value of words, and recognize the value of the written medium, particularly for strategy planning.

\- Use information technology in your personal work environment. Being perceived as a user of the technology purveyed can help you become a more effective promoter of the IS function within the organization.

\- Establish daily “quiet time” for reflective, organizing activities.

This study has provided empirical evidence that the CIO's role is indeed different from that of the MIS manager: the CIO is an executive rather than a functional manager. As the senior executive charged with bridging the gap between information technology and other functional units, and between the organization's strategy and its use of information technology resources, the CIO's role is primarily a strategic one. And this strategic role seems to be influenced primarily by two factors: the CIO's degree of resource allocation authority and informal acceptance. As Synnott (1987) declares, "The CIO is not a new name for the same old thing" (p. 47). We certainly agree.
