## Abstract

National commissions and scholarly reports on the status of contemporary higher education have frequently been critical of the college experience; the emphasis on transmitting fixed bodies of information and a failure to develop problem solving and critical thinking skills have been cited as serious weaknesses in higher education systems. Colleges and universities have additional reasons to redevelop central pedagogies for students. Individuals need to learn at higher rates of effectiveness and efficiency than ever before because of rapidly growing bodies of relevant information and the escalation of knowledge and skill requirements for most jobs.

Recent developments in computer hardware, software, and communication technologies create exciting new opportunities for the educational use of these technologies. The objective of this study is to go beyond the traditional classroom instructional modes (e.g., lectures and class discussions) to develop and evaluate computer-supported pedagogical approaches. More specifically, this study investigates whether the use of a group decision support system (GDSS) in a collaborative learning process enhances student learning and evaluation of classroom experiences.

The findings of a study involving 127 MBA students indicate that GDSS-supported collaborative learning leads to higher levels of perceived skill development, self-reported learning, and evaluation of classroom experience in comparison with non-GDSS supported collaborative learning. Furthermore, the final test grades of the group of students who were exposed to GDSS-supported collaborative learning were significantly higher than those of the other group of students who participated in the experiment.

Keywords: Computer-mediated learning, cooperative learning, computer-supported team learning, educational technology

## Introduction

Learning and educational effectiveness at all levels has become a major national issue during the last decade. For example, national commissions and scholarly reports on the status of contemporary higher education have criticized the pedagogical approaches that focus on conveying fixed bodies of information and view students as passive recipients of knowledge (Bok, 1986; Boyer, 1987). Bok (1986), in his report on higher education, concluded that:

The vast and rapidly growing accumulation of information and knowledge has implications at all levels of education. In the Colleges, the most apparent need is to charge the emphasis of instruction away from transmitting fixed bodies of information toward preparing students to engage in continuing acquisition of knowledge and understanding. In terms of pedagogy, the preparation for continuous learning implies a shift toward more active forms of instruction . . . . It is time, therefore, to think seriously about multiplying the opportunities for students to reason carefully about challenging problems. Such an effort will presumably call for greater emphasis on promoting active discussion in class (p. 165).

In many cases, this often-cited failure to actively engage students in the learning process has been attributed to an over reliance on the lecture method of instruction. For example, the lecture method, resulting in long periods of uninterrupted instructor-centered, expository discourse, relegates students to the role of passive “spec-tators” in the classroom (Johnson, et al., 1991).

Colleges and universities have additional reasons to rethink and redevelop central pedagogies for students. The increasing pace of technological change, globalization, and shifts in public policy have bred a turbulent and competitive business environment. To keep up with the rapid changes in turbulent business environments, employees need to learn at a more effective and efficient rate than before. Estimates of expenditures on staff development initiatives in organizations range up to \$210 billion annually (Wexley and Latham, 1991), yet less than 10 percent of those funds may produce behavioral changes on the job (Georgenson, 1982). Thus, the need for enhancing learning process effectiveness and efficiency is urgent in both the academic and business environments.

Many educators, students, and employers intuitively feel that the integration of information technology (computing and communication technologies) into the classroom will enhance learning and the students' ability to apply knowledge and skills to problem-solving situations. Despite this belief, pedagogic practice has not yet fully integrated information technology (IT) into the curriculum and classroom activity, and computers remain greatly under-utilized in this context.

In the past, research examining educational use of computers has been dominated by CAI (Computer-Assisted Instruction) applications, where an interactive computer program is used for delivery of different instructional capabilities including drill and practice, test taking, and simulation. Several researchers have advocated expanding the role of computers in the educational process beyond the traditional CAI applications. Bork (1985) states that too often, people applying computers to learning situations “tend to transpose books and lectures, and so they miss the opportunity for use of the computer for creating responsive and active learning environments” (p. 7). Improvements in the price/performance ratios of hardware and communication devices and extended capabilities delivered through software tools create exciting new opportunities for the educational use of computers and provide unique possibilities for the integration of information technology into college classroom activities and interactions. Norman (1990) surveyed the current state of computers in the classroom and concluded that a more integrated approach was needed. He then proposed the concept of an electronic teaching theater that takes advantage of hypermedia to facilitate information retrieved and information processing in the college classroom. Leidner and Jarvenpaa (1993) examined the use and outcomes of computer-based instructional technology in the context of graduate business education. They explored the use of computer-based teaching methods and their impacts on classroom interactions and learning. Briggs and Ramesh (1992) report on the development and implementation of an electronic learning environment (Exemplar) based on group support technology.

The integration of information technology with the college classroom, however, is by no means trivial and is not simply a matter of training the instructor and the students in the use of the computers. Effective use and integration of computers into the classroom requires a departure from the traditional instructional mode so that technology-mediated communication in the classroom becomes pedagogically superior to the alternative modes of instruction. Research is needed to provide information and insight into the impact of emerging information technology on the learning process.

The objective of this study is to go beyond the traditional classroom instructional modes (e.g., lectures and class discussions) and to evaluate a computer-supported approach that may enhance learning outcomes and student classroom experiences. More specifically, this study investigates the effectiveness of computer-mediated collaborative learning (CMCL) in terms of student learning and evaluation of the classroom experience.

## Conceptual and Theoretical Research Framework

## Theoretical background

A review of literature and research in the area of cognitive learning theory reveals that although no single or unified learning theory exists, three attributes of effective learning processes can be readily identified:

1. Active learning and construction of knowledge;

2. Cooperation and teamwork in learning; and

3. Learning via problem solving.

## Active Learning and Construction of Knowledge

Cognitive models of learning stress that learning is an active, constructive and goal-oriented process (Shuell, 1986; Wittrock, 1978; 1986). Learners construct meaning from the material studied by processing it through existing mental structures and then retaining it in long-term memory where it remains available for further processing and possible reconstruction (Johnson, et al., 1991). According to this concept, learning is best accomplished by engaging students in constructing knowledge by acquiring, generating, analyzing, manipulating, and structuring information.

## Cooperation and Teamwork in Learning

Some learning theories emphasize learning's social genesis and suggest the view that it is a social process that occurs more effectively through interpersonal interactions in a cooperative (versus a competitive) context (Piaget, 1926; Vygotsky, 1978). Several studies have pointed out the positive motivational and effective cognitive aspects involved in group-oriented learning processes (see Brown and Palincsar, 1989, for an extensive review of relevant studies). For example, teamwork in learning extends the locus of metacognitive activity by providing triggers for cognitive dissatisfaction outside the individual. Team members can monitor individual thinking, opinions, and beliefs and provide feedback for clarification and change (i.e., learning). Furthermore, an individual's exposure to alternative points of view can challenge his/her initial understanding and thus motivate learning (Glacer and Bassok, 1989). Cooperation and teamwork can further support learning by providing social support and encouragement for individual efforts.

## Learning via Problem Solving

The notion of learning via problem solving (i.e., learning by doing) is supported by the view that learning is a process of building and transforming mental models (cognitive representation of elements comprising a domain and their interrelationship) (Ansari and Simon, 1979; Klahn, 1984; Neches, 1987; Siegler 1986). Transformations entail changes in organization and structure of knowledge and primarily occur in the context of problem solving. Learning is then believed to be expedited in challenging problem-solving situations in which mental models are tested, extended, and refined until they are effective and reliable in solving that problem.

Learning strategies that encompass these three attributes of effective learning have been promoted over traditional strategies that involve passive (versus active), competitive (versus cooperative), and individualistic (versus group-oriented) learning (Shuell, 1986).

This paper focuses on one such strategy—collaborative learning—that embodies the three attributes of effective learning described in this section.

## Collaborative learning

Collaborative learning evolved from the work of psychologists such as Johnson and Johnson (1975) and Slavin (1987). It involves social (interpersonal) processes by which a small group of students work together (i.e., cooperate and work as a team) to complete an academic problem-solving task designed to promote learning (i.e., get actively involved and participate in problem solving). Thus, the collaborative learning concept is based on the three premises of effective learning described in the previous section. In fact, collaborative learning has been advocated as the primary means of operationalizing the three attributes of effective learning (Johnson, et al., 1991). According to Whipple (1987), in the collaborative learning approach, “collaborative activities lead to emergent knowledge, which is the result (not summation) of interaction of the understandings of those who contribute to its formation” (p. 5).

Collaborative activities enhance learning by allowing individuals to exercise, verify, solidify, and improve their mental models through discussions and information sharing during the problem-solving process (i.e., while working on the assigned academic task). Several studies have reported on the effectiveness and benefits of collaborative learning in higher education. Collier (1980) summarizes several reports of increased student involvement in courses as a result of collaborative learning procedures. Cooper, et al. (1990) observed that collaborative learning increases student involvement with the course material and with each other as they work together in small groups in performing an academic task.

Numerous studies have shown that increased student involvement in the learning process promotes problem solving and critical thinking skills. For example, Bligh (1972) reviewed close to 100 studies of student learning at the college level. He concludes that students who interact with other students and are engaged in discussion of their ideas are less likely to have irrelevant or distracting thoughts and spend more time in synthesizing and integrating ideas and concepts compared with students who listen to lectures. Kulik and Kulik (1979) reviewed the research on college teaching-method effectiveness. They conclude that student discussion groups (a form of collaborative learning) are more effective than the lecture method in promoting student problem-solving skills. Similarly, McKeackie (1980) and McKeackie, et al. (1987) observed that working in interacting groups facilitates students' acquisition of critical thinking skills and metacognitive learning strategies (i.e., self-monitoring and learning how to learn). Smith (1977; 1986) studied college classes in a variety of disciplines and found that collaborative procedures (student-student interactions) are related to higher levels of critical and active thinking and lower levels of rote memorization.

Collaborative learning procedures have also been found to be more effective than traditional instructional methods in promoting student learning and academic achievement. Johnson, et al. (1981) and Slavin, et al. (1985) found that compared to individual and/or competitive instructional methods, collaborative instructional methods involving cooperative procedures are more effective in promoting student learning and achievement.

Collaborative learning procedures have also been shown to enhance student satisfaction with the learning and classroom experience. Several studies investigating college-level learning found that students who follow in-class collaborative learning procedures and actively interact with each other are more satisfied with their learning experience and evaluate their courses more favorably than students who are exposed to the traditional lecture method (Bligh, 1972). Kulik and Kulik (1979) in their review of literature on college-level learning, reached a similar conclusion. They report that students who have exposure to collaborative methods (e.g., discussion groups) had a more favorable evaluation of their classroom experience. These and other research findings have led to a growing interest in use of collaboration learning in higher education as a viable and effective instructional strategy (Cooper, et al., 1990).

The objective of this study is to investigate whether the effectiveness of collaborative learning can be further enhanced through application of computer and communication-based capabilities in the form of group decision support systems (GDSS).

GDSS refers to an integrated set of hardware, software, and communication capabilities aimed at improving group interactions and task performance during face-to-face meetings (DeSanctis and Gallupe, 1987; Pinsonneault and Kraemer, 1990).

## Enhancing the effectiveness of collaborative learning: The potential role of GDSS

GDSS typically supports groups through one or a combination of the following four mechanisms: process support, process structure, task structure, and task support (Nunamaker et al., 1991). Process support is provided through an electronic communication infrastructure (electronic messaging capabilities obtained through a local area network). Process structure refers to techniques or rules, such as electronic Nominal Group Technique (NGT) and agendas, that direct the pattern, timing, or content of group interactions. Task support refers to the information and computational infrastructure (e.g., external databases and computational models) provided by the GDSS (DeSanctis and Gallupe, 1987). Task structure refers to the analytical techniques and models (e.g., a budgeting model) for processing task-related information and task accomplishment.

These four mechanisms are the primary means by which a group decision support system increases the effectiveness or efficiency of face-to-face interacting groups. This is accomplished by increasing group process gains and/or reducing group process losses. Process gains refer to the synergistic aspects of the group interaction that improve group performance relative to the individual member performance. Process losses, on the other hand, refer to certain aspects of the group interactions that impair group performance relative to the efforts of individual members working alone. The following are examples of group process gains and process losses adapted from Nunamaker, et al. (1991).

## Group Process Gains:

\- A group as a whole generates more information and alternatives compared to the average group member;

\- Groups are more effective and objective in evaluation and error detection tasks;

\- Working in a group may motivate the individual member to perform better;

\- Interactions among the group members lead to synergies.

## Group Process Losses:

\- Member participation in the group process is fragmented (i.e., group members should take turns in speaking);

\- One or a few individual members may dominate group discussions and monopolize the group's time;

\- Fear of negative evaluation (evaluation apprehension) causes members to withdraw and avoid participating in the group discussions;

\- Higher volumes of information generated during the group process creates a condition of information overload for individual members.

Numerous laboratory and field studies have shown that GDSS capabilities and features can facilitate group interactions and improve group performance by increasing process gains and reducing process losses. $^{1}$ For example, electronic communication channels increase the amount of information and alternatives generated by groups (a process gain) by providing simultaneous input channels and thus, eliminating or reducing fragmentation of member participation (a process loss). Anonymity of electronic input, another feature of most GDSSs, can decrease or eliminate evaluation apprehension (a process loss) leading to an increase in member participation and the amount of information generated by the group (a process gain).

Based on the literature and bodies of research in the areas of collaborative learning and group decision support systems cited earlier, and since collaborative learning encompasses face-to-face interacting groups, the following specific hypothesis was formulated.

Hypothesis: GDSS enhances the effectiveness of collaborative learning (defined in terms of self-reported learning and evaluation of classroom experience) by increasing group process gains and decreasing group process losses.

This hypothesis can be further explained in terms of the impacts of GDSS capabilities on the underlying theoretical premises of collaborative learning: active learning and construction of knowledge, cooperation and teamwork, and modification of mental models via problem solving. More specifically:

\- GDSS increases effectiveness of collaborative learning by increasing student participation and active involvement in knowledge construction by facilitating generation, exchange, and analysis of information during the learning group interactions.

\- GDSS increases effectiveness of collaborative learning by supporting cooperation and teamwork among the students through facilitating information sharing and group process support and process structuring.

\- GDSS increases effectiveness of collaborative learning by facilitating evaluation and modification of student's mental models through exposure to alternative perspectives and increased and rapid feedback from group members.

The impact of GDSS on collaborative learning was evaluated in an empirical study involving MBA students.

## Study Design

## The subjects

The subjects (77 males, 50 females) for the experiment were MBA students enrolled in three core courses in management information systems. $^{2}$ All the three classes were taught by the same instructor. Of these students, 79 attended class in a classroom equipped with GDSS hardware and software (the teaching theater, described below) and 48 attended class in a traditional classroom setting. The average age of the subjects participating in the experiment was 27.2. A post-experimental analysis indicated that there was no significant difference in the background of the subjects in terms of GMAT scores, information system experience, and prior familiarity with the GDSS tools employed in the experiment.

## Procedure

All participants attended a semester-long course in management information systems. The course met once a week for a period of two hours and forty minutes. To control for potential experimenter bias, an identical set of classroom protocols and procedures was followed by the instructor in each of the three classes. Lecture notes were followed very closely, and the same material in an identical sequence and form over the same time period was presented to the students in each class. Furthermore, the textbook, cases, and in-class group exercises were identical for the three courses involved in the study. The primary difference between the courses was the use of the GDSS tools in the teaching theater classroom to support student interactions during the collaborative learning group exercises.

The teaching theater has 20 custom-designed desks capable of supporting two students each. Each desk is equipped with an MS-DOS, 25 MHZ, 386-based computer with 8 MB of memory, a 17-inch high-resolution monitor, and a keyboard. All of the computers are linked via a local area Novell network. The GDSS software installed in the teaching theater is VisionQuest™ by the Collaborative Technology Corporation of Austin, Texas. Version 2 of VisionQuest used in this experiment consists of nine tools aimed at group process support: brainstorming, comment cards, compactor, point allocation, ranking, rating, scoring, subgroups selection, and voting. A brief description of VisionQuest tools is presented in Table 1. Audiovisual equipment in the teaching theater consisted of two 6 foot by 9-1/2 foot rear projector screens for displaying the contents of computer screens, a white board, and an overhead projector.

The students in the two classes that met in the teaching theater were instructed in the use of VisionQuest through specific examples and “hands-on” tutorials during the first two weeks of the semester. At the conclusion of the second week of classes, a homework project, designed to give the students further practice with the VisionQuest software system, was assigned. The students were given a week to complete the assigned homework project, during which the instructor held daily office hours to assist the students and respond to their VisionQuest questions. At the start of the third week of class, a quiz testing the students’ knowledge and proficiency in setting up and working with VisionQuest tools was administered. Both the VisionQuest homework assignment results and the high grades earned on the quiz indicated that the students who met in the electronic classroom were adequately trained on the use of VisionQuest.

The third class regularly met in a traditional classroom with no computer support. The classroom was equipped with an overhead projector and a white board.

The specific collaborative learning structure employed in the study was a cooperative technique called Student Team-Achievement Division (STAD) developed by Slavin (1987). STAD procedures are displayed in Figure 1. At the outset of each class, the instructor introduced a topic in a relatively short lecture (40-45 minutes). Reading material (articles and textbook chapters) on the topic were previously assigned and the students were asked to complete the assigned readings prior to each class meeting. The objective of the lecture was to review and highlight the key concepts in the assigned readings and to answer student questions. At the conclusion of the lecture, the group learning task was assigned. The task involved analysis of a short (2-3 pages) business case related to the concepts and issues covered in the lecture and the assigned readings. The 10 business cases used in the study were adapted from Laudon and Laudon (1991). The cases facilitated learning by providing a problem-solving context for developing and refining mental models of the concepts covered in the lectures (e.g., strategic information systems).

Student groups who were randomly assigned to work together as a team then engaged in the group case analysis. The teams were required to submit a short summary of their analysis at the conclusion of the group case analysis. The team case solutions were graded by the instructor and were counted toward the final course grade. The student groups were formed by the instructor in the second week of the semester. One group was reduced from four members to three when a group member dropped the course in the third week of the semester (prior to the start of the study). The composition of student teams remained the same throughout the semester.

Two important features of STAD technique (and collaborative learning in general) are: positive interdependence and individual student accountability. Positive interdependence means that students within the learning groups should feel responsible for one another's learning. Individual accountability means that although students are expected to help one another learn, each student is assessed individually concerning his/her mastery of the concepts and the material involved in the group learning exercises. Thus, specific procedures (such as the one described in the next paragraph) should be built into collaborative learning approaches to ensure individual accountability as well as cooperation and positive interdependence among the student team members.

In the specific collaborative learning procedure employed in this study, positive interdependence and individual accountability goals were obtained through individual team member questioning at the conclusion of the group learning exercise. That is, individual team members were randomly called upon to explain, discuss, or answer specific questions regarding the team case analysis and solution. Every student was at some point called upon during the semester. Individual responses were graded by the instructor and were counted toward the class participation component of the final course grade. Furthermore, for each team, the group case analysis grades were weighted by the individual response grades. This was done to ensure positive interdependence among the team members where team members felt responsible for one another's learning. $^{3}$ Overall, the students participated in 10 collaborative learning exercises following the procedures described in this section.

## Experimental manipulation

Two classes met in the teaching theater equipped with VisionQuest, and the third class met in a traditional classroom. All the teams in the three classes followed the STAD collaborative learning procedures displayed in Figure 1. The experimental manipulation involved use of GDSS capabilities by the student teams in the teaching theater to support the team activities during the group case analyses. That is, the teams in the teaching theater were required to use VisionQuest tools in support of the assigned academic tasks (group case analysis). The student teams in the teaching theater classroom were not restricted from face-to-face verbal communication and were free to use VisionQuest electronic tools in any combination and sequence that they deemed appropriate to generate ideas, share and structure information, and comment on issues in support of their in-class discussions. For example, one VisonQuest dialog created by a student group included both a brainwriting tool to generate ideas and a rating tool to rate the quality of these ideas. Additionally, the dialog featured a comment card tool that was employed by the group to elaborate on and refine the three most highly rated ideas.

The student teams that met in the traditional classroom worked on the same assignments, but no computer tools for support of their in-class group interactions were made available.

## Dependent measures

Consistent with the research literature in the area, collaborative learning effectiveness (the dependent variable of the study) was measured in terms of students' perceptions of their learning and their evaluation of their classroom experience. The 28 items used to measure students' perceived learning and classroom evaluation were included in the post-course questionnaire adapted from Hiltz (1988). Hiltz' questionnaire was developed to assess the relative effectiveness of an online course and was in turn based on a thorough review of the literature on learning/teaching effectiveness, particularly Centra's (1982) summary. Five-point Likert-type scales were used to measure all items. The learning and evaluation items were subjected to separate principal component analysis, followed by varimax rotations. Results indicated the presence of five (70.9 percent variance explained) and three factors (67.1 percent variance explained) for the learning and evaluation items, respectively.

Of the five factors derived from the self-reported learning items, three coherent scales with acceptable alpha reliabilities were obtained. These scales, displayed in Table 2, included perceived skill development (consisting of six items such as critical thinking and ability to synthesize), self-reported learning (consisting of three items such as understanding basic concepts and learning facts), and learning interest (consisting of three items such as discussing topics outside the class and doing additional reading). Two coherent scales with acceptable alpha reliabilities were obtained from the evaluation items. These scales, also displayed in Table 2, included class evaluation (consisting of four items such as finding the course to be a good learning experience) and group case exercise evaluation (consisting of three items such as group work contributing to learning). Means, standard deviations, and correlations of these five scales are presented separately by experimental condition in Table 3. Alpha reliability coefficients for each scale are provided in the diagonal.

It was impossible to connect questionnaire responses with these test scores because the questionnaires were administered anonymously. Nevertheless, it was possible to use these scores as a proxy measure of subjects' learning achievement and assess test score differences by the experimental condition.

## Data Analysis and Results

## Questionnaire data

Bivariate correlations revealed that the learning and evaluation scales were moderately intercorrelated. Therefore, a one-way multivariate analysis of variance was conducted to compare the questionnaire responses of subjects who participated in the computer-mediated (i.e., GDSS-supported) collaborative learning process with those who participated in the manual collaborative learning process. Results indicated a significant effect for use of GDSS to support collaborative learning (Holeling's T = 41.27, p < .05). Subsequent univariate analysis of variance (displayed in Table 4) revealed that, across all learning and evaluation questionnaire items, the computer support had significant effects on participants' affective reactions: perceived skill development, self-reported learning, interest in learning the subject, evaluation of classroom experience, and evaluation of group learning exercise (group case analysis). Inspection of the means reported in Table 3 indicates that students who met in the teaching theater and used GDSS capabilities for support of their collaborative group learning exercises had more positive reactions than students who were taught in the traditional classroom with no GDSS support for their group exercises. Results are presented graphically in Figures 2 and 3.

One possible explanation for these findings is that participants who were exposed to computer-mediated collaborative learning in the teaching theater classroom had more positive reactions because they expected to receive higher grades in the course. However, this hypothesis is not supported by the data. A t-test of the mean expected grades in the teaching theater (M = 1.42) and traditional classroom (M = 1.32) revealed no significant differences ( $t(101) = -.89$ , non-significant). Moreover, the correlation between expected grades and the learning and evaluation scales was not significant for the total sample or within the treatment condition (see Table 3). Therefore, the positive reactions of students exposed to the computer-mediated collaborative learning cannot be attributed to differences in expected grades.

In order to explore the relationship and the relative importance of the learning scales to evaluation scales, a stepwise regression between these two variable sets was conducted. One (of the three) learning scale was significantly related to evaluation of the collaborative group learning exercise (group case analysis). A positive coefficient was obtained for skill development and group case analysis evaluation ( $\beta = .25$ , $\Delta R^2 = .06$ , $F = 6.47$ , $p < .05$ ). The stepwise regression results also indicated that two of the learning scales were significantly related to the evaluation of the classroom experience: skill development ( $\beta = .24$ , $\Delta R^2 = .24$ , $F(1, 98) = 6.42$ , $p < .05$ ), and self-reported learning ( $\beta = .54$ , $\Delta R^2 = .29$ , $F(2, 97) = 20.03$ , $p < .001$ ). This indicates that students with higher levels of perceived skill development and learning rated the group learning exercises more favorably.

## Test score data

Test scores for participants who were exposed to GDSS-supported collaborative learning in the teaching theater classroom were compared with those participants in the traditional classroom. Analysis indicated no significant differences in midterm exam scores (teaching theater M=89.72, traditional classroom M=90.92, t(125)=1.23, non-significant). However, participants in the teaching theater classroom achieved higher final exam scores (M=88.23) than did participants in the traditional classroom (M=83.97, t(125)=-3.92, p<.001). While it is not clear if the more positive reactions of the teaching theater classroom participants led to higher final test scores, the data do suggest that the teaching theater classroom in which the students were exposed to computer-mediated collaborative learning may produce better learning outcomes.

## Summary and Discussion

In this study, the impact of an emerging information technology—a group discussion support system—on collaborative learning was investigated in a field setting.

MBA students enrolled in management information systems courses participated in the study. Students in the experimental group used a GDSS (VisionQuest) to complete in-class collaborative learning exercises. More specifically, student teams consisting of four individuals used Vision-Quest tools (e.g., brainstorming, comment card, etc.) to support group analysis of business cases using a collaborative learning technique called STAD developed by Slavin (1987). Student teams in the control group used STAD technique to analyze the same cases, but no GDSS was made available for support of the teams' learning processes. Data analysis supported the research hypothesis, indicating that overall, students' affective reactions to the computer-mediated collaborative learning process were more positive than the manual collaborative learning process. That is, students who used GDSS in support of their group learning activities perceived higher levels of skill development, learning, and interest in learning relative to students who did not use GDSS. Furthermore, students who used GDSS had a more positive evaluation of the classroom experience and the group learning activities relative to students who did not use GDSS to support their group learning process.

Although the specific mechanisms by which a GDSS may increase the effectiveness of collaborative learning were not investigated in this study, some of the students' comments in response to the open-ended post-study questionnaire items provide further insight into possible interactions between GDSS features and specific learning mechanisms discussed earlier in the paper. For example, one student commented that what he/she liked best about the in-class group exercise was “the opportunity to pursue others’ comments; to draw on others’ experiences and thought processes without ever having to ask them.” Exposure to different perspectives and others’ opinions facilitates formation and/or modification of mental models, thus increasing learning effectiveness. Another student commented that he/she liked “the ability to aggregate data and opinions to get an overall feeling about case issues [the learning task].” This comment suggests that GDSS enhances learning by facilitating active construction and development of emergent knowledge during group activities. Furthermore, it appears that the information structuring and information sharing feature of GDSS contributes to collaborative learning effectiveness by enhancing cooperation and teamwork as evidenced in the following student comment: “Group analysis with computers was very effective. Linking each others’ ideas supported group discussion well.”

It would be interesting for future research to pinpoint the specific attributes and capabilities of GDSS (e.g., idea and information generation, data categorization and aggregation, alternative evaluation, analytical capabilities, etc.) that contribute to group learning outcomes and effectiveness. It would also be important to study the impact of the sequence and combination of these capabilities on the learning outcomes. This line of inquiry would have important implications for development of software environments that are particularly well-suited for support of group learning processes.

Another interesting finding of this study was that the final course grades of the students who were exposed to the computer-mediated collaborative learning were significantly higher than the students who did not use computers in support of their collaborative learning activities. On the other hand, no significant differences in midterm exam grades existed between the two groups of subjects. The implication of this finding is that the impact of the computer-supported group learning process on student achievement may be cumulative and realized over time. Longitudinal studies will be useful in investigating this phenomenon.

Previous research in education, cited earlier in the paper, suggests that collaborative learning is an effective, viable, and perhaps preferred alternative to individual and lecture-oriented teaching methods in higher education (Cooper et al., 1990; Kulik and Kulik, 1979; McKeackie, et al., 1987). This current study indicates that the effectiveness of collaborative learning in terms of learning outcomes and student affective reactions may be further enhanced through the use of GDSS capabilities. With the declining cost of computer hardware and software and the subsequent increase in prevalence of computer-equipped classrooms, computer-mediated collaborative learning provides an opportunity for enhancing the educational process through the application of information technology. It is important, however, to point out that computer-mediated collaborative learning represents a departure from the traditional instructional method. Therefore, adequate time and effort by the instructor should be allocated for successful implementation of this new teaching method. It is believed that the positive educational outcomes of computer-mediated collaborative learning make for high returns on investment of instructor time and effort.

## Limitations of the study and suggestions for future research

The field setting of this experiment led to a number of limitations. These limitations are discussed in this section so that they can be addressed and improved upon in future research studies.

One of the limitations of the study was that students from the treatment conditions (that is, students in the electronic classroom and those in the traditional classrooms) may have interacted with one another. Learning and student perceptions might have been influenced by these interactions. Future studies should attempt to reduce the potential impact of student interactions by administering the treatment during different semesters or at different geographic locations (e.g., different campuses). Furthermore, the cell sizes in the two treatment conditions were unequal (two classes met in the electronic classroom, and one class met in a regular classroom). Ideally, two sections of each course in each treatment would be desirable.

Another limitation of the research is that the experimenter was also the instructor for the classes involved in the study. Although the same exact lecture notes and “scripts” were followed by the instructor in all the three classes, the experimenter bias phenomenon cannot be ruled out. Future studies should be conducted in settings in which the instructor is blind to the research question and hypothesis.

Finally, although the experiment was conducted over a course of several weeks (a semester) and despite the fact that the midterm exam grades were not significantly different across treatments, novelty effect of the teaching theater cannot be completely ruled out. This is because sometimes such effects emerge over a relatively larger period of time. Studying students in electronic classrooms over a number of semesters might diminish this effect.
