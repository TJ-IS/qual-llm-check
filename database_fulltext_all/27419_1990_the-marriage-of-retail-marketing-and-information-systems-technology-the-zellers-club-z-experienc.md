# The Marriage of Retail Marketing and Information Systems Technology: The Zellers Club Z Experience

By: Kenneth R. Wightman
J. Pascal Inc.
901 Bleury
Montreal, Quebec
Canada H2Z 1M5

## Abstract

This paper examines the major influences and events that led to the creation and implementation of Club Z, a very successful frequent buyer program, by Zellers Inc., a Canadian mass merchandiser and a division of the Hudson's Bay Company. While the paper describes this major retailer's innovation in the adaptation of computer technology to store-level marketing, it also analyzes some of the significant aspects of the Club Z implementation from an IS management perspective: how a champion emerged to advocate and protect the project at the highest executive level; how an outside systems consulting firm was brought in to manage the Zellers IS function during the project life; and how the cultural problem of anticipated store resistance to the required new technology was overcome.

Keywords: Computer applications, administrative data processing—marketing, management of computing and information systems, project and people management—systems development, training

ACM Categories: H.4.m, J.1, K.4.3, K.6.1

## Introduction

Zellers Stores, with \$2.1 billion in sales in 1989, is a profitable major discount store retailer with over 200 stores across Canada. It is similar in operation to chains in the United States such as Wal-Mart and K mart. Based in Montreal, Quebec, Zellers has approximately 25,000 employees and has the second largest market share in Canada among department store operators. Zellers is a division of the Hudson's Bay Company Retail Group, which also operates The Bay, Simpsons, and Fields stores in Canada. In retail parlance, Zellers is a “mass merchandiser” because it appeals to a national consumer base of primarily middle-to-lower income families, offering a wide range of fashion goods and domestic staples (e.g., health and beauty aids, hardware, consumer appliances and electronics, toys, bedding). Instore staff (cashiers and salespeople) that customers meet are friendly though deliberately few in numbers, which is compensated for by merchandise that is laid out in an easy-to-find manner. The foundation of Zellers' marketing mix, in addition to everyday low pricing, is an intensive, sustained advertising program. Ads are delivered through television and a weekly national sales flyer program distributed in newspapers and by mail. Extremely tight cost controls are critical to success in this industry.

In August 1986 Zellers launched what is probably the most successful mass merchandising promotion in North American retail history. Called Club Z, it is a frequent buyer program, modelled after the airlines' frequent flyer programs. Club Z has won several awards in Canada and the United States. Now more than 50 percent of Canadian households are Club Z members.

Club Z members (membership is free and all shoppers are entitled to join) receive Club Z points at no charge based on the amount of purchases they make in Zellers stores. Each \$1 of purchase is worth 100 points; points can be redeemed by customers for “gifts” ordered from a specially created catalogue. The gifts offered represent merchandise not found in the regular store assortment. The gifts start at 30,000 points for a set of glasses, for example, and rise to 1,500,000 points for an RCA Camcorder.

When a customer (of whom the majority are women) makes a purchase in a Zellers store, she or he receives with a purchase slip the amount of points accumulated to date. The cashier asks for the customer's membership card number prior to entering the sale. Points are accumulated online in a central database at Zellers' main computing facility in Toronto.

One of the attractions from a marketing perspective is that the customer does not need to retain, and later redeem, paper coupons to receive points; all points are registered for members in the central computer, and point status can be looked up in the store at the customer's request. Electronic points, unlike paper coupons, cannot be lost or misplaced. Customer concerns over handling and possibly misplacing paper coupons and thereby losing points are avoided. In addition, store management can offer extra Club Z points to stimulate sales on selected or all merchandise purchased for prescribed periods of time; thus, an extra 10,000 points might be advertised for each \$100 of specified women's outerwear purchases to stimulate slow-moving sales in this merchandise segment. Zellers also has the potential through the Club Z system to track purchases by Club members and glean useful marketing data, which can be used, for example, to create targeted direct mail programs.

The primary strategic objective of Club Z was to create and retain customer loyalty, and it is clear that the program has, in fact, accomplished this with the result of increased retail sales market share.

What makes this program so unique is the way it has combined marketing and information systems (IS) technology to give Zellers a truly compelling competitive advantage. Several retailers, including Sears and S & H Green Stamps in the United States, have subsequently introduced their own computer-tracked frequent buyer programs.

The remainder of this paper examines the background to Club Z's development and its place in Zellers retail strategizing; how the Club Z program was implemented from a systems and marketing perspective; and the lessons learned from the Club Z experience and further systems-based marketing opportunities for retailers.

## The Background to Club Z's Development

Until 1985, Zellers Information Systems (IS) Division had not, for a long period of time, produced any major systems that could fundamentally impact the way Zellers operated. In fact, as senior non-IS executives became increasingly frustrated with lack of delivery from the IS division, morale in the division became very low. The reasons for this non-delivery are complex, but were due in some part to Zellers' senior management. They had not given a strong sense of direction to their IS people, nor had they or the IS group found the means to exploit the value of leading-edge systems. Systems development had progressed up until then in a somewhat unstructured and unplanned fashion. No long-range IS plan had been created and agreed upon by top management. Although Zellers was known as a strong, aggressive retailer, its systems did not constitute part of its competitive strength—systems were perceived to be rather more of a back-room support process. As a totality, Zellers' portfolio of major systems did not amount to competitive parity with many other similar large retail operators.

New leadership was installed in both the Zellers IS Division and at the top corporate level in mid-1985, and a fresh, aggressive approach to the exploitation of systems was progressively introduced. The whole role and mission of the IS Division within the company rapidly changed.

A top-level, non-IS executive (one of the new arrivals) emerged with the idea for Club Z and in effect became the champion for Club Z throughout its implementation life. Zellers systems resource allocation had until then been very “hit or miss” and certainly lacked top management involvement. The idea for Club Z was not conceived as the product of any overall systems planning or business planning process, but rather was initiated with considerable urgency in recognition of its unique potential to give competitive advantage. The Club Z champion had attempted to introduce a similar marketing program at his prior retail company, but the proposal had not received the necessary support.

It is within this exciting and fostering climate that the idea of Club Z was conceived and implemented. It has been observed that truly major systems initiatives in an organization will typically be brought forward by someone only if there is a supportive climate that encourages risk-taking and innovation. The level of career risk inherent in sponsoring a Club Z-type system is clearly high, both for systems and marketing people; the climate must, however, encourage people to search for systems-based strategic breakthroughs. In addition to his superb insights into the power of IS technology in retailing and his own responsibility for the systems area of the business, the Zellers champion was also an influential member of the small inner group who effectively plotted Zellers direction. The credibility of this person was well enough established to gain the endorsement of such a large scale initiative by the senior team: if the principle advocate had been at even one level lower in the organization, it is doubtful the senior group would have been influenced enough to go ahead with so massive a project.

While Club Z itself was not the product of a formal business planning effort, during its initiation, systems planning was entered into. A firm of consultants was engaged to assist management in completing a long-range systems plan that would be fully supportive of Zellers business objectives and strategies. In fact, a “vision” paper, endorsed by the president of Zellers, was prepared for, circulated to, and discussed with all senior management. The paper, entitled “Zellers in the 1990’s,” was used as a basis for communicating the senior management’s strategic views to all management staff, and the paper anticipated that Zellers would increasingly look for an operating and strategic edge through the application of technology, particularly information technology, to the business.

The strategic impetus for Club Z arose from the following considerations:

## Need for differentiation

Large mass merchants offer a very similar product mix, at similar prices, in similar stores. Typical of a mature industry, the bases for differentiation are limited. Club Z promised a fresh and new dimension for differentiation. Excitement would be generated among customers as their point levels grew, based on purchases they were going to make anyway; Club Z gifts would be promoted as a “bonus” to the customer. In addition, there would be a wide range of promotional flexibility available through the Club Z program (for example, double point days for otherwise slow shopping days such as Monday). It was anticipated that because of the difficulty of implementing Club Z from a systems perspective, competitive response to Club Z would not be rapid, and anticipated “breathing space” would allow Zellers to draw the attention of customers who had not regularly shopped at Zellers, if at all, in the past. If Zellers could retain these customers through sustained everyday low pricing and other elements of the marketing mix, subsequent “me-too” competitive response might be too late.

## Buying leverage

As a profitable retailer, Zellers had a well-developed strength in its world-wide vendor network and its highly competent buying team: Zellers could leverage this merchandise sourcing ability to acquire high-quality, cost-effective merchandise from its vendor base, specifically for the Club Z program. This merchandise, as mentioned earlier, was not to come from the regular merchandise assortment so that the “gifts” might appear extra special to customers.

## The accessibility factor

The marketing effectiveness of frequent flyer programs now offered by the airlines gets mixed reviews. The programs seem necessary in order to maintain competitive parity because all major airlines have them. No airline can claim a unique competitive advantage from its program, even the originators. In the case of Club Z, it was believed that a frequent buyer program could engender significant customer loyalty because a broad range of gifts could be offered, and, more importantly, everyone could expect to have access to the gifts. To obtain even short-range “free” airline flights, many airline miles must be flown, and so typically these programs are targeted for business people. Frequent flyer awards are beyond the access of most people. With Club Z, however, gifts could be earned through the points arising from normal, everyday purchases. If customers were willing to concentrate their buying at Zellers for basic household items such as toothpaste and disposable diapers, the accumulated points would be meaningful. A minimum purchase of only \$300 is required to get some kind of gift. Also, the more frequently customers came to Zellers stores for planned purchases, the more likely they would make additional “impulse” purchases.

## The anticipated decline of retail credit

Founded during the Depression, Zellers had, since the 1950s, offered credit to its customers through its own private-label credit card. Necessarily high rates of interest charged by bank and department store retail cards have come under increasing attack in Canada by consumers' associations and the federal government. Retail credit, as well, was thought to be ineffective insofar as credit card operators must charge high interest rates to offset the costs of very non-productive receivables from those who pay off their accounts every month. Or they only use the card for convenience, not as a credit instrument. Some senior management also anticipated that debit cards would eventually supplant credit cards. If retail credit was in a stage of maturity and was no longer the proper vehicle to build customer loyalty, wouldn't it be advisable to replace it, or at least parallel it, with a Club-Z-like marketing device for building customer loyalty?

The creation and implementation of Club Z by a leading retailer represented a major evolution in an industry that has, until recent years, lagged in information technology innovation. But this same industry has tremendous opportunities to exploit available IS technologies as an integral part of its core business strategies.

The information intensity matrix in Figure 1, adapted from Porter and Millar (1985), positions retailing as a high-intensity use of information in two ways. The first is the information required by the retailer to control the physical processes inherent in the retail value chain (i.e., ordering, distribution, and tracking of merchandise); the second is the information required by the customer to purchase and use merchandise (e.g., information on complex, expensive products, like stereo system operating functions). It is not coincidental that the companies often cited for their strategic use of systems (or sometimes called “competing through computing”) are those that have complex distribution problems and/or have large customer bases. The two classic examples are: American Airlines and American Hospital Supply. Zellers Club Z falls exactly within this type of business operation in the way it addressed an opportunity involving marketing to many customers through a complex, multi-outlet physical distribution network.

Although the demands for excellent information management by distribution-intensive businesses are evident, there are also, on the other side of the coin, many strategic opportunities available through creative systems usage. Consider the sheer volume of valuable marketing information to which large retailers and other complex distribution enterprises have primary and often exclusive access.

## The Implementation of Club Z

This section addresses the practical management issues encountered in the Club Z implementation. While Club Z was championed by one individual, its creation and execution were guided by a task force comprising staff from advertising, the store, merchandising, systems, human resources, and finance. The problems encountered can be classified into the “business domain” and the “technology domain” using the terminology of Parker and Benson (1988). This discussion is focused on the systems (or technology) issues, but in brief some of the major business issues were:

\- Introduction of methods to distribute gifts in a timely way to customers through the vendor-distribution center-store network, recognizing that these gifts needed to be physically segregated from regular merchandise for control and replenishment purposes because they did not “fit” into existing replenishment modes.

\- Selection and sourcing of appealing and even unusual gifts for the catalogue that would be cost-effective and would offer excitement to customers.

\- Design of a unified media campaign in terms of media types, timing, amount, duration, and themes.

\- Familiarization of 200 stores with new operating procedures to support Club Z.

\- Financially, how to account for outstanding Club Z points as a liability in view of forecasted point redemption levels and future gift costs.

By far, the major hurdles and challenges encountered in the Club Z implementation were in the IS technology domain. Once the fundamental marketing plan had been agreed on, the ability to actually launch Club Z depended on the availability of an interactive, online system between the stores across Canada from St. John's Newfoundland to Victoria, British Columbia, and the Head Office in Montreal. The system needed to have the following functional capabilities:

\- To advise customers in the stores instantly of their accumulated point status.

\- To collect points by member and to print out status reports at the Head Office regarding point and membership levels by store.

\- To enter customer gift requests at the store level, which would be sent to the Head Office and distribution centers for replenishment.

\- To track activity by gift (of which there were about 100) so that management would know fast and slow-moving gifts, and gift preferences by area of the country.

In addition to creating the software for these capabilities in a limited period of time, new systems hardware needed to be installed to support Club Z functions and other critical functions required in the stores. Zellers had been reliant on its in-store POS (point-of-sale) hardware as the only data input and communications device available. POSs were, for the most part, available only after business hours for offline data input and were not designed for general data entry functions. The majority of in-store back office controllers also had limited storage capacity and were at the end of their service life. Zellers clearly did not have the technological “platform” on which to implement the Club Z functions or, just as significantly, other major applications that were in the planning, but unrelated to Club Z.

The important point is that Club Z provided the main justification to go ahead with the investment in much of the IS hardware infrastructure on which Zellers could build other badly needed systems. In highly summarized form, the technical problems were addressed as follows:

\- Mainframe capacity was increased to support the greatly augmented data processing and storage needs arising from Club Z.

\- The national communications network was upgraded to facilitate greater data reliability and data transmission speed.

\- An IBM PC was installed in each store which served as: a communications controller between stores and the Head Office; a data input and communications terminal; and an in-store controller to permit extra POS functions and greater in-store data storage.

In order to extend the service life of its point-of-sale investment comprising over 3,000 POS devices, Zellers employed a New Jersey-based firm that specializes in “bridging” older POS investment to newer communications and processing technologies. The firm accomplishes this by using an appropriately programmed IBM PC-AT that acts as a communications controller and a POS controller.

Given the scope of the Club Z project, it is not surprising that its execution became the dominant activity for over a year, not only in the IS Division but also in the other Head Office support areas. To accomplish this daunting IS task, both Zellers general management and IS management agreed that extra IS resources were needed.

The Montreal-based systems consulting firm of DMR was hired to run a major portion of Zeller's IS activities. With extensive experience in large-scale systems work, including the systems development work for the Los Angeles Summer Olympics, DMR brought in highly qualified managers to work with Zellers' former systems people, thereby creating a large, unified task force. The intention was to have DMR continue developing Zellers systems on a permanent basis after the Club Z implementation. In fact, in February 1987, after the completion of Club Z systems, all Zellers' programmers and analysts (62 staff) at manager levels and below were transferred to DMR employment, although they remained physically resident at Zeller's offices. With the 45 DMR consultants already at Zellers, the combined office staff numbered 107 after the transfer. For the most part, Zellers' IS employees welcomed the chance to join a major systems consulting firm such as DMR given the broader career opportunities it would offer them. Although space does not permit a full discussion of the merits of this approach in this paper, the “marriage” worked well enough to successfully complete all Club Z systems requirements on time. It should be noted that Zellers still maintained a smaller “residual” IS Division, comprising store point-of-sales systems, office and end-user systems, and business systems planning. DMR was responsible for all project management, programming, and systems analysis functions.

The new technology brought about a “cultural” problem that needed to be overcome. Some stores had latent concerns—how would the store staff become conversant with the new technology and procedures that were being introduced into the stores? At about the same time Club Z was introduced, an electronic mail service to the stores was introduced in order to reduce communications costs. Store staff now had to operate terminals for sending and receiving messages, looking up customer point status and making online adjustments. In the future they would be working with online markdown bulletins and other more sophisticated applications. A training team was assembled to properly train all store staff, and a Head-Office help desk was provided. In addition, a communications video was distributed to all stores to explain the benefits of the new technology. The training programs were effective. Consequently, the morale at Zellers stores and the Head Office was very high at the time Club Z was launched in August 1986.

To re-emphasize, during the crucial period of deciding whether to actually implement Club Z, it was evident to senior management that the success of Club Z would depend on the ability of the IS team to deliver the promised software and hardare. From a marketing perspective, if the system worked as designed, the probability was high that Club Z should be an overwhelming and unprecedented success. The decision to go ahead with the Club Z roll-out was a “bet-your-company” move: if the Club Z systems failed at any one of the many linkage points at the time of introduction in August 1986, the damage to Zellers’ hard-won goodwill among its customers would be incalculable. The sense of urgency the implementation of Club Z engendered in the company was intense, as was the ability of Club Z to serve as a “rallying” point for the entire IS organization, whose morale increased appreciably over the course of the project. From the

IS management viewpoint, because of the authentic success of Club Z, it was encouraging to see such a high level of commitment in the IS group. The dedication and resolve of not only the IS group but the many people who worked hard to accomplish the Club Z goal was admirable.

## Lessons Learned and Future Directions

In the final outcome, the Club Z launch was a monumental success. Three million Zellers customers were signed up as members in a short period of time. Zellers market share climbed throughout the fall of 1986. Since the introduction of Club Z, several retailers have started similar programs such as Sears STAR (Sears Treasure and Reward) and Gold Circle's Bonus Card. One of the more prominent recent introductions has been since S & H Greenstamps "Giftsaver Card" (Newsweek, 1989). Similarly, a giant Quebec-based grocery chain, Provigo, has introduced "Le Club Orange," which is a variant of Club Z. Le Club Orange (Les Affaires, 1989) offers gift certificates and other membership benefits in exchange for which customers (1.5 million people pass through Provigo stores every week) fill out a 17-question form. Using data from these questionnaires, Provigo hopes to design direct mail programs itself, or in collaboration with others. In effect, Provigo wants to create a large customer database.

One possible deficiency of Zeller's Club Z is that it did not, in the initial customer registration phase, ask customers for any information beyond the usual name and address. Zellers also does not yet track member buying behavior in terms of what they purchase, but this is an opportunity that can be pursued.

In retrospect, while Club Z was a smashing success, not enough resources were allocated to create systems that track and control gift inventory and replenishment, as well as point redemption patterns. These and other shortcomings have subsequently been addressed; they do not, however, detract in any material way from the overall effectiveness of Club Z as a marketing tool.

What are the future directions that similar systems-based marketing programs may take for retailers? Retailers are in a unique position to exploit the sheer availability of consumer buying information—they can capture much additional marketing information at the point of sale or elsewhere in the store, and by recording the basic sales transaction. Why not take the basic sales information, collect even more, and use it to retailers' marketing advantage? For example, what if the cashier recorded how many people shop with children? How long do people have to wait to be served? Conceivably, retailers may in the future be able to track the paths customers take through the store, which would assist in store design and merchandise layout. The possibilities are endless. At least three forces have inhibited this direction in the past:

\- The time taken to enter data at the point of sale by cashiers;

\- The cost of processing this massive amount of information; and

\- The lack of techniques to analyze and act on the information once captured.

Systems and marketing breakthroughs are crashing these barriers. With UPC (Universal Product Code) bar-code scanning, mass merchants can now economically process customers more quickly through the checkouts and record more information at the same time. Costs of data communications, storage, and processing continue to fall, making mass data processing a more realistic option for the retailer. And finally, retailers have awakened to the realization of the raw “information power” they have access to in the consumer products and services distribution channel—they control and monopolize direct information about customers’ purchase patterns, which consumer products manufacturers themselves don’t have primary access to. A recent article in The Economist (1985) describes this awesome power in the following way:

Imagine products on the supermarket shelf selecting the customer, rather than the other way around. A revolution in the business of collecting and interpreting information about what people buy is bringing that day closer. Companies that master this new intelligence will aim their marketing and distribution so precisely that they will pick all the customers they want.

Retailers and market-research companies stand to gain most; makers of consumer products, national television stations, and even advertising agencies may lost out. That, at least, is the theory. But there is a risk: the sheer volume of information may muddle and confuse, spoiling the aim of the would-be market snipers.

The organization and the technology to begin this marketing revolution are already available (p. 63-65).

The article points out that only 16 percent of trade promotions (coupons, discounts, contests, etc.) are profitable, although almost 2/3 of U.S. marketing expenditures are devoted to that area. Systems can help close that productivity gap.

In this vein, Zellers Club Z constitutes a revolutionary and winning example of the promise of large-scale systems wedded to mass marketing. Opportunities for more such applications are at the industry's doorstep.

## About the Author

Kenneth Wightman is senior vice president of Finance and chief financial officer of the Pascal Group of Companies based in Montreal, Quebec. He received his bachelor of commerce and master of commerce degrees from McGill University and his MBA from Concordia University. He is a chartered accountant. Prior to joining Pascal, Mr. Wightman worked at Zellers Inc., a national mass merchandiser and division of the Hudson's Bay Company; at Bell Canada; and at Arthur Andersen & Company. Mr. Wightman has led projects in large-scale systems development, including those in the areas of merchandise planning, inventory control, and credit information systems. In addition, he has coordinated strategic planning for the information systems function. He has previously won prizes from SIM and the Planning Forum for writing in the areas of CEO involvement in business planning and creating retail systems for competitive advantage. Mr. Wightman was actively involved in all phases of Zellers Club Z implementation.
