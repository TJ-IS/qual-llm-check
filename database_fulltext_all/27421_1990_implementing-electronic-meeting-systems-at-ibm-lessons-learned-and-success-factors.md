## Abstract

Businesses of the future will rely more than ever on the work of teams. Making better use of the time teams spend in meetings will be a high priority, as will being able to adapt rapidly to change. Electronic meeting systems (EMS), which apply information technology to support the meeting process, can help accomplish these goals. This paper describes and discusses the implementation of EMS at IBM in an alliance with the University of Arizona. During the past three years, the project has grown from initial support for a single site to 33 IBM sites, with more on the way. Over 15,000 people have used the ever-expanding and evolving EMS tool kit. Use of EMS has improved group performance by an average of 55 percent, with even more dramatic reductions in project calendar time. The lessons learned and success factors at IBM can assist managers in effectively introducing EMS to their organizations.

Keywords: Electronic meeting systems, group decision support, implementation, meeting productivity

## Introduction

Business managers spend a tremendous amount of time in meetings—estimates range from 35 to 70 percent. This will only increase as the business environment becomes more complex and turbulent, thereby intensifying demands on a manager's time and capabilities. Making better use of the time spent in meetings is becoming a high priority, along with rapid adaptation to change. Team and work group activities represent a broad spectrum of meeting processes, which include deliberation, negotiation, consensus building, decision making, generation of alternatives, problem solving, and planning. Because any combination of these activities may be necessary during a group's meeting, support must be flexible.

The information systems field currently provides considerable computer-based support for individuals (decision support systems, spreadsheets, word processing) and the organization as a whole (expert systems, distributed databases, local area networks). Electronic meeting systems (EMS) make use of information technology to support the meeting process. Computer software, computer hardware, work group facilities, and meeting procedures all combine to provide support for a group in completing its task. This support may be in the form of application of structured problem solving techniques, access to outside databases, facilitation of group member participation, or simply electronic recording and storing of the meeting's process and outcome. In its broadest interpretation, the EMS concept expands the traditional perception of meetings as gatherings of participants who are physically in the same room at the same time (Dennis, et al., 1988).

The EMS technology used in the implementation described in this paper is a result of several years of research and prototype development at the management information systems department at the University of Arizona (Heminger, 1989; Martz, 1989; Nunamaker, et al., 1989; Vogel, et al., 1990). The system provides a local area networkbased set of flexible software tools and incorporates some basic problem-solving techniques, such as brainstorming, idea organization, alternative generation, and alternative selection. The tools can be executed in any combination in order to match the pattern of work group activities required by the task. Finally, while the system supports traditional meeting activities, it also generates a new and different environment for group meetings. The impact of this new environment on groups in the work place is generating particularly great interest.

In 1987, the University of Arizona and IBM entered into an agreement to evaluate University of Arizona EMS technology at IBM sites to explore the implications of EMS in corporate settings. An initial site was designated; software was installed and evaluated. Based on findings in the first phase of development, eight additional sites became operational for the second phase of analysis. Transfer of a second generation of extended EMS tool support and wider expansion to 33 sites has been completed based on “Phase 2” successes (McGoff, et al., 1990). The implementation of EMS under this joint project are beginning to change the way in which meetings can be conducted.

The purpose of this paper is to describe and discuss the EMS implementation within IBM that was achieved through a close partnership with the University of Arizona. The chronology of events and experiences in the implementation process are outlined. The management structure incorporated by IBM to “mainline” the research into the mainstream of IBM’s implementation is presented, as are results showing improvement in group productivity from IBM’s use of the system. Lessons learned from the implementation experience are reviewed. The paper concludes with a description of critical success factors determined by the implementation experience.

## Chronology of Events and Experiences at IBM

This section outlines the chronology of events and experiences associated with implementation of University of Arizona EMS software at IBM. Throughout the process, issues of facility and software development, as well as procedures, management structure, facilitation, and operational use, were addressed. Site selection and software installation were preceded by visits by corporate personnel to UA facilities. In early 1987 these visits included “hand-on” use of the work group support tools by IBM employees to address a major corporate planning program. Satisfaction with the process and results prompted a desire to install similar capabilities within IBM for operational use on a day-by-day basis. An IBM corporate site was designated and corporate and operating sponsors were identified. The implementation process was initiated in the spring of 1987 with a site visit by Arizona researchers. ## Phase 1 EMS operationalization

The site chosen for EMS Phase 1 installation and evaluation was a multi-line manufacturing plant having approximately 5,000 employees located in the rural setting of Owego, New York. The staff chosen included a project leader who had experience in work groups and working knowledge of the site's diverse applications, a technical support person from the microcomputer support center that serves the entire site, and a facilitator from the site's education department. Most importantly, the management structure accepted the University of Arizona as a research and development partner in the overall project. In April 1987, a team from UA visited the corporate site to meet with designated corporate site sponsors and demonstrate group support software capabilities to other interested site personnel. Strong emphasis was placed on flexibility and close communication between the two groups to encourage quick software change response with minimal “red tape.”

In May 1987, a prototype version of UA group support software was installed temporarily in a training room for site use. This enabled site technical and facilitation personnel to become somewhat familiar with the software prior to formal installation and allowed the UA to customize the software to better meet the needs of the site. It also gave the site team a “head start” in creating operational procedures. Also in May 1987, UA was called upon to provide facility design consultation on a room to be constructed at the IBM site. UA worked to create an environment that could be aesthetically pleasing as well as functional in terms of supporting workstation and audio/visual requirements (Vogel, et al., 1987). The room design replicated the design of the operational facility at the University of Arizona, which was the same facility used during IBM’s visit. The corporate site facility is illustrated in Figure 1.

In the IBM room, a U-shaped table is equipped with 10 networked microcomputers. An additional microcomputer attached to a large screen projection system operates as a facilitation machine on the network to permit display of work done at individual workstations, as well as aggregated information from the total group. An adjacent small room is designated for “back room” functions, such as the printing of session results.

In September 1987, the UA team installed the software on the completed site system and trained the site system's staff in the operation and maintenance of each software module. They performed an initial dry run of the entire planning process. The EMS tools installed were:

\- An electronic brainstorming tool that supports idea generation, allowing group members to simultaneously and anonymously share comments on a specific question.

\- An issue analyzer tool that helps group members identify and consolidate key focus items resulting from idea generation. Support is also provided for integrating external information to support identified focus items.

\- A voting tool that provides a variety of prioritizing methods including Likert scales, rank ordering, and multiple choice. All group members cast private ballots. Accumulated results are displayed.

\- A policy formation tool that supports the group in developing a policy statement or mission through iteration and group agreement.

To prepare for the system's use by corporate groups, the site team spent two weeks gaining additional experience in both using the software and integrated audio/visual support. Sessions were conducted first by UA facilitators, followed by sessions jointly facilitated by IBM and UA personnel. These were followed by sessions facilitated solely by corporate site personnel with UA observation. Subsequent visits by UA team members helped site facilitators gain experience with use of the planning and decision tools. All of these activities were directed toward transfer to IBM of control of the EMS project.

## Completion of phase 1 evaluation

Evaluation of the IBM EMS site was concluded in December 1987. Tool use and facilitation procedures had evolved over three months of operational use. For example, corporate groups often found it desirable to rank a list of issues several times, based on different criteria, whereas a single ranking generally had been used by UA facilitated groups. Basically, however, the existing tools and procedures proved to be appropriate for the various work group applications at the site.

As a final step in the initial operational evaluation, a report to IBM executives was prepared by members of the corporate site team, who summarized experiences and participant data collected during Phase 1 operational use of the UA group support software. This very positive report emphasized man-hour savings, user and facilitator commentaries on the system process, output effectiveness, efficiency, and user satisfaction. Ultimately, a decision was made by IBM to extend EMS availability to additional corporate sites.

## Phase 2 EMS operationalization

Based upon the Phase 1 findings, IBM and UA entered into a second contract for joint EMS research. This contract detailed seven tasks, which included the introduction of new EMS tools, access to research data, the development of a business case, and the potential for joint software development. From IBM's perspective, this contract represented a new level of resource commitment (facility space, hardware, and manpower) to the implementation of EMS at IBM.

IBM used three basic criteria for Phase 2 site selection: (1) a site with more than 5,000 employees; (2) a wide site mission; and (3) a good “quality management” program. In addition, sites were chosen to test the applicability of EMS across different site missions, tasks, and levels of implementation support. For example, sites with primary tasks other than manufacturing, such as information systems design and customer relations, had EMS installed in Phase 2.

Continuing software developments at UA, combined with initial positive reactions to the first set of tools, led to the introduction of extended software functionality. The additional tools, designed to complement the first set introduced, were:

\- An idea organizer that supports the “nominal group technique” (in which group members have an opportunity to develop a list of issues independently), allowing them to share in a “round robin” with other members. This can lead to discussion and prioritization. The idea organizer also complements electronic brainstorming as an aid to organizing generated ideas.

\- An alternative evaluator tool that provides multi-criteria decision-making support. A set of alternatives can be examined under flexibly weighted criteria to evaluate decision scenarios and tradeoffs. Results are displayed in a variety of graphic and tabular formats.

\- A questionnaire tool that provides support for researchers, group leaders, and facilitators in designing an online questionnaire, as well as summarizing participant responses. The questionnaire is dynamic in the sense that additional questions can be triggered, based on selected response values.

\- A topic commentor tool that supports idea solicitation and addition of details related to a list of topics. Participants enter, exchange, and review information on self-selected topics. Each topic may be subdivided as required to solicit more focused comment.

\- A file reader that provides participants with immediate read-only access to previously stored material at any point in a group session — even during the use of another tool. Users may browse stored material and return to interactive meeting participation at their own discretion.

\- A group dictionary that enables group members to formally define a word or phrase in order to focus their own thoughts or provide reference for future group work. The process can be interactive and iterative, if appropriate, to encourage members to participate in arriving at definition consensus without non-productive verbal arguments.

In April 1989, the University of Arizona hosted a three-day session to mark the acceptance of the Phase 2 software tools and prepare for their distribution internally throughout IBM. Representatives were present from each of the IBM sites, as were members of the UA technical and facilitation support team. Aspects of the underlying theory behind each tool, as well as its operational characteristics, were addressed at this session. Changes and enhancements made in response to feedback from IBM use were demonstrated. Hands-on training in use of the new tools was conducted jointly by personnel from the initial site and UA. Technical questions concerning file structures, disaster recovery, report formats, and software “back doors” were fielded by university team members. Lessons learned from laboratory and field experience using both the initial set of tools and the Phase 2 tools were shared with the IBM facilitators. In return, IBM facilitators posed real-world scenarios for consideration by UA in its design of new tools. The IBM facilitators returned to their respective sites to initiate operational use of the Phase 2 tool set within IBM. The end of the training session signaled the transfer of control over distribution of the Phase 2 tools to IBM.

## Extended support and research contract

In August 1989, IBM and UA were joined by Ventana Corporation in completing an extended support and research contract based on Phase 1 and

Phase 2 project successes. Ventana Corporation had recently been established under the auspices of the Arizona Technology Development Center (a wholly owned subsidiary of the University of Arizona Foundation), whose mission is to facilitate the successful commercialization of technologies resulting from University of Arizona research. Ventana Corporation's responsibilities under the contract include maintenance and training support for the group support software in use at IBM sites.

Continued research funding is also being provided by IBM to the University of Arizona over the next three years. Research objectives reflect a continuation of the prior focus and extension to project and organizational levels of analysis. Four specific projects have been designated for attention over the next two years, with additional projects remaining to be defined. The overall University of Arizona/IBM objective is to integrate the studies with prior research to provide a perspective on the implications of using group support systems to link individual, group, project, and organizational levels of analysis. An integrative approach with a team of researchers using a combination of software engineering, laboratory, and field studies is currently in progress.

Additional tools are in the process of being developed and released to IBM. These include:

\- A stakeholder identification and assumption surfacing tool that is used to systematically evaluate the implications of a proposed policy or plan. Stakeholders and their assumptions are identified, scaled, and graphically presented to the group for discussion and analysis.

\- An enterprise analyzer tool that provides support for capturing characteristics of a business subsystem organization including data sets and information systems. The tool provides a foundation for impact analysis as well as definition of general processes and the relationships between those processes.

\- A semantic graphics browser that provides a graphic browsing system for reviewing related stored information (e.g., from the enterprise analyzer). Users can move through a “world space” and “zoom in” on specific areas of interest for more detail in graphic and text formats.

\- A group writer tool that allows members of a group to work together on the same document with varying levels of editing access. Users are not restricted to editing the same screen at the same time. Each user may be working on a separate portion of the document and see changes made by others in real time.

\- A group outliner tool that extends the ability of topic commentor to dynamically address hierarchies of topics. Participants enter, exchange, and review information on self-selected topics. Participants may also suggest new topics to be added to the structure for group discussion.

\- A group matrix tool that enables group members to define the relationships among cells, based on columns and rows of information objects. Users can see group consensus form as relationships are changed as a consequence of group members' appreciation of alternative perspectives.

In summary, 33 EMS facilities have been established internally at IBM; interest exists for broader commercial applications. The EMS software has been used over the past three years by more than 15,000 participants. Group participants have represented all levels of management, including production line workers, line managers, and the plant manager and his executive staff. Tasks addressed by the groups have typically been of a planning and problem-solving nature (e.g., strategic planning process problems, factors contributing to cost overruns, workload elimination and functional area data processing needs. See McGoff, et al. (1990) for examples). Some are cross organizational, involving many functional areas; others represent many management layers within a particular functional area. The majority of tasks are complex to the extent that they require creativity and have no known “right” answer, tasks that generally require input from a group.

## Results from Use

As a part of the implementation process, problem-solving groups from throughout the Owego site were encouraged to use the facility. The first groups participating in sessions brought with them myriad problem domains, ranging from requirements analysis to vendor negotiations to site project planning. Group membership was not restricted and included production floor workers as well as senior executives. The average size of these groups was eight people.

Before using the facility and without knowledge of its automated support capabilities, each group leader was required to recommend and document a feasible project schedule for the accomplishment of that group's objectives, based on previous experience with similar projects. This schedule defined historical parameters for the project and a baseline for comparison with the efficiency of the automated support. The plan provided by the group leader was then translated into an outline for use of the automated support tools. After completion of a session, expectations concerning use of the tools were compared with the initial expectations of the outcome. The output from the sessions also was independently evaluated by a knowledgeable third party to gain a measure of what it would have taken to arrive at that level of accomplishment using traditional manual processes.

Data collected included computer logs, pre- and post-session questionnaires, and facilitator observations plus follow-up interviews with managers and participants. Specific performance measures included the time required for completing the project, number of meetings required, the length of individual meetings, the number of people in the group meetings, as well as cost measures (divided into administrative and man-hour costs). Additional measures were taken of user satisfaction with the process and results. Results were startling, as indicated in Table 2. Man-hours were saved in every case.

The case results provide strong support for the contention that use of electronic meeting systems will improve the performance of work groups. Results show that man-hours fell an average of 55.51 percent on a per-session basis and 61.71 percent on a total man-hour savings basis. Furthermore, administrative costs fell; calendar time was reduced; and the number of meetings necessary to complete a project diminished. Comparison of the estimated and actual case parameters shows significant results at the p = .0001 level using a matched-pairs t-test. Additionally, results project the value of an automated work group hour to be equal to 2.61 historical work group hours.

The data reported in Table 2 align well with data recorded in the pilot test that preceded formal data collection. In the pilot test of 11 groups, an average man-hour savings of 61 percent was reported in conjunction with a 92 percent average reduction in calendar time required to complete a project. Results from data subsequently collected from nine groups at a second IBM corporate site in a metropolitan setting reflected average man-hour savings of 55.6 percent. Interestingly, the second site is primarily administrative, as opposed to the manufacturing orientation of the initial site, adding to the credibility of the initially reported time savings. Additional data collected within IBM, independent of direct UA involvement, further confirmed the cost effectiveness of tool use, with man-hour savings ranging from 40 percent to 75 percent. Cumulatively, these results strongly confirm the robust nature of man-hour savings and demonstrate that time savings are independent of the influence of particular site characteristics on a group level of analysis.

In general, high levels of performance in terms of man-hour savings were strongly correlated with the initial size of the task; larger task size meant greater savings. Additionally, performance was correlated to the degree to which the group's task was stated clearly and concisely. Larger groups (i.e., size eight to 10) tended slightly to outperform smaller groups relative to expectations. Man-hour savings were independent of individual knowledge completeness of group members, ongoing nature of the group, degree to which cooperation was required, and composition relative to number of management levels or different departments represented. However, groups that were more formal, more recently established, or less cohesive tended to achieve higher levels of man-hour savings relative to expectations from similar groups that met without benefit of automated support. These groups also tended to be larger.

Comments by participants praised the ability to access and preserve data over time in a focused atmosphere conducive to information sharing. The benefits of making effective use of member time were noted consistently, as was the significant reduction in elapsed project time and corresponding lack of group member “burnout.” The flexibility of the facility software was commended, as was the quick turnaround of documented output from the EMS. High levels of enthusiasm were commonplace. Overall, it was shown that automated support for groups tends to change the way people work together in terms of average meeting size, group structure, and methods of addressing complex problems.

Historically, meeting size has been constrained by the need to convene in a face-to-face environment with little or no support for multiple conversations. Many smaller meetings have been necessary to address complex problems, with different groups addressing various subproblem facets. Differences between groups typically have been resolved by holding additional meetings with representatives from the various groups.

Automated support is able to remove these traditional meeting constraints because it promotes efficient information exchange and sharing by better matching the communication structure to the characteristics of the individuals who comprise groups. The mechanism of human or group parallel processing allows group size to increase without adversely affecting group efficiency. Because additional members can be simultaneously involved, equivocality among various perspectives is reduced, and problem resolution is expedited. Group effectiveness is thus improved. Furthermore, the need for additional meetings to resolve intergroup differences is decreased, thereby increasing overall project efficiency. The number and frequency of meetings is reduced substantially, resulting in shorter project elapsed time without group member “burnout.” The end result is significant reduction in the total number of people hours expended to address and resolve complex questions. Individual, group, project, and organizational productivity are all enhanced. In fact, there currently is a three-week waiting list for use of the original decision room. Since the beginning of use of the system in October 1987, the room has been fully utilized, according to the records of those responsible for facilitating sessions. Comparable high levels of utilization have been reported by other site facilitators.

## Lessons Learned

The overwhelmingly positive nature of the results of EMS implementation at IBM was striking. Process and outcome effectiveness, efficiency, and user satisfaction were consistently higher when EMS sessions were compared with meetings having no automated support. Furthermore, repeated users of the automated system consistently had higher mean scores on questions of process effectiveness. A cumulative comparison of man-hours expended resulted in a reported 56 percent savings attributable to EMS use. Full facility return on investment (ROI) is estimated at less than one year. EMS use has begun to change the way IBM groups work. Lessons learned include:

## 1. Anonymity is particularly beneficial in the meeting process

Historically, group meetings have been plagued by such characteristics as member status incongruities, fear of reprisals, “groupthink,” etc. The anonymity offered by EMS can be used to attack some of these dysfunctions. For example, the system has been used by IBM managers as follow-up to performance evaluations. IBM has a formal review of its managers each year. Employees make their responses through an online questionnaire and the results are tabulated and provided to the managers. Some managers have brought groups into the site’s decision room to get more detailed feedback on their ratings. In these cases, anonymity has proven extremely beneficial for surfacing the issues underlying the performance ratings of managers. One IBM manager made the statement that the meeting could not have been conducted manually and that anonymity first allowed the meeting to be conducted and, second, to be productive.

## 2. The effective number of participants in the meeting process is increased—the EMS process is changing the structure of work groups

Over the first nine months of data collection, the average size of the groups assigned to use the decision room grew slightly. More importantly, the group composition changed significantly to include more levels of management—higher group structure—and to cover more functional areas—wider group structure.

## 3. EMS meetings are characterized by higher levels of participation

Interview reports from the groups using the EMS show high levels of participation in the meeting process. Interestingly, expanded participation also has carried over into the everyday workplace. For example, one manager reported that group members stay involved by more often approaching the leader for discussions.

## 4. Fewer meetings over less time are required to solve problems

Projects that would normally be addressed through a large number of small meetings spread out over several months are now being handled with a smaller number of larger meetings, often with a total elapsed time of less than a month. Through EMS use, IBM meetings include a larger number of stakeholders who can meet efficiently and resolve the equivocalities that typically exist in conjunction with complex problems. The combination of communication efficiency made possible by use of the technology, coupled with stakeholder synergism, creates a sense of achievement seldom achieved in typical meeting situations.

## 5. Participants stay focused on the task at hand

Reports from facilitators and group leaders show group members are staying focused on their task throughout the meeting. In addition, an internal study shows low levels of non-task interactions (actions not directly associated with the completion of the task) in EMS groups when compared with historical meetings.

## 6. The pre-planning of meetings takes on increased importance—this includes participant and tool selection as well as expectation management

EMS tools can be arranged in a variety of fashions to meet particular group needs. Prior to use of the group support facility, session initiators or group leaders meet with facility personnel to discuss how the tools might best be used to address that particular group's question. IBM is finding that the pre-planning session is particularly important in terms of clarifying the goals and objectives of the group as well as managing expectations of the process and results. The end result is a session agenda that is realistic and has a reasonably high probability of successfully meeting the group's needs.

## 7. Post-meeting distribution of the session data is crucial

The results of the session produce what IBM terms a “newspaper.” The session newspapers are distributed to the group participants and exchanged between groups when appropriate. For example, two IBM groups, one for managers and one for employees, conducted sessions concerning the same general problem. Afterwards, they traded newspapers in order to exchange group viewpoints and perspectives. Newspapers have formed the basis for managerial decisions and have provided documentation for future sessions. They have been used in presentations and technical reviews, have fed project management workshops, and been displayed at bi-monthly meetings to keep the group focused until project completion.

## 8. Low levels of participant computer competence have not deterred effective use

Observers have often raised concerns about the keyboarding skills needed to use the system. Every effort has been made in the EMS design to minimize the number of keystrokes required so that the group members are able to contribute easily. For example, electronic brainstorming requires using only one key, function key 10, in addition to the alphabet keys, to contribute a comment to the session. A voting ballot can be organized and submitted using only two function keys and two arrow keys. The most complex module, idea organizer, requires using only five function keys plus the page up, page down, and arrow keys for a participant to contribute significantly to the group process.

The system is content-oriented. It does not matter whether misspelling, grammatical errors, or syntax mistakes appear throughout the sessions as long as the meaning of the comment is accurately transmitted. No one knows who has mistyped and, if necessary, errors can be cleaned up later, prior to printing the report. Experience has shown that resistance to typing in information is quickly overcome by the desire to address the issues on the screen and contribute to the meeting process. Complementing this experiential data are the results reported on more than 425 self-reported questionnaires. In these data, IBM participants classified themselves as below-average typists (2.79 on a five-point scale, 3 is average), but still reported high levels of “satisfaction with computer process” (4.10 on five-point scale, where 3 is average) and 4.16 on five-point scale where 3 is average on whether the computer process was better than the manual.

## 9. The meeting room environment should match the characteristics of the group

IBM has installed the technology at 33 sites. Each of these decision rooms has its own “mission” to support. Several sites sponsor manufacturing groups whose members deal with problems at the production line level, while other sites support major IBM contracts with business associates, and still others deal with groups composed of IBM employees and potential customers. The environment of each room must match its own mission. Rooms geared to production lines should be located near the production floor and reflect an appropriate tone; customer-oriented rooms should have a marketing influence and, finally, management and contract-oriented rooms should have meeting room or executive boardroom overtones.

## 10. Software systems must be flexible to meet the variety of group applications

EMS software should be designed to handle the variety of team and group work applications found within a single organization. IBM has been able to use the Arizona EMS technology for such applications as workload reduction meetings held by production-line workers, division-level strategic planning meetings held by site managers, department manager reviews, and meetings of groups composed of multi-management-level participants. Tools have been adapted and created as group needs have changed. For example, the task most often reported for a Phase 1 work group session concerned workload reduction in the manufacturing plant. These sessions worked to identify tasks in the production line as possible candidates for elimination or change. The groups used three criteria: greatest impact on productivity if the task were eliminated, greatest impact on morale if the task were eliminated, and the easiest task to eliminate. The facilitator could easily adapt the voting tool to a group need by changing the criteria. For example, design groups are sensitive to scheduling delays and may therefore want to substitute a new criterion, such as evaluating the impact on the meeting schedule if task eliminated, in place of the morale criterion used by the production groups. As time progressed and IBM's facilitators found criteria alteration to be more and more useful, a new tool module was developed and inserted into the system. This module, alternative evaluator, readily facilitates the evaluation of a list of alternatives according to each list of criteria. The results can be tabulated and displayed to the group. The tool also permits changing the weights of criteria, thereby providing a primitive “what-if” analysis.

## 11. A decision center will not operate on its own—an infrastructure of staff and support is crucial to EMS success

Over the brief time period reported, the staffing of the decision center already has changed. Initially there were three positions: manager, facilitator, and technician. IBM's current thinking calls for five positions: manager, two facilitators, one facilitator-in-training, and technician. Additionally, IBM is implementing procedures to increase facilitator communication across sites as well as formal facilitation training and education.

## 12. EMSs help provide an organizational memory concerning related meetings

In several instances, the output of EMS meetings has taken on the characteristics of an organizational memory. In one case, three IBM groups from two different sites where able to hold an “asynchronous” meeting. One group worked in the EMS facility and produced a “newspaper.” The second group used the facility to pick up from the stopping point of the previous group. A third group followed. A final newspaper was produced and presented to all three groups. Here, the electronic software provided a constant memory and held the group’s “positions” in the idea generation process. A very important aspect of this scenario is that the subgroups were able to meet when convenient for them and did not have to postpone meetings until all groups could meet. Other examples of organizational memory center on the idea of using the EMS to provide accurate records of meetings to help justify reports and decisions. Both participants and group leaders have been pleased with the fundamental ability of EMS to capture all information put into the system.

## 13. EMSs provide structure and control mechanisms for the meeting

One of the biggest problems with work groups is imposing an organized meeting structure on participants. For example, almost everyone knows that verbal criticism in meetings can dampen creativity, so most facilitators will impose the guideline of “no criticism” in their verbal brainstorming sessions. However, this rule is not enforceable. Inevitably, someone criticizes someone else and the meeting degenerates. The EMS software helps provide structure and control for a meeting. Continuing the brainstorming comparison, the EMS guarantees anonymity, thereby removing any personal attribution of comments. The content of the comment is open for criticism, not the person making it — as is the intent of the guideline. The other tools operate in the same general manner, allowing the group to evaluate focus items and voting results “in the raw” and without any prejudice associated with group member status.

## 14. Propensity to use the EMS reveals its value

An additional measure of success is what may be termed “propensity to use.” The success of the EMS software can be measured by observing outward signs that the organization has attributed value to it. The items listed below give evidence of the software’s value to IBM.

a. Funding for a second research project was granted to the University of Arizona in order to provide additional EMS tools to the site and to explore in more detail the effects of EMS software at the primary site.

b. Higher levels of management have used the system and publicly championed its use. It has been reported that higher management has on occasion pre-empted the decision room for use by its own groups.

c. The work group facilities are in constant use and the facility coordinators report that a three to four-week backlog of requests for their use are not unusual.

d. The final report from an independent internal study was positive and included the recommendation that the parent company support the decision room activity.

e. The site's parent organization is distributing the technology to other sites and has committed resources to the construction of several new facilities. The software is currently installed at 33 sites, and IBM has plans for more.

Organizational commitment as a success factor recognizes the need for practical demonstrations and use of a system by organization members prior to installation to develop support and obtain sufficient resources for effective project completion. Also included is the need to address a “real” organizational question and otherwise meet organization needs and objectives (e.g., reducing time spent in meetings).

An executive sponsor who is committed and informed is crucial to implementation success. Responsibilities include making the initial implementation request, allocating resources, approving time schedules, monitoring progress, providing high-level feedback, and championing the system to end users. IBM designated its Systems Integration Division headquarters as the executive sponsor.

An operating sponsor is particularly helpful to provide quick feedback and assistance in achieving implementation objectives. The realities of executive workloads and the time required for implementation combined with increasingly sophisticated applications and data management problems discourage sole reliance on an executive sponsor. IBM dedicated a project coordinator to the project.

Dedicated facilities with attention to aesthetics and user comfort are extremely important to successful EMS implementation. Facilities that look like laboratories or training rooms with EMS installed as an afterthought tend to evoke poor response from executive users. Facilities designed with EMS in mind can stimulate a variety of uses extending beyond planning and decision-making support. The IBM project had facilities and hardware resources dedicated solely to the EMS software.

Reciprocal site visits recognize the need for informed personnel who understand the environments in which EMS software can be successfully operated. Effective automated support for groups involves interrelated aspects of the facility, hardware, software, facilitation, and end users. Reciprocal site visits and associated discussions provide a common frame of reference for project participants. Site visits between IBM and UA occurred once a quarter, on the average.

Communication and liaison extending beyond site visits is a key element in maintaining responsiveness to questions arising from sessions and suggestions for software modifications. As a substitute for “phone tag” frustrations, UA provided IBM employees direct access to the development staff through electronic mail and occasional teleconferences.

Fast iteration of software changes is critical to meet the evolving needs and desires of a corporate site during implementation. The “newness” of EMS to corporations combined with an implementation team’s unfamiliarity with corporate operations precludes the possibility of a comprehensive implementation prescription. Prototyping, periodic changes, and evolutionary strategies are a way of life in EMS implementation. IBM and UA intentionally minimized “red tape” to facilitate quick software updates.

Training is necessary for site personnel at technical, facilitation, and end-user levels. Each area has its own interests and needs. Storyboards and “hands-on” use of the technology are particularly helpful training aids to complement traditional documentation and tutorials. IBM and UA participated in joint training of facilitators and technical staff throughout the project.

Transfer of control to site personnel is essential to assure that the system will be used by those for whom it was intended. The process begins with overlapping support from the implementation team followed by implementation team observation and feedback, and culminates in the transfer of complete operational control to site personnel. This transfer includes adequate education and training to make each room self-sufficient. Once UA had conducted initial training at IBM's first site, the transfer of control of the system use was completed. IBM made all operational decisions from which sites should have EMS to when new versions of EMS software should be released.

Cost/benefit evaluation is crucial to expansion of EMS activities beyond initial trials. While resources may initially be allocated on “faith” or limited exposure, evidence of return on investment is helpful if not essential to extended organizational commitment and longer term institutionalized EMS use. IBM conducted an independent study to establish a cost/benefit analysis of EMS.

Software usage flexibility is essential to meeting the evolving needs of groups. Tasks that can be addressed tend to be limited only by the imagination of the users. Corporate cultures and previous manual processes play an important role in deciding how an EMS is to be used in any given situation. Finally, as groups become increasingly experienced, needs and desires tend to change. IBM has used EMS software successfully in a wide variety of work group and team applications.

Facilitation support is an important aspect of getting new users quickly productive in an EMS environment and generally assures that the tools are used in the most appropriate manner to meet a group's needs. Pre-planning sessions and help for members during a session can help ensure the best collective use of a group's time. IBM has escalated the facilitation aspect of its EMS by including more full-time facilitators.

Meeting managerial expectations is the ultimate indicator of successful EMS implementation. EMS technology by nature tends to evoke thoughts of automated decision making. Corporations and organizational users need to appreciate an emphasis on “support.” Appropriately used, EMS provide efficient and effective support. IBM’s project team is making detailed presentations to management concerning expectations for EMS.

## Conclusion

This paper describes and discusses the implementation of EMS at IBM. Implementation of EMS is complicated by a much broader array of issues and activities than are associated with more traditional data processing systems. Issues such as the appropriate management structure, site selection, facility design, facilitator training, and transfer of control all take on added importance. The paper reports the implementation's impacts in all of these areas. In summary, IBM's project management was structured to take advantage of the University of Arizona's development team for EMS research and development while IBM applied the EMS technology to business teams and work groups dealing with real business problems.

IBM has continued to identify new areas for potential application. The implementation proceeded smoothly and system usefulness has been demonstrated. The corporate decision to develop additional sites underscores EMS implementation success. The paper concludes with a presentation of success factors identified from the implementation experience. To facilitate cross-site comparison, data continue to be collected at IBM sites. Each site has a major focus (e.g., customer partnerships, product development cycle) through which to further investigate the domain of EMS applicability as well as provide opportunities for replication and independent confirmation of findings at the initial site. Additional IBM personnel have been trained, and management personnel have been assigned to provide extended service to accommodate the waiting lists for facility use. Each site has its own group of facilitators and support staff. In light of these experiences at IBM, the authors feel strongly that electronic meeting systems will play an important role in shaping organizations and institutions in an increasingly competitive international business climate.
