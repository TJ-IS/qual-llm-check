## Abstract

A growing problem for many organizations is spiraling telecommunications costs coupled with the need to respond faster to changing market conditions. To handle such a situation, TRW's Space and Defense Sector management authorized a new integrated voice and data digital network for 20,000 employees located in 65 buildings. TRW's top management saw the network as an opportunity to integrate the company's islands of information, which were isolated within separate data networks. The project took four and one half years from design to implementation at a cost of \$45 million. The management techniques employed resulted in critical milestones being met not only on time but also 10 percent under the original budget. The new network provided a single access mechanism that resulted in some unanticipated advantages that went beyond the original goals of fast response and cost containment. The network successful ly addressed such problems as developing effective data networking policies, network sizing in a multi-vendor environment, sharing excess information system capacity, centralizing the network while keeping the information systems decentralized, and overcoming the “not controlled here” syndrome. TRW’s experiences should prove valuable for large information-intensive companies attempting to meet similar challenges.

Keywords: Networking, telecommunications, data network, network maturity cycle, fast response networks

## The Challenge

In the early 1980s, top management at TRW's Space and Defense Sector faced a challenge: it needed to reduce spiraling communication costs yet still maintain profitable growth amid high competition. Increasing telecommunications costs were seen as a potential obstacle to making the company more effective. The need for faster response capabilities resulted from turbulent market conditions, characterized by the shortening product cycle of the high technology marketplace (Bower and Hout, 1988).

TRW decided to invest capital assets toward a new network infrastructure designed to limit communications costs and provide flexibility for fast response. To stay competitive, large information-intensive companies like TRW face no other option. The flexibility of an integrated digital network allows companies to adapt quickly to meet the demands of shorter product life cycles.

After two and one half years of planning and two years of installation, the data services and telecommunications departments at TRW completed their integrated voice, data, and image digital network on schedule and 10 percent under budget. Costing \$45 million, the new network for TRW's Space Park complex comprises 65 buildings with over 20,000 employees. It involved the installation of:

\- A 30,000 line digital private branch exchange (PBX)

• 25,000 integrated voice and data digital lines

• Security video services

• 200 communications rooms

\- A campus area backbone network (CABN) containing:

1. A common, campus-wide, intrabuilding Space Park local area network (SPLAN) with the potential for 30,000 drops

2. 38,600 feet of coaxial cable for the SPLAN

3. Over 700 miles of fiber optic cable

4. 10,000 miles of twisted pair wire

The planning, procurement, and implementation processes described in this paper demonstrate how a company can negotiate the best price for a network that contains escalating telecommunications costs and enables fast response.

## The TRW Context

TRW is a multinational corporation comprising three business segments, one of which is the Space and Defense Sector. Each segment operates relatively independently within TRW, except in financial reporting and overall corporate strategy.

The Space and Defense Sector is driven by its product groups, each of which contain separate operating divisions. Project teams within these operating divisions provide revenue and, as major profit-makers, enjoy a great deal of autonomy. Project teams are usually housed in a common location and vary from five to over 400 members. Heavy scientific, engineering, and business requirements make swift, easy access to information critical to the success of the Space and Defense Sector's high-tech operations.

## 60 percent move factor

Space and Defense saw an average of 60 percent of its work force move annually, costing, on approximate aggregate, \$5 to \$10 million in hard dollars. Every voice and data line that had to be moved, added, or changed cost an average of \$500. The loss in soft dollar productivity was far more difficult to quantify objectively, but it probably represented an equal or greater value.

Changes to the existing network system had to be made manually. This caused significant disturbances in office environments, especially during complex line changes. Consequently, the system for reconfiguring the network took too long and was inefficient.

Often when a team was assembled for a new project, it would not use the abandoned network cabling that had supported the previous computing system. Eventually all the available network conduit space became clogged with cables for which there were no records. Even more time was wasted because the local telephone companies (GTE and Pacific Bell) moved, added, or changed lines on a time schedule that suited their own priorities, not TRW's.

## Explosive growth

Space and Defense Sector revenue grew from \$1 billion to \$3 billion between 1979 and 1988. This explosive growth resulted in increased demand for more information services. Within this period, AT&T divested and the telecommunications industry became more deregulated. At the same time desk top information technology grew exponentially.

## Providing network services: trying to hit a moving target

The variety of isolated, heterogeneous data processing systems in use—IBM, DEC, Prime, Sun, Apollo, and an assortment of other computing systems and data networks—made the task of providing network services even more complex. The standard reliance on the telephone company for most network services was deemed infeasible because of uncertainty resulting from AT&T's divestiture and the escalating costs of local telephone service.

During the turbulent period between 1982 and 1989, Data Services and Telecommunications management faced a moving target—a company with constantly changing network needs and significant management changes. This required a network with the flexibility to meet the challenges of an evolving organizational configuration.

## Needed: a cost-effective network to enable fast response

An integrated digital network offered the opportunity for faster response because it would reduce the costs of assembling project teams. The new network could be reconfigured quickly and easily to suit team members' new locations. Instead of moving to a common location, members could remain where they were and be connected through the new network. The end result would be easier network reconfiguration and, subsequently, faster response at a lower cost.

Numerous technical and organizational obstacles had to be overcome to ensure the network project's success. In the long run, the technical obstacles, while formidable, were less problematic than the organizational culture obstacles.

## “Not controlled here”

The corporate culture of the Space and Defense Sector encourages entrepreneurship and permits autonomy of action to go along with this philosophy. The potential benefits include fast, innovative responses to market demands (Guterl, 1989).

One characteristic of the emphasis on autonomy is a desire for control over information resources, which each project unit felt was possible only when it owned and managed them. This phenomenon can be characterized as the “not controlled here” (NCH) syndrome. NCH syndrome among project units resulted in an ad hoc approach to acquiring network resources. This led to a proliferation of underutilized, abandoned resources contained in isolated data networks.

## Sharing excess capacity and linking islands of information

An integrated network would permit newly formed teams to access unused computing resources of existing project units (see Figure 1). This would be possible because the network's new high-speed digital lines would offer distribution capabilities for any existing computing systems at the Space Park complex.

Connecting previously isolated project units, the digital links could make sharing of database information easier even if computing systems were incompatible because the network would provide gateways to ensure system interoperability.

Data Services and Telecommunications management sought to meet the disparate networking needs of a large, multifaceted organization and change corporate culture so that NCH syndrome would not prevent the best use of computing capacity. Management also had to orient the organization toward viewing information as a common resource available to all units that stood to benefit from access to it.

While the telecommunications department had centrally managed the voice network through the local telephone company's Centrex offerings, the multiple data networks had not been centrally managed. Centralized network management of voice, data, and image transmissions required a digital network capable of integrating all types of communications.

The integrated network would allow project teams access to computing resources without controlling how they utilized those resources. A centralized digital network facilitated the simultaneous evolution of centralized common business applications and decentralized project-specific applications.

Proactive network planning also provided a new basis for cooperation between the data services and telecommunications departments, which had to work together more closely and eventually merged to form a new data services organization. The network upgrade stimulated this merger and occurred over the four-year planning, procurement, and implementation period (see Figure 2). This merger also created a new primary role for the data services organization's director, that of a network manager.

## Goals of the new network

Data services management selected an integrated digital network to create a new network infrastructure. The primary goals of the network were to:

\- Reduce the cost of doing business in an information-intensive industry

\- Promote movement of information instead of people

\- Improve sharing of excess computing capacity

\- Create the flexibility to reconfigure a network to meet any organizational structure

\- Create an envelope containing all communications with a single point of demarcation for incoming and outgoing communications

\- Allow autonomous business units, such as project teams, to focus on applications development instead of network procurement and management

\- Provide TRW's employees with the best available information technology that would have the features and functions needed to help them accomplish their tasks

## Network maturity cycle

A company's network function matures through four stages (Housel and Davidson, 1988). During the first stage, the adhoc stage, networks proliferate without the benefit of centralized planning. During the second stage, the isolation stage, networks are planned and controlled by individual project, business, or functional units. In the third stage, the integration stage, management moves to integrate the multiple networks to take advantage of economies of scope and scale and the potential for synergistic collaboration among units and functions. The fourth stage of development, the strategic stage, occurs when voice, data, and image networks become a centrally integrated aspect of the company's strategic plan for beating the competition (see Figure 3).

A company's network maturity places it in this rough continuum. TRW, for example, was in the isolation stage before implementing its plan to install an integrated network across the Space Park complex.

The network maturity cycle provides a context for viewing how the sector evolved from multiple, unconnected, ad hoc data networks to isolated networks controlled by the product groups and their project units. The organizational evolution of data services and telecommunications paralleled stages two and three of the network cycle in that these units represented isolated voice and data networks before the upgrade to an integrated network. The model also predicted the next step in network maturity, a move to a strategic network.

## Bringing the Network Vision to Life

Based on broad cost and benefit forecasts, top management directed the data services and telecommunications departments to develop a detailed analysis of the sector's current network needs. This needs analysis would form the basis for three important activities: requests for information (RFI), an internal promotional program supporting the network plan, and eventually, requests for proposal (RFP) (see Figure 4).

An overall project management team was created to help coordinate planning, procurement, and implementation of the new network. The team consisted of internal technical, engineering, and project personnel from the engineering, data services, and telecommunications organizations.

The procurement processes were the same as those developed for revenue-producing programs, such as defense satellite projects. This program management approach took the integrated digital network—a large scale, complex procurement—and broke it into “bite-sized” sub-projects. Each subproject team was responsible for a budget and subject to the scrutiny of regularly scheduled (i.e., weekly, monthly), independent progress and cost reviews. Any changes in baseline progress and cost requirements had to be justified to the project team during the reviews.

Baselines were never changed because the project management team wanted to keep track of how closely they were staying on schedule and within cost parameters. Adhering to baselines forced subproject management to fix problems that might prevent them from meeting baseline requirements.

Vendors had to offer project management systems that paralleled TRW's. This allowed the project team to integrate the vendors within TRW's overall network cost scheduling control system. Vendors were responsible for regularly scheduled cost and progress reviews with TRW's subproject management teams. This approach gave TRW, not the vendors, control over the project.

## Establishing Evaluative Criteria

Two overriding criteria were that the network had to control escalating telecommunications costs and provide the utility and flexibility for rapid reconfiguration to enable fast response. These general criteria were further defined by specific network criteria, including flexibility, performance, relative cost (e.g., current versus future costs for services), reliability, interoperability, functionality, modularity, maintainability, ease of operation, environmental requirements, and footprints (e.g., housing required for equipment). This list of criteria was used to evaluate and compare current systems against proposed systems.

## User Characteristics

The new network had to serve distinct populations, i.e., administrators, engineers, scientists, management, and support personnel. In 1984, 53 percent of the user population were engineers and scientists, 20 percent were management and administrative workers, and 27 percent were support personnel. This population used 85 percent asynchronous and 15 percent synchronous data communications. Information of this kind was critical in estimating future usage patterns.

## Identifying Existing Networks

Current network systems had to be catalogued in order to understand what it would take to replace and augment them. The existing systems included Centrex for most voice services, several small PBXs, a demanding SNA network, multiple LANs, and point-to-point dedicated data circuits. The new network had to integrate the existing SNA and LAN networks, provide gateways between systems, and replace the voice and dedicated point-to-point data links supplied by the local telephone companies.

## Estimating User Devices

During the analysis, the team collected detailed information on the number and type of data and voice devices. The voice equipment numbers were easily assembled because most of this equipment was purchased from the local telephone companies and was readily available through billing information. Determining the number of data devices was much more difficult because, due to independent procurements, there were no central records.

## Understanding Current Operations

It was necessary to understand the operational activities required to meet current service needs of the diverse user population. Because the new network would be managed internally, many of the services, which were bundled into the vendors' standard charges, needed to be understood.

## Current Network Costs

Current costs for providing network services were difficult to obtain because the information was in many different places, e.g., project units, functional units, and telephone companies. However, the following cost data were collected:

• Capital depreciation of existing plant

\- Human resources costs to run the existing systems

\- Access charges for long distance communications

\- Point-to-point circuit costs

\- Line and station equipment rental and purchase costs

\- Transmission equipment costs (e.g., multiplexers, modems)

• LAN equipment costs

\- Computational support (e.g., mainframe time, PCs, applications software)

\- Aggregate numbers of moves, adds, and changes

While this list is not exhaustive, it represents the primary cost components that had to be accounted for in order to estimate aggregate network costs.

## Managing the RFP Processes

The project team developed a series of requests for information (RFI), inviting vendors into the bidding process for the various parts of the new network contract. There were multiple RFIs because the team requested information on the PBX, CABN, and SPLAN. Responses to the RFIs formed the basis for the requests for proposal (RFPs) to follow. The RFI responses were helpful to the team in developing basic cost and boundary parameters for the network elements.

The RFPs were developed paragraph by paragraph, line by line, to include painstaking details of the network architecture, technical standards, environmental requirements, vendor relationships, and quality assurance criteria for each element of the network.

## Network architecture

The architecture specifications required a general description of the network design. For example, the team requested a more distributed architecture to meet system security and availability needs. The responses to the RFPs had to show how the proposed architecture would meet growth or contraction contingencies. The team did not want a network that would “top out” before the 10-year pay-back period. The long-term objective for the network was to provide transmission capacity for all of TRW's growth needs for the next 20 years.

The vendors communicated their architectural approach at the subsystem level so that a management framework could be developed for the more detailed evaluation of the network operations, elements, and standards. The team requested that the vendors unbundle their hardware and software offerings as much as possible to allow a more detailed function versus cost analysis.

## Technical standards

The new network had to meet a series of current technical standards and be flexible enough to meet future standards. The vendors had to show how their proposed systems would subscribe to the specific standards for data and voice communications and evolve toward future standards.

In addition to responding to the hardware requirements, vendors had to demonstrate how they would comply with city, county, state, and federal government codes and regulations governing rights of way, system safety requirements, etc.

The software specifications were as detailed as those for the hardware. The features and functions of the software that the vendors must meet were specified. Vendors were also requested to demonstrate how they would help TRW accomplish the detailed surveys necessary in completing control tables for the PBX. The vendors' approaches to providing software safeguards, such as personal identification numbers and passwords, also had to be listed.

Warranties for software and hardware were requested, as well as system documentation (owners manuals) for each element. Along with the warranty, the level and duration of maintenance and operational support for the network elements had to be included in the contract price as specified against a basic requirement.

## Environmental requirements

A significant cost driver, which can prove expensive if overlooked, is the environmental requirements for a system's installation and operation. Environmental features include:

\- Space to house switching units and equipment and wiring in communications rooms

\- Special air conditioning required for system operation

• Special equipment power requirements

\- Safety features, such as bracing (for earthquakes), backup generators, etc.

## Vendor relationships

The vendors had to demonstrate how this interface would be accomplished to perform the network installation tasks. For example, they had to identify what they were responsible for, what the subcontractors were responsible for, and what TRW was responsible for in the network installation.

Vendors had to provide project plans showing schedules for completion of milestones and what kind of plant changes were necessary to install the network on schedule. These elements of planning were essential to accomplish facilities changes and upgrades without interfering in the project's overall critical path plan. They also had to specify the availability of resources that would be allocated to accomplish these plans so that evaluations could be made as to how realistic the plans were.

## Quality assurance criteria

The RFPs required the vendors to test and verify each hardware and software element of the network as it was installed to meet quality assurance criteria. The staged testing and verification provided benchmarks that helped keep the project on schedule. Quality inspection of incoming network hardware and transmission media (e.g., station equipment, wiring, fiber optic cables, conduits) was required. TRW also wanted to ensure that the vendors were providing identical versions/revisions of a given product and that the current version would not become obsolete before it was installed.

## Evaluating the RFPs

The RFP evaluation team, comprising the overall project management team, accounting personnel, procurement personnel, and price-cost analysts, provided a cumulative level of expertise that would not have been possible if a single department, such as the Data Services Department, had completed the evaluation alone.

When the technical evaluations were made, a final evaluation was performed that aggregated the results of the technical evaluations and examined total cost and risk. The risk evaluation centered on whether the vendors:

\- Had developed an adequate management plan

\- Could provide the resources to perform the installation on schedule

\- Were delivering the type of products proposed

\- Could deliver services or products not in their standard offerings

\- Had the product inventory and/or ability to produce the required quantities and types of products

\- Were able to identify subcontractor/supplier support mechanisms

The total cost of each acceptable bid was detailed and extended to reach an overall and final price for the project. This final aggregate price had to be compared with the promised hard dollar cost savings to determine if the network upgrade was worth the price.

Once the final bids had been received and evaluated, the overall project management team and TRW Procurement and Contracts management had to produce a negotiations plan representing the strategy for getting the lowest “reasonable” price.

## Implementation

The detailed RFPs and requirements analysis provided the input to the implementation plan. The implementation plan included:

\- Plan scope

• Technical design criteria

• The implementation approach (i.e., phased)

• A list of user requirements

\- Pre-installation preparations (e.g., user surveys)

\- Organizational structure of implementation team

\- Automation systems requirements (e.g., project planning tools software)

• Conditional and final acceptance test plan

\- Facilities plan (e.g., switch and communications rooms)

\- Operations plan (e.g., how to transition from implementation to an operations environment)

\- User support plan (e.g., cutover support operations training)

• Security plan (e.g., PINs, passwords)

\- Chargeback and accounting plans (e.g., PBX line usage, billing)

\- Training plan (e.g., user training for new telephones)

\- Network configuration plan (e.g., numbering plan addressing schemes, cable labeling, and line assignments for on-site telephones)

Because the implementation plan was so detailed, the subproject teams were able to keep to a very tight schedule even when problems arose.

Two levels of automation were used to support project implementation. The first level was software designed to provide the detailed day-to-day scheduling and tracking of tasks. The second level provided consolidation schedules to allow a high-level view of scheduling and tracking based on the critical path method. This automation led to flexible project management because it allowed:

\- Examination of ripple effects from any schedule changes

\- Logistical experimentation using what-if simulations

\- Identification of conflicts in scheduling resources

\- Examination of budgetary constraints and conflicting deadlines

## 10 percent savings

One reason most network implementation projects run over budget is that users request many modifications to the system. To limit the impact of this common problem, the team created the Configuration Control Board to limit modifications to the original contracts. The board, made up of users and members of the implementation sub-project management teams, reviewed all requested changes and kept them to a minimum.

The Configuration Control Board reviewed each change against the contract and determined if it was outside the contract scope. When changes were justified, the board performed a technical and price justification for each change, which was then negotiated by procurement and contract support personnel.

Taking into account the likelihood of modifications, the contracts were negotiated in such a way that the implementation teams had windows of time within which network modifications could be made without additional costs to TRW. It was fortunate that these time windows for modifications had been negotiated because some unanticipated resource changes did occur during the two-year period.

Rigorously managing the change control process resulted in a 10 percent savings on the original projected budget for the network upgrade. Since the project budget was based on precise cost estimates and was comparable to similar-sized projects in peer companies, coming in 10 percent under budget represented a true cost savings.

## The need for data communications policies

Even with extensive planning, developing sound data communications policies is necessary because employees do not always use data applications as planned. An unanticipated problem arose during the network installation: the use of PBX lines for prolonged high-speed data transmissions. To take advantage of network flexibility, a number of low-speed PBX lines were allocated for use by the casual dial-up data user.

A more limited number (i.e., 12 per building) of high speed lines were available for short-term data communications. Users could connect these lines temporarily while being connected to the SPLAN or backbone network services, which could easily handle long session, high-speed data applications.

Users of the new, uncomplicated PBX lines did not want to become connected to the SPLAN. Consequently, they were all identified as casual users. The long session times associated with most data applications can prove hazardous to PBXs that are set up for short session times (i.e., the 2-3 minute average time of a telephone call). Data applications vary widely in terms of transmission speed or bandwidth, network topologies, and session times. This inherent variability makes it very difficult to predict data application usage patterns, which in turn, makes it difficult to size a network for data applications. This problem is common in many information intensive companies.

A clearly defined network policy regarding use time of the special PBX lines was necessary to bring the problem under control. Users were informed of the problem through a series of widely distributed educational bulletins.

TRW learned a number of lessons throughout the planning, procurement, and implementation process. Faced with the need to provide a network infrastructure enabling fast response and containing costs, other companies can learn much from TRW's experience.

## Reflections on Creating a Network Infrastructure

The core characteristics of TRW's network planning, procurement, and implementation processes that kept it on schedule and under budget were:

\- Proactive strategic planning that emphasized the long term in maintaining standards that provided flexibility for the inclusion of new information technology without the need for system replacement

\- Detailed requirements analyses that led to baselines against which current network services could be compared to new network services to ensure that TRW was on the leading edge—not the bleeding edge—in meeting corporate business objectives

\- Cost-benefit analyses that were constantly updated and communicated to upper management throughout the iterative network planning, procurement, and implementation processes to ensure upper management's consent and support

\- The generation of detailed network specifications that defined requirements, described systems, and defined acceptance criteria in sufficient detail that each requirement could be uniquely defined in a single paragraph in the RFP

\- RFP processes that included the detailed network specifications, enumerated terms and conditions permitting TRW implementation modification flexibility, withheld payments conditional on vendor performance, identified of vendor performance relationships and material warranties, and prepared an evaluation plan before the RFPs were released

\- Rigorous RFP evaluation processes performed by the project team and other independent reviewers to ensure objectivity and prevent “group think,” combined with a detailed “what-if” scenario generation method to develop a negotiation plan

## - Implementation processes based on:

1. Exacting project scheduling and performance monitoring

2. Timely project reviews

3. Independent project management reviews with upper management

4. A Configuration Control Board made up of not only project personnel but also representatives from procurement and contracts, functional management, support services, and security

5. Cost/quality/performance tracking against original baselines, with changes allowed only in the event of a major contract change

6. Incremental implementation payments to provide incentives for vendor performance

These processes should help other companies stay on schedule and within budget, provided similar program management techniques are understood and applied.

## Program management techniques

This paper should convince information systems executives in other large companies that the key to success in planning, procuring, and implementing a network lies in the use of rigorous program management processes. Applying the following generic rules will help ensure success:

1. Assign responsibility to the subproject level

2. Religiously maintain baseline requirements

3. Integrate vendor sub-teams within the overall project plan

4. Control contract and network modifications

5. Develop a negotiations plan

6. Draw on the firm's diverse areas of technical expertise

## Take a leadership role

It is very difficult to change a company's overall view of computing and network resources. The best way is to build a concrete case for the new approach, one that specifies how it will reduce operating costs, lead to increased productivity, and not threaten users' control over their unique computing applications needs.

Information systems executives need to take the lead in helping change their company cultures. By helping to formulate a general business strategy that demonstrates how a network infrastructure can help meet strategic objectives, including fast response and reduced overhead, they will help bring their companies' networks into the strategic phase of network maturity and will reap the benefits of this positive change.

## Anticipate unanticipated advantages

While demonstrated cost savings may be a prerequisite for top management approval of a new information network, it may not represent the greatest advantages of such a system. Newly networked, the Space and Defense Sector was able to meet customer contract requirements that could not have been met with the old system. This provided the ability to meet new contract provisions without investment of significant capital.

For the Space and Defense Sector, the days of business as usual on outmoded networks are over. Any company wishing to stay competitive in the information age must ensure its network infrastructure is up to the task.
