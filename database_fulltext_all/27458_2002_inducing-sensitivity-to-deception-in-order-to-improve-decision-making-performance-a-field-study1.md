## Abstract

When an organization's members depend on the data contained in computer-based systems, they become vulnerable to strategic information manipulation. That is, they become susceptible to situations where their decision-making behaviors can be influenced by others able to access and manipulate this data.

This paper describes the results of a field experiment that examines the effects of alternative interventions aimed at inducing sensitivity to the possibility of manipulated data on professionals' task-related decision behaviors: deception detection, false alarms, and task accuracy. While traditional training had no effect on detection success or the issuance of false alarms, warnings about data quality resulted in better detection success. Warnings combined with just-in-time training resulted in better detection success but at the cost of an increased number of false alarms. Higher levels of detection success increased task accuracy and the time spent solving each problem. A higher number of false alarms was associated with lower levels of task accuracy.

Keywords: Information quality, data security, data integrity, error detection, deception detection

## The Effects of Sensitivity to Deception and Domain Experience on Deception Detection and Task Accuracy

With sustained exposure to and use of information technologies, computer users develop trust in the accuracy of the data stored within a computer system (Parasuraman 1987; Wiener 1985). Yet anywhere from 1% to 10% of data items in critical organizational databases are estimated to be inaccurate (Klein et al. 1997a). In most cases, such inaccuracies are due to errors that occur during data input or data transcription. When an organization's databases are relatively open and visible to the public, such as those for airline reservations systems, errors are regularly found and corrected (Orr 1998). On the other hand, for more-private databases, such as those used by law enforcement agencies, finding and correcting errors are generally neither simple nor regular. Typically, few if any sanctions are applied for submitting invalid data. Moreover, few procedures are in place for finding and correcting data inaccuracies (Laudon 1986). Once errors are introduced, they may remain in these databases for long periods of time.

While invalid data potentially produces devastating results, most database errors manifest innocent mistakes. Nonetheless, some undoubtedly arise through the intentional introduction of invalid data. Zmud (1990) calls the practice of introducing bad data to further one's own ends "strategic information manipulation." The more an organization's members depend on and trust in computers, the more vulnerable they become to strategic information manipulation.

If access can be gained to sensitive databases, perpetrators can embed inaccuracies within an organization's stored data. If done well, such database inaccuracies could result in organizational decision makers making suboptimal or wrong decisions or failing to recognize the need to make a decision. On the other hand, if these same decision makers are concerned about the potential that deceptive data has been introduced into organizational databases, sustained error detection efforts on their part might reduce the time and effort spent making decisions.

The realm of data error has a long history of research across a variety of fields of study. Those most relevant to the study of strategic information manipulation include the areas of vigilance (Adams 1987; Davies and Tune 1969; Weiner 1987), attention (Parasuraman 1984), and human ability to detect errors in data (Klein et al. 1997a; Klein 1996; Johnson et al. 1993). Related research has also examined how individuals' trust in automation is influenced when errors are introduced into systems (Muir 1994; Muir and Moray 1996). Not surprisingly, workers were observed to spend more time monitoring systems considered to be error-prone. In addition, Muir found that workers' trust in a system degrades but gradually recovers with time following the detection of perceived errors. These findings have been supported in a number of studies (e.g., Bisantz et al. 2000; Lee and Moray 1992). However, prior research has not addressed a context in which intentional errors are introduced into an organization's information systems.

The study described herein examines three alternative strategies that an organization might take to heighten members' awareness that strategic information manipulation might have occurred. A research model is developed that associates these strategies, as well as an individual's experience in a task domain, with both detection success and the incidence of false alarms. In turn, further relationships are developed that relate detection success and the incidence of false alarms to two decision-related outcomes: task accuracy and the average time to complete a task. A field experiment, used to collect the data used in examining the research model, is described. Finally, the results, their meaning and implications, and the study's conclusions are discussed.

## Development of the Research Model

Certain individuals have been recognized as being motivated to misrepresent or restrict information in order to influence the behaviors of others (Feldman and March 1981; Johnson et al. 1993; Miller and Stiff 1993). Information manipulation and deception are complex phenomena that draw interest from many different fields. However, as Zmud (1990) noted, new information technologies have created the potential for such strategic information behaviors to impact organizations in significant ways. To better understand the manipulation of computer-based information and the detection of deceptive acts, literature reviews were performed on (1) the detection of deception and errors in data, (2) sensitivity to the possibility of deception and its effect on successful detection, (3) domain experience and its association with detection, and (4) the relationship between deception detection and task accuracy.

These reviews and our own conceptualizing suggested the model in Figure 1. Here, two overarching constructs (interventions to increase sensitivity to deceptive data and domain experience) are shown as influencing deceptive data detection capabilities (detection success and the incidence of false alarms). These deception detection capabilities, along with domain experience, are shown as influencing task outcomes (task accuracy and time per scenario). The constructs and hypothesized relationships among the constructs are now developed.

## Detection of Deception and Errors in Data

Most prior studies have shown that the human ability to detect deception is little better than chance (DePaulo et al. 1985; DePaulo and DePaulo 1989; Feeley and de Turck 1995; Maier and Thurber 1968). Communication research has discovered a number of cues that individuals tend to emphasize in their attempts to detect deception in face-to-face conversation—some visual, some verbal, and some paralinguistic (Zuckerman and Driver 1985). However, even when provided with such cues, humans are still not able to detect deception about half the time.

In the context of computer-based communication, many cues are missing because the sender of the message is neither seen nor heard. As a result, the receiver must rely on textual content and the context within which this content appears for cues regarding potentially deceptive acts. While intuitively this may seem disadvantageous, some research indicates that “removing the human” may actually improve individuals’ deception detection accuracy (DePaulo et al. 1985; Maier and Thurber 1968; Zuckerman et al. 1981). This provides support for the notion that humans seem capable of detecting deceptive data within contextually embedded databases.

In addition to poor performance at detecting deception using traditional modes of communication, prior research suggests that humans are poor at detecting errors within computer-based information systems (Laudon 1986; Rickets 1990; Wiener 1987). Nonetheless, recent studies conducted by Klein and her colleagues suggest that humans can detect errors more successfully in computer-based information than was previously thought (Klein 1996; Klein et al. 1997a, 1997b). These studies show the importance of (1) arousing study participants to the possibility of the existence of errors in the data being examined, and (2) directing study participants to identify data errors as part of their assigned task. Sensitivity to the possibility of deception thus surfaces as critical to increasing the likelihood of individuals being able to detect deceptive data.

## Sensitivity to Deception: Training and Warning

A common basis underlying error detection research is signal detection theory (Davies and

Parasuraman 1981). For example, it underpins work on the accuracy and biases of internal auditors' risk judgments (Blocher et al. 1986), human attention (Sperling 1984), and vigilance (Davies and Tune 1969). A primary contribution of signal detection theory is the recognition that two types of outcomes are possible when individuals strive to detect a signal (i.e., deceptive data) from background noise (i.e., non-deceptive data): (1) successful detections (i.e., deceptive data is discovered) and (2) false alarms (i.e., non-deceptive data is incorrectly identified as being deceptive).

Sensitivity to the possibility of deception might best be conceived as a continuum of awareness, with the effects of different interventions ordered along this continuum. On the basis of signal detection theory, it is unclear where the “ideal” intervention lies on such a continuum: an individual may become overly sensitized to the possibility of deception, thereby triggering an excessive number of false alarms. Thus, it becomes important to distinguish among interventions with regard to their likelihood of producing optional task outcomes.

Two types of interventions that are most likely to be used to increase individuals' sensitivity to deceptive data are general training on deception detection (DePaulo et al. 1982; Zuckerman et al. 1984) and explicit warnings about the likelihood that deceptive data has been embedded within a database (Miller and Stiff 1993; Parasuraman 1984; Stiff et al. 1992).

Participants trained on reliable indices of deception (e.g., message duration, response latency, pauses) have exhibited enhanced accuracy (de Turck et al. 1990). However, individuals who do not practice what they have learned also tend to lose their detection abilities over time (Navarro and Schafer 2001). Together, these findings suggest that while benefits are surely to be derived from traditional classroom training (i.e., where a time lag exists between when the training occurs and when the task to which the training is to be applied takes place), much of this value may be lost by the time it is applied. On the other hand, just-in-time training (i.e., training that occurs immediately before the target task) is likely to prove more effective (Globerson and Korman 2001). Overall, however, we expect that traditional training will increase an individual's ability to detect deceptive data and that this will result in both greater success in detecting deceptive data and a lower frequency in the issuance of false alarms (i.e., the time lag would inhibit oversensitization):

H1a Traditional training to find deception in computer-based data will be positively associated with detection success.

H1b Traditional training to find deception in computer-based data will be negatively associated with the issuance of false alarms.

While individuals are generally poor at detecting deception, existing evidence indicates that, if an individual's suspicion is aroused, the individual's ability to detect deception increases (Miller and Stiff 1993; Parasuraman 1984; Stiff et al. 1992). In essence, the warning places the receiver in a heightened "state of alert." As Stiff et al. (1992, p. 342) noted, "suspicion aroused by a third party is an important part of veracity judgment." Therefore, we expect:

## H2a Warnings about possible deception in computer-based data will be positively associated with detection success.

Unlike traditional training for deception detection, however, warnings about the possible presence of deceptive data may serve to increase the number of false alarms that a warned individual issues. Not only do warnings positively affect detection performance, they may also have a positive effect on false alarms (Burgoon et al. 1994; Parasuraman 1984; Toris and DePaulo 1985). Therefore, we expect:

H2b Warnings about possible deception in computer-based data will be positively associated with the issuance of false alarms.

Not knowing exactly where the effects of traditional training and of warning might lie on the awareness continuum, we decided to also create a situation of heightened sensitivity through a combination of just-in-time training and explicit warning. With such an intervention, individuals would not only be explicitly warned, but this warning would be reinforced because they would be trained in how to recognize potentially deceptive practices. Therefore, we expect:

H3a: The combination of just-in-time training to find deception in computer-based data and warnings about possible deception in computer-based data will be positively associated with detection success.

Application of the same logic as applied for hypothesis H2b (i.e., the state of alert from the warnings would swamp the rational behavior induced through training) suggests that this heightened sensitivity would produce an increase in false alarms. Therefore, we expect:

H3b The combination of just-in-time training to find deception in computer-based data and warnings about possible deception in computer-based data will be positively associated with the issuance of false alarms.

## Domain Experience

Research based on signal detection theory (Davies and Tune 1969) suggests that individuals with greater knowledge and experience in a task domain are better equipped to detect a wider set of signals from background noise. Similarly, studies on expertise indicate that experts in a task tend to base their actions on patterns of information (i.e., their evolved knowledge base) developed over time primarily through experience (Glaser and Chi 1988; Larkin et al. 1980). As a consequence, individuals characterized by greater task domain experience should outperform those with less experience (Klein et al. 1997a). Johnson et al. (1993) observed this outcome in their study of accounting fraud detection. Thus, individuals experienced in the task domain should have a clearer understanding of the nature of “good” data in context. As a result, they should exhibit greater success at recognizing “bad” data in context (i.e., deceptive data). Similarly, experienced individuals should also be less likely to identify good data as bad and thus to issue false alarms. Therefore, we expect:

H4a Domain experience will be positively associated with detection success.

H4b Domain experience will be negatively associated with the issuance of false alarms.

Performance on any given task is determined in part by the attributes of the individual performing the task. For any given task, on average those with greater capabilities to successfully complete a task should outperform those with lesser capabilities. Capability development within a particular task domain typically occurs over time with increased experience in the domain and increased exposure to the tasks common to that domain. On average, it follows that more-experienced individuals should outperform less-experienced individuals. Research evidence supports this conjecture (Chang et al. 1997; Simnett 1996). Therefore, we expect:

H4c Domain experience will be positively associated with task accuracy.

H4d Domain experience will be negatively associated with the time it takes to complete the task.

## The Effects of Detection Success and False Alarms on Task Accuracy

Successfully detecting (and implicitly either correcting or discounting) falsified data in an organization's databases is useful to the organization. Poor data could lead decision makers to (1) make suboptimal decisions or wrong decisions or (2) fail to recognize the need to make a decision. Thus, identification of deceptive data should result in better decision making. However, as discussed earlier, finding deceptive data is a difficult task that takes time and often disrupts the decision maker's task concentration. Thus, we expect that individuals who are more successful at deception detection will spend more time on (and devote more attention to) finding deceptive data than those who are less successful at deception detection. Therefore, we expect:

H5a Detection success will be positively associated with task accuracy.

H5b Detection success will be positively associated with the time to complete a task.

The issuance of false alarms should also affect an individual's task performance. If task performance depends on processing relevant data and if some relevant data is dismissed via a false alarm, then the quality of decision performance should suffer. Additionally, the time and attention directed toward the false alarms adds to the time spent on the decision task. Therefore, we expect:

H6a The issuance of false alarms is negatively associated with task accuracy.

H6b The issuance of false alarms is positively associated with the time to complete a task.

## Research Methodology

A field experiment was conducted at a military training installation in the Southeastern United States using 206 uniformed service personnel attending a human-resources career-track training course. Participation was mandatory by order of the director of the training course. Prior to undertaking the experimental session, participants read and signed a statement of understanding that data would be collected for research purposes. To avoid compromising the study, participants were told the data was being collected to set more appropriate standards for completing the exercises. All participants were given the opportunity to opt-out of having data collected on them, but they had to complete the exercise regardless of this decision. Only one participant requested to opt out, leaving a total of 205 participants. At the end of the treatment period, the true nature of the study was revealed, and participants were again given the opportunity to opt out. No participant chose to do so.

## Research Design

Participants took part in the experiment as part of their standard training at the military installation. All had been assigned to the installation for training purposes. As such, participating in training exercises was their assigned job at that time. Participants, then, were expected to take seriously this training exercise, as was the case with all other training in which they engaged while stationed at this installation. Failure to do so was considered a dereliction of duty punishable by the unit commander.

As is normal at this training installation, classes were organized according to three personnel categories, roughly commensurate with experience in the human resources area: Apprentice, Officer, and Craftsman (described below). Participants were assigned by the military to classes according to criteria such as the participants' experience in the human resources field, the availability of classes, openings in participants' assignment schedules, and other logistical considerations.

The experimental task consisted of a series of 20 scenarios that were developed by the first author. They required each participant to make management decisions using data obtained from a widely used military human resource information system (henceforth called PerCon). Participants had 90 minutes to complete the scenarios. The style and content of the deceptions in the scenarios were taken from the Johnson et al. (1993) taxonomy of deceptive tactics (Table 1). (Note that certain of these tactics were inappropriate for this task context, which used an existing database and typical decision exercises.) These tactics help to establish the type of activities a deceiver might use to manipulate information. If receivers or users of the information are made aware of these deceptive tactics, Johnson et al. believe they can then learn to detect deception by hypothesizing that a tactic has been applied and then examining the data for the assumed tactic.

Two human resource specialists and four course instructors confirmed that the scenarios closely resembled tasks the participants would normally be assigned at their duty stations. They also confirmed that each scenario had a correct answer derived from the data available in PerCon (see Appendix A for the scenarios used in determining this study's task-accuracy measure). Furthermore, a class of 18 officers (see description of officers below) was used in a pilot test of the full exercise. No problems surfaced during this pilot test.

## Participants

As mentioned earlier, three categories of participants were involved in the study. Those in the Officer groups were college graduates (bachelor's degree required), had attended a military academy, the Reserve Officer Training Corps, or Officer Training School, and had been appointed to a commission in the U.S. Armed Forces. Officers typically have had some formal computer training while in college. Most were relatively new to the human resources field, although some may have gained human resource (and PerCon) experiences from prior enlisted experience or via cross-training from other career fields. Those in the Apprentice groups were high school graduates who had recently enlisted in the military and were being assigned to the human-resource career field. While some in this group may hold college degrees, the typical Apprentice has no formal education beyond high school and little formal computer training. However, some in the apprentice course had transferred from other military service career fields. Those in the Craftsman groups were noncommissioned officers in the military. Many held college degrees. All must have completed on-the-job training in the human resource field throughout their careers to qualify for the Craftsman course. Thus, most were quite experienced in both the human resource field and in working with the PerCon system.

All three groups were used in the experiment for three reasons. First, they represent the population of human resource specialists in the branch of service used. Removing one would have detracted from the representativeness of the study. Second, one of the groups had significant experience in the domain while the other two did not. Using all three assured a broad distribution in domain experience across participants. Third, the unit leadership expressed an interest in the study, and all course supervisors requested that their courses be included.

The segmentation of participants provided an additional benefit. By custom and regulation, little interaction occurs across the three categories of participants, thereby reducing the chances of “leakage” across interventions. Officers do not routinely fraternize with enlisted soldiers, for example. Also, leakage within a category of participants was unlikely to occur. Although many courses were always being offered for each participant category at the installation, each was at a different point in its curriculum. Upon arriving at the base, the first author selected a course for the experiment based on where it was placed in its curriculum. The only exception was the traditional training groups. These could be trained at one point in time and take part in the task exercise at another point in time.

## Procedures

Sensitivity to deception was induced by using three different interventions: traditional training to detect deceptive data, warning to the possibility of deceptive data being present in the PerCon system, and exposure to both warning and just-in-time training to detect deceptive data. Control groups were included for each category of participant to increase the variance within the data set. Control groups received no intervention to induce sensitivity to deception.

Participants assigned to either the traditional or just-in-time training interventions underwent a short training program taught by the first author, who instructed them on the deception methods highlighted in the Johnson et al. taxonomy. Precautionary measures were taken to ensure the students would not be confused by the presence of the first author. First, the researcher performed the instruction but wore his uniform and master instructor badge at all times. Most course instructors were military members. By his wearing his uniform and instructor badge, the participants believed the researcher to be just another instructor. The students were informed that the researcher was on temporary duty assignment to provide additional computer security awareness training due to some recent hacker attacks on Air Force information systems. Conveniently, the news media had recently published an Associated Press article reporting a hacker attack on the U.S. Air Force personnel and financial systems at the Pentagon. Second, all students are required to have an instructional unit on Communications Security (COMSEC). This instruction is typically held early in the courses while computer instruction (e.g., PerCon) is near the end. For this traditional training intervention, students received their training on the taxonomy during COMSEC instruction (approximately two weeks prior to the task for Craftsmen participants and four weeks prior for Officer and Apprentice participants) but heard no more of it for the remainder of the course. Third, the first author developed a training lesson plan and training materials in accordance with military guidelines.

Participants exposed to the warning intervention were informed at the beginning of the task session that a Personnel System Manager (PSM) working for the unit was about to be involuntarily discharged. PSMs are the systems administrators of PerCon. They were also told the PSM tampered with the data in the system in order to get back at the unit, which could affect their work on the exercise they were assigned to accomplish. Participants were told that they were required to work alone but that they should record on a discrepancy-reporting sheet any problems they encountered while interacting with the PerCon database. Such a situation is not unusual. The throughput required to get students trained and back to the field is immense. When situations happen like the PSM scenario or some other disruption to training, the standard operating procedure is to make do and press on.

Participants assigned to the just-in-time training and warning intervention received training (described above) and were warned (as above) immediately before the task exercise took place. That is, they were trained and warned during their PerCon instruction briefing.

During the task exercise session, all groups were given discrepancy-reporting sheets that were of the same type provided for other areas of instruction. They were also informed that this portion of their training was to be accomplished independently without any discussion with classmates. Neither the discrepancy-reporting sheet nor the requirement to work independently was considered unusual in this environment. The students were instructed to turn in all their materials upon completion or at the end of the time period.

An “experience questionnaire” (see Appendix B) was administered to participant groups in a classroom setting the morning of the day each group was to undertake the PerCon task exercise. However, the PerCon session itself did not occur until the afternoon.

For the task exercise, the first researcher and the PerCon systems administrators strategically manipulated data elements within the PerCon database to be accessed by participants. Data elements are single incidents of a field in a database. Data elements in 15 of the 20 scenarios were manipulated. In total, 17 of the approximately 221 elements that participants would have to view to resolve these 20 scenarios were manipulated. Overall, then, less than 8% of the data elements that had to be viewed were deceptive. For the analysis reported here, only those eight scenarios for which determining the best answer depended on finding the deceptive data were used in deriving the measures used for false alarms, detection success, and task accuracy. These eight scenarios had a total of nine deceptive data elements out of a total of 62 data elements that participants were required to view, which means that 14.5% of the elements examined were deceptive.

## Research Model, Variable Descriptions, Measures, and Instrumentation

The research model was provided earlier as Figure 1. The dependent variables are task accuracy and the average time spent per scenario. Each variable has one indicator. Number of correct answers (i.e., the ratio of scenarios solved correctly to the number of scenarios solved) is the measure of task accuracy. A ratio was used because some participants did not attempt all eight of these scenarios. The average time spent per scenario was calculated by dividing the time spent by a participant on the exercise (90 minutes or a lesser amount of time if the participant finished early) by the number of scenarios completed (up to the complete set of 20). Note, we were not able to isolate the time participants spent on the eight scenarios of interest. For example, if a participant provided answers for 10 of the 20 possible scenarios during the full 90-minute period, the average time spent per scenario would be nine minutes.

There are two mediating variables. The measure of a participant's success in deception detection was again derived only from the eight scenarios that required successful deception detection for accurate task resolution. The indicator used for detection success is the ratio of the number of deceptive data elements successfully detected to the number of scenarios that the participant completed. The measure of the number of false alarms issued by a participant was also a ratio: the number of false alarms generated by each participant divided by the number of scenarios that the participant completed. A false alarm is defined as otherwise valid data wrongly identified as being invalid. Hence, the false-alarm ratio can be greater than one, because participants might have wrongly labeled multiple data elements as false for any given scenario.

The study's treatment variable, sensitivity to deception, was operationalized as three separate constructs: traditional training to detect deception, a warning that deceptive data may be present, and a combination of just-in-time training and warning. All three constructs had a single indicator. Each indicator was dichotomous: the treatment existed or it did not.

Finally, domain experience, an antecedent variable, was operationalized in terms of participants' experience with both the human resources career field and the PerCon information system. Specifically, three indicators were used: depth of experience in the career field, breadth of experience in the career field, and length of experience with the PerCon system. Support was found for six of the study's 14 research hypotheses, and marginal support was found for a seventh hypothesis. When participants received a warning just before starting the task that the database may have been tampered with, they were better able to detect deception (H2a). When participants received a warning and training about deceptive practices, both just before starting the task, they were also better able to detect deception (H3a). Furthermore, they erroneously identified more valid data elements as false (H3b). Warning alone had no effect on the issuance of false alarms (H2b). Deception detection training delivered several weeks before the task started also had no effect on either detection success (H1a) or the frequency of false alarms (H1b).

Domain experience had an effect only on the number of scenarios solved correctly, with more experience being related to a higher proportion of correct answers to problems attempted (H4c). Experience had no relationship with detection success (H4a), frequency of false alarms (H4b), or time spent per scenario (H4d).

Success at detecting deceptive data was positively associated with task accuracy (H5a), with participants taking more time per scenario (H5b), as predicted. The frequency of false alarms was not related to the average amount of time spent per scenario (H6b), but it was marginally and negatively related to the proportion of problems correctly solved (H6a).

## Limitations

A key concern with this study lies with the amount and difficulty of the embedded data deceptions. Overall, the study's participants did not perform as well as expected in detecting the embedded errors. We cannot rule out alternative instrumental explanations for this poor performance, such as these errors being too difficult to identify or the participants being given too little time to detect errors. While the research design had the objective of reflecting the time pressures and likely errors that might be found in practice, research aimed at better understanding how and why data users are able to detect embedded errors would be valuable.

Another concern with our analysis lies with the implicit assumption that false alarms will invariably contribute to poor task performance. In some organizational settings, suspected information manipulation might be easily evaluated with only a minimal (if any) impact on decision performance. The only cost, therefore, is the time required for detection and evaluation. However, in high-tempo organizations where decision making must be accomplished quickly, these costs may be significant. Research focused on better understanding the nature of the relationships among false alarms, task performance, and the situational context is needed.

A limitation of the study was our failure to develop and apply an instrument that measured a participant's sensitivity to the potential of data deception. In this way we would have obtained an independent assessment of the “strength” of the study's interventions. With such an instrument, we may have been able to fine-tune the three interventions (i.e., content as well as the time lag associated with traditional training). The development of such an instrument is a topic for future research.

Another limitation relates to our decision not to have a separate just-in-time training intervention. As explained early, we combined the just-in-time training and warning interventions in order to induce a high-sensitivity condition. As a consequence, we are unable to determine the effects of the just-in-time training intervention in isolation from the warning intervention. This issue might be pursued in future research.

A final limitation was that our pilot test used only officers. Including Craftsmen and Apprentices in our pilot tests might have surfaced issues that would have resulted in refinements in the study's procedures and/or decision scenarios.

## Implications for Practice and Research

This study contributes to both management practice and academic research. It provides a first look at the effects of strategically manipulated information and the influence of tactical actions and other factors that might counter those effects. The manipulation of the PerCon database did not require special programming or system access to accomplish. On the contrary, the manipulations were conducted as if someone had gained user (not system administrator) access and made changes within the context of the existing data integrity constraints. Over time, intended (or unintended) data manipulation via simple user access (typically not a concern to systems administrators) could produce hundreds of subtle information manipulations.

Managers should note the low success rates of the participants in detecting deception. For all 20 scenarios, on average the most-experienced participants detected fewer than four of the 17 embedded deceptions. For the eight scenarios used in this analysis, where determining the right answer depended on finding deceptive data, the most-experienced participants detected on average only one bad data element. Only 10 of the 205 participants found three or more of the nine bad data elements contained in these eight scenarios. No participant found more than six. While this simulated environment possessed many features of a realistic setting, the atmosphere was less chaotic than would be experienced in practice. Thus, these research outcomes underscore the importance of the research topic.

As suggested by prior research and supported by the findings here, sensitivity to the threat of deception is critical if users are to attend to detection behaviors. This finding strongly suggests the need for organizations to maintain a level of vigilance regarding deception practices among those employees who regularly interact with vulnerable computer-based systems. Warnings about the possible corrupted state of the data, whether accompanied by training or not, were associated with more successful deception detection. Furthermore, successful detection was associated with improved task performance.

Nonetheless, managers must also take into account the relationship between the numbers of false alarms issued and task performance. We found marginal support for a negative relationship between the frequency of false alarms and task performance. Some false alarms can be tolerated as the price that must be paid to discover intentionally misleading data. But how many false alarms are too many? Managers will have to determine how many their operations can tolerate. In a real-world setting, each piece of data labeled as possibly deceptive must be checked to determine if it is false. More false alarms means that more work must be undertaken to check the anomalies. What is more troubling, however, is that more false alarms were associated with fewer correct answers. At some threshold level, too many false alarms seems to lead to faulty problem solving. Additional research is needed to determine where that threshold exists and whether decision makers can be sensitized to the possibility of deceptive data just up to that threshold.

Managers will also need to recognize that successful deception detection seems to be associated with participants taking more time to solve problems. Firms must make explicit trade-offs among detecting false data, the benefits that arise, and the resulting increase in the time needed to solve problems. Understanding the nature of such trade-offs is also a valuable research area.

Based on the findings from this study, we make the following recommendations to managers who work in a similar context to our experiment and who are concerned about the impact of falsified data: Warn workers about the potential for false data in the database. Warnings are associated with better detection of deceptive data, and detection success is associated with better task performance. Recognize, however, that it will take longer for workers to find deceptive data and perform the task successfully. Use both warnings and just-in-time training to improve deception detection and task performance even more. Recognize, however, that more false alarms will be issued, which in turn will lead to a degradation in task performance.

High levels of experience were also found to be associated with high levels of task performance. The impact of experience on task accuracy was strong and direct, without any increase in the time required for task accomplishment. Interestingly, however, experience had no statistically significant relationships with detection success or the frequency of false alarms.

To the extent our findings can be generalized, managers who have available pools of experienced and inexperienced employees will have a choice about what strategy to employ. If successful task completion alone is important and experienced employees are available, managers might not want to focus on detecting deceptive data. Experienced workers seem to be able to compensate for deceptive data and derive good problem solutions anyway. On the other hand, if finding deceptive data and reaching good solutions are both important, managers can use inexperienced workers to do both. However, they must be warned about the data beforehand and given the additional time needed to undertake both the detection and task work. If both types of employees are available but time is not, managers may assign experienced workers to focus on the task and inexperienced workers to focus on deception detection. However, the increased cost of having multiple workers employed on the task and on detection simultaneously may exceed the cost savings associated with saving time. These various strategies and how they might be implemented are all topics for future research.

## Conclusions

As organizations and their members increasingly depend on computer-based information, it becomes more important to enhance the abilities of the users of these information systems to detect whether strategic manipulations of information have occurred. The computer security literature clearly shows that computer-based methods of deception prevention and detection are not sufficient—human involvement is necessary. The results of this study indicate that human ability at detecting deceptive data in computer systems can be improved through inducing greater sensitivity to the possibility of deception. Greater sensitivity can also improve problem solving where finding deceptive data directly affects finding the right answers to problems. The development of practices aimed at improving deception detection ability thus seems critical to an information-intensive organization. Nonetheless, managers need to determine how much they are willing to pay for higher levels of detection success. The number of false alarms may increase, and more time may be spent on problem solving. A balance must be struck, but just where that balance exists is highly contextual.

Much remains to be learned about human deception detection capability in the context of computer-supported decision making. Would other types of interventions help humans to better detect data deceptions? What are the advantages and disadvantages of these interventions? Can the same decision maker be expected to handle both deception detection and decision making tasks? What task load can be applied on decision makers before deception detection and task performance suffer? To what extent might triggering of warnings or deception detection itself be automated? Answers to these and other questions must await further research.

## Scenario 3

The records of four officers must be reviewed for possible reassignment. Review the records and determine which officer, based on his/her date arrive station, duty title, and tour status is most eligible for the assignment. Conduct an inquiry and review the officers' date arrived station, duty title, and tour status. The names and SSANs are provided below. Who should be up for the assignment?

(Circle one) A, B, C, D (Duty Assignment Data, p. 2)

Chen, Terry R. xxxxx9960

Fitch, David L. xxxxx1503

Jones, Taylor xxxxx9417

Smith, Matthew, M. xxxxx7526

## Description of Deception

This deception is a somewhat more difficult to detect. All applicable fields are found in the Duty Assignment Data menu on page 2. One of the key fields in this menu is the Air Force Specialty Code (AFSC). This is a 4 digit alpha-numeric code that tells what career fields the officers are in. Included with the AFSC is often a prefix indicating that the individual is on some special duty assignment. For instance, a "T" prefix indicates the officer is in an instructor's position. A "C" prefix indicates a command position. In this scenario, one of the officers has a "T" prefix indicating that he is an instructor. However, he also has a duty title of "Student." Students are not considered for assignments. Because the duty title information was modified to suggest an incorrect decision, this form of deception would be called "mimicking" in the taxonomy. All of the participants understand the AFSCs, but only those with experience in the field (or possibly another field) would understand the significance of the prefixes. Those respondents should note this discrepancy.

## Scenario 5

Four Master Sergeants (MSgts) on your base have recently been selected for promotion to Senior Master Sergeant (SMSgt). Your Chief, Military Personnel Flight (MPF) wants to run a quality control check on all promotions and wants you to review the MSgts' records to look for commonality. He/She wants to learn if there is anything about these MSgts' records that might help other MSgts meeting future promotion boards. A list of four MSgts and their SSANs is provided below. Review the selectees last Enlisted Performance Report (EPR), Decorations, and Degree levels. Do you see any commonality?

(Circle one) Yes. No. (EPR Info, Awards and Decs, Enlisted Qualification Data)

Booker, Douglas, M. xxxxx1895

Hsin, Ren xxxxx5802

Johnson, Lance L. Jr. xxxxx8964

Jones, Dayton C. xxxxx2600

## Description of Deception

As the scenario states, all of the Master Sergeants have been recently selected for promotion to Senior MSgt. The respondents must review the records of the selectees and determine if they have commonality that the Chief, Military Personnel Flight may be able to provide to other MSgts coming up for promotion. In this case, however, one of the selectees has a significantly low Enlisted Performance Report (EPR). On the EPR scale, a rating of 5 is high and a rating of 1 is low. The EPR systems is somewhat inflated. For those at the MSgt grade of rank, anything less than a 5 is undesirable and would likely prevent a person from being promoted. In this scenario, one of the selectees has a 3 EPR. This would certainly prevent promotion. The respondents should pick up on this and flag it for investigation. This might be considered as “decoying” in the taxonomy. Airmen learn fairly early in their careers about the performance report system. Personnel specialist in all groups of this study received training on the EPR system as part of their course curriculum. However, because the information is new to the less seasoned troops, this is probably a moderately difficult catch for them.

## Scenario 10

A Training Group commander wants to select a Top Tech team. All candidates must have at least one year time on station, must have a Personnel AFSC or Communications AFSC, and must have a "T" prefix. Do all candidates meet the criteria?

(Circle one) Yes. No. (Duty Assignment Info, p2)

Calvin, Adam W. xxxxx0733

Rios, Naomi xxxxx5067

Smith, Taylor xxxxx9417

## Description of Deception

Top Tech is a competition for instructors. Competing in Top Tech is considered an honor for only the most capable instructors on a training base. Winning the competition is a boon to the instructor and the base. Thus, only the best instructors are selected to attend. Usually the first step is to determine who the qualified instructors are. In this scenario, the respondents must pull the data in the listed individuals and determine if they meet the selection criteria. However, in this scenario one of the members has an AFSC indicating that he is a novice in his field. This individual erroneously has a "T" prefix. A discussion of AFSCs and prefixes can be reviewed in the description of deception section in Scenario 3 above. Because the supporting evidence points to selecting the member with erroneous data, this deception can be considered double play in the taxonomy. This is a rather difficult deception to detect and it should separate the experienced Personnel specialist from the novice.

## Scenario 12

A Wing Commander wants to ensure consistency in duty titles (i.e. all squadron commanders have duty titles as squadron commanders). For the following members, run and inquiry on their duty titles and supervisors duty title. Are they pretty much consistent?

(Circle one) Yes. No. (Officer Performance Report--OPR Info)

Amabile, Clyde E. xxxxx1702
Bainbridge, Marvin C. xxxxx9902
Smits, Gerard, A. xxxxx6962
Watson, William, K. xxxxx5680

## Description of Deception

Duty titles play an important role in the Air Force, much like they do in many civilian businesses. Often members are considered for special duties and new assignments based on their duty titles. Also, a list of duties titles is included in the promotion brief (a summary of an officer's career) for each officer's record at a promotion board. Problems with duty titles can be detrimental to an officer's career. In this scenario, the participants are asked to look for consistency in the officers' duty titles and their supervisors' duties titles. However, one of the officers has a duty title that is not commensurate with his rank and position in the Air Force. In fact, his duty title indicates he has a job at a higher echelon than his supervisor. This may be a moderately difficult catch for novices, but fairly easy for seasoned human resource specialists. This deception might be considered repackaging in the taxonomy.

## Description of Deception

This deception is slightly more difficult than the others. Sgt Jones is a student. The other NCO's have duty titles commensurate with individuals who might win quarterly awards. It is not possible for a student to win a Wing quarterly award. In order to win, a candidate must demonstrate outstanding duty performance and compete at a formal board. ## Scenario 15

Four airmen are being considered for a (Temporary Duty assignment) TDY. Their unit commander wants to select the airman with the most time on station for the job. Their names and SSANs are provided below. Which airmen should be the one to go TDY? (Duty Assignment Data, p. 2)

Anderson, Marie M. xxxxx3620
Bruno, Bertha L. xxxxx1911
Jones, Thomas G. xxxxx6562
Wilson, Jacob A. xxxxx5231

## Description of Deception

This should be an easy catch for the more experienced personnel. Some of the troops are students. As such, they are not authorized to go on a temporary duty assignment (TDY). Some of the other troops have pending permanent change of station (PCS) assignments. These people are also unauthorized to go on the TDY. Failure to catch these discrepancies could lead to unfulfilled missions, loss of valuable time, and significant confusion for the member selected, the TDY commander, and other with individuals with a stake in the assignment. This deception technique is another form of mimicking.

## Scenario 16

A Wing commander has learned that the next 4 personnel who leave the base will not be replaced for 6 months. Candidates A thru D below are possible losses. Pull the duty title, time on station information, and tour status for each. Who will be the first to leave? (Duty Assignment Data, p. 2)

Allen, Robert Smith xxxx7385

Anderson, William xxxxx5722

Chang, Gerard, A. xxxxx6962

Jones, Claude, A. xxxxx6927

## Description of Deception

In this scenario, all but Chang have information in their records that would disqualify them from an assignment. Likely, many respondents will focus on each members “Date Arrived Station” information to determine who has been on base the longest. A closer look at each record however would reveal that one member is on a controlled tour (must stay on base for 3 years). The other members have derogatory information indicating they cannot leave until it is closed out. This will take some time. When personnel are PCS’d (Permanent Change of Station) without a replacement, a losing commander has many choices to make. For example, they must decide whether to leave that position unfilled or fill it from the available resources on base (at the cost of another position). Identifying the wrong person in this case can have a chain reaction effect of confusion. The selected member, the person designated to fill the position (if any) and his or her supervisors, the assignments element of the Military Personnel Flight are all affected by this decision. This deceptive practice could be identified as dazzling.

## Scenario 19

Four NCOs have been selected to attend the NCO Academy in residence. Their names and SSANs are provided below. If any, write the name(s) on the line below. (EPR Info)

Jones, Clare J. xxxxx8040

Matthews, Elizabeth, D. xxxxx8687

Watson, Gary W. xxxxx4048

## Description of Deception

In this scenario, one of the members listed, Matthews, already attended the Academy. The goal here is to decoy respondents from noticing this situation. This scenario has respondents examining WMP information. The professional military education information like NCO Academy attendance is also noted. If a respondent only looks at WMP information, Matthews would be recommended for attended. Resources would be expended to send her only to find out later that she also ready attended. While this appears to be a small consequence of the deception, it wastes Technical Sergeant (TSgt) Matthews's time, her supervisor's time, the resources of the Personnel flight, and more. This deception is best described as decoying.
