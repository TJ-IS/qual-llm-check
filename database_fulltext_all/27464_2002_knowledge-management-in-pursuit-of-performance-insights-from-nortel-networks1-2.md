## Abstract

From 1994 through 2000, Nortel Networks transformed itself from a technology-focused to an opportunity/customer-focused company. By 2000, Nortel was a profitable, innovative leader in the telecommunications industry. The change was the result of an ambitious effort to redesign its entire new product development (NPD) process such that time-to-market was significantly reduced. NPD is highly knowledge-intensive work based on the individual and collective expertise of employees. The primary focus of this case study is on Nortel's efforts to reengineer the front-end of its NPD process and capitalize on knowledge assets. This effort was built around a process-oriented knowledge management (KM) strategy, involving a tripartite and systematic focus on process, people, and technology. Through our case analysis we develop a model of KM success by exploring Nortel's KM strategy and the managerial, resource, and environmental factors that influenced Nortel's success. Nortel's experiences suggest lessons for other firms attempting to manage knowledge assets in core business processes.

Keywords: Knowledge management, knowledge management systems, new product development, organizational knowledge management, corporate strategy

## Introduction

The divestiture of AT&T in 1984 and the Telecom Reform Act of 1996 spawned intense competition in the telecommunications industry. This competition yielded an explosion in the development of new and innovative telecommunications technology that, in turn, provides consumers with higher quality and lower-cost telecommunications products and services. A key player in this industry is Nortel Networks, a leading equipment manufacturer whose customers include the inter-exchange carriers (e.g., AT&T, MCI), the Regional Bell Operating Companies (RBOCs), various large corporations, and numerous Internet Service Providers (ISPs).

In late 1994, two executives at Nortel were concerned that the company's products and services idea "war-chest" was empty. Continuous innovation through new product development (NPD) was deemed a mission-critical business strategy to ensure that Nortel could distinguish itself from its competitors, win over new customers, and maintain the loyalty of existing customers. By 2000, Nortel was a profitable, innovative leader in the telecommunications industry. The change was the result of an ambitious effort to redesign its entire NPD process such that time-to-market was significantly reduced. An important element of Nortel's reengineering effort was the front-end of the NPD process, specifically concept development and selection activities. If innovative ideas—the inputs to and impetus for development projects—could be more effectively and efficiently focused, expanded, and filtered, then the rate of innovation could potentially be increased.

The effort to reengineer Nortel's front-end NPD process was built around a process-oriented knowledge management (KM) strategy. Nortel's KM strategy considered both internal resources (core competencies) and external market factors (customer requirements, competitive position). The redesigned front-end NPD process, called Galileo, allowed Nortel to implement a consistent and structured approach for developing and screening product ideas that met customers needs and made more efficient use of Nortel's knowledge resources. The execution of Nortel's strategy was advanced with the implementation of a KM tool, called Virtual Mentor, designed as an electronic performance support system (EPSS). The Galileo process, formalized in Virtual Mentor, allowed Nortel to (1) efficiently leverage multidisciplinary knowledge assets in the NPD front-end process, (2) improve NPD decision-making processes, and (3) facilitate learning and knowledge exchange. In essence, Nortel's process-oriented KM strategy linked disparate knowledge resources (e.g., experts, internal/external databases, customer information, archives, etc.) to a value creating business purpose.

This article describes and reflects upon the experiences of Nortel in its journey to leverage and manage knowledge in the front-end of NPD, one of its core business processes. Through the Nortel case, we address two related research questions: (1) how does a systematic and tripartite focus on process, people, and technology enable the planning and adoption of KM solutions that meet an organization's business goals? and (2) what factors conspire to influence the success of KM initiatives intended to enhance the performance of core business processes? In the next two sections we discuss the theoretical background and research context for our study. This is followed by a discussion of Nortel, its KM strategy, and the implementation and use of the Galileo process through Virtual Mentor. Drawing from a conceptual framework offered by Holsapple and Joshi (2000, 2002), we then explore the factors that influence the success of KM initiatives. In doing so, we offer new insights and extensions to guide future research and practice.

## Background

A successful KM strategy identifies a firm's key leverage points for achieving business results. Since knowledge is context-specific (Sviokla 1996), KM will be most powerful when it addresses a particular domain like NPD. NPD is highly knowledge-intensive work based on the individual and collective expertise of employees (Leonard and Sensiper 1998). As a practice, KM addresses the issues of creating, capturing, transferring, and applying knowledge-based assets (Alavi and Leidner 2001, 1999; Davenport et al. 1998; Davenport and Prusak 1998; Grover and Davenport 2001; O'Dell and Grayson 1998). As a research discipline, KM draws from the resource-based theory of the firm wherein services rendered by a firm's tangible resources create competitive advantage (Barney 1991; Conner 1991). Building on this view, the knowledge-based theory of the firm posits that the services rendered depend on how the tangible resources are combined and applied—a function of knowledge, an intangible resource (Alavi and Leidner 2001). Ultimately, how well a company leverages and manages its tangible and intangible resources in NPD is a critical determinant of how successfully organizational knowledge creation can be carried out (Nonaka and Takeuchi 1995). Improving new product development productivity comes from making more effective and efficient use of knowledge resources, not from working people harder.

A review of the literature suggests, however, that there has been very little research on how to successfully develop and implement KM solutions to enhance performance, particularly in core business processes (Massey et al. 2001). Rather, much of the current KM research has either focused on the use of various technologies to acquire or store knowledge resources (Alavi and Leidner 1999, 2001) or it has been conceptual in nature (c.f., Becerra-Fernandez and Sabherwal 2001; Gold et al. 2001; Grover and Davenport, 2001; Huber, 2001). Although technology drove the initial interest in KM, both academics and practitioners have begun to realize that effective KM solutions must also address processes and people in the business. For example, while intranets and information repositories may provide a means for people to access knowledge, these technology approaches often fall short in helping people apply knowledge in the context of process work.

In this article, we describe how Nortel's systematic focus on process and the underlying needs of its people led to the development and implementation of a KM tool, an electronic performance support system (EPSS), that supported the diverse needs of knowledge workers. The paradigm of EPSS integrates principles found in various research streams, including artificial intelligence, computer-based training, information systems, and performance-centered design. While all software tools are intended to support performance in some fashion, an EPSS is distinguished from other approaches (e.g., traditional systems development, expert systems development) by its attention to enabling performance in the context of knowledge work. That is, the EPSS and work tasks are integrated such that support is provided in the format that best matches the task facing a particular user (Brown 1996; Karat 1997; Raybould 1995).

Despite the complexity of KM, conceptual frameworks exist that can be usefully applied to empirically explore KM in organizational contexts. In this article, we empirically test and extend the conceptual framework offered by Holsapple and Joshi (2000, 2002) and offer a refined model of KM success. The Holsapple and Joshi framework, based on a Delphi panel of researchers and practitioners in KM, proposes three main classes of factors (managerial, resource, and environmental) that may conspire to influence the ability of an organization to achieve its KM objectives. Our research provides a deeper understanding of how and why these factors enable the planning, adoption, and success of a KM solution within the complexities of a practical setting. The richness of the case study method is an invaluable empirical method for explanation when the focus of research is on “how” and “why” questions that seek to understand operational links needing to be traced over time (Yin 1994).

## Case Context

To set the specific context for the Nortel case, Figure 1 shows a traditional phase-review model of the NPD process. At each review point, decisions are made to continue, terminate, or recycle projects. An NPD project is initiated when a concept is funded and moved forward into development. During development, there are various phases of activities simultaneously involving different functional areas in the organization. As projects progress through development, resource commitments increase and decision criteria change.

Despite the fact that the front-end concept development and selection activities drive all subsequent decisions, research has shown that these activities are the least understood and most poorly managed in the entire innovation process (Cooper and Kleinschmidt 1986; Khurana and Rosenthal 1997). Commonly referred to as the “fuzzy front-end,” the early phases typically are characterized by ill-defined processes and ad hoc decisions carried out by multiple individuals (Cooper and Kleinschmidt 1986). Concept development involves transforming a raw idea into a robust concept through careful definition of the underlying technologies, identification of expected customer benefits, and an assessment of the market opportunity. A concept should be sufficiently developed so that decision makers can sense whether it is worth committing resources for further exploration. Concept selection involves choosing which new product concepts will be funded and initiated as projects for further development. Research indicates that many front-end concept selection decisions are made without the use of objective evaluation criteria (Cooper and Kleinschmidt 1986). These decisions are often based on informal discussions with no checklists or criteria. Making such important decisions strictly on “gut-level” feel without any systematic or comparative analysis is not conducive to achieving competitive advantage through continuous innovation.

## Need for Change at Nortel

In late 1994, a senior executive and board member initiated a project to ensure that Nortel could continue to innovate and distinguish itself from competitors. A task force comprised of representatives from engineering, business planning, and marketing was formed and several tasks were initiated. First, the task force sought to validate the sponsoring executives' perception that the empty idea war-chest phenomenon was due to a lack of innovative capability in the organization. In February 1995, the task force conducted an informal email survey of three diverse employee segments (Sales and Support, Global Services Planning, and Marketing Development $^{3}$ ) to canvas for new ideas. Within three weeks, 112 new product and service ideas were received. Consistent with past research, the results of the informal survey indicated that the internal pool of new ideas from employees was, in fact, quite large (Crawford 1994).

In a parallel effort, the task force investigated the front-end of Nortel's existing NPD process to determine where its current idea sources originated. According to past research, a majority of ideas come from suppliers, customers, and published industry information (Crawford 1994). Similarly, Nortel's existing NPD process was found to be primarily customer-driven. In fact, customer requests were mostly for extensions in functionality to existing products and services. The task force concluded that the move toward increased competition and industry deregulation made the current NPD strategy of reacting to existing customers' requests for extensions a very risky proposition. Nortel management realized that a completely customer-responsive approach to product development would not produce really new product or service innovations.

Further assessment of Nortel's NPD process revealed a more troubling finding: there was no formal way in the existing process to capitalize on employee-generated ideas. Consistent with general NPD research (Crawford 1994; Cooper and Kleindschmidt 1995), the survey results suggested that while employees had the knowledge to offer new and innovative product/service ideas, the organization did not provide a path or necessary support (e.g., data, resources, tools, processes, or incentives) to leverage this innovative capability.

As a result of its findings, in May 1995, the task force argued to the executive sponsors that the primary cause of the empty war-chest was that Nortel's existing NPD process provided no formal mechanism to systematically absorb internally generated ideas. Nortel's problem was a business process obstacle rather than a lack of innovative capability. Furthermore, the lack of use of internal idea sources suggested that Nortel was not fully leveraging its own knowledge base. The new competitive environment mandated that Nortel anticipate new market opportunities, capitalize on emerging technological capabilities, and make more optimal use of its resident knowledge assets. The executive sponsors issued a new mandate to the task force:

Define a consistent and structured approach for developing, screening, and cataloging product and service ideas such that the value of Nortel's intellectual capital can be realized (Nortel Project Document, June 1995).

In July 1995, an internal group was formed and charged with the task of addressing this problem. The name given to this effort was Project Galileo. $^{4}$ The Galileo team included representatives from information systems, psychology, human-factors, and product development as well as engineering, business planning, and marketing.

The Galileo team devised a process-oriented KM strategy for reengineering the fuzzy front-end of Nortel's NPD process, involving three major components: process, people, and technology. The first component centered on the creation of the front-end process that would leverage Nortel's knowledge base. The front-end process needed to facilitate idea-to-concept development along multiple dimensions (e.g., customer and technical requirements), improve decision-making with regard to market viability, and enable the capture of innovative ideas. Since a process provides context, designing it first allowed Nortel to figure out what types of knowledge employees needed to carry out activities, and what types of knowledge needed to be shared (and in what form) across activities. The newly designed process would improve the handling of knowledge by clarifying tasks and promoting an integrative, cross-disciplinary view of the front-end.

The second part of the KM strategy—people—focused on determining the factors that would affect the behavior of employees performing process tasks. Whereas the first step of process design assisted in identifying knowledge needs, the second step focused on how to leverage and/or develop employee's knowledge. At the same time, the Galileo team recognized that other factors might influence the ability or willingness of employees to carry out tasks, such as access to necessary resources and incentive systems. Process tasks needed to be logical, with desired outputs specified. Simultaneously, adequate resources needed to be available to carry them out. The success of the new process depended on employees sharing knowledge, and thus required identification of the incentives for sharing and the consequences of engaging in the process. Since employee behavior was influenced by consequences (positive or negative), the team recognized that feedback would be an essential factor to the success.

The final component of the process-oriented KM strategy was technology. Many KM initiatives involve some application of technology to help organize, codify, distribute, and maintain knowledge resources (Leidner 2000). It is important to note that the Galileo team began with process design, as well as a deep understanding of the people involved in the process in terms of their knowledge needs and behavioral influences. These precursor activities generated the foundation upon which a KM tool was specified.

In the end, the Galileo team believed the process-oriented KM strategy with its tripartite focus on process, people, and technology enabled the planning and adoption of solutions that met the organization's stated business goal. The project leader viewed the strategy as a critical means to force Nortel and its employees to think about the front-end NPD process in new ways:

We understood the goals, yet had to figure out a way to achieve them. Starting from scratch allowed us to work to create a process that would look at a new idea holistically. We needed to consider ideas not just from an internal perspective, such as our technical competencies, but also from the customer's perspective.

## Process Design

To begin their efforts, the Galileo team conducted extensive external benchmarking to gain a better understanding of best-in-class front-end NPD processes. The team conducted case studies with companies renowned for their innovative capabilities (e.g., 3M, Hewlett-Packard, Sun Microsystems). They reviewed academic publications on critical success factors in NPD and they studied the best-practice guides published by various professional organizations (e.g., Center for Quality Management, Product Development and Management Association).

Beginning in August 1995, the Galileo team mapped and formalized the process logic, i.e., the sequences, relationships, and interrelationships associated with the front-end concept development and selection tasks. The Galileo team worked with an internal cross-functional NPD team of experts in order to surface and capture knowledge relevant to the front-end NPD process. Interviews and brainstorming sessions with the experts revealed that, before a conclusive decision could be made regarding funding, initial ideas needed to be more fully developed into robust concepts through further marketing, business, human factors, and technical analyses. Based on this finding, the Galileo team developed detailed evaluation criteria for each of the four areas. Standardized criteria in each area would provide a framework for idea-to-concept development and also provide a consistent structure for the concept selection decision.

In December 1995, the Galileo team presented a four-phase front-end NPD process and a set of standard criteria to their corporate sponsors. The Galileo process (see Figure 2) consisted of (1) idea qualification, (2) concept development, (3) concept rating, and (4) concept assessment.

The idea qualification phase provided a quick mechanism whereby many ideas could be narrowed to a few viable ones for concept development. A set of questions was created to prescreen an idea at a very high level analysis of market and technological readiness. In the con-

Addressing these questions provided the necessary data, information, or knowledge to develop embryonic ideas into complete and robust concepts so decision makers could subsequently evaluate them. However, given that Nortel’s existing concept selection process was predominantly ad hoc, it had been very difficult for decision makers to make “apples-to-apples” comparisons between concepts. Thus, the concept rating phase was designed to provide quantitative assessments of concepts for comparative purposes. In concert, Galileo’s standardized criteria and formats, qualitative explanations, and quantitative ratings allowed concept assessment by decision makers to be more formal, consistent, and comprehensive. As the Galileo project manager explained:

By starting with a clearly designed process, this allowed us to develop a comprehensive understanding and appreciation of each area, what the underlying metrics were, and why each matter. For example, human factors had previously not been included in external NPD models. Human factors considerations allow us to think from the customer's perspective. While we were designing the Galileo process, the real challenge was to change everyone's mental model from thinking about "go/no go" decisions to thinking about enabling "why/why not" decisions. Once we were done with the Galileo process, the next challenge was how to implement it.

## People

Upon review of the proposed Galileo process, the executive sponsors were very concerned that the first three phases would be very labor intensive. Like many companies, Nortel regularly used cross-functional teams to develop new products and services. However, like most companies, Nortel did not use cross-functional teams to develop and evaluate new product concepts in the front-end. Maintaining dedicated cross-functional teams to carry out Galileo concept development activities was deemed neither cost effective nor organizationally feasible. Thus, the executive sponsors challenged the Galileo team to find an alternative way to implement the process.

The team faced a particular challenge in that a diverse set of individuals (e.g., engineers, marketers, project managers, executives) had the knowledge and positions required to complete different aspects of the front-end Galileo process (see Figure 2). Concept assessment was in the domain of decision makers who had the authority to approve further development and allocate resources. However, it was not a foregone conclusion whose domain concept development should be. Since the use of expert cross-functional teams to qualify and develop every idea was not feasible, the Galileo team sought to shift the burden of concept development (the first three phases in Figure 2) to the original idea generator. In addition to idea generators and decision makers, the Galileo team identified a third type of knowledge worker with a potential stake in the front-end NPD process: process owners. A process owner, a rather novel concept for many organizations, would be charged with tracking progress of the NPD process.

Given the apparent constraints, the team thoroughly analyzed the diverse, yet interdependent, needs of the three knowledge worker groups who would be engaged in the Galileo process using Rummler and Brache's (1992) model, shown in Figure 3. This model provided a useful mechanism for mapping the knowledge processes, required inputs and desired outcomes associated with each knowledge worker group as related to the four phases of the Galileo process.

By considering the complex interrelationship of knowledge worker, content, and context, this approach facilitated the definition of the necessary "know-how" associated with the "know-what" of the front-end NPD process (Ruggles 1998). The approach also guided the team as they explicitly considered motivational factors (i.e., feedback and consequences) that would ultimately influence each knowledge worker to accept the Galileo process.

## Technology

Since the idea generators (e.g., engineers) typically did not possess sufficient knowledge to address the other content dimensions (e.g., marketing, business, human factors) in the Galileo process, the team evaluated various implementation alternatives according to cost-effectiveness and feasibility given Nortel's current organizational structure, resource availability, and culture. Training all of Nortel's employees in the four content areas required by the Galileo process was not cost effective or feasible. Alternatively, while creating a corporate directory of internal subject-matter-expert (SME) mentors in each area and assigning them (as needed) to each idea generator seemed to be a good alternative, the reality was that these experts were already over-extended. Furthermore, Nortel's functional organizational structure meant that each area of functional expertise reported to a different senior executive. Thus, no one at the division level in Nortel had the jurisdiction to mandate that corporate-wide internal experts assist idea generators. $^{5}$ Based on all this, the Galileo team concluded that technology—the last component of the process-oriented KM strategy—was the best implementation solution for the Galileo process.

In order to develop a technology-based KM tool, the Galileo team explored a relatively new paradigm, Electronic Performance Support Systems (EPSS) (Gery 1997). An EPSS would provide access to integrated information, knowledge, learning experiences, advice, and guidance at “the moment of need.” Whereas KM is a conscious strategy of helping people share and put knowledge into action, EPSS technology provided Nortel the means to do so.

In late January 1996, the Galileo team presented the EPSS alternative to the executive sponsors. It was clear that using technology would address the human resource and organizational constraints identified earlier. For Nortel, technology would be useful for managing the knowledge inherent in and generated from the front-end NPD activities. Based on this, the executive sponsors provided additional funding and resource support to continue the project.

In February 1996, with the addition of system development experts to the Galileo team, efforts began to create Nortel's KM tool, Virtual Mentor. The cross-functional team identified and followed several general system design requirements. Virtual Mentor was designed to facilitate and oversee the four phases of the Galileo process for each knowledge worker: idea qualification, concept development, concept rating, and concept assessment (see Figure 2). The tool was designed to be intuitive and user friendly and flexible in use so that idea generators can work iteratively and not in a forced sequential manner. Virtual Mentor also provided learning opportunities for knowledge workers and cataloged the information associated with all ideas, thus providing a record of internal innovation activity and a possible legal safeguard should patent rights become an issue. Finally, Virtual Mentor was designed to minimize the administrative overhead associated with overseeing complex systems and business processes.

![](/api/attachments/MPW7KYG6/fulltext/images/b205866ad8b0e0ffb401c861cf8f1ea3d36773192eab332070f6a63a5bfb40a4.jpg)  
Figure 4. Overview of Virtual Mentor

The earlier analyses revealed that each knowledge worker group required different kinds of support, and each would add or draw different content to/from the system—thus, unique interfaces were designed for each group. Virtual Mentor provided knowledge-based support to idea generators so they were not faced with ill-structured tasks attributable to knowledge inadequacies. This support was provided on-demand through context-specific advice for each question or activity in the first three phases of the Galileo process such that the assistance of a human SME is emulated. Similarly, decision-based support was provided to decision makers, enabling them to compare and contrast the various concepts' salient attributes as they relate to business needs and goals. Virtual Mentor provided this support by codifying and structuring concept information in such a way that it can be evaluated and compared in an objective and consistent manner. Such support enabled decision makers to make informed why/why not funding decisions for new product/service concepts. Finally, productivity-based support was provided to process owners tasked with tracking the progress of both idea generators and decision makers who interface with the system. Virtual Mentor provided this support via a customized interface to the system that automated many of the search and monitor tasks that would traditionally be carried out manually.

Since a fundamental intermediate purpose of KM is to build some degree of shared context among diverse knowledge workers participating in a process, Virtual Mentor was designed with this in mind. Virtual Mentor supported not only each knowledge worker in his/her "local" language, but also provided company-specific functional "language" translations of data, information, and knowledge as needed during process phases. ## Validation

In June 1996, the project team set forth to validate the Galileo process and the tool. This was important for three reasons. First, the project team needed to validate that the Galileo process was, in fact, aligned with the organization's business goal of differentiation through innovation. Second, the team needed some proof that Virtual Mentor supported and addressed the factors that influence individual work behaviors. Finally, given Nortel's decentralized functional structure, it was important to get managers to "buy-in" to a radically new approach (Sviokla 1996).

A pilot test was conducted using the 112 ideas that had been generated earlier by employees from Sales and Support, Global Services Planning, and Marketing Development. The original idea generators worked through the Idea Qualification phase, yielding a list of 13 ideas that passed initial screening criteria and warranted further development. This subset of ideas was developed into robust concepts as the idea generators worked through the Concept Development and Concept Rating phases. Following this, two executive-level decision makers completed the Concept Assessment phase and selected seven robust product and service concepts for funding. These seven concepts were ultimately handed over to the appropriate group, Nortel's Customer Interaction Program (CIP), $^{7}$ for further development.

For the corporate sponsors, the pilot test provided a “proof-of-concept.” The sponsors believed that the process-oriented KM strategy, as carried out by the Galileo team, had provided a means to exploit Nortel’s knowledge assets and change the capabilities of those involved in the front-end NPD process. The pilot test results also laid to rest a major concern associated with many KM initiatives: is there incentive for knowledge workers to share their ideas and knowledge? Interviews with idea generators revealed the perception that getting a product or service concept funded depended more on good presentation and sales skills than on the actual quality or merit of the concept. Following the pilot test, idea generators commented that the Galileo process could provide a way to “level the playing field” by allowing good concepts, not good sales people, to rise to the top. Additionally, idea generators found the experience worthwhile as it provided a learning opportunity in areas not typically in their domain of expertise. One participant reflected, “I was really guided through the process. Not only did I learn what the questions meant, but the glossary and reference sources helped me answer them.” They had immediate access to the rationale for the why/why not decisions associated with their concepts. Similarly, decision makers indicated that the increased certainty and comparative why/why not analyses improved the decisions directly attributed to them, thus alleviating the professional risk associated with decision-making. One commented, “Everything was put in a format that was easy to digest. It really switched my thinking and allowed me to consider the relative advantages of concepts.”

## Implementation

In August 1996, the Galileo project team received Nortel's prestigious Systems Engineering Award for Innovation, garnering broad coverage for the project in corporate news. As mentioned earlier, the Galileo project was a part of a larger strategic initiative to reduce Nortel's development cycle time and time-to-market for new products. In the fall of 1996, the Galileo project leader presented the internally designed Galileo process and Virtual Mentor tool to the corporate sponsors of a parallel initiative as well as to several divisional managers. He emphasized the alignment of the Galileo process with strategic goals and showed how Galileo and the Virtual Mentor tool, with strong linkages between context and content, encouraged innovation, knowledge sharing, and enhanced decision making. Ultimately, Galileo became the front-end of Nortel's reengineered NPD process, called "Time-to-Market." In late 1997 and early 1998, divisions began to implement the new time-to-market process.

An ingredient vital to Nortel's implementation of the time-to-market strategy in the late 1990s was its spending on research and development (R&D) (MacDonald 2000). The return on this investment in R&D was greatly improved by tying concept generation, development and evaluation more closely to what customers wanted. Simultaneously, then CEO and President John Roth dramatically reorganized Nortel to support the implementation of the time-to-market strategy. Nortel had been organized by function, with marketing, product management, and research operating independently. Everything was coordinated from headquarters by corporate staff, who set spending limits. Roth set up a new structure based on lines of business (e.g., wireless, optical). R&D was integrated with each business line, thus bringing R&D closer to customers. Each line consisted of multiple product groups headed by general managers with significantly more autonomy. Decentralized decision making was aimed at speeding up time to react to market opportunities within respective lines of business. Each business line had a vice president for technology, responsible not only for leading new product initiatives for their respective lines, but together formulating and coordinating Nortel's overall strategy with time-to-market as a guiding framework. The why/why not approach, originally conceived by the Galileo team, became the new way of conducting business.

## Outcomes

The following two examples illustrate new product ideas that were initiated through the Galileo front-end process and subsequently were developed through the time-to-market process and successfully launched into the marketplace. In a partnership with lead customer Ameritech, Nortel developed a telephony application that works with caller ID to identify 100% of incoming calls. Ameritech subsequently introduced this application to their customers as Privacy Manager $^{SM}$ in 1999. The Nortel project manager responsible for this project believed that time-to-market, in particular the Galileo front-end, provided a whole new perspective and approach to product development. Not only did Nortel capitalize on its own knowledge and expertise, but the interactive nature of the front-end allowed Nortel to engage Ameritech in the process as well. Concurrently working through questions in the front-end enabled good conversations between people at Nortel and Ameritech. The Nortel project manager commented, “Privacy Manager $^{SM}$ was not just a product [technology] solution. By engaging Ameritech early, we considered their customers’ requirements [via the human factors component of Galileo], as well as how the product could be marketed very early on.” Similarly, Nortel’s Extreme Voice Division developed the Internet Call Waiting product that allows users to receive phone calls while connected to the Internet, thus replacing the need for a second phone line. Nortel’s customers Ameritech and Sprint introduced Internet Call Waiting to their customers in early 2000.

Delivering Internet Call Waiting is part of Nortel's campaign to build the high performance Internet. It is the first of an entire family of innovative applications Nortel is working on to provide to Internet users (News Release, June 22, 2000).

These examples illustrate that the success of the Galileo process and the Virtual Mentor tool as the front-end of the time-to-market process. This helped enable Nortel's transformation from a technology-focused company, seeking to offer "bells and whistles" products, to a customer/opportunityfocused company that could move quickly. In late 1994, Nortel had been concerned about its innovative capabilities. The process-oriented KM strategy that led to the creation of the Galileo process and Virtual Mentor tool, its integration into the parallel time-to-market effort, and Nortel's reorganization appeared to improve Nortel's innovative capabilities by 2000. Overall, the insights generated during the development of the Galileo front-end NPD process and the Virtual Mentor tool created a strong appreciation among Nortel's top management for the value of consciously managing organizational knowledge and knowledge creation processes. In addition to the above impacts, this project was the impetus for two significant organizational changes at Nortel. First, a new executive position for a corporate learning officer (CLO) and staff was created. The CLO and staff were charged with developing and implementing a corporate-wide KM strategy. ## Discussion

Nortel is clearly not the first organization to improve its competitive stance by redesigning a core business process like NPD. But the company is quite remarkable in the extent to which it transformed itself in the last half of the 1990s. A key component of this transformation was the process-oriented KM strategy enacted by the Galileo team. The Galileo team successfully applied the strategy to improve the front-end innovation process and support the performance of those individuals working in the process. Evidence suggests that the subsequent integration of the team's efforts into the overall time-to-market process along with changes to Nortel's organizational structure appeared to improve Nortel's position in the competitive marketplace.

Why was the process-oriented KM strategy so successful? The conceptual KM framework, offered by Holsapple and Joshi (2000, 2002), provides a foundation to explore this question. As described earlier, Holsapple and Joshi posit that the success of KM initiatives depends on three main factors or influences: managerial, resource, and environmental. In the following sections, we illustrate and interpret how these influences facilitated Nortel's process-oriented KM strategy with its tripartite focus on process, people, and technology. Our analysis of the Nortel case study contributes a context rich empirical explanation of critical factors influencing KM initiatives. By providing a deeper understanding of these factors and how they conspire to enable success, our explanation both validates and extends the descriptive conceptual frameworks currently available to KM theory and practice.

## Managerial Influences

Managerial influences that establish enabling conditions for KM may include leadership, coordination, control, and measurement. Past research suggests that lack of commitment of top leadership to a KM initiative would likely lead to failure in transforming the way(s) in which the organization leverages knowledge assets (Hiebeler 1996; Holsapple and Joshi 2000, 2002; Kotter 1995; Schein 1992). More specifically, however, we found that leadership is needed to ensure that any KM strategy is aligned with business strategy. Leadership establishes an appreciation of knowledge assets relative to core business processes. At Nortel, the initial impetus for the Galileo project came from business strategy, driven by senior management. Deregulation and a changing competitive marketplace signaled that long-term viability required a new direction so it could generate increasing returns and continuing marketplace advantages. Eventually the process-oriented KM strategy emerged, but initial efforts focused on defining the business problem. Nortel's efforts exhibit characteristics known to influence the project success, i.e., successful projects make a conscious rhetorical link with strategic business goals (Orlikowski 1993). At Nortel, sponsoring executives emphasized the need for change, driving all subsequent efforts by the Galileo team.

Coordination, control, and measurement all relate to process, the first component of the Galileo team's process-oriented KM strategy. Coordination involves the management of dependencies among process activities such that knowledge can be developed and leveraged in each, and ultimately integrated across all (Holsapple and Winston 1996; Malone and Crowston 1994). Yet, as our case evidence shows, before dependencies can be managed, process activities must be arranged in time, and the requisite skills and knowledge necessary to carry out activities must be identified. The Galileo team recognized that knowledge was meaningless when disconnected from the people and activities that generate and use it. In designing the Galileo process, the goal was not to simply capture knowledge, but rather facilitate the incremental process of knowledge creation. As such, knowledge should be treated as a process of flows, rather than a product or outcome. Subsequently, the deep understanding of the front-end NPD process (or other processes like sales, operations, or customer service) allowed for systematic design of support for and management of its inherent knowledge processes.

Simultaneously, control addresses the validity and utility of the knowledge activities in the front-end process. A primary objective driving the design of the Galileo process was to create a front-end that could be implemented in a consistent fashion across time and people. By drawing from external best practices, internal cross-functional expertise, and conducting a pilot study, the Galileo team was able to show validity and sell the utility (relevance) of the process. The success of the Galileo initiative came from integrating a range of skills and expertise on the project team. Effective KM needs to address complex interrelationships among people, process, and technology in a balanced manner. As such, any initiative must draw from a range of skills, independent from functional influence that may serve to upset the balance. Validity and utility were both important factors that influenced corporate executives' decision to continue funding the Galileo project and ultimately integrate the Galileo process into the time-to-market initiative.

Disciplined project management at Nortel further illustrated coordination and control. Nortel carefully defined the scope of the project, including preliminary and updated cost estimates and launch dates. This is important because sufficient resources are vital to a project's success. To maintain project momentum, and effect change as quickly as possible, the team was constantly vigilant about the scope of the project. There were many instances when the team was nudged in different directions and encouraged to broaden the scope. It is essential to avoid creep in project scope or it may become prohibitively costly and unwieldy. This can lead to a downward spiral of waning support for a behemoth, never-ending KM project. To build and maintain support for the project, the team consciously promoted, tested, and sought continuous feedback for its efforts while being mindful not to “oversell” potential benefits.

Finally, measurement is essential for evaluation of the impacts of KM initiatives and consequently leadership, coordination, and control. Obviously, linking KM to financial results or bottom-line performance is a feasible approach (Stewart 2001). When a KM project is targeted toward the value-creating activities of a business process, traditional indicators of process performance are applicable. By 2000, our case data suggests that more efficient and effective capitalization of knowledge assets, and a why/why not mental model, improved Nortel's rate of new product launches. At the same time, it is important to recognize that the business strategy of differentiation through innovation drove not only changes to Nortel's innovation process, but also requisite changes to its organizational structure. CEO Roth's reorganization of Nortel along lines of business allowed for decentralized decision making and faster response to market opportunities. Aligning Nortel's organization structure with the capabilities of a time-to-market strategy, including the Galileo front-end, was a key factor that led to improved marketplace performance. In dramatically reorganizing Nortel, Roth conveyed a "sense of urgency" (Kotter 1995), and put in place managers and vice presidents who shared his vision of evolving Nortel from a technology-focused company to one that was fast moving and opportunity-focused. Nortel's success in the late 1990s provides evidence of the impact of not only the process-oriented KM initiative, but also this leadership. As Alavi and Leidner (2001) suggest, KM works within the complexities and dynamics of organizational phenomenon.

Our case data suggests that the impacts of KM should be measured not only with “hard” financial data, but also in terms of the “soft,” non-financial implications (Grover and Davenport 2001). As described earlier, KM efforts related to the NPD process created a strong appreciation at Nortel for the value of consciously managing organizational knowledge and knowledge creation processes. Starting with a single business process can lead to acceptance for a KM-based reengineering of other processes (Maier and Remus 2001). Transferring the KM principles of the Galileo process to the new Business Ventures group is an example of this. The creation of the CLO and staff, charged with developing and implementing a corporate-wide KM strategy, provides additional evidence of the impact of the original KM project at Nortel.

## Resource Influences

In additional to managerial influences, financial, human, and knowledge resources may enable KM success. As evidenced at Nortel, a key to the success of the Galileo KM initiative was the on-going funding and investment for necessary human and technical resources. By providing financial resources to the Galileo team, top-level management helped send a clear message that managing knowledge was mission critical.

Directly related to human and knowledge resources was the second component of the process-oriented KM strategy: the people. Once the Galileo process had been defined, it became clear that the knowledge skills of Nortel's employees could facilitate or constrain the process. Knowledge was essential for performing the four phases of the Galileo process. However, the Galileo team took a broader view of human and knowledge resources, i.e., they did not focus solely on knowledge needs and gaps. While much has been made of the need to assess the know-what associated with the know-how (Stewart 2002), our case demonstrates that knowledge needs and gaps provide only part of the picture. Other factors affect the use and creation of knowledge by individuals.

As illustrated at Nortel, issues related to process tasks, performance specifications, consequences, and feedback provide a deeper and broader view of the work and motivational environment in which knowledge workers create, share, and use knowledge. The Galileo team conducted a careful study of the needs of all knowledge workers: idea generators, decision makers, and process owners. The team recognized that for every employee who comes up with an idea, there are scores of disgruntled up-and-comers who can't get anyone at their giant corporation to listen to their ideas. Therefore, for idea generators, the standardized process provided a more uniform and fair opportunity to share and develop their ideas for review. Conversely, while decision makers already had status and power, the process codified and structured concepts in a standard format so that they could be evaluated in a more objective, consistent, and comprehensive manner. Better decisions, leading to potential gains to the organization, can enhance the power base of decision makers even more (Sviokla 1996). Thus, by considering and faithfully incorporating multiple needs into the Galileo process, Nortel was able to establish bottom-up support for the project. Ultimately, the success of the radically new front-end of the time-to-market process necessitated significant motivation on the part of knowledge workers to change work behaviors.

## Environmental Influences

Whereas managerial and resource influences are internal, environmental influences on KM are often external, e.g., customers, competition, regulatory, and technology shifts. A precipitating factor driving Nortel's new business strategy was the governmental deregulation of the telecommunications industry, and subsequent increased competition. At the same time, the environment influenced the Galileo team as it put in place the third component of the process-oriented KM strategy: technology. As seen at Nortel, before technology was even considered, the Galileo team developed a deep understanding of both the process and people tasked with process activities. By understanding "what to do" (process) and human and knowledge resources (people) first, the Galileo team was able to more precisely specify technology use and requirements. Without this, technology may have only a random effect on performance (Gold et al. 2001; Huber 2001). As evidenced by Nortel's experiences, however, the real challenge concerns the complex interplay between context, content, and the knowledge workers who pull the process pieces together. In and of itself, technology is not likely sufficient for effective KM; rather, the key to success is the creation of links between process, people, and the supporting technology. Nortel recognized that technology was the medium, not the message.

## A Model for KM Success in Light of Nortel

Figure 5 presents our model for KM success that emerged from our case study. The conceptual KM framework offered by Holsapple and Joshi served to focus our interpretation of the Nortel case along broad factors (managerial, resource, and environmental), proposed as enablers of the success of KM initiatives. Our research indicates that the efforts of the Galileo team represented a complex process of organizational transformation, which was, in fact, facilitated by the confluence of these factors and respective subcomponents. Thus, a key finding of our study is that successful KM initiatives like Nortel's cannot be disentangled from broader organizational factors and changes. In the end, our case study contributes to theory and practice by not only demonstrating the links between influencing factors and Nortel's process-oriented KM strategy, but also by providing rich empirical insights as to how and why they influenced success. By providing a deeper understanding of these factors and how they conspire to enable success, we both validate and extend the KM frameworks currently available. With very little past research available to guide KM initiatives in core business processes, our model of KM success may be used by researchers as a foundation for further exploration as well as by practitioners interested in benchmarking process-oriented KM initiatives.

## Conclusion

Knowledge management (KM) is a topic that has gained increasing attention since the mid-1990s. Since knowledge assets may produce long-term sustainable competitive advantage, proponents argue that the nurturing, cultivating, and harvesting of knowledge assets are as important as managing other organizational assets like labor and capital. To date, KM research has considered a broad array of issues, addressing, among other things, building databases and intranets, measuring intellectual capabilities, establishing corporate libraries, installing groupware, enacting cultural change, and fostering collaboration. KM has seemed to absorb all kinds of theoretical approaches as well as practical activities, measures, and technologies. However, before organizations can realize the “grand promise” of KM, the first issue that should be addressed is: What knowledge needs to be managed and to what performance ends?

As illustrated in the Nortel case, KM initiatives work best when knowledge is managed within a context where value is created. For Nortel, the unit of analysis and management was a core business process: new product development. Within this perspective, many KM goals are characteristic of business process reengineering goals. Once a process is clearly defined, the next step should be determining what data, information, and knowledge the people tasked with process work need, i.e., connect knowledge to work and work to knowledge. The analysis of a business process and the people tasked with process activities can then be used to more precisely specify the role of technology. Our field study illustrates that technology capabilities are only effective when there is alignment with the business, work activities, and people that will make use of them.

Given the limited research available to date to guide the development and implementation of KM systems to leverage and manage knowledge-based assets in core business processes, Nortel's approach provides rich insights regarding how to successfully guide this process. Our case analysis explored the tripartite process-oriented KM strategy as a mechanism for systematically understanding and bringing about the changes necessary to enhance organizational, process, and knowledge worker performance. In the end, Nortel was able to plan and implement solutions that met the organization's strategic business goals.

This case research illustrates that the success of a process-oriented KM strategy depends on key enabling factors: managerial, resource, and environmental influences. Extending past conceptual frameworks, we offer a model of KM success that helps explain how initiatives like Galileo at Nortel can be successful and what challenges are to be expected in the implementation process. Driven by a new business vision, Nortel made a significant investment in reengineering its whole NPD process in order to implement a new time-to-market strategy. To support this strategy, Nortel changed its organizational structure and shifted the way people thought about product development from a go/no go mindset to why/why not. Together, these changes resulted in a significant increase in performance of its product development process.

An important insight we can draw from this field research and analysis is the realization that KM cannot be generically applied. As with other topics within information systems, there is often pressure to view problems of KM as a technology issue. Perhaps, when an organization knows what knowledge it needs (tasks are well understood, activities are mostly routine, and problems familiar) a technology-driven KM strategy may work. By contrast, when an organization does not know what knowledge it needs relative to an ill-defined process, a process-oriented KM strategy is likely more appropriate. When innovation is central to the organization's value proposition, as in Nortel's case, the strategy may require lending structure to the innovation process, bringing cross-functional expertise to bear on new problems, and facilitating learning. In addition to NPD, we believe that a tripartite focus on process, people, and technology is relevant to other business processes such as customer relationship management and enterprise resource management. By focusing on the process, and then combining the richness of human expertise with the efficiencies of technology, an organization will likely be more successful in managing its knowledge assets. We hope that the lessons underlying Nortel's initiative will help guide and encourage other firms in their own endeavors.
