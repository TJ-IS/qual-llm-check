# INFORMATION TECHNOLOGY AND ADMINISTRATIVE EFFICIENCY IN U.S. STATE GOVERNMENTS: A STOCHASTIC FRONTIER APPROACH<sup>1</sup>

Min-Seok Pang Fox School of Business, Temple University, 1810 North 13<sup>th</sup> Street, Philadelphia, PA 19122 U.S.A. {minspang@temple.edu}

Ali Tafti College of Business Administration, University of Illinois at Chicago, 601 S. Morgan St., Chicago, IL 60607 U.S.A. {atafti@uic.edu}

M. S. Krishnan Stephen M. Ross School of Business, University of Michigan, 701 Tappan Street, Ann Arbor, MI 48109 U.S.A. {mskrish@umich.edu}

This paper explores value creation from government use of information technologies (IT). While the majority of studies in the information systems (IS) discipline have focused on discovering IT business value in for-profit organizations, the performance effects of IT in the public sector have not been extensively studied in either the IS or the public administration literature. We examine whether IT improves administrative efficiency in U.S. state governments. Utilizing IT budget data in state governments, the census data on state government expendi tures, and a variety of information on public services that states provide, we measure technical efficiency with a stochastic frontier analysis and a translog cost function and estimate the effect of IT spending on efficiency. Our analyses provide evidence for a positive relationship between IT spending and cost efficiency and indicate that, on average, a \$1 increase in per capita IT budget is associated with \$1.13 in efficiency gains. This study contributes to the IS literature by expanding the scope of IT value research to public sector organizations and provides meaningful implications for elected officials and public sector managers.

Keywords: IT value, public sector, U.S. state governments, stochastic frontier analysis, translog cost function

## Introduction

A vast range of research has been conducted in the information systems (IS) discipline over the past two decades to determine whether information technology (IT) provides sufficient payoff, be it tangible or intangible, to justify the enormous investments in IT in the private sector (Bharadwaj et al. 1999; Brynjolfsson 1993; Melville et al. 2004). A large segment of the literature has provided evidence that IT investments do contribute to considerable performance improvement and value creation within organizations and across value chains (Banker et al. 2006; Barua et al. 1995; Rai et al. 2005). However, most studies in the IT value literature have centered on the for-profit business context.

While a few studies have examined IT value in the public sector (e.g., Garicano and Heaton 2010; Lee and Perry 2002; Lehr and Lichtenberg 1998), the IS literature has not paid as much attention to the performance impact of IT in the public sector as in the for-profit sector. By comparison, Melville et al. (2004) review and synthesize 198 papers on IT value in the business setting that were published in IS journals. We view this as an opportunity for new research for several reasons. First, the size of the public sector and its influence on the overall economy have continued to grow over the past several decades. The ratio of total government spending in the United States to gross domestic product (GDP) (Larkey et al. 1981; North 1985) has grown from approximately 30 percent in 1970 to 38 percent in 2008.<sup>2</sup> Second, a vast amount of spending in IT has occurred in this sector in recent years. A survey conducted by the National Association of State Chief Information Officers (NASCIO) reveals that, on average, U.S. state governments spent approximately 5 percent of tax revenues on IT in fiscal year 2004 (NASCIO 2005). In comparison, according to the InformationWeek 500 survey in 2005, on average, IT expenditures in for-profit firms were approximately 3.6 percent of sales revenue in 2004. Thus, we believe that given the recent fierce debates about budget deficits and “big government” in U.S. politics and media, it is imperative for policy makers to understand whether this massive amount of IT spending does contribute to performance improvement and value creation in public organizations.

We choose U.S. state governments as our focal context to study IT value in public sector organizations for the following reason: State governments have used IT extensively for internal administration and delivery of public services such as education, social welfare, healthcare, and law enforcement. Like for-profit firms, many state governments have strategically adopted IT to achieve greater efficiency and to provide more responsive public services (NASCIO 2005; Newsom and Dickey 2013). Increasingly, IT has become an indispensable tool for major strategic initiatives in state governments. For example, the 14 U.S. states that implemented their own online Health Insurance Marketplace according to the Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act spent more than \$3.7 billion by 2013, with assistance from the federal government.<sup>3</sup>

Therefore, a legitimate question, from the perspective of taxpayers, is whether these efforts indeed generate sufficient value to the public. However, studies in the IS field do not provide as adequate answers to this question as they do in the private sector setting. We believe that this is why a report from the Center for Digital Government (2006, p. 1) states, “What seems to have been proven in the private sector has been greeted with suspicion and delay in the public sector.” In a similar vein, Vivek Kundra, a former federal chief information officer, notes that

we wanted to look at this \$80 billion that we’re spending every year on IT—how do we make sure that taxpayer dollars are not wasted on investments that don’t produce dividends for the American people?” (Fortune 2011, p. 56).

Given the sectoral differences between business and government organizations, previous studies in the business context would not suffice for IS researchers to make a convincing case for public-sector IT value. We aim to provide concrete empirical evidence that is informative to public-sector practitioners.

In addition to the paucity of empirical studies, this study is motivated by our observation that the approach to measure the performance effect of IT investments in the private sector may not be appropriate in the government context. We find that the production function framework, which has been a de facto approach in the IT value research since the 1990s (e.g., Brynjolfsson and Hitt 1996; Dewan and Kraemer 2000; Hitt and Brynjolfsson 1996), may not be directly applicable in our context. State governments produce a range of goods and services that have diverse objectives and are difficult to quantify with a single measure, such as profits in a commercial business. We were not able to find any aggregate measure for government outputs that corresponds to sales or value-added in the private sector. In addition, a production function assumes that outputs are endogenous; that is, firms maximize the amount of outputs for a given level of input resources. On the other hand, economic theory suggests that it is more appropriate to assume that in a government setting, outputs (public services) are exogenously given (i.e., set by various laws and policies) and that the governments attempt to minimize expenditures required to produce a mandated level of outputs (Caves et al. 1981; Kumbhakar and Lovell 2000; Ray 1982).

This point leads us to adopt a stochastic frontier model with a cost function (Aigner et al. 1977; Caves et al. 1980) to study the impact of IT on administrative efficiency, which is defined as the extent to which a government provides public goods and services with limited resources (Downs and Larkey 1986, p. 6; Stiglitz 2000, p. 149). Utilizing IT budget data from NASCIO and the census data on state government finances, we estimate the relationship between internal IT spending and cost efficiency in U.S. state governments.

Our analyses provide empirical evidence that this relationship is positive and statistically significant. In other words, larger IT investments are related to greater efficiency in the states. All others being equal, our results indicate that, on average, a \$1 increase in per capita IT budget is associated with a \$1.13 reduction in per capita operational expenses and capital depreciation. In addition, we conducted a series of robustness checks using alternative specifications and estimation approaches. Overall, our results remain consistent under these robustness checks. Finally, we find that the impact of IT on cost efficiency is contingent on the size of private-sector IT industries, the share of rural population, and the political dividedness in state governments.

Taking an interdisciplinary approach with IS, public economics, and political sciences, our research contributes to the literature by expanding the scope of IT value research to an area in which, to the best of our knowledge, little previous work in the IS discipline has been devoted. This paper is one of the first IS studies to provide quantitative empirical evidence that IT helps governments do more with less, even in the apparent absence of a profit motive and competitive pressures. Furthermore, we identify the moderating impacts of demographic, economic, and political environments on returns from IT and the complementarity between the privatesector IT industry and the public-sector IT spending. We believe that these effects have not been identified in prior IT value studies. Thus, this study offers new insight and knowledge on IT value for researchers, policy makers, and publicsector practitioners.

The remainder of this paper proceeds as follows. The next section offers theoretical background and hypothesis development. We then explain in detail our empirical approach, as well as data sources and measures, followed by the presentation of our main results and a series of sensitivity analyses. The paper concludes with discussions, limitations, and future research directions.

## Theoretical Background and Hypothesis

## The Effects of IT Spending on Cost Efficiency in State Governments

Prior literature on IT business value has documented the positive relationship between IT spending and cost efficiency. For example, Mitra and Chaya (1996) and Santhanam and

Hartono (2003) show that firms with larger IT spending report lower costs of goods sold (COGS) and selling and general administration (SGA) expenses. Aral and Weill (2007) also show that larger investments in transactional information systems are associated with lower COGS. While the effect of IT on costs of goods sold may not be directly applicable to the public sector, efficiency benefits of IT do apply in this context, because state governments are mandated to deliver necessary services to citizens with as little revenues (tax ollection) as possible, as in the balanced budget requirements in 45 states (NASBO 2008).

IT investments in government organizations can lead to cost efficiency improvements in two ways. First, as in for-profit organizations, IT can automate manual, paper-based, and error-prone operations, thus enhancing the productivity of administrative processes. For instance, in 2004, the State of Michigan completed the Human Resource (HR) Optimization Project, an interdepartmental initiative to increase the effectiveness and efficiency of statewide HR operations (NASCIO 2007). The state reports that this project contributed to approximately \$4.8 million in budget savings over the following two years. Automating organizational processes that directly interact with citizens can also yield substantial efficiency improvements in state services. For example, in 2004, the New York State Higher Education Services Corporation launched e-TAP (Tuition Assistance Program), which offers an online application channel for TAP (NASCIO 2006). The state reports that by transforming a paper-based application process to a Web-based one, it could dramatically reduce administrative costs associated with the TAP program, such as printing, mailing, and processing personnel.

Second, automated, digitized business processes help governments enhance transparency and accountability in administration, which in turn further improve cost efficiency by curbing excessive expenditures by government officials. The theory of bureaucracy from the political sciences literature maintains that one of the primary interests of government officials is to maximize the size of expenditures, which represents their power and influence ( Miller and Moe 1983; Niskanen 1968). Rooted in the principal–agent model, the theory argues that in the absence of proper controls over expenditures and accountability mechanisms by legislatures and citizens (principals), government officials (agents) will spend government resources (tax revenues) less efficiently than in the presence of such controls, thereby undermining cost efficiency. For example, a government agency may retain a larger number of employees than is needed for its function, or it selects vendors or contractors that provide goods and services more expensively than others but have special interests or favoritism on behalf of certain agency officials.

Automation and digitization in business processes enable legislatures and citizens to monitor government expenditures and decision making more effectively. For instance, many state governments, such as New York, Virginia, and Missouri, publish their detailed expenditure records at the individual transaction level on a real-time or daily basis through online channels such as Open Book New York, Commonwealth Data Point, and Missouri Accountability Portal, respectively.<sup>4</sup> Such a level of transparency would not be achievable without sufficient investments in enterprise systems and IT infrastructures. According to bureaucracy theory, such improved transparency can help prevent excessive spending by government administrators (Banks 1989; Banks and Weingast 1992), leading to greater cost efficiency.

In line with the foregoing arguments, we offer the following hypothesis regarding the impact of IT spending on state government cost efficiency:

Hypothesis 1. IT investment is positively associated with administrative efficiency in U.S. state governments.

## Contextual Effects

In addition to the direct impact of IT spending on state cost efficiency, we investigate the moderating effects of three factors: private-sector IT industries, share of rural population, and political dividedness of state government. These three factors represent the economic, demographic, and political environments in a state, respectively.

First, we expect that the impact of IT budgets on cost efficiency is greater when a state has larger private-sector IT industries. We draw on the research and development (R&D) literature to formulate this hypothesis. Several studies in the R&D context measures knowledge spillovers across firm boundaries (e.g., Breschi and Lissoni 2001; Griliches 1992; Knott 2008; Knott et al. 2009; Tambe and Hitt 2013). These studies show that knowledge output in one firm affects R&D productivity in other organizations. The literature also shows that such knowledge spillovers tend to be localized (Almeida and Kogut 1999; Audretsch and Stephan 1996; Bresnahan et al. 2001; Jaffe et al. 1993). Research on R&D spending and productivity further demonstrates that knowledge spillovers also exist between the public and the private sectors (Cohen et al. 2002; David et al. 2000; Levy and Terleckyj 1983;

Leyden and Link 1991). Empirical evidence suggests that private-sector R&D spending is positively influenced by public-sector R&D outputs such as R&D subsidies to private firms or research outcomes from public research institutes. In addition, both R&D effectiveness and overall productivity in business firms are positively associated with government R&D (Levy and Terleckyj 1983; Leyden and Link 1991). This complementarity can be explained as follows: Publicsector R&D, which usually centers on basic fundamental sciences, facilitates private-sector R&D. Private-sector R&D focuses on applied research, engineering, and commercialization of knowledge and outcomes from public-sector research. Private-sector research organizations can build their research capabilities through government R&D contracts or subsidies (David et al. 2000). Public-sector R&D also provides a pool of competent research talent to private-sector research organizations (Cohen et al. 2002).

We theorize that similar public–private complementarity exists in our setting. In states with greater IT industry production, governments have access to a broader pool of talented IT professionals than other states. Anecdotal evidence shows that many public-sector IT professionals and managers, such as those in Virginia, New York, and Washington, are hired from the local private sector (Government Technology 2007, 2010, 2011). IT workers with private-sector careers can bring their knowledge and experiences into state IT management, so that the state IT organization can utilize its IT resources more effectively for performance improvement. IT employees with private-sector experiences are likely to be more proactive and open to adopting more current, up-to-date technologies. Almeida and Kugot (1999) and Tambe and Hitt (2014) find that movement of technology workers between firms enables knowledge transfer. Furthermore, the presence of a large, local IT sector provides state governments with a greater pool of capable IT vendors with more advanced technology products and quality services. The governments of states that have large IT industry sectors, such as California, Massachusetts, and New York, have easier access to local IT suppliers. Thus, considering the fact that state governments are large consumers of IT, the states with larger, more competitive local IT industries can purchase IT products and services and hire IT workers with more competitive prices and wages, respectively, than others can.<sup>5</sup>

In summary, we argue that states with greater IT industry production can realize greater marginal returns from IT investments. This prediction is also in accordance with a country-level study by Dewan and Kraemer (2000), who find that returns to IT investments are greater in developed countries than in developing countries. In line with this discussion, we propose the following hypothesis:

## Hypothesis 2. The positive relationship between IT investment and administrative efficiency is stronger in states with larger private-sector IT industries.

Second, we hypothesize that the impact of IT investments on state efficiency is accentuated when a greater portion of a state’s population resides in rural areas. To effectively serve all citizens, be they in rural or urban areas, a state must set up and operate regional offices across the state for such services as driver’s license, voter registration, tax collection, and courthouses. For the same size of population, states with a more urban population would require fewer regional offices than those with more rural residents. For example, we obtained the share of urban population from the U.S. Census and the number of Department of Motor Vehicles (DMV) field offices in all U.S. states from DMV.org. The correlation between the share of urban population and the population per DMV office is 0.53. In other words, the larger the urban population, the more residents are served by each DMV office. For example, while both Maryland and Wisconsin had similar population levels (5.7 million) as of 2013, the shares of urban population in the two states in 2010 was 87.2 percent and 70.2 percent, respectively, and the average number of residents being served by each DMV office in the two states was 178,100 and 56,500, respectively. Because Wisconsin’s population is more geographically dispersed, more DMV offices are required to serve the population, suggesting that it is more challenging for Wisconsin to operate efficiently.

We argue that efficiency improvements from digitized business processes, IT infrastructures, and online services for citizens are greater in states that serve more geographically distributed populations. Since a state with more rural residents maintains more physical offices per capita, as described above, it is more costly for the state to monitor and control state resources that are geographically dispersed around the state. This exacerbates cost inefficiency in governments (Banks 1989; Banks and Weingast 1992; Miller and Moe 1983). However, prior research in IT and transaction cost economics suggests that the use of communication technologies and enterprise systems reduces internal monitoring costs (Baker and Hubbard 2004; Gurbaxani and Whang 1991; Hitt 1999; Rawley and Simcoe 2003). Thus, efficiency improvements from IT-enabled monitoring and supervision should be greater as state organizations become more dispersed. In addition, online channels for government services are not only more beneficial to residents in rural states, but also save costs that rural states would otherwise incur through physical locations. Therefore, we propose that the impact of IT spending on state cost efficiency is greater in rural states than in urban states.

Hypothesis 3. The positive relationship between IT investment and administrative efficiency is stronger in states with a larger rural population.

Third, we hypothesize a moderating effect of the political environment in a state. In particular, we test the impact of what the political sciences literature calls a divided government on efficiency returns to IT spending. A divided government refers to a situation in which the chief executive of a government (a governor) and the legislature are affiliated with different political parties (Alt and Lowry 1994; Edwards et al. 1997).

As discussed above, in the political sciences literature, the relationship between the legislature and the executive branch is modeled by the principal–agent framework (Bawn 1997; McCubbins et al. 1987, 1989; Moe 1984). In this relationship, the legislature (principal) supervises and oversees the activities and decision making of the executive branch (agent) with a variety of control mechanisms such as formal investigations, performance evaluations, and approval of agency appointees (Calvert et al. 1989; McCubbins and Schwartz 1984; Wood and Waterman 1991). The theory of political control on bureaucracy asserts that such monitoring and control aim at making the agencies’ activities and decisions more congruent with the interests of constituencies. It further asserts that this supervisory role of lawmakers can be more effective in a divided government (Bawn 1995; Epstein and O’Halloran 1996). In a unified government, in which the executive branch and the legislative body are controlled by the same party, the legislature is likely to support the chief executive’s strategic direction and initiatives and to allow executive agencies broader discretion in policy execution. On the other hand, in a divided government, the legislature is likely to be more active and stringent in monitoring and supervising the executive agencies and to provide less discretion to them.

We argue that IT management will be more effective in the presence of more legislative controls over IT management. In the absence of such controls, IT management and investments might not fulfill the legislative goals and directives. For example, without clear strategic priorities, IT spending could be made in functions that would not improve overall state government performance. IT management may fail to upgrade an obsolete, outdated IT infrastructure in a timely manner (Mark and Rau 2006), thus boosting costs for maintenance and undermining flexibility in business processes. IT managers may have vested interests with certain vendors or service providers and, as a result, devote an excessive amount of expenditures to them. Thus, we expect that, in a divided government, the legislature oversees IT management and spending in a more active manner, increasing the efficiency effect of IT investments. To this end, we offer the following hypothesis:

## Empirical Methodology

## Measurement of Efficiency in State Governments

We estimate the impact of IT investments on administrative efficiency with a two-stage estimation approach, which several studies in public economics have adopted (e.g., Davis and Hayes 1993; Geys 2006; Lovell et al. 1994; Worthington 2000). We first measure the cost efficiency of state governments in a stochastic frontier analysis (SFA) with a translog cost function (Aigner et al. 1977; Caves et al. 1980). Figure 2 describes SFA and a cost frontier. In the second stage, we regress the estimated efficiency on IT measures and other exogenous factors that the public economics literature has identified to influence government efficiency.

SFA has been widely used in public-sector efficiency measurement (e.g., De Borger and Kerstens 1996; Geys 2006; Worthington 2000). For instance, Davis and Hayes (1993) estimate the efficiency of police departments in 141 Illinois municipalities based on a production function with SFA. Grossman et al. (1999) and Conroy and Arguea (2008) adopt a similar approach in estimating efficiencies in 49 U.S. metropolitan cities and Florida public elementary schools, respectively. To the best of our knowledge, however, prior studies on government efficiency do not include IT as a factor affecting government efficiency.

Several studies on IT business value adopt an SFA approach (e.g., Beccalli 2007; Chang and Gurbaxani 2013; Lee and Barua 1999; Menon et al. 2000; Mukhopadhyay and Mangal 1997). For example, using a production function framework with firm-level data, Lin and Shao (2000) and Shao and Lin (2001) find that IT investments are positively associated with technical production efficiency. Lin (2009) and Chen and Lin (2009) find a similar effect at a country level.

Although a number of prior IT value studies have adopted a production function model to examine the impact of IT in the for-profit business setting (e.g., Brynjofsson and Hitt 1996; Dewan and Kraemer 2000; Dewan and Min 1997), we believe that the cost function estimation with SFA is more suitable in our context for the reasons presented below.

First, it is difficult to find an appropriate aggregate measure to gauge the collective amount of outputs that state governments produce. While in the for-profit context, such measures as sales or value-added can account for aggregate outputs, the literature has not established a single measure that represents a variety of public services that state governments offer such as education, transportation, or public safety, each of which has widely different characteristics from the others. There may be various measures for the output of a single public service (e.g., a dropout rate for education), but to the best of our knowledge, we could not find a collective output measure for state governments that corresponds to sales or valueadded.

Figure 2. Stochastic Cost Frontier Model

Second, prior literature in economics has pointed out that outputs can be considered exogenous under the following conditions: (1) when production is regulated (Caves et al. 1981), (2) when production is price-inelastic (Ray 1982), and/or (3) when outputs cannot be stored (Kumbhakar and Lovell 2000). Under these circumstances, an efficient producer minimizes use of inputs, given the amount of outputs that need to be produced. This is the case in many service industries such as utilities (Schmidt and Lovell 1979), banking (Fenn et al. 2008; Pulley and Braunstein 1992), and transportation (Gillen et al. 1990). The production of public services by state governments shares almost all of the characteristics mentioned above.

With respect to the first characteristic, various federal and state laws, policies, and statutes regulate state government production. For example, the Social Security Act requires every state to provide a healthcare benefit program (Medicaid) to residents with limited incomes. In addition, state governments are in charge of maintaining the Interstate Highway System within each jurisdiction under the Federal-

Aid Highway Act of 1956. These are among many examples in which federal laws provide exogenous directives on state government production. For such outputs, it is outside the purview of state governments to manipulate the amount of outputs.<sup>6</sup> In terms of the second, as production is regulated, state governments should maintain a certain level of the services, even when input prices (labor or capital) or output prices (tax revenues) change. Even for outputs whose provision is under the jurisdiction of state governments such as education, adjusting a production level to such changes as declining tax revenues requires policy making by state agencies, deliberation among stakeholders and interests groups, and legislative processes, all of which usually take several months or years. Thus, it is important to treat state government production as price-inelastic, at least in the short run. Finally, with respect to the third characteristic, state government outputs such as education or public safety are consumed immediately upon production. As there is essentially no inventory for state outputs, the states need to maintain the stability of production, which again renders outputs exogenous.

While previous IT productivity studies in the government context adopt the production function model, we believe that, at least in the state government setting, the cost function approach is preferred. For example, Lehr and Lichtenberg (1998) examine IT productivity in U.S. federal agencies such as the Library of Congress, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), and the National Park Service. Garicano and Heaton (2010) study the impact of computer use on local police performance. In both studies, the production unit produces one single type of product (e.g., public safety from a police department or regulatory services from the FDA), which makes it more straightforward to use a production function. In contrast, state governments produce a broad range of diverse services, whose outputs are hardly quantifiable into a single measure.<sup>7</sup>

In sum, in contrast to the standard production function model that assumes outputs to be endogenous, in the current research context, it is more appropriate to treat the outputs as exogenous and to posit that state governments attempt to limit the use of inputs accordingly. Thus, we adopt the cost function approach in our empirical analysis.

## Estimation Methods

The empirical approach is based on Pang (2011). Our key interest in measurement is technical cost efficiency. Formally, Koopmans (1951) defines a producer as being technically efficient if and only if it cannot increase the production of an output without increasing any input or decreasing any other outputs. Figure 2 describes the measurement of technical efficiency. Each dot represents a state-year observation. In the first-stage estimation, SFA derives a cost frontier (line a-d-b), which presents the least amount of inputs that can produce the given amount of outputs (Kumbhakar and Lovell 2000). For example, points c and d represent the actual and the least amount of costs, respectively, that produce the output at e. Technical inefficiency (TIneff) of stateyear c is measured by the ratio of actual costs to the cost frontier (i.e., the ratio of line c-e to line d-e). By definition, TIneff is always greater than 1. Appendix A presents the details of the first-stage estimation.

In the second-stage of our estimation, we reverse TIneff to obtain technical efficiency by taking Eff = 2 – TIneff for ease of interpretation. Now we can estimate the impact of IT on technical efficiency by regressing $E f f$ on IT and the control variables (z). Consistent with prior research (Brynjolfsson 1993), we use a two-year lagged IT variable in our model.

$$
\begin{array}{r} E f f _ {k, t} = g \Big (\delta_ {0} + \delta_ {I T} I T _ {k, t - 2} + \sum \delta_ {z i} z _ {i, k, t} \Big) \\ + \upsilon_ {k} + \varsigma_ {t} + \xi_ {k, t} \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

To account for state-specific unobserved heterogeneity $( \upsilon _ { k } ) ,$ we estimate Eq. 1 with a fixed-effects regression with Driscoll and Kraay (2006) standard errors for spatial dependence, which account for interstate correlation in residuals. We adopt a spatial dependence error structure following the public economics literature, which identifies a spillover effect between jurisdictions in policy-making and implementation (Baicker 2005; Besley and Case 1995). We also include year dummies to account for time-variant unobservables (ς ) reflecting nationwide changes in social, economic, and political circumstances. We will examine whether the coefficient of IT, $\delta _ { I T } ,$ is positive and statistically significant for Hypothesis 1. For our moderation hypotheses (H2, H3, and H4), we will add three interaction terms $( \delta _ { m j } I T _ { k , t - 2 } \times m _ { j , k , t - 2 } )$ in Eq. 1 and confirm whether their coefficients are significant.

## Measures and Data Sources

In the first-stage estimation, we consider three input measures and four output measures. First, the total cost (C) is the sum of per capita annual operation expense (salaries and payment for supplies and materials) and capital depreciation (buildings and equipments). We obtained operation expense figures from State Government Finances by the U.S. Census Bureau (Table 1). Capital depreciation data are acquired from Comprehensive Annual Financial Reports posted on states’ Web sites. Capital depreciation is reported in 2001–2009, and some states do not post all nine-year reports on their Web sites, limiting our sample size to 428 state-years. Detailed information on data collection and measures is available in Appendix B. All dollar terms are adjusted for 2005 dollars with the price indexes provided by the Bureau of Economic Accounts.

For output measures, we choose the four most representative public services U.S. state governments supply: education, public welfare, transportation, and public safety. We selected four proxy variables for each area as shown in Table 1, following prior studies in public economics. We also chose three input price measures in the cost function estimation: capital, labor, and IT (Table 1). The summary statistics and the correlations among the variables in the first-stage estimation appear in Tables 2 and 3, respectively. To affirm our assumption that our output and input price measures are exogenous, we conducted Granger causality tests and did not find evidence showing that the outputs and the input prices are endogenous with respect to the costs. The measures used in the second-stage estimation (Eq. 1) are shown in Table 4. IT intensity, our key independent variable (Anderson et al. 2006; Bharadwaj et al. 1999), is measured by the per capita IT budget of central IT offices (IT1) and the ratio of IT budget to total general expenditure (IT2). These figures were obtained from the NASCIO Compendium of Digital Governments in States published in 2002 and 2005 (NASCIO 2002, 2005). This publication reports IT budgets (central IT offices and executive branches) in more than 40 states from 2001 to 2004. We use the IT budgets of central IT organizations for our IT intensity measures.<sup>8</sup> The IT budget figures are available for 145 state-years between 2001 and 2004, but combining them with the sample in the first-stage estimation leaves us 143 state-year observations. t-tests indicate that, with respect to population, GDP, and total expenditures, the states in our sample do not differ significantly from the others that are not in the sample (Table D2 in Appendix D). Table D3 in Appendix D shows which states are present in the second-stage estimation.

We measure three moderating factors for H2, H3, and H4 as follows. First, for the private-sector IT industries (H2), we measure per capita production (m ) in the IT industries.<sup>9</sup> The share of rural population (H3) is measured by comparing the percentage of population residing in rural areas to the total state population (m ). To measure the dividedness of a state government (H4), we measure a political distance between a governor and legislature (m ) as an absolute value of $( | 2 z _ { 5 } - z _ { 6 } | )$ , which measures the political distance between the two branches.<sup>10</sup>

The public economics literature provides us with economic, sociological, and political factors affecting technical efficiency in government administration. We control for these factors in our second stage estimation. The details for the control variables appear in Table 4 and Appendix B. In Tables 5 and 6, we present the summary statistics and the correlation table for the measures, respectively, in the secondstage estimation.

## Results

## The Baseline Estimation

Table 7 shows the result of the first-stage stochastic frontier estimation. The models both with and without interaction terms are significant according to Wald statistics, and a loglikelihood test rejects the hypothesis of $\sigma _ { u } = 0$ in the two estimations, indicating the possible presence of technical inefficiencies in the sample. Figure 3 shows the average technical efficiency of each state in 2001–2009 from the firststage estimation.

Table 8 presents the second-stage estimation for technical efficiency. The coefficient of population is negative, meaning that states with a smaller population demonstrate greater cost efficiency. We find that the coefficient of household income is negative and significant. This is consistent with the findings in the economics literature (e.g., Geys 2006) that a large base of tax revenues is a source of inefficiency. If a state government expects that it has a sufficient capacity to collect revenues, its administration tends to become inefficient. On the other hand, the coefficient of per capita GDP is positive and significant.<sup>11</sup> This suggests that in states with advanced service industries, such as Connecticut, Massachusetts, and New Jersey, all of which have both high per capita GDP and efficiency scores (Figure 3), voters demand efficient government administration with greater transparency (Davis and Hayes 1993; Geys 2006; Knack 2002). States in which Democrats have more control in legislatures demonstrate higher cost efficiency. As predicted by the fiscal illusion hypothesis, more federal grants are negatively associated with state cost efficiency.

When we include IT intensity variables into the estimation (columns 2 and 3, Table 8), we find that the coefficients of IT intensity (IT1 and IT2) are positive and significant at the 1 percent level, providing evidence of a significant relationship between IT investments and cost efficiency. From the coefficients and the estimated efficiency, we are able to calculate the average cost reduction from IT expenditure as follows: From the first-stage estimation, we first calculate the cost frontier (the minimum possible cost) of each of the 428 observations from the actual cost and estimated technical efficiency.<sup>12</sup> From this, we can obtain the average cost frontier for all observations, which becomes \$2,566. The coefficient of IT1 in column (2), Table 8, is 4.4 × 10<sup>-4</sup>, meaning that, all other things being equal, a \$1 increase in per capita IT budget is associated with a 0.044 percentage-point increase in cost efficiency, which amounts to \$2,566 × (4.4 × 10<sup>-4</sup>) = \$1.13 per capita. This illustrates a positive net contribution of IT investments to cost savings in states. The estimation with a random-effects regression produces a similar finding (see Table 8, columns 4 and 5).

One might raise a concern of causal relationships in our second-stage estimation. A positive association between IT intensity and technical efficiency may imply that moreefficient states invest more in IT than do less efficient states. To address this concern, we estimate Eq. 1 with the fixedeffects generalized method of moments (GMM) estimation with instrumental variables (IVs). We use the following IVs: average per capita IT budget in neighboring states that share geographic boundaries with the focal state, the number of local IT industry firms per capita, and the number of paid employees in local IT industry firms per capita. The details on the IVs are available in Appendix D.

We find that estimating Eq. 1 with the fixed-effects IV estimation does not produce a substantially different result, as shown in columns (6) and (7) of Table 8. The two validity tests (Table D4 and D5 in Appendix D) demonstrate that the IVs are valid. Hansen J tests show that the IVs are not correlated with the residuals from columns (6) and (7); that is, they are exogenous (Hansen 1982). While the coefficients of some of the IVs are insignificant in Table D4, Kleibergen-Paap LM tests show that the IVs are generally correlated with the IT variables (Kleibergen and Papp 2006). Hausman tests also do not support the hypothesis that the fixed-effects IV estimation systematically alters the coefficients of the variables, thus indicating lack of significance in the effects of potential endogeneity.

## Robustness Checks

To ensure the robustness of our findings, we conducted a series of sensitivity analyses with alternative measures and specifications. As reported above, in the second-stage estimation, we regress cost efficiency on IT intensity with a two-year behind lag $( I T _ { k , t - 2 } )$ . In Table D6, Appendix D, we reestimated Eq. 1 by varying the lag length from zero to four years. The coefficients of IT intensity are positive and significant with a lag longer than one year (columns 5–10). This supports a lagged effect of IT investments for performance improvement.

We have further checked for the robustness of our results by substituting output measures with alternative ones. For example, in the first stage, we estimated cost efficiency with the dropout rates in public secondary schools for education (Y ) instead of the higher education enrollment, with all other variables unchanged. We also substituted the prison inmate measure for public safety $( Y _ { 4 } )$ with the crime rate (the number of crime incidents per capita). We went through a similar process for public welfare (Y<sub>2</sub>) and transportation variables (Y ) and found that the relationship between IT intensity and cost efficiency is positive and significant. Also, alternatively, considering that technical efficiency may be affected by the quality of state government services, we added two quality measures—crime rate and dropout rate—as control variables in the second-stage estimation. We did not obtain a widely different result.

We detected several influential observations. First, we found that Hawaii demonstrates unusually low efficiency (lower than 4σ below mean). Even after removing this state from the first-stage estimation, we witnessed no change in the direction and statistical significance of IT intensity, and the overall technical inefficiency is still present in the remaining sample. Second, we noted that the IT spending figure in Delaware is unusually high (greater than 6σ above mean), leading us to exclude this state in the second-stage estimation (Table 8), even though our main results are robust to the inclusion of Delaware.

In contrast to our two-stage estimation approach, Kumbhakar et al. (1991) and Fenn et al. (2008) use a one-stage estimation approach, in which u in Eq. A3 (Appendix A) is modeled as a function of exogenous factors (z and IT). One-stage maximum likelihood estimation generates the coefficients in both Eq. 1 and A1. Wang and Schmidt (2002) argue for this onestage estimation because of a potential bias in the two-stage approach. However, in our research setting, the two-stage estimation is advocated. The one-stage approach, in which the IT intensity measure is incorporated in the first-stage estimation, reduces the number of available observations to 143, while the two-stage estimation can utilize all 428 available observations. Thus, from the two-stage approach, we are able to obtain more consistent estimates for state cost efficiency. For a comparison, we did estimate the model with the one-stage approach as shown in Table D7 and D8, Appendix D, showing that the coefficient of IT intensity is negative and significant, which is consistent with our baseline estimation.<sup>13</sup>

The cost function we employ is a total cost function. As a robustness check, we estimate a variable cost function model in which a producer minimizes variable costs given the size of fixed capital (Caves et al. 1981). This does not produce a substantially different result. Farsi et al. (2003) discuss the need to account for state-specific unobserved variations in the first-stage frontier estimation. Even though we address this to some extent through state-level fixed-effects in the second stage (Table 8), we also reestimate the cost function with a fixed-effects frontier model proposed by Greene (2005) (Table D9, Appendix D). The subsequent second-stage estimation shows that the coefficients of IT intensity are positive and significant at the 10 percent level. We also estimate state government efficiency with a deterministic data envelopment analysis (DEA) (Banker et al. 1984, Appendix C). Table D10 shows that using the DEA approach instead of SFA does not change the main result drastically.

Finally, in Table 8, IT intensity accounts for only the budget figures of central IT organizations managed by state CIOs in 143 state-year observations. We choose to present this as the baseline result because the NASCIO Compendium reports executive branch IT budgets only for 29 states. We believe, however, that our use of the central IT function budget does not cast doubt on our findings. First, the executive branch IT budget is highly correlated with the central IT budget (0.51). Second, we believe that our IT intensity measure reflects the extent to which state governments perceive IT as strategic resources for statewide performance improvement, which we measure. A state whose central IT function spends more in IT than other states is likely to invest in and deploy IT in a coordinated manner with an enterprise perspective (Tanriverdi 2006), compared to states whose executive branches make uncoordinated IT spending for silo systems. When we use IT intensity that includes IT budgets of both central IT functions and executive branches (N = 76, Table D11, Appendix D) as a robustness check, the relationship between IT and cost efficiency is still positive and statistically significant.

So far, we have presented a range of sensitivity analyses with various substitute measures for outputs and alternative models such as a variable cost function and a fixed-effects frontier model. These analyses consistently demonstrate a positive relationship between IT intensity and technical efficiency.

The contextual effects we find illustrate (1) the complementarity between the public and the private sectors with respect to IT investments, (2) a stronger influence of IT in a digitally challenging environment, and (3) the importance of political controls on IT management. Our research suggests that for effective utilization of public sector IT resources, government officials need to proactively attract capable IT talent and providers in the private sector. Our findings offer meaningful implications for IT governance in the private-sector firms as well. In particular, the finding on the moderating effect of government dividedness supports the proposition that boards of directors need to be involved in IT management for effective utilization and strategic alignment of IT resources; they play a principal role on behalf of shareholders, just as elected representatives do for citizens (Nolan and McFarlen 2005).

## Discussion and Conclusion

In this study, we investigate the impact of IT investments in U.S. state governments on state administrative efficiency. We find that more IT spending is associated with greater cost efficiency in U.S. states. The baseline estimation and a range of the sensitivity analyses offer strong support for our main hypothesis. Our analysis reveals that, on average, every \$1 additional investment is associated with approximately \$1.13 in cost efficiency benefits. We also find that this association increases with the size of the local IT sector, the share of rural population, and the state government’s political dividedness.

This study provides meaningful policy implications for elected officials and managers in the public sector. Due to budget shortfalls persistent in many state and local governments across the U.S. in recent years, IT budgets have become an early target for cuts. For instance, a NASCIO report shows that 64 percent of the state CIOs expected to face a reduction in their IT budgets from 2011 to 2013 (NASCIO 2010). Our research suggests that cutting IT investments is not a wise move; instead, governments need to strategically leverage IT as a tool to overcome the fiscal crises. IT managers in governments can use our findings to justify and convince citizens and their elected lawmakers, who authorize IT funding, that there are sufficient returns to IT.

Integrating prior research from IS, political sciences, and public economics, we contribute to the IS literature by expanding the boundary of IT value research to the public sector. Not only does the public sector continue to grow, but its significance over the entire economy cannot be underestimated. Furthermore, as in the private sector, the size of IT spending in public sector organizations is substantial as well. Hence, it is imperative for policy makers to understand and articulate how to justify the enormous amount of IT expenditures that governments make every year. However, to the best of our knowledge, few quantitative empirical studies in IS have addressed this issue in a scientific and rigorous manner. We expect that this study facilitates further accumulation of scientific knowledge on public-sector IT value, which will be instrumental for IS researchers to inform public-sector practitioners.

Undoubtedly, the present study is not without limitations. Despite its many advantages and appropriateness for this research context, stochastic frontier analysis is sometimes prone to specification errors (Lovell 1993). It may be the case that government outputs are endogenously determined by inputs. However, our primary result still stands with DEA (Appendix C), which is relatively free from this limitation (Gong and Sickles 1992). In addition, while we control for as many key factors as possible and include state and year fixedeffects in our estimation, there is potentially unobserved heterogeneity, which could lead to bias due to omitted variables or simultaneity. Specifically, one might raise a concern as to whether IT investments are strategic choices driven by efficiency concerns in state governments. We attempted to control for this endogeneity issue with IVs (Table 8, columns 6 and 7), but given that our dataset does not provide a complete picture of IT spending in state governments, we were not able to fully rule out this possibility.

Since this is one of the few studies on IT value in the public sector, there are several ways to extend this study. First, our research can be replicated in other not-for-profit contexts such as municipal or federal governments or other public organizations. Second, we mainly focus on whether IT makes government administration more efficient, because this effect can be measured quantitatively in a more straightforward manner. However, it would also be of significance to see whether IT has more profound, transformative impacts on public organizations. For example, we expect that IT transforms governments from bureaucratic, silo organizations to leaner, more transparent, and agile ones with tighter coordination between agencies and rapid, timely responses to public needs (Sunstein 2013). Also interesting would be how IT and the Internet have changed the interrelationship among governments, citizens, and private-sector organizations. Future research can explore this role of IT in the public sector.

Finally, it would be promising to study the impact of IT investments on the quality of public services. Researchers can explore whether enhanced efficiency of public services achieved through IT investments actually leads to a higher quality of services, as reflected in quantitative measures or perceived by the public. In this line of research, one could adopt the production function model for an empirical basis. For example, one could examine whether a more extensive use of crime-related information resources by law enforcement agencies with data mining and warehouse techniques leads to a lower crime rate. Administrative processes in public welfare programs streamlined by IT may improve the satisfaction of welfare beneficiaries. In sum, we believe that this study can open a research stream in understanding the role and performance implications of IT in the public sector organizations.

## Appendix A

## Technical Details of Stochastic Frontier Estimation

In the first stage, we measure cost efficiency based on a multiproduct translog cost function with n outputs and m input prices, which is given by

$$
\begin{array}{l} \ln C _ {k, t} = \alpha_ {0} + \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \alpha_ {i} \ln Y _ {i, k, t} + \frac {1}{2} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} \alpha_ {i j} \ln Y _ {i, k, t} \ln Y _ {j, k, t} + \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {m} \beta_ {i} \ln w _ {i, k, t} \\ + \frac {1}{2} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {m} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {m} \beta_ {i j} \ln w _ {i, k, t} \ln w _ {j, k, t} + \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {m} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} \gamma_ {i j} \ln w _ {i, k, t} \ln Y _ {j, k, t} + \varepsilon_ {k, t} \end{array}\tag{A1}
$$

where k and t are subscripts for state and year, respectively. $C _ { k , t }$ is the total cost of state k at year $t , Y _ { i , k , t }$ indicate the amount of outputs, and $w _ { i , k , t }$ are the input prices. The interaction terms in Eq. A1 are used for estimating economies of scale or input price elasticity, which are outside the scope of our research. In estimation, the constraints for homogeneity with a degree of one in price have to be imposed (Caves et al. 1981; Ray 1982).

$$
\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {m} \beta_ {i} = 1, \quad \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {m} \beta_ {i j} \ln w _ {j} = 0, \text {   and   } \quad \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} \gamma_ {i j} \ln Y _ {j} = 0 \quad \text {   for   } i = 1, 2, \dots , n\tag{A2}
$$

These constraints ensure that when all input prices w are multiplied by x, the total cost C is multiplied by x as well, making the cost function homogenous with a degree of one.

In SFA, a frontier is considered to be stochastic, based on a rationale that even maximum production levels may be influenced by various unobserved factors, random shocks, or statistical noise. A model suggested by Aigner et al. (1977) and Meeusen and van den Broeck (1977) assumes that a residual $\varepsilon _ { k , t }$ in Eq. A1 consists of two parts.

$$
\varepsilon_ {k, t} = v _ {k, t} + u _ {k, t}\tag{A3}
$$

Here, v represents a random error and is assumed to follow a normal distribution of $\boldsymbol { \nu } _ { k , t }$ $\mathrm { \Delta } N ( 0 , \sigma _ { \nu } ^ { 2 } )$ ). u $\boldsymbol { u } _ { k , t }$ refers to a technical inefficiency factor, which in nature is greater than or equal to zero. Here, it is assumed to follow an exponential distribution. Thus, $u _ { k , t }$ is always positive. Aigner et al. explain that $\nu _ { k , t }$ represents random factors that influence production but are outside of a firm’s control. Thus, $\nu _ { k , t }$ is thought to be part of the cost frontier. In contrast, $u _ { k , t }$ is viewed as being under the firm’s control and originating from such causes as mismanagement or organizationa slacks. The parameters in Eq. A1 along with the standard deviation of the two residual terms $( \nu _ { k , i }$ and $u _ { k , t } )$ can be estimated using maximum likelihood estimation, and the details are presented in Aigner et al.

Based on the estimated parameters (the coefficients of Eq. A1 and $\sigma _ { \nu }$ and $\sigma _ { u } )$ , we can obtain an unbiased estimate of the inefficiency of each observation using the approach outlined in Jondrow et al. (1982). They propose the following unbiased estimator for $u _ { k , t } .$

$$
E \left(u _ {k, t} \mid \varepsilon_ {k, t}\right) = \mu_ {*} + \sigma_ {*} \left[ \frac {\phi \left(- \mu_ {*} / \sigma_ {*}\right)}{1 - \Phi \left(- \mu_ {*} / \sigma_ {*}\right)} \right]\tag{A4}
$$

where $\mu _ { * } = \frac { \sigma _ { u } ^ { 2 } \varepsilon } { \sigma _ { u } ^ { 2 } + \sigma _ { \nu } ^ { 2 } } \quad , \quad \sigma _ { * } = \sqrt { \frac { \sigma _ { u } ^ { 2 } \sigma _ { \nu } ^ { 2 } } { \sigma _ { u } ^ { 2 } + \sigma _ { \nu } ^ { 2 } } }$ , and $\Phi ( \cdot )$ and $\phi ( \cdot )$ refer to the cumulative and probability density function of a standard

normal distribution, respectively. However, for ease of interpretation, we are more interested in estimating technical cost inefficiency (the ratio of actual cost to cost in the frontier), rather than $\boldsymbol { u } _ { k , t }$ itself. Since the cost function in Eq. A1 is expressed in logarithm of cost, exp ${ { u } _ { k , t } }$ represents the technical inefficiency that we are seeking to measure. Battese and Coelli (1988) suggest an estimator for the technical inefficiency $T I n e f f _ { k , i }$ of state k at year t as follows:

$$
T I n e f f _ {k, t} = E \left(\exp \left\{u _ {k, t} \right\} \mid \varepsilon_ {k, t}\right) = \left[ \frac {1 - \Phi \left(\sigma_ {*} - \mu_ {*} / \sigma_ {*}\right)}{1 - \Phi \left(- \mu_ {*} / \sigma_ {*}\right)} \right] \exp \left\{- \mu_ {*} + \frac {1}{2} \sigma_ {*} ^ {2} \right\}\tag{A5}
$$

In the second-stage of our estimation, we reverse $T I n e f f _ { k , i }$ <sub>t</sub> to obtain technical efficiency by taking $\mathit { E F F } _ { k , t } { = } 2 - T I n e f f _ { k } ,$ <sub>t</sub> for ease of interpretation.

For output measures, we choose the four public services: education, public welfare, transportation, and public safety. Although state governments may provide a wide range of public goods and services, from an estimation perspective, it may not be feasible for us to include all of these variables in our cost function model. This is because adding more output variables $( Y _ { i } )$ to Eq. A1 will lead to more regressors and interaction terms, thus decreasing the degrees of freedom. In addition, beyond a threshold, we may find collinearity in state output variables, posing other challenges in the estimation. Thus, we are faced with a tradeoff between selecting output variables to comprehensively capture state government production and balancing the number of variables to manage feasibility in estimations. We decided to include the four most representative state government outputs: education, public welfare, transportation, and public safety. According to 2008 Government Employment and Payroll statistics from the U.S. Census Bureau, these four areas comprise 68.06 percent of the total state government personnel

Note that our output measures—higher education $( Y _ { 1 } )$ , Medicaid $( Y _ { 2 } )$ , highway (Y<sub>3</sub>), and correction $( Y _ { 4 } )$ —capture only public services provided solely by state governments, not by federal and local governments. Highway (Y ) and correction $( Y _ { 4 } )$ only account for the facilities operated by state governments. Also, historically and constitutionally, education is not under the purview of the U.S. federal government (http://www.cato-at-liberty.org/education-and-the-constitution/), and local governments do not operate higher educational institutions (Kane et al. 2003). In addition, the Social Security Act of 1965, Title XIX stipulates that Medicaid is administrated by the state governments.

## Appendix B

## Description of Measures

## Total Cost (C) – Current Operation Expense

Operation expense is defined as “direct expenditure for compensation of own officers and employees and for supplies, materials and contractual services” (U.S. Census Bureau 2006, p. 126). From State Government Finance published by the U.S. Census Bureau, we took current operation expense, divided it by the annual population estimate, and adjusted it for 2005 dollar with the price index for GDP provided by the Bureau of Economic Accounts.

## Total Cost (C) – Capital Depreciation

We referred to the “Notes to Financial Statements” section in states’ comprehensive annual financial reports to obtain annual capital depreciation. Among several capital asset categories, only buildings and equipment and related asset categories such as fixtures or vehicles are considered, because states have discretion in reporting the depreciation of other types of capital assets. For example, some states categorize infrastructure as depreciable assets, while others consider it non-depreciable. We also included the asset of primary governments and excluded that of discretely presented component units because many states do not report the capital figure of such units. Per capita capital depreciation was calculated and adjusted for 2005 dollars

## Labor Price (w<sub>1</sub>)

The State Government Employment & Payroll data published by the U.S. Census Bureau contains the monthly payroll for full-time and part time staff employed by state governments. We took the sum of full-time and part-time payroll and divided it by the number of full-time equivalent employees.

## Capital Price (w<sub>2</sub>)

From the State Government Finances, “interest on general debt” was divided by mean debt level (the average of “debt at end of fiscal year” at the same year and that of the previous fiscal year).

## IT Price (w<sub>3</sub>)

It was obtained by the Producer Price Index (PPI) from Bureau of Labor Statistics. Specifically, we use PPI in the category of Computer and Electronic Product Manufacturing.

## Education (Y<sub>1</sub>)

From the State Higher Education Finance Survey, the number of students enrolled in public post-secondary educational institutions was divided by the population estimate.

## Public Welfare $( \pmb { Y } _ { 2 } )$

From Medicaid Summary Table provided by the Center for Medicare & Medicaid Services, the enrollee population was divided by population estimate.

## Transportation $( \pmb { Y } _ { 3 } )$

From Highway Statistics published by the Federal Highway Administration, we took the length (miles) of rural and urban roads owned and maintained by state highway agencies and divided it by the population estimate.

## Public Safety $( Y _ { 4 } )$

From National Prisoner Statistics provided by the Bureau of Justice Statistics, we divided the total number of inmates in state correctional facilities by the population estimate.

## IT Intensity (IT1 and IT2)

The 2002 NASCIO Compendium of Digital Governments in States provides the actual IT budget figures in 2001 and 2002. The 2004-05 Compendium covers the actual budgets in 2003 and 2004. IT1 was calculated by dividing actual IT budget by the population estimate. IT2 was derived by dividing the IT budget by “general expenditures” from State Government Finances.

## GDP and Income $( z _ { 2 }$ and $\pmb { z } _ { 3 } )$

The public economics literature argues that economic and fiscal conditions of a government affect its efficiency (Geys 2006). For instance, De Borger and Kerstens (1996) and Grossman et al. (1999) predict that higher income can be a greater tax revenue source, opening a room for inefficiency in administration. We thus include median household income (z ) and per capita state $\mathrm { G D P } \left( z _ { 3 } \right)$ as control variables in the second stage estimation (Eq. 1).

## Federal Grant $( z _ { \phi } )$

The fiscal illusion hypothesis (Geys 2006; Grossman et al. 1999) suggests that a large influx of external revenues from a higher level of governments is a source of inefficiency. Hence, we also control for per capita federal government grants (z ) to each state government in Eq 1. From State Government Finances, “intergovernmental revenue from federal government” was divided by the population estimate.

## Governor’s Political Affiliation $( z _ { 5 } )$ and Party Control of Legislatures $( z _ { \theta } )$

We include Garand’s (1988) political indicators—governor’s party affiliation (z ) and party control of legislature $( z _ { 6 } ) \AA$ —because they represent political environments that affect state government efficiency. Governor (z ) is equal to 1 if the governor is Republican and 0 otherwise. For the legislature $( z _ { 6 } ) ,$ we calculated the ratio of Republican state representatives in state house and Republican state senator in senate, respectively, and took the sum of two. For Nebraska, which has a unicameral legislature, we multiplied the percentage of Republican by two.

## Tax Complexity (z<sub>7</sub>)

The fiscal illusion hypothesis also predicts that the more complex a state tax system, the more inefficient the state (Garand 1988). Tax complexity was measured by a Herfindahl index of seven tax categories: personal income tax, corporate income tax, property tax, sales tax, license tax, severance tax on natural resources, and other taxes. It is given by $\sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { 7 } t _ { i } ^ { 2 }$ , where $: t _ { i }$ is the ratio of tax revenues in Category i to total tax revenues. The more complex a tax system, the smaller this measure becomes.

## Rural Population $( m _ { 2 } )$

The U.S. Census Bureau defines an urban area as a densely settled core of census tracks or blocks that meet minimum population density requirements (1,000 per square mile in most cases). The rest of the areas are designated as rural. The percentage of urban population to the total population in all U.S. states is reported in the Decennial Census only in 2000 and 2010. Suppose, for example, the urban population share as a percentage in 2000 and 2010 are $u _ { 2 0 0 0 }$ and $u _ { 2 0 1 0 } ,$ respectively. We estimate the percentage of rural population in year t between 2000 and 2010 by $1 0 0 - \left( u _ { 2 0 0 0 } + { \frac { \left( t - 2 0 0 0 \right) \left( u _ { 2 0 0 0 } - u _ { 2 0 0 0 } \right) } { 1 0 } } \right)$ . For example, the urban population percentage in Indiana in 2000 and 2010 is reported to be 70.8 percent and 72.4 percent, respectively. We calculate that the urban population share in 2002 is $7 0 . 8 + { \frac { { \big ( } 2 0 0 2 - 2 0 0 0 { \big ) } { \big ( } 7 2 . 4 - 7 0 . 8 { \big ) } } { 1 0 } } = 7 1 . 1 2 \%$ and the rural population share is $1 0 0 - 7 1 . 1 2 = 2 2 . 8 8 \%$

## Divided Government $( m _ { 3 } )$

It was calculated by $| 2 z _ { 5 } - z _ { 6 } | .$ . We multiplied z by 2 because $z _ { 6 }$ represents the sum of the ratio of Republican members in the House of Representatives and the Senate. This measure takes a value between 0 and 2, and the larger this value, the more divided the government $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ the more lawmakers are in the opposition party). If this value is equal to 0, all the representatives are in the same party with the governor. If it is equal to 2, the entire body of elected lawmakers are in the opposition party.

## Estimation with Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA)

The productivity measurement literature also widely uses DEA. Unlike SFA, a parametric model with econometric estimation, DEA assumes that the cost frontier is deterministic. Instead of imposing a functional form as in Eq. A1, it finds a cost frontier that envelops observations by solving a linear programming problem for each observation. The public sector efficiency studies also use this DEA approach. For example, Bessent et al. (1982) and Ruggiero (1996) introduce an application of DEA in analyzing productivity of public education. Ganley and Cubbin (1996) and Cook et al. (1994) illustrate use of DEA in efficiency measurement of correctional facilities and highway maintenance, respectively.

As a robustness check, we measure state government efficiency with the input-oriented variable return-to-scale DEA model put forth by Banke et al. (1984; BCC model) to check whether IT intensity is still positively associated with DEA efficiency. We adopt an input-oriented mode following our assumption that state governments minimize the use of inputs given the amount of outputs to produce. As with SFA, we obtain technical efficiencies (DEff) for each state government observation using DEA in the first stage and regress them on IT intensity and control variables in the second-stage estimation.

$$
D E f f _ {k, t} = g \left(\delta_ {0} + \delta_ {I T} I T _ {k, t - 2} + \sum \delta_ {z i} z _ {i, k, t}\right) + v _ {k} + \varsigma_ {t} + \xi_ {k, t}\tag{A6}
$$

Again, we expect the coefficient of IT intensity to be positive and significant. Details on the linear programming model are available in Banker et al. (1984).

We use the same four output variables in Table 1. We use two input variables in our DEA analysis: per capita capital depreciation and operation expenses. The correlation in efficiency measures between SFA and DEA is 0.5039. Table D10 in Appendix D demonstrates that the coefficients of IT intensity are positive and significant. We also measure state government efficiencies with a constant return-to-scale model (Charnes et al. 1978) and with an output-oriented BCC model and regress them on IT intensity and control variables (Eq. A6). Using these alternative DEA models does not change our findings substantially.

We use the following instrumental variables (IVs): average per capita IT budget in states that share geographic boundaries with the focal state (IT-Neighbor), the number of IT industry firms per capita (IT-Firm), and the number of paid employees in IT industry firms per capita (IT-Employee). The IT industries include computer and electronic product manufacturing, computer systems design and related services, and information and data processing services. The correlation of IT1 (per capita IT budget) with IT-Neighbor, IT-Firm, and IT-Employ is 0.2469, -0.2044, and -0.2564, respectively.

We choose the average IT budget in neighboring states as an instrument, since the public economics literature argues that policy making in such areas as tax systems, welfare provision, and other administrative matters can be affected by decisions made by neighboring jurisdictions (e.g., Baicker 2005; Case et al. 1993). For example, Figlio et al. (1999) show that welfare policies in one state are significantly correlated with those in neighboring states, a phenomenon called welfare competition. It may be the case with IT investments. We do not expect, however, that IT spending in neighboring states will affect overall cost efficiency of the focal state.

The other instrumental variables—the number of IT firms (IT Firm) and employment (IT Employ) in local IT sectors—represent competitiveness of local IT industries. The majority of U.S. states enact state laws or policies that give preferential treatment to in-state vendors over nonlocal providers in procurement biddings.<sup>1</sup> For example, Ohio laws require an Ohio bidder for procurement of supplies and services, including IT, to receive a 5 percent preference. An Ohio-based vendor with the lowest bid must be selected for any service to an Ohio state agency, as long as its price is less than or equal to 105 percent of the lowest non-Ohio bids. Therefore, as the number of IT-providing firms competing for government IT procurement in one state increases (i.e., the IT industries are more competitive), the state is able to pay more competitive prices for its IT supplies and services, decreasing the state’s dollar amount in IT investment. Such preferential treatment policies limit IT procurement across state boundaries, such that states that have small, noncompetitive IT industries are still required to give priority to local suppliers in IT procurement. This may be the case with IT employment. If there is a greater supply of IT workers available in the local IT job market, a state would be able to pay more competitive salaries for its IT hires, thus reducing IT spending as well. After exhaustively searching through the extant case examples and literature, we could not find any plausible evidence that IT-Firm and IT-Employ have any direct influence on state cost efficiency.
