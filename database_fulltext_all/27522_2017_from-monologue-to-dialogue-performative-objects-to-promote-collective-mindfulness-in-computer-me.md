# FROM MONOLOGUE TO DIALOGUE: PERFORMATIVE OBJECTS TO PROMOTE COLLECTIVE MINDFULNESS IN COMPUTER-MEDIATED TEAM DISCUSSIONS<sup>1</sup>

Aaron M. Curtis Computer Information Sciences Department, Brigham Young University–Hawaii, 55-220 Kulanui Street, Box 1919, Laie, HI 96763 U.S.A. {aaron.curtis@byuh.edu}

Alan R. Dennis Operations and Decision Technologies Department, Kelley School of Business, Indiana University, Bloomington, IN 47405 U.S.A. {ardennis@indiana.edu}

Kelly O. McNamara Management and Information Systems Department, College of Business, Mississippi State University, Mississippi State, MS 39762 U.S.A. {kmac@business.msstate.edu}

Organizations often build virtual teams to draw information from different parts of the organization to enable better decisions. However, communication patterns among virtual team members often resemble multiple monologues (in which team members broadcast what they know) rather than dialogues (in which team members mindfully share information they have and consider information from others). The result is that team members often fail to integrate the new information they receive from other team members into their mental models, and decision quality suffers. Collective mindfulness is a team-level process in which team members heedfully contribute to the team discussion, align their own actions with the actions of others, and develop shared mental models. Collective mindfulness is more than a sum of individual mindfulness in much the same way that organizational learning is more than the sum of the individual learning of its members. We compared teams using a traditional synchronous text chat tool to teams using a combination of tools designed to promote collective mindfulness (chat plus a whiteboard). The results suggest that teams using chat plus the whiteboard had increased collective mindfulness, and that this increased collective mindfulness led to improved decision quality. Not all teams were effective in using the whiteboard, indicating it was not the provision of the whiteboard that mattered, but rather how teams appropriated it. We identified four appropriations, the combination of which led to increased collective mindfulness and decision quality: information aggregation, discussion of information distribution, discussion of decision criteria, and voting. If any one of the appropriations was missing, collective mindfulness and decision quality suffered.

Keywords: Collaboration technology, team, group, collective mindfulness, whiteboard, information exchange, information integration, appropriation

## Introduction

Organizations often build virtual teams to draw information from different parts of the organization to enable better decisions. Virtual teams use a variety of information and communication technologies (ICT) such as cell phones, email, wikis, blogs, text messaging, web conferencing, and special purpose collaboration tools to enable information exchange among team members (Dennis et al. 2008). Even though the diversity and novelty of ideas increases when more information is exchanged, members often have difficulty developing shared mental models (Miranda and Saunders 2003) and frequently fail to use exchanged information to improve decisions (Brodbeck et al. 2007; Dennis 1996a; Heninger et al. 2006).

In other words, although ICT enable team members to exchange more information, team members frequently engage in a series of monologues—broadcasting their own information—rather than engaging in a thoughtful dialogue with other participants (Dennis and Valacich 1994). The hallmark of good dialogue is collective mindfulness, a pattern of behaviors that shows “heedful interrelating” (Weick and Roberts 1993, p. 361) and is mindful, attentive, deliberate, and intelligent (Butler and Gray 2006). Collective mindfulness is a teamlevel process in which members mutually contribute to the discussion, align their actions with those of other team members, and form shared mental models (Weick and Roberts 1993). Past research suggests that, in general, teams using text-based ICT exhibit little collective mindfulness, in that team members are often not aware of the contributions of their colleagues (Brodbeck et al. 2007; Dennis 1996a; Heninger et al. 2006). We argue that one reason for the limited effectiveness of text-based ICT tools for team decision making is that the design of these tools promotes less mindful behaviors.

It has long been argued that it is important to fit the technology to the needs of the task and the team (Goodhue and Thompson 1995; Zigurs and Buckland 1998), and that outcomes depend on this fit and how the team appropriates the technology (Dennis et al. 2001). Teams can appropriate technologies as they choose, but some technologies have dominant appropriation paths that induce teams to appropriate them in predictable ways (Dennis and Reinicke 2004). So the key question is, can we design ICT to deliberately influence how teams appropriate them to increase collective mindfulness?

In this paper, we use the concepts of sociomateriality (Cecez-Kecmanovic, Galliers et al. 2014; Leonardi 2013; Mutch 2013; Orlikowski and Scott 2008) and of the design of performative objects from the science of design (Niedderer 2007) to argue that ICT tools can be intentionally designed to induce greater collective mindfulness (see Butler and Gray

2006). We begin with collective mindfulness and examine how team behaviors exhibit more or less collective mindfulness. We argue that increased collective mindfulness at the team level can lead to increased team decision quality. Next we introduce the concepts of sociomateriality and performative objects to examine how they can enable and constrain behavior to promote more collective mindfulness. We then describe an experiment comparing the collective mindfulness and decision quality of teams using two different forms of ICT, traditional synchronous text chat, and a combination of tools designed to increase collective mindfulness (chat plus a whiteboard). Our study contributes to theory (by showing that collective mindfulness is a useful construct to understand ICT use), to design research (by demonstrating that some ICT lead to more collective mindfulness), to future empirical research (by presenting a behavioral measure of collective mindfulness), and to practice (by offering guidelines to improve collective mindfulness).

## Theoretical Background

The study of ICT use has a rich history spanning several decades (for reviews, see Dennis et al. 2001; Fjermestdad and Hiltz 1998/1999; Nunamaker et al. 1996/1997; Pervan 1998). This work has investigated a variety of interventions based on social processes (Dennis et al. 1997/1998) or technology characteristics (Dennis 1996a, 1996b) providing a variety of recommendations to improve communication (Dennis et al. 2008), whether it is conducted in text-based media such as e-mail or text chat, or richer media such as desktop videoconferencing.

Our study focuses on team discussions using text-based ICT because such media are commonly used and their problems are well-known (Heninger et al. 2006). Teams using textbased ICT often engage in multiple monologues in which participants broadcast information with little attention to others (Dennis and Valacich 1994) rather than mindful dialogues in which they heedfully work together (Weick and Roberts 1993). When we think of monologue, we may picture a lone actor revealing his thoughts as Hamlet does in his epic “To be or not to be.” In more concrete terms, a monologue is communication where messages are not based on feedback from recipients. Team communication often devolves into multiple monologues (Dennis and Valacich 1994), with members contributing information, but not considering the information from others. As a result, information is overlooked, and performance suffers (Dennis 1996a, 1996b).

In contrast, a dialogue is a coordinated interchange among two or more people. Dialogue is like a tapestry, weaving together threads of various colors to produce a unified picture. Dialogue is a mode of communication in which messages are composed by multiple individuals and incorporate feedback from others (Fay et al. 2000). Dialogue results in a more thorough consideration of information leading to better team performance (Hilmer and Dennis 2001).

The production of multiple monologues or a shared dialogue often hinges on the extent to which team members are mindful of others’ contributions. We use the construct of collective mindfulness as a theoretical lens to understand the team-level activities whereby team members engage in monologue or dialogue, and do or do not interact in ways that are heedful of others.

## Collective Mindfulness

Collective mindfulness can be examined at the organization level or at the team level (Carlo et al. 2012; Levinthal and Rerup 2006; Pentland 1999; Weick and Roberts 1993). We examine collective mindfulness at the team level, as members engage in text-based discussions focused on making a decision. In this context, collective mindfulness is a pattern of activity characterized by attention to and heedful interrelating with others’ comments. Individual mindfulness is seeing what is present and being open to new information (Langer and Moldoveanu 2000; Sternberg 2000). Collective mindfulness is not the sum of individual mindfulness; it is mindfulness at the unit level. “Collective mindfulness is to individual mindfulness as organizational learning is to individual learning: a theoretical elaboration of cognitive concepts at the level of an organizational entity” (Butler and Gray 2006, p. 215).

A team with some individuals behaving mindfully or one with some routine mindless behaviors may or may not be collectively mindful. Collective mindfulness is a result of individual behaviors, but not a summation of individual behaviors. It is “a totality with intricately connected and interdependent components” (Carlo et al. 2012, p. 1102). Collective mindfulness is a set of behaviors by which team members heedfully share information with the team, consider novel information from others, and develop a shared mental model of the situation.

Collective mindfulness is manifest as team members engage in three interrelated behaviors: contributing, aligning, and representing (Pentland 1999; Ryle 1949). Contributing involves performing individual actions. Aligning involves connecting those individual actions with the related activities of other team members. Representing involves identifying relationships among the actions of other team members to develop a shared mental model of the task and team. In the following sections, we describe each of these behaviors in more detail. Examining any one behavior in isolation can be misleading; it is the totality of action not the sum of its individual aspects that comprise collective mindfulness (Carlo et al. 2012). There is a tension among behaviors (Carlo et al. 2012); for example, contributing is important to mindfulness, but contributing too much or too little can bring one’s actions out of alignment (Dennis 1996a, 1996b).

Contributing Individual Actions. Performing individual actions within a social context is called contributing (Weick and Roberts 1993). Through their individual actions, team members provide cues that allow the team to be defined through identification of the relationships among their behaviors and the actions of others. As team member contributions generate the pattern of interaction (Cooren 2004), other individuals, both inside and outside the team, are able to define the team through its coordinated interrelations (Arrow et al. 2000).

The contributions of team members are manifest through varying activities depending on the type of team. Contributions in a team sport would involve the enactment of specialized roles (Brockmann and Anthony 1998). Members of bands contribute to a unified sound by collectively performing individual music (Kamoche et al. 2003). Within a team discussion, member contributions in communicating with others create the emergent conversation (Cooren 2006).

Aligning the Individual Activities of Team Members. Individual activity can vary in the degree to which it is aligned with others’ activities (Asch 1952). When new activities are aligned with past activities, they are “converging with, assisting, or supplementing” those activities (Weick and Roberts 1993, p. 365). Alignment occurs as an individual’s contribution responds to and builds on previous activities (Pickering and Garrod 2004).

The aligning of team member behavior is an empathetic activity—one that is carried out as individuals identify with others. As individual activity increases and members become more capable of identifying the relationships among member behaviors, the social identity of the team develops and matures. This social identity is reflected in the degree to which individuals see themselves as similar to an in-team and distinct from others outside the team (Ashforth and Mael 1989). When individuals perceive team members as being similar to themselves, they have an increased ability to empathize with them. This empathy allows them to internally simulate or experience what they perceive others to be experiencing (i.e., they can feel the emotions and physical sensations they believe others are feeling; Oberman and Ramachandran 2007).

This empathetic response results in alignment of cognitive activities and behaviors during team interaction. During discussions, participants increasingly mirror each other’s word choice, syntax, grammatical patterns, and themes (Branigan et al. 2000). Aligning behaviors have been observed in many contexts, including social media sites for photo sharing (Zheng and Wei 2013) in which members of dyads were more likely to share similar photos.

The aligning of behaviors in the absence of ongoing contributing and representing activities does not by itself constitute collective mindfulness. Collective mindfulness is an interrelating behavior that can be described as resilient rather than routine (Butler and Gray 2006). A choral chant could be characterized as being made up of highly aligned behaviors in that all members are saying the same words with identical message structures. However, memorized chants could be performed in a mindless routine. For team behavior to be characterized as collective mindfulness, it must be a dynamic system that can select the proper pattern of activity to incorporate and respond to new inputs (Levinthal and Rerup 2006).

Representing Within-Team Relationships. Individuals need to perceive that a team exists and develop a mental model of the team and the task—that is, they must identify and make sense of the relationships among the actions of others as a team activity toward performing the task. The identification of these relationships (between and among members and the task) and the development of a shared mental model is described as representing behavior (Weick and Roberts 1993). Individuals look for cues to identify the members of the team, the task, and the tools (i.e., information and resources) that members use to accomplish the task (Arrow et al. 2000; Pentland 2005). These relationships can be based on appearance (e.g., dress), synchronous activity (e.g., marching together), and interdependent activity (e.g., throwing a ball back and forth). For example, a sports team can be identified as being made up of team members (i.e., the individuals wearing the same uniform and trying to advance in the same direction), the task (i.e., score a goal), and the tools (i.e., a soccer ball) that comprise the team.

Participants in a face-to-face team discussion are usually easy to identify, as they are present in the same physical space engaged in conversation, whether speaking or listening. In a virtual environment, representation can be more difficult. Participants may be less aware of each other because they are located in different physical locations. They can only see each other through a computer screen, or only hear each other through a phone or speaker, or—in the extreme—only sense each other’s presence through typed text in an onscreen window. In these cases, the task (i.e., the theme or topic of the conversation), and the ICT tools and the information shared through them, rather than the visual image or audio sounds tie the team together and enable representing. Developing a shared mental model understood by all team members is more difficult in the virtual environments (Miranda and Saunders 2003). Representing is important because unless team members identify and understand the task, the task processes, and the roles and relationships among team members they will be unable to contribute effectively and align their behaviors to the needs of the team.

Observing Collective Mindfulness. How can we distinguish between a pattern of team behavior that exhibits collective mindfulness and one that does not? Collective mindfulness is achieved by the holistic performance of all three activities, contributing, aligning and representing (Pentland 1999). Team activity may feature a high level of contributing without an equal level of representing or aligning behaviors. Such misalignment among the activities does not reflect collective mindfulness and is illustrated in multiple monologues in which a high level of information exchange is not matched with aligning and representing of contributions. Drawing from the band example discussed earlier, if a band member could neither see the conductor, nor hear the music contributed by fellow band members—or simply ignored them—the music produced could be out of tune or out of sync with the music being played by the rest of the band.

In a team discussion, a team member may simply listen to other team members and develop a very detailed representation of the relationship among the contributions of the other team members without participating in the discussion. Such a person is effectively engaging in representing behavior, but is neither contributing nor aligning his or her contributions to the team discussion. Such a person would not be helping the team achieve collective mindfulness. Likewise, a team member may have developed a representation of the team and still fail to interrelate individual contributions to the represented team-level processes. However, this probably occurs relatively infrequently (i.e., in cases in which the team member is disagreeable or does not feel they are, in reality, a member of the team under consideration).

## Collective Mindfulness Influences Decision Quality

Team decisions should be based on the integration of all relevant information (Dennis 1996a). We focus on situations in which team members do not have identical, complete knowledge of all relevant information, a situation that is commonly faced by teams drawn from different parts of the organization (Dennis et al. 1997/1998; Miranda and Saunders 2006). In this situation, team members typically have common information known to all team members, but each also has some unique information that only they know. This situation has become known as a hidden profile task and has been commonly studied in information systems, management, and psychology (Zheng and Wei 2013). In order to find the ideal solution to a hidden profile task, team members must share the information they have, consider the information they receive from others, and work together to reach consensus on the decision.

When teams exhibit collective mindfulness, they interact heedful of the activities of their members and the information they contribute. Collective mindfulness enables a more thorough consideration of available information (Swanson and Ramiller 2004; Weick and Roberts 1993; Weick and Sutcliffe 2006). Although individual mindfulness can help a person be more heedful of the contributions of others, collective mindfulness means the work of paying attention to and making sense of available information is distributed among team members. The effect can be likened to a soccer team in which team members bring their unique perspective (based on where they are on the field) and their available resources (based on their roles, training, and expertise) to bear in coordinating a response to their contextual environment (where the ball and the players on the other team are located and moving to) (van Fenema 2005). When the task of making sense of the available information is distributed among team members, the team as a whole can perform more intensive cognitive “heavy lifting” thereby making sense of an information environment that would otherwise be overwhelming for individual members of the team. This distribution of making sense of the available information comprises team-level information processing.

When the team performs a task that can benefit from teamlevel information processing, the degree to which they contribute, align, and represent the available information increases the likelihood that the team will perform well (Stasser and Birchmeier 2003). This is because participants can pool the information storage and processing capacity distributed within the team (Brandon and Hollingshead 2004) to collectively make sense of a complex and dynamic information space (Cox and Greenberg 2000; Miranda and Saunders 2003).

Past empirical research has not studied the effects of collective mindfulness on team performance; however, there has been empirical research on some of the components of collective mindfulness. The effects of increased information contribution have had mixed effects on team performance; sometimes increased contribution helps, but other times it does not (e.g., Dennis 1996a, 1996b; Dennis et al. 1997/ 1998). Contributing has typically been studied independent from aligning, so it is not surprising that contributing has been shown to have mixed effects. Shared mental models (a.k.a. shared task understanding) has been shown to improve team performance (He et al. 2007; van Ginkel and van Knippenberg 2008, 2012).

Collective mindfulness is a holistic construct, so focusing on individual components is not as useful. As team members heedfully attend to the actions of other team members, they will create better representations of other team members’ actions and information. They will use this improved representation to contribute needed information to the team discussion, and align their contributions with the actions of other team members. Mindfulness is a recursive process, in that contributions from others will shape a team member’s representation, which is used to align his or her contributions to the team’s needs, which improves the representations of other team members (and his or her own), which in turn induces others’ contributions to be more aligned, and so on. Through this process, a mindful dialogue emerges in which team members contribute information and integrate the information from others into a shared mental model of the decision situation, until eventually reaching consensus on a good decision.

In contrast, consider a set of less mindful multiple monologues. Team members would contribute information without heeding the contributions of others, so contributions would not be well aligned. They would focus on their own actions and would not develop good representations of the information and activities of others. They would discuss information but it would be discordant, much like a band whose members play their own instruments without attention to those around them. Shared mental models of the situation would be hard to reach and the team decision would be based on incompletely shared and considered information. Teams still could arrive at the ideal decision, but it would be less likely. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H1: Increased collective mindfulness behavior leads to improved team decision quality.

## Using ICT to Influence Collective Mindfulness

As Carlo et al. (2012) note, “IS research has not explored how IT artifacts relate to or influence the quality of collective mindfulness” (p. 1083). Ironically, well-designed, reliable ICT that promote efficient, routine behavior may induce less mindful behavior because routine behavior often deteriorates into less mindful actions (Butler and Gray 2006). Faulty ICT may actually do more to induce mindful behavior, although they are less likely to improve performance (Carlo et al. 2012). A better approach may be to design ICT and encourage their appropriation in ways that support the underlying behavioral actions of mindfulness (Carlo et al. 2012)—that is, contributing, aligning, and representing (Pentland 1999; Ryle 1949).

Text-based ICT are good at promoting contributing behaviors (e.g., information exchange), but are less effective for promoting aligning and representing behaviors (Dennis 1996a). For decades, such ICT have enabled parallel production in which team members can exchange messages without having to wait for another to finish speaking (Dennis et al. 1988). ICT has also decreased the costs of exchanging messages. The time, money, and effort required to send an e-mail, a text message, an instant message, etc., is much less than the resources required to transmit the same message using a nondigital medium, such as a conference call.

Although these ICT tools promote contributing, they have a negative effect on the team’s ability to integrate information (Dennis 1996a, 1996b)—to engage in aligning and representing behaviors. Structures that encourage aligning and representing behaviors occur naturally in face-to-face discussions through turn-taking and the expectation that contributed messages should be related to immediately preceding contributions. However, these structures are lacking in many ICT-based conversations (Dennis 1996a; Herring 1999; Miranda and Saunders 2006). In ICT discussions, the challenge becomes to find ways to help team members more effectively engage in aligning and representing activities without inhibiting their ability to contribute.

Performative Objects. The concept of a performative object from the science of design (Niedderer 2007) provides a theoretical lens to understand how to guide team member behavior toward more aligning and representing. Performative objects “communicate and cause mindfulness of others in the context of social interaction by means of a modification of function” (Niedderer 2007, p. 4). The design of a performative object contains strong cues for how it should be used (Leonardi 2011; Norman 1990). These cues create dominant appropriation paths so that although users are free to choose how to use it, the design of a performative object enables and constrains behaviors to encourage users to appropriate it in specific ways (Dennis and Reinicke 2004), ways designed to encourage specific behaviors (Niedderer 2007).

The concept of performativity in the science of design (Niedderer 2007) is similar to the concept of performativity in sociomateriality (Cecez-Kecmanovic, Galliers et al. 2014; Cecez-Kecmanovic, Kautz, and Abrahall 2014; Kautz and Jensen 2013; Leonardi 2013; Mutch 2013; Orlikowski and Scott 2008). Performativity is a “central idea” of sociomateriality (Orlikowski and Scott 2008, p. 460) which argues that the social and the material (e.g., technology) are “inherently inseparable” (Orlikowski and Scott 2008, p. 456)—although this view of inseparability is not universally held by scholars of sociomateriality (see Leonardi 2013; Mutch 2013). In sociomateriality, performativity refers to a material object having the ability to act in a way that “contributes to the constitution of the reality that it describes” (Orlikowski and Scott 2008, p. 461).

Sociomateriality scholars argue that material objects are performative in that they have agency and can act. Thus behavior is an integration of human agency and the agency of material objects, although there is disagreement over the nature of material objects. Scholars in the school of “strong” sociomateriality argue that “material is not a not a given or fixed substance, or just a product of human agency, but is iteratively performed through intra-actions” (Cecez-Kecmanovic, Galliers et al. 2014, p. 812). Scholars in the school of “weak” sociomateriality, argue that

materiality predates the actions to which it will be put and the perceptions it will help create. Put more simply, users are introduced to a technology whose materiality has already been preconfigured for them. Although they may be able to change its materiality, even their perceptions of what changes could be made are constricted, to a large degree, by the initial materiality that they encounter when first using the technology (Leonardi 2013, p. 69).

Niedderer (2007) and her colleagues in the science of design (e.g., Lockton et al. 2013; Oxman 2009; Taylor 2013) hold views that are closer to the weak school of sociomateriality; they believe that a material object has materiality separate from its users and thus “provides people with capabilities that they can use to accomplish their goals” (Leonardi 2010, p. 4<sup>2</sup>). As scholars of design, they take this one step further by contending that material objects can be intentionally designed to induce certain social behaviors (Niedderer 2007).

They argue that material objects are the mediator of social meaning, because when we use objects to communicate, interaction is transformed from a dyadic human to human relationship into a triangular human to object to human relationship (Niedderer 2007). Direct human to human interaction is possible, but the dominant nature of the object (ICT) means that most interaction occurs through this triangular human–object–human relationship. Therefore, the object can be intentionally designed to enable certain behaviors and constrain others.

Performative objects also provide cues as to how the function of the object is embedded within and reflective of the social context in which it is used (Niedderer 2007; Norman 1990). These cues and the characteristics of an object’s design make it possible for the user to see that the object enables and constrains certain forms of social interaction around its use; its design embodies a plan of action for its use (Niedderer 2007). This design encompasses both the physical structure and features and functions it provides, as well as what those symbolize, such as the user’s emotional response to the design, the cultural symbols it invokes in the users, and the social activities it exemplifies (Ligo 1984; Niedderer 2007).

When someone uses a performative object, the object enables its user to recognize that they are part of a greater institution of being in much the same way that members using a shared language are part of the same community (Haugeland 1982). Performative objects are inherently social in that their interfaces reflect a multiuser design that “provide the facilities for its many users to organize themselves within and around it” (Krippendorff 2006, p. 10). These objects provide affordances through which the users identify what they are able to do with the shared object and become more mindful of their actions in the social context of use (Gibson 1979).

Niedderer (2007) focuses on the design of performative objects intended to promote mindful social interaction and facilitate the creation and maintenance of a team. This form of performative object provides cues that prompt users to engage in aligning and representing behaviors. These cues are provided through the user interface and exhibit the ability to accept inputs or receive action from multiple individuals in a social context, thus communicating its designed intent that it be used by a team (thereby facilitating representing behaviors). Performative cues are also provided through features that enable the alignment of inputs so as to facilitate its use by multiple team members (thereby promoting aligning behaviors).

For example, “La Grolla” is a traditional shared drinking cup in northern Italy that has many separate spouts (see Figure 9 in Niedderer 2007, p. 14). The appropriate cup is used to match the number of people. If three people attend a dinner, then a three-spouted cup is used; if there are six people, then a six-spouted cup is used. One person takes a drink from one spout and then passes it to the next person who drinks from the next spout and passes it to the next and so on. This shared drinking links the individuals together, and encourages them to be more mindful of their drinking behavior and the behaviors of others. The cup encourages them to build better shared mental models of social drinking (e.g., amount and sequence) and to better align their contributions (i.e., their drinking) to the shared experience of the others. The multiuser cues from the spouts of the shared cup thereby promote more mindful behavior (Niedderer 2007).

Designing ICT as Performative Objects. Building the concepts of task–technology fit (Dennis et al. 2001; Goodhue and Thompson 1995; Zigurs and Buckland 1998), we argue that ICT can be designed not only to fit the task, but also as performative objects (Leonardi 2011; Norman 1990) that create dominant appropriation paths (Dennis and Reinicke 2004) that change team behaviors. Our focus is on ICT tools designed to induce collective mindfulness, so our focus is on features that provide cues to facilitate contributing, aligning, and representing behaviors within teams. In this context, contributing is generally well done (Dennis 1996a, 1996b), so we need ICT that induce better aligning and representing behaviors. Thus the goal in the design of performative ICT is to induce team members to collectively identify and construct relationships among member contributions, thereby increasing aligning and representing.

There are many ways that one could design ICT to be performative objects. In this study, we examine only one approach; we use a combination of two commonly available commercial tools, rather than developing our own tools. We use a synchronous text chat tool, in conjunction with a shared whiteboard which has been called a dual-space environment (Çakır et al. 2009; Zemel and Koschmann 2013). Teams working in dualspace environments often use the whiteboard to create a representation of the problem and its information as they work toward a solution (Dillenbourg and Traum 2006; Çakır et al. 2009; Zemel and Koschmann 2013).

A shared whiteboard enables dynamic multi-user editing of a shared document in a pattern of interaction known as reactive writing. Reactive writing occurs when “writers create a document in real time, reacting and adjusting to each other’s changes and additions (Sharples et al. 1993) without significant preplanning and explicit coordination” (Lowry et al. 2004, p. 78). Reactive writing resembles a dialogue more than monologue in that it features a high degree of coupling and mutual production of content. Whereas traditional synchronous-text chat tools produce a static list of the chronological postings, reactive writing within shared whiteboards produces an organic body of text that continues to change until the discussion is finished.

There are few differences between a shared whiteboard and synchronous text chat for contributing behaviors. In both tools, participants can contribute at will. There is no need to wait for others to finish before contributing. Both tools enable contributing behaviors.

The two key differences between a whiteboard and a text chat are the ability to place text in a specific place on the screen and to edit (and move) text, which affect aligning and representing behaviors. The ability to add new text in close physical proximity to related text on the whiteboard is important for aligning, both literately and theoretically. Aligning behaviors can occur among users of synchronous text chat tools through the use of written references to previously contributed content, but the connections are sometimes not as easy to make because prior comments are not always easily referred to and because they can scroll off the screen. As a result synchronous text chats are often disjoint, with many separate conversation threads and questions and answers and other related contributions not easily processed together (Herring 1999), often resulting in multiple monologues (Dennis and Valacich 1994).

The whiteboard enables users to signal others that their contributions are related to previous contributions by physically placing their contributions near the previous text. Alignment is instantly obvious and is more likely to trigger reactive writing. This shared text provides a focal point for team member interactions (Bouamrane et al. 2004). The amount of text and the presence or absence of text also can indicate where additional contributions are needed, triggering contributing behaviors when they are needed, and reducing them when there is a host of information on a specific topic. Both lead to improved aligning.

The physical collocation of related items also improves representing behaviors. It is easier for team members to update their mental models of the team’s task because related information is presented together, instead of being spread out through separate comments (perhaps requiring scrolling) in a text chat (Zemel and Koschmann 2013). The ability to edit text also dramatically increases representing behaviors. Awareness of others’ activities is essential as participants read and change what others have contributed; one cannot make good changes to others’ work unless one has understood it and identified how it should be revised to better fit one’s own mental model. These activities are the quintessential representing behaviors. Representing may also be increased as team members view the edits made by others as they occur in real time. Watching others edit contributions (perhaps their own contributions) increases representing behaviors as team members strive to understand the changes that were made.

The creation of this focal point—an organic shared text reflective of the living, dynamic patterns of interaction occurring within the team discussion—enables the shared whiteboard to function as a performative object to increase aligning and representing behaviors. As the whiteboard draws discussion participants’ attention to the team interaction, team members increase in their ability to accurately perceive and mentally represent the relationships among the actions of the team members. The integration (i.e., aligning) of user contributions increases the coupling of the discussion and provides opportunities to address disagreements, which helps team members to identify relationships among shared textual contributions and to align their contributions within the developing whiteboard (Mitchell et al. 1995). Therefore:

H2: Teams interacting with a shared whiteboard and synchronous text chat will exhibit increased collective mindfulness behaviors compared to teams interacting only through synchronous text chat.

Adding a second workspace to the text chat may create a new issue. Team members must focus their attention on two different workspaces. They have to deliberately decide whether to use the chat or whiteboard to make a contribution and have to read both, which increases cognitive load (Çakır et al. 2009). The increased cognitive load may create dual-task interference (Çakır et al. 2009; Zemel and Koschmann 2013), which impairs information processing (Heninger et al. 2006). Thus the provision of dual work spaces has the potential to reduce decision quality through a direct route separate from collective mindfulness. Prior research notes that increased collective mindfulness does not always increase performance; the lack of routines that help induce collective mindfulness may have a separate direct negative effect on performance (Butler and Gray 2006; Carlo, et al. 2012). Although we do not hypothesize such a direct negative effect, we include it in our analysis as a control.

## Methodology

## Participants

A total of 280 undergraduate business students participated as members of 56 five-person teams. A total of 27 teams were randomly assigned to use a traditional ICT (synchronous text chat), and the other 29 teams were assigned to use a traditional ICT plus a shared whiteboard. Four teams in the chat plus whiteboard treatment failed to follow instructions and did not use the whiteboard; they were removed from the analysis, giving 25 teams that completed the treatment. One member of eight teams failed to complete all the survey items, so these teams were removed from the analysis, leaving 44 teams (22 teams in the chat treatment and 22 teams in the chat plus whiteboard treatment). The average age of final participants was 19.6 and 33% were female.

## Task

The task was a hidden profile student admissions task adapted from prior research (Dennis 1996a; Fuller and Dennis 2009). Participants were presented with five fictitious applicants and were instructed they could only admit three of them to the university. This constraint was designed to encourage participants to more thoroughly consider their options by removing the option of simply admitting or rejecting all of the applicants. This constraint also reflects real world conditions in which one cannot take advantage of all opportunities.

We chose this task because students were familiar with the basic requirements of university admissions, as they had all participated in the application process (Fuller and Dennis 2009; Heninger et al. 2006). The task contains 14 attributes of information about each applicant. The task from Fuller and Dennis (2009) was adapted with the help of admissions staff to reflect our university’s admissions process. The task profiles and procedures were refined through two rounds of pilot testing from the undergraduate student population. Students participating in the pilot study did not participate in the final experiment. The final applicant profiles were validated with our admissions office to ensure that when all of the information was available, the intended decision for each applicant (accept or reject) was the best choice.

Hidden profile tasks are designed to mimic tasks in the real world in which different team members have different information (Stasser and Titus 2003). Each participant is given slightly different information, although no information is conflicting, so that participants arrive at the discussion favoring different choices. In our case, participants were provided with two types of information: common information and unique information. Common information was information given to all team members, while each piece of unique information was provided to only one or two team members. Information was asymmetrically distributed, so that a majority of the team members (three) had incomplete information that led them to make poor decisions. In other words the unique information contained facts that, when put together, outweighed the common information. The team could reach the correct decision for each applicant by exchanging and integrating the unique information that was collectively available to the team.

## Treatments

There were two treatments. In both treatments, participants communicated only through a text-based ICT; no verbal communication occurred. Thus the human–object–human relationship of technology mediated communication was established. The treatments did not differ in gender (χ² = 0.000, p = 0.992), age (t = 0.261, p = 0.794) or GPA (t = 0.393, p = 0.695).

Participants in the traditional ICT treatment used the synchronous text chat tool from Microsoft NetMeeting™ (Version 3.0.1). This tool featured a single-window interface. The window was divided into two main sections. The lower section took up approximately 20% of the window. Participants typed messages in a text box in the lower section and transmitted the message by either pressing “Enter” on the keyboard or by pushing a “Send” button located to the right of the text box. Transmitted messages were displayed in the upper section of the window in a sequential order with the older messages at the top of the display area. As more messages were added, the older messages scrolled off the top of the display area. The pseudo-anonymous message sender ID preceded each message (i.e., P1, P2, P3, P4, or P5). All text within the messages had a uniform font size and style. The window was sized to occupy approximately one fourth of the desktop space on the right side of the screen (see Figure 1).

Participants in the chat plus whiteboard treatment used NetMeeting as described above, plus the Live Sharing feature of Microsoft OneNote™ 2007 (version 12.0.4518.1014). OneNote was selected for three reasons. First, it provides a shared whiteboard that can be constrained to specific dimensions, so that contributions cannot scroll off the screen. The shared whiteboard was displayed in full-window mode and the window was sized to occupy approximately three-quarters of the screen to the left of the text chat window (see Figure 2). Second, it provides an environment that, to some degree, was familiar to the participants as it is part of the Microsoft Office suite. We anticipated that this familiarity would help reduce the potential for difficulty from the use of a novel tool. Finally, pilot testing indicated the latency of synchronizing text added in OneNote to all participants was approximately 4 seconds, which was sufficient for the needs of this study.

## Decision Quality

The first dependent variable was decision quality. Teams received one point for each decision that matched the correct decision as determined by the undergraduate admissions office. If the admissions office recommended rejection or acceptance of a given applicant, and the team made the same decision, they received one point. Since the task presented five applicants, teams could receive from zero to five points as their decision quality score.

## Collective Mindfulness

The second dependent variable was collective mindfulness. Observational measures were used to assess the occurrence of the three behaviors that make up collective mindfulness: contributing, aligning, and representing. We used information exchange as a measure of contributing, textual alignment as a measure of aligning, and shared mental models as a measure of representing. Collective mindfulness was modeled as a formative construct of these three constructs because the integration of these three behaviors is collective mindfulness.

Contributing (Information Exchange). The degree to which team members contributed (i.e., shared information) was measured by counting the number of pieces of unique information conveyed to the other team members (either in the whiteboard or the text chat), as has been done in previous research (Dennis 1996a, 1996b). There were 12 pieces of unique information for each of the five fictitious applicants in the task, thus giving a total of 60 pieces of information. One rater reviewed each of the chat transcripts and whiteboards and marked which of the 12 pieces of unique information in the complete profile for each applicant were included. Information was only considered to be exchanged if it was accurate. Information exchange was therefore calculated as a percentage (x/60) of the total unique information available to the team. A second coder independently coded a sample of 25% of the transcripts to ensure the coding instructions were followed consistently. Inter-rater reliability, calculated as 1 minus the number of disagreements divided by the total number of codings, was 94%, which is adequate.

Aligning (Textual Alignment). We used the messages shared via the synchronous-text chat tool and the shared whiteboard to measure the degree of aligning among team members. We constructed semantic networks from the chat transcripts and whiteboard transcripts of each team. We used social network analysis (Zheng and Wei 2013), specifically the measure of in-degree network centralization as a percentage indicating the degree to which all the messages contributed by team members connected to a core set of topics which anchored the team discussion. Thus, in-degree network centralization reflects the degree to which each message was aligned with the core topics of the team discussion. See the appendix for more details.

Representing (Shared Mental Models). The existence of shared mental models was measured by assessing the similarities among team members’ conceptual model of the criteria (i.e., SAT, GPA, grade trend) and the relationship between the criteria and the applicants. After the team made their decisions, participants were provided with a questionnaire asking them to provide a justification for their team decisions and to identify the criteria influencing their preferences. Participants were not provided with the criteria in advance, so we could use this as a measure of representing behaviors. Past research suggests that students have a reasonable understanding of the criteria for undergraduate admissions (Dennis 1996a, 1996b).

The open-ended responses for participants within each team were aggregated into a single text file and analyzed using map analysis, a textual analysis technique designed to identify semantic networks (Carley 1997). Within each aggregated text file, ties were created between each participant and the words they used to describe their decision criteria. A social network transitivity was used to calculate a percentage score that represented how much the words used by each participant overlapped with those used by the other team members. The greater the overlap, the greater the shared mental models among team members. See the appendix.

## Procedures

Participants were seated so that no team members were sitting next to each other (to prevent verbal discussion). The researcher introduced the task and provided written instructions summarizing the task and the profiles containing the incomplete information for the applicants. Participants completed the admissions task individually and recorded their individual admissions decisions on a pre-discussion questionnaire. Participants were informed that they had received incomplete information and that they should share their information with their team and pay careful attention to the information they received from others. Participants were trained on the ICT tool(s), and used them to discuss the task (for up to 20 minutes). Once they reached consensus on the admissions decisions, they recorded their decisions on a paper form. Participants then completed a post-discussion questionnaire and were released.

## Results

## Measurement Model

The measurement and the structural models were analyzed using PLS-Graph (Chin 1998). Collective mindfulness is a formative construct. Establishing discriminant validity of items thought to be formative is still an open issue in the metrics literature (Gefen and Straub 2005). Formative indicators are often used to form latent constructs because of a theoretical relationship among these constructs that may not necessarily be comprised of statistical correlation (Diamantopoulos and Winklhofer 2001). Each indicator is theorized to independently influence rather than reflect the overall value of the latent construct. Determining whether it is appropriate to include indicators of formative constructs cannot simply be based on the correlation among indicator variables. One must consider whether the latent construct can be properly defined without a given variable (Diamantopoulos and Winklhofer 2001).

We calculated Pearson Correlation Coefficients for the three indicator variables of the formative construct of collective mindfulness. None of the correlations among these variables were significant. As we explained earlier in the paper, teams can experience a high degree of one aspect of collective mindfulness (e.g., contributing) with a very low degree of another (e.g., interrelating). Shared mental models among team members may not be correlated with the amount of information exchanged, particularly if those shared mental models focus on a narrow set of topics. Thus, we have theorized that all three of these behaviors (contributing, aligning, and representing) make up the indicators of the formative construct of collective mindfulness, even if they are not correlated—and in fact prior research suggests that contributing is not related to representing because teams that contribute much information often do not integrate it (i.e., engage in representing) (see Dennis 1996a, 1996b; Dennis et al. 1997/1998).

Two suggestions have been given for establishing the validity of formative constructs: (1) assess the relationship between the formative construct and a theoretically related previously established measure comprised of reflexive indicators (Diamantopoulos and Winklhofer 2001), and (2) evaluate the t-scores of the weights for each of the formative indicators (Liang et al. 2007). The results of these suggestions support the measurement model.

First, we modeled the relationship between the formative construct made up of the three behaviors of collective mindfulness and a previously developed reflective construct designed to assess perceptions of collective mindfulness (Kanawattanachai and Yoo 2007; Yoo and Kanawattanachi 2001). The questionnaire included four items from Kanawattanachai and Yoo (2007). Responses for the four items were averaged across team members to obtain team-level item scores. These four team-level scores were used in a PLS model as reflective indicators of a perceived collective mindfulness construct. The path coefficient between the formative observed collective mindfulness behaviors and the reflective perceived collective mindfulness was significant (β $= 0 . 2 9 5 , \mathrm { t } = 1 . 9 9 , \mathrm { p } = 0 . 0 2 6 )$ , suggesting that the behavior construct predicts perceptions.

Second, the evaluation of the t-scores of the formative indicator path weights indicates a measure of validity for the formative construct. The output of the bootstrapping procedure for the structural model in PLS also includes the t-values for the path weights of each of the indicators. Following Liang, et al. (2007), we present the mean weights, standard errors, and t-values for each of the formative indicators in Table 1. Contributing had a t-value of 2.02 (p < 0.05), aligning had a t-value of 8.93 $( \mathbf { p } < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , and representing had a t-value of 0.67 (p > 0.10). Although representing did not have a significant t-value, it is a theoretically distinct component of the latent construct of collective mindfulness. It would therefore be improper to omit it from the construct (Diamantopoulos and Winklhofer 2001). We therefore retain all three components.

## Structural Model Assessment

The statistical significance of the parameter estimates was determined by using a bootstrapping procedure using 500 subsamples. The results are shown in Figure 3. ICT used is a categorical variable representing the two treatments. Collective mindfulness is the formative construct of the three behaviors, and decision quality is the outcome variable.

Hypothesis 1, that increased collective mindfulness behavior leads to improved team decision quality, was supported (β = 0.601, t-value = 5.30, p = 0.001). The model explained 31.6% percent of the variance in team decision quality. Hypothesis 2, that teams using text chat plus a shared whiteboard would exhibit increased collective mindfulness behaviors compared to teams interacting only through text chat, was also supported (β = 0.386, t-value = 3.52, p-value = 0.001). The model explained 14.9% of the variation in collective mindfulness.

We tested three other models as robustness checks. The model in Figure 3 tests the effects of providing a whiteboard in addition to text chat, yet we know that it is not simply the provision of a tool that influences outcomes, but also how teams appropriate it (Dennis et al. 2001). The first author analyzed how the teams used the whiteboard and identified six appropriations or patterns of use, which were not mutually exclusive. Two raters (the second author and a graduate student) independently used the coding scheme developed by the first author to code whether or not each team used each of the six appropriations. The raters agreed on all but six codings (95% agreement), and the differences were resolved.

The most popular appropriation was summarizing the team decision (employed by 19 teams). These teams used the whiteboard to record the final results of the team decision process. Frequently, this was enacted by presenting a list labeled something like “Consensus Picks” or “Final Decision” and listing the names of the applicants selected by the team for admission.

The second most popular appropriation (17 teams) was aggregating information about the applicants. This involved designating a space on the whiteboard for each applicant and placing the information about them in that space. Using the whiteboard to document what the team knows has been observed in prior studies of dual interface environments (Çakır et al. 2009; Dillenbourg and Traum 2006; Zemel and Koschmann 2013). For example, Figure 4 illustrates how one team organized their information around the initials of each of the applicants (e.g., CC, PP). Interestingly, participants quickly adopted the convention of contributing their comments under the headings, but were reluctant to move or edit the comments. This pattern illustrates the ability of spatial organization provided by the whiteboard to enable aligning behaviors.

Polling team members for their individual preferences was used by 10 teams. Members placed their votes for or against an applicant under the labels that identified each team member (e.g., P1, P2). A few teams used a more structured way of organizing votes such as a table.

Second, the ways in which teams appropriated the whiteboard had different effects on decision quality and perhaps collective mindfulness. Thus, it is not the provision and use of the whiteboard that matters, but rather how it is appropriated. The sociomateriality of the white board is a joint product of the material agency of the technology and the human agency of the team. Some appropriations were common (summarizing decisions, aggregating information), some less common (discussing decision criteria, discussing information distribution). We conclude that the whiteboard functioned as a performative objective, but its effects were not uniform; it triggered common appropriations as we anticipated, but also less common ones.

Third, some appropriations can be linked to differences in performance. We anticipated that teams would use the whiteboard to aggregate information, and teams in all but the lowest performing cluster did; thus it is necessary but not sufficient. More successful teams were more likely to use the whiteboard to vote, and discuss decision criteria and information distribution.

As an additional post hoc test, we reexamined the research model by replacing the treatment indicator variable with an index variable designed to capture the extent to which the teams appropriated the whiteboard. The index was the sum of the number of these four whiteboard appropriations, ranging from 0 to 4, so that a zero indicated the team used the whiteboard, but did not appropriate it in any of these ways, while a four indicated that the team adopted all four appropriations. If the team was in the control treatment and did not receive the whiteboard, the index was set to -1 to distinguish it from teams that received the whiteboard but did not use any of the four appropriations. Figure 6 shows that the number of ICT appropriations increased collective mindfulness (β = 0.479, tvalue = 5.24, p = 0.000, R<sup>2</sup> = .229), which in turn improved decision quality (β = 0.480, t-value = 3.23, p = 0.001, R<sup>2</sup> = .232). The direct link between appropriations and decision quality was not significant (β = 0.05, t-value = 0.03, p = 0.974). As an aside, we note that the mean decision quality for teams with zero to four of these appropriations was: 1.00, 2.86, 3.00, 4.00, and 3.67, respectively; the mean for the chatonly control treatment was 2.95. We believe this provides further evidence that appropriation influences outcomes.

## Qualitative Analysis of Chat Use

We repeated the same post hoc analysis for the appropriation of the chat tool in the chat treatment, but omitting doodling. Two raters (the second author and a graduate student) independently coded the transcripts for the five appropriation behaviors and agreed on all but eight codings (93% agreement) and the differences were resolved. We used the twostep cluster analysis (with SPSS defaults), which found four clusters (see Table 4) of “good” quality (> .5). There were no differences in collective mindfulness (F(3,18) = 0.35, p = .216) or decision quality (F(3,18) = 1.87, p = .172). There were significant differences for decision summary (F(3,18) = 33.27, p = .000), voting (F(3,18) = 9.69, p = .000), and decision criteria (F(3,18) = 16.91, p = .000), but not for information aggregation (F(3,18) = 0.62, p = .61) or information distribution (F(3,18) = 2.51, p = .092).

The patterns here are less clear. Teams in some clusters used similar appropriations to teams with the whiteboard (voting, discussion of criteria and information distribution) but information aggregation was uniformly low. There were no differences in collective mindfulness or decision quality across the clusters, so we conclude that the failure to perform information aggregation rendered moot the other appropriations; that is, information aggregation is necessary for success and without it, other appropriations don’t matter.

## Discussion

Our results show that the use of a shared whiteboard in addition to text chat increased the extent to which teams exhibited collective mindfulness behaviors and that as collective mindfulness increased, so too did team decision quality. However, the whiteboard also had a direct negative effect on decision quality, indicating other factors were also at work. The effect on collective mindfulness (R<sup>2</sup> = 15%) is what Cohen (1992) would call “medium,” while the effect on decision quality (R<sup>2</sup> = 32%) is “large.” Not all teams appropriated the whiteboard in the same way (indicating its sociomateriality), although there were two common appropriations (indicating its ability to function as a performative object). Our post hoc analyses of both the whiteboard and chat treatments suggest that four appropriations had the strongest effects on collective mindfulness and decision quality. Information aggregation was necessary but not sufficient; without information aggregation, none of the other appropriations mattered. However, information aggregation did not improve collective mindfulness and decision quality unless it was coupled with discussion of information distribution, discussion of criteria, and voting.

Collective mindfulness is a team level construct that incorporates both information exchange and information processing at the team level. Most prior research has focused on information exchange among team members, yet information processing is also important to team outcomes (Dennis et al. 2008). To our knowledge, no prior research has used a controlled study to show that collective mindfulness has large effects on virtual team performance.

Our research shows that the use of different ICT tools can induce different amounts of collective mindfulness which in turn affects decision quality. The effects of this ICT (a shared whiteboard plus the chat) had only a medium effect on collective mindfulness. Interestingly, we also found direct negative effect of whiteboard use on decision quality, although this direct effect was about half the strength of the effects of collective mindfulness (β = 0.601 versus .326). Carlo et al. (2012) suggest that faulty ICT may induce more mindful behavior while not improving overall performance, so perhaps this effect should not be unexpected. The means in Table 2 show that there was no meaningful difference in decision quality between the teams using the whiteboard and those without it. Thus it is not the presence of the whiteboard that affects performance, but rather how teams appropriate it that matters.

We did not direct teams how to use the whiteboard, because there is no prior theory or empirical research to derive how whiteboard use can increase collective mindfulness. Teams appropriated the whiteboard in different ways, thus demonstrating the sociomateriality of its use. Some appropriations were common, suggesting the material agency of the whiteboard produced some dominant appropriation paths (Dennis and Reinicke 2004). Other appropriations were not very common suggesting the human agency of team members produced the appropriation.

We argued that the provision of two tools could increase the cognitive load and create dual-task interference (Heninger et al. 2006). Our participants had much experience with text chat tools, but far less experience with whiteboards, so it is not surprising that the introduction of a new tool might initially reduce performance; there is often a learning curve effect in which performance drops after the initial adoption of a new technology and then gradually improves over time as users gain experience (Lapre and van Wassenhove 2001; Pisano et al. 2001). It may also be that the greater functionality available in the whiteboard compared to the chat software increased its complexity by providing more ways to appropriate it.

We believe this research makes four contributions. First, our findings suggest that describing ICT as performative objects is a useful theoretical lens for understanding ICT use. Second, the large positive relationship between collective mindfulness and decision quality suggests that further research is warranted to understand collective mindfulness and to develop ICT to more strongly influence it. Third, we developed a behavior-based measure of collective mindfulness that differs from prior measures that use the perceptions of team members. Fourth, we identified a set of whiteboard appropriations that are linked to improved performance.

## ICTs as Performative Objects

We extended Niedderer’s (2007) conceptualization of performative objects by proposing that the intentional design of

ICT could facilitate the achievement of collective mindfulness. The results suggest that viewing ICT in terms of their ability to function as performative objects is a useful lens. One contribution of this paper is to theorize about and provide some initial empirical evidence for the relationships between ICT characteristics and the team-level process of collective mindfulness and team performance.

To say that an individual adopts an ICT and appropriates its social structures into his or her communication patterns is something of a misnomer. The use of ICT requires at least two individuals. For communication to occur, both individuals must adopt the same ICT and appropriate it in mostly similar ways. Thus, the adoption and use of the ICT occurs at the team level. Although multiple monologues can be transmitted individually, a dialogue is cocreated, not transmitted. From this perspective, the team-level construct of collective mindfulness is theoretically different from individual-level information exchange. Our theorizing about ICT needs to view ICT as incorporated within team processes rather than simply influencing them.

At a higher level, this research has implications for sociomateriality. As Cecez-Kecmanovic, Galliers et al. (2014) note, there are many different theoretical views of sociomateriality which has the potential to devolve into a “battleground” (p. 810) or “jargon monoxide” (Sutton 2010). We believe that a diversity of views is healthy and important, particularly for a new theoretical perspective as it matures. We are critical realists and thus our views of sociomateriality align with those of Mutch (2013) and Leonardi (2013).

We view the material objects (the ICT tools, in this case) as having a material existence prior to their use, since in our study we deliberately selected and configured them as instruments to fit the needs of the task and team (Dennis et al. 2001; Goodhue and Thompson 1995; Zigurs and Buckland 1998), and also to induce certain social behaviors (Niedderer 2007). These material objects were the product of social action, but the participants in the study had no involvement in that production; the materiality of the tools was decided for them.

Materiality exists independent of people, but the actions that that materiality enable and constrain do not (Leonardi 2013). A hammer has certain material properties, but it can be used as a paperweight, doorstop, dog chew toy, etc., depending upon the goals and understandings of the user. Thus the materiality of a software tool does not change, but its material agency does (what the tool does as it is used), as indicated by the fact that the tools were used in different ways by different teams. Leonardi (2013) uses the concept of imbrication (the gradual overlapping and interlocking of distinct elements into one composite) as a metaphor for how the material and social become sociomaterial. The text chat software was familiar to our participants so they had developed a deeper understanding of how to use it—the process of imbrication had run longer— and thus similar routines had developed. The whiteboard was more novel, so routines had not developed, and its greater flexibility perhaps increased its complexity.

From our perspective as critical realists, one important implication that our study has for the understanding of sociomateriality is the intentional design of software tools as performative objects that can influence social behaviors. These tools are essentially inert, lacking agency, but containing the seeds of potential agency through their capabilities to enable and constrain behavior, and their design cues that hint at these capabilities. It is not until they are engaged by their users that their material agency becomes constitutively entangled with social agency to coproduce the emergent sociomaterial behaviors. Different teams used the performative objects in different ways, which shows the sociomateriality of team behaviors; the emergent behavior was influenced both by the material (the software tools) and the social (which differed from team to team). Our study shows that it is possible to influence the emergent sociomaterial behaviors by changing the design of the material objects and the potential agency they contain, although the resulting sociomaterial behaviors are not entirely consistent from team to team.

In addition to identifying ICT tools that can serve as performative objects, more research is needed to identify ways to structure team interaction to better enable aligning and representing behaviors. A number of teams struggled to determine their course of action once they discovered they had different information. Because the teams had no history of working together, they lacked a repertoire of activities with which to respond to the challenges of unanticipated or ambiguous events (Mark and Semaan 2008). There is some evidence that process restrictiveness that constrains how technology can be appropriated improves performance, provided the constraints lead to good work processes (Dennis et al. 2001; Wheeler and Valacich 1996). We did not provide participants with any process structures, because prior research does not provide sufficient guidance on how to use whiteboards to increase collective mindfulness.

## Collective Mindfulness and Performance

The results of this study indicate that collective mindfulness increases team decision quality. This study thus identifies a mediating behavior that helps explain how ICT use influences team outcomes. By increasing our understanding of collective mindfulness, future research can identify “levers” that can be used to influence team decision quality by increasing collective mindfulness. This study demonstrates that collective mindfulness is a relevant team behavior that can be observed, quantified, and influenced. Future research needs to extend our understanding of how collective mindfulness influences decision quality.

Research on collective mindfulness has largely focused on its effects on performance, which is important, but teams have other equally important functions (i.e., developing relationships among members and fulfilling members’ needs for growth) (Dennis and Reinicke 2004; McGrath 1991). If teams exhibit greater collective mindfulness (especially aligning and representing), then they may also develop stronger relationships (Maynard and Gilson 2014; Miles and Kivlighan 2008; Weick and Roberts 1993). Likewise, greater collective mindfulness (especially representing) may promote individual growth (Weick and Roberts 1993; Weick and Sutcliffe 2006). Research suggests that members may place a greater value on relationships and growth than on performance (Dennis and Reinicke 2004). Thus, future research is needed to understand how collective mindfulness influences these outcomes because they may have a greater impact on use over the long term (Dennis and Reinicke 2004).

## Measuring Team Collective Mindfulness

This study also provides a methodological contribution. Drawing from Weick and Robert’s (1993) seminal work on collective mindfulness in organizations, we constructed a working definition of collective mindfulness and a measurement technique that can be applied at the team level within the context of team discussion. We defined collective mindfulness as the co-occurrence of three interrelating behaviors: contributing, aligning, and representing. Contributing behaviors were operationalized as the degree to which team members provided new information. Aligning was operationalized as the degree to which team member contributions were centralized around a set of core topics. Representing was operationalized as the degree to which team members developed a shared mental model of the problem space.

We developed and applied a semi-automated technique based on semantic network analysis to create a formative quantitative measure of collective mindfulness. This measure is based on within-team communication patterns and on openended post-discussion responses by team members. The technique provides a behavioral alternative to self-report measures that is independent of participant perceptions. It also requires less time than qualitative textual coding or ethnographic techniques. This technique expands the researcher’s toolkit in a way that increases the feasibility of quantitative experimental research on collective mindfulness. Future research can refine this analytical technique and apply it to a broader range of settings.

## Appropriations Linked to Improved Performance

Our post hoc analyses suggest that some appropriations improve collective mindfulness and decision quality. Teams that appropriated the ICT tools primarily to summarize their decisions exhibited low collective mindfulness and decision quality (see Cluster 1 in both Table 3 and 4). Conversely, teams that appropriated it to aggregate information, discuss information distribution, discuss decision criteria, and vote exhibited high collective mindfulness and decision quality (Cluster 4 in Table 3). Teams that aggregated information but did not discuss information and criteria, and vote (Clusters 2 and 3 in Table 3) or discussed information and criteria, and voted but did not aggregate information (Clusters 2, 3, and 4 in Table 4) exhibited collective mindfulness and decision quality that appears randomly distributed around the means.

Aggregating information and discussing its distribution may improve aligning and contributing, because it helps team members better understand what information everyone has and the unique information they themselves have. Discussion of criteria and voting may improve representing by explicitly showing what others’ mental models suggest is important. Our analyses indicate that the combination of the three behaviors (contributing, aligning, and representing) improves decision quality; miss any one and decision quality does not improve.

Future research should investigate whether it is the combination of these appropriations that improves performance or whether some other combination is equally effective. Likewise, we need research to design and test interventions (e.g., ICT features) that may influence appropriation and thereby improve collective mindfulness and decision quality. Should cues be embedded within an ICT to help teams appropriate them (Feldman and Pentland 2003; Krippendorff 2006), such as whiteboard templates that teams can select among (Haake et al. 2005), or are restrictive processes that constrain use better (Wheeler and Valacich 1996)?

## Limitations

The limitations include those common to laboratory experiments. Student participants may vary significantly from nonstudent populations in age and work experience (Sears 1986).

For reasons of experimental precision, the laboratory task occurred within a relatively short time period which may limit the applicability of these findings to teams whose members interact over longer periods of time, whose membership and patterns of activity fluctuate over time, and whose members develop experience using the whiteboard and chat together (Arrow et al. 2000; Weick and Roberts 1993). The sample size was small (220 participants in 44 teams), so we used PLS, although recent work suggests that PLS path estimates and R<sup>2</sup> values should be interpreted carefully, especially when binary measures are used (Bentler and Huang 2014; Goodhue et al. 2012; Henseler et al. 2014; McIntosh et al. 2014; Rigdon 2012).

## Implications for Practice

The provision of a whiteboard in addition to text chat increased collective mindfulness, but did not affect decision quality. Instead, the way in which teams used the whiteboard was important; some uses led to improved decision quality, while others did not. Managers should be aware that performance can be enhanced by a combination of three behaviors: contributing, aligning, and representing. As members engage in representing, they better understand the connections and relationships among member activities. With this increased awareness, team members can more effectively contribute and align their individual actions with the team behavior and better represent the total information available to the team.

We recommend four guidelines for using a whiteboard together with a chat. First, create a space to aggregate information contributed by different team members. Second, identify the unique information that team members have; these two guidelines can be implemented by creating a table of information to which members add rows of information. Third, discuss the relative importance of the criteria to develop a shared mental model. Finally, use formal voting to identify the preferences of members to see where agreements and disagreements lie.

## Conclusion

In summary, our research shows that teams which exhibit greater collective mindfulness, in terms of contributing, aligning, and representing, make better decisions. ICT that induce team members to aggregate information, discuss the distribution of information, discuss decision criteria, and conduct a formal vote can improve collective mindfulness. We believe this calls for more research on collective mindfulness and on how ICT can induce these behaviors.

## Details of Textual Analysis for Representing and Aligning Behaviors

## Representing

The open-ended responses to the justification question for all participants within each team were aggregated into a single text file and analyzed using the Automap software (Carley et al. 2006). This software provides a semi-automated means of identifying the concepts within a body of text and the relationships (based on proximity) among those concepts. Within the aggregated text file, we grouped each participant’s response to the justification question into one paragraph and began each response with the participant identifier (e.g., P1, P2, P3, P4, and P5).

Figure A1 contains a simplified version of what the justification text file might look like. In this figure, each participant’s justification is represented by a single statement. This sample is much more abbreviated than were the actual justifications. In practice, most participants wrote several sentences of justification. If we were to use an actual sample, the analysis in our example would be so large that it would obscure the mechanism of the map analysis function we are attempting to explain. Thus we use this simple example for illustration purposes.

The software built a semantic network from the text by establishing relationships between the words that occurred within each response. Links were established between each participant and the words they used, which include the criteria and values most relevant to them. In cases where a participant’s justifications were similar to those of another member of their team (i.e., they used the same words), links would be established across their individual responses.

With the semantic network analyzed in this study, the vertices were the participant identifiers and words participants used in their justifications. Edges exist if the vertices (i.e., words) occurred within the same paragraph of the aggregated justification text. Directional edges were constructed between vertices if they occurred within the same message, with words occurring earlier in the message pointing toward words occurring later in the message.

The text was imported into Automap 2.7.67. Punctuation marks were removed from all transcripts prior to processing. Doing so allows the software to recognize that GPA and G.P.A. are the same acronym and that “scores,” and “scores” and “-scores” are all the same word. AutoMap’s MapAnalysis function features two settings that we configured for processing the text: (1) the window size and (2) the windowing reset. The window size setting was used to configure the allowable distance between words for a relationship to be identified between them. A window size of two would indicate that only words that are next to each other can have a relationship between them. The windowing reset setting allows the user to determine the point at which the processing window will be reset (e.g., at the end of each sentence, at the end of each paragraph, or after a certain number of words). In constructing the semantic network, we set the window size to include all words until the windowing reset point and set the windowing reset to the end of each paragraph. With this setting, relationships would be identified between all the words in a given participant’s response while ensuring that relationships would only be established between participants if they used the same sets of words in their justifications.

The semantic network data from the MapAnalysis of the justification texts were imported into ORA version 1.9.5.3.5 for further analysis. To assess representing, we used the network measure of transitivity. Transitivity is a measure of clustering based on the number of triples and triangles within a network (Börner et al. 2007). A triple is a collection of three connected vertices with two edges. A triangle is a collection of three connected vertices with three edges (between v1 and v2, v2 and v3, and v1 and v3) (Friedkin 1998). As seen in FigureA4, there are 8 possible combinations of edges between vertices 1 through 3 in an undirected graph (Wasserman and Faust 1994). Four of those possible combinations (E, F, G, and H) can be described as having triples. Only one of those combinations (set H) creates a triangle.

The transitivity of a network is based on the percentage of triangles found in the network compared with the number of triples in the network (Friedkin 1998). Transitivity is calculated using the following formula (Börner et al. 2007):

$$

$$

Transitivity is an appropriate metric for assessing the connectedness of the justification text networks. If members of the team have achieved a greater degree of shared mental models, the similarity of topics and concepts included in their individual justifications will be reflected in an increased number of triangles among the concepts across the network. If team member justifications were completely unique and exhibited no shared mental models, the network would be comprised of isolated clusters and the transitivity value would be much lower.

## Aligning

We used the messages shared via the synchronous-text chat tool and the shared whiteboard tool to measure the degree of aligning among team members. Using the same MapAnalysis configuration settings within AutoMap described in the previous section, we extracted network data from the body of messages exchanged among team members. The text from the text chat transcript and the shared whiteboard transcripts were combined into a single text file for each team. Within the text chat transcript, each message was stored on a separate line with the participant identifier included at the beginning of each message. Although the participant identifiers were not visible in the shared whiteboard transcripts, the OneNote software stores the identifier of the participant who last edited a block of text within its XML structure. Using these embedded identifiers, we were able to construct a series of statements from the OneNote whiteboard that were structured in the same manner as the messages in the text chat transcripts—with a participant identifier followed by a statement. Text snippets that were deliberately proximate to each other in the shared whiteboard were grouped together in the text file. For example, if one member of the team placed a heading in the whiteboard labeled “Charles academic Information,” and other members contributed their information beneath that heading, we included the heading in each member’s statement.

For example, Figure A5 includes a block of text from a OneNote whiteboard used by one of the teams in our analysis. The information is organized under the heading of “Mary.” The underlying XML in the OneNote file reveals that most of this block was written by P4, but that the fourth line in the block was written by P5. Figure A6 illustrates the coding for this text block. This coding allows each comment included under a heading to be teamed together while also associating each contribution with the participant who made it.

Figure A7 shows a sample of text from a text chat transcript. It can also be seen in Figures A5 and A6 that punctuation marks were removed from the text (as was done for the justification text).The semantic network resulting from the analysis of the sample text is illustrated in Figure A8.

The participant identifiers and the words of each message were used as vertices in the semantic network. Directional edges were constructed between vertices if they occurred within the same message, with words occurring earlier in the message “pointing toward” words occurring later in the message. Figure A8 shows the network extracted from the text chat sample in Figure A7. Once again, this is just a small sample to illustrate the analysis technique.

Once these texts were prepared, network centralization of the extracted semantic network was used to measure the degree of aligning among team member contributions. Network centralization refers to the extent to which the connectedness within a network is organized around a core set of vertices (i.e., the extent to which the network has a centralized structure) (Scott 1991). The formal definition of centralization relies on the vertex-level measure of in-degree centrality. The in-degree centrality of a vertex refers to the number of edges directed at that vertex (Freeman 1978; Monge and Contractor 2003). Vertices with a high in-degree centrality form hubs within a network (Kleinberg 1999). The in-degree network centralization calculates the differences between the in-degree centrality of each vertex and the largest in-degree centrality within the network and divides the sum of those differences by the maximum possible in-degree (n-1) (Scott 1991), where n equals the number of vertices in the network. More formally, we can define the in-degree network centralization as $\Big ( \sum _ { 1 < i < n } \overline { d } - d _ { i } \Big ) \big / \big ( n - 1 \big )$ where d = the in degree centrality of vertex i and $\vec { d } = m a x \{ d _ { i } | 1 \leq i \leq n \}$

The in-degree centralization provides an appropriate metric for assessing aligning in semantic networks based on texts developed through team discussions. Using in-degree centrality rather than total degree centralization prevents the participant identifiers from overweighting the network analysis. If we had utilized the total degree centralization, the participant identifiers (P1, P2, P3, P4, and P5) that occurred at the beginning of each message would have comprised a significant component of the structural core of the network and would have biased the results of the analysis. Within the networks considered in this study, the vertices with the highest in-degree centrality are those words that are preceded by the greatest variety of words across the messages. This set of vertices makes up the structural core of the network.

During the course of a team discussion, words within the structural core make up a cluster of topics which serve to anchor the team discussion. This core includes words conceptually related to the task (e.g., grades, success, credit, trend) as well as commonly occurring connecting words (e.g., a, the, and). Although one may argue that only the task-related words should make up the core, dialogue is reflected in the alignment and mutual use of both structure (i.e., the same connecting words and phrases) and content (i.e., task specific words) in discussion (Pickering and Garrod 2004). The extent to which words contributed by team members are connected to this core reflects the degree of aligning being carried out by members of the team. A sample visualization from a semantic network used to assess aligning is illustrated in FigureA9. The size of the nodes corresponds to their in-degree centrality.
