# ECONOMIC EXPERIMENTS IN INFORMATION SYSTEMS<sup>1</sup>

Alok Gupta

Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, MN 55455 U.S.A. {alok@umn.edu}

Karthik Kannan

Krannert School of Management, Purdue University, West Lafayette, IN 47907 U.S.A. {kkarthik@purdue.edu}

Pallab Sanyal

School of Business, George Mason University, Fairfax, VA 22030 U.S.A. {psanyal@gmu.edu}

It has been often suggested that much of the widespread use of economic experiments has been possible only because of the developments in information technology. Yet, the information systems (IS) domain has been largely lagging in its use of the methodology. This commentary is aimed at spurring the use of economic experiments in the IS domain by explaining the advantages of the methodology, and by defining its norms for the benefit of both the authors as well as the reviewers.

Keywords: Economic experiments, information systems

## Introduction

Historically, economic methods were considered nonexperimental much like those in other fields such as astronomy. However, since the 1980s, we have seen a rapid growth in the use of experiments in economics to study human behavior and/or market outcomes. In 2002, economic experiments gained further attention as Daniel Kahneman and Vernon Smith were awarded the Nobel memorial prize for their pioneering work. One of the major purposes that economic experiments initially served was, as in any other scientific domain, testing theories. Although testing and updating traditional theories remain the focus of many studies (e.g., Plott and Zeiler 2005), the purpose of economic experiments has expanded over the years. For instance, experiments now act as test beds for studying properties of new market mechanisms (e.g., spectrum auctions). They are also conducted to explain behavioral factors that lead to deviations from rational decisions (e.g., Ariely et al. 2003). Roth (1986) suggests that economic experiments fall on an “imaginary continuum” (p. 245) between those testing and updating formal economic theories and those providing a direct input to policy-making processes.

There are two styles of research employing economic experiments: experimental economics, which usually refers to Smith’s program, and behavioral economics, which usually refers to Kahneman’s program.<sup>2</sup> Given that the information systems (IS) domain is beginning to adopt economic experiments, there are a number of fundamental issues that are worth commenting on that are common to both styles of economic experiments. Therefore, this article focuses on the use of experiments to study human decision-making in an economic context where subjects are incentivized by cash or other forms of extrinsic reward.

Economic experiments can add tremendous value to the IS domain in its current state of affairs. Information technology (IT) continues to advance, with organizations and individuals increasingly becoming information-intensive. Consequently, we are observing that more industries are getting disrupted by a variety of technologies (e.g., Uber disrupting taxi services, 3D printing disrupting traditional supply chains, etc.). Even as information intensiveness has increased, the human aspects to acquiring, consuming, and valuing of information from the decision-making standpoint continue to be important if not more salient. In fact, most of the interesting behaviors occur from the way humans physiologically process information and make decisions based on that information. IS offer powerful technologies to mold, process, analyze and present human decision makers with information. Although traditional economic thought contends that human beings can efficiently process vast amounts of information to make effective decisions driven by economic incentives, empirical evidence suggests otherwise. Thus, economic experiments in IS can bridge the gap between the rational economic models and the capabilities of human decision making. In fact, the interactions between the economic incentives and increased information availability create a number of opportunities for researchers, many of which can be studied using economic experiments. Accordingly, we are observing an increasing number of IS researchers using this methodology (e.g., Adomavicius, Curley et al. 2013; Bapna et al. 2010; Cason et al. 2011; Hashim et al. 2011; Rice 2012; Tsai et al. 2011) to study a variety of important IS issues. Despite an increasing interest in the methodology, due to its relative newness to our community, there is lack of clarity regarding the acceptable standards of its usage among authors and, from our experience, reviewers. Our research commentary is aimed at addressing this gap by introducing the accepted norms of the economic experiments, and providing a framework to guide future research in IS.

Generally, in the IS literature, problems embedded with incentives and strategic interactions (e.g., allocation of bundled assets, online advertisements, etc.) have been studied through analytical modeling and/or empirical analysis of secondary data. Although these methodologies have their benefits, they are also not without limitations. For example, game theoretic models of human behavior usually impose strict rationality assumptions on agents, but research has found that humans do not always behave consistent with that assumption. In addition, without access to the private information of agents, empirical analyses of secondary data may not help explain how behaviors (e.g., an agent faking her cost information) lead to observed outcomes. Moreover, analyses of secondary data can make it difficult to infer causation. Furthermore, in some cases, it may be difficult to establish causal relationships because of issues such as endogeneity. Economic experiments, through its use of controlled settings and exogenous manipulations, can overcome many of these limitations (Ertac and Maximiano 2010).

The use of laboratory experiments to develop theoretical insights and guide the design of systems (e.g., in the development of the technology acceptance model by Bagozzi et al. (1992) or the task–technology fit model by Goodhue (1995)) is not new to IS. But experiments in much of the related literature do not explain how economic incentives drive human behavior, or how the behaviors lead to economic outcomes. Economic experiments can address these issues. Carefully constructed experiments with appropriate economic incentives that seek to induce instinctual behavior while at the same time testing the limits of bounded rationality (Simon 1955) are useful in not only developing valuable insights about human behavior but also designing economic systems. For all the aforementioned reasons, the value that economic experiments offers to studying problems in the IS domain is immense.

This commentary is organized as follows. In the following section, we describe the background related to economic experiments where we also comment on the different types of questions that can be answered through this method. Subsequently, we provide an overarching view of how IS problems relate to some of the question types. In the same vein, we describe some open questions that would be of interest to IS researchers. Finally, we use the economic experiments norms to present some suggestions and guidelines for reviewers.

## Economic Experiments: Background

In this section, we provide a brief historical perspective of experimental economics and, while doing that, we also highlight the unique features of economic experiments. One of the earliest economic works involving human subjects was Thurstone’s (1931), in which he attempted to study individual choices. Specifically, he was interested in plotting the indifference curves for individuals based on their responses to hypothetical questions. Wallis and Friedman (1942) criticized this work because it lacked “actual stimuli” (p. 179). The criticism has resonated well beyond the original experimental work: economic experiments have come to primarily rely on subjects’ behavior determined by their monetary incentives.

In fact, the use of monetary incentives is a distinctive feature of economic experiments that also differentiates it from another social science approach, experimental psychology. As Ariely and Norton (2007) mention, both approaches are similar in focusing on the phenomenon that need to be represented in an experiment to provide meaningful insight, and also by omitting other aspects given the constraints on what can be accomplished. The key difference between the approaches is the manner in which the experiments are operationalized. In experimental psychology, the design focus is on the contextual aspects that are most crucial to the realworld representations; consequently, the design often involves cover stories, confederates, and deception. However, in economic experiments, the emphasis is placed on a design that ensures that the economic incentives in an experiment represent the incentives in the real world and that participants will attempt to maximize.

Experimental economics also benefited from other timely events. Right about the same time as Wallis and Friedman, specifically in 1944, von Neumann and Morgenstern’s Theory of Games and Economic Behavior was published. This book had a profound impact on not only experiments involving individual choices but also economic experiments overall (and economics as a whole). Game theory provided further impetus to experimental economics. As it sought to provide insights on both individual behavior and economic environments, game theory lent itself for experimental validation. By the end of the 1950s, there was plenty of interest in testing general theories using controlled experiments. Concepts such as randomization, tight experimental control, internal and external validity—which are required in any experiment, in any domain—also became important in experimental economics around the same time frame.

Some notable researchers in behavioral economics whose works have significantly influenced the domain are described next. Allais (1953) led to the systematic analysis of violations to the expected utility theory. Following the experiments by Dresher and Flood, Flood (1952, 1958) created the prisoner’s dilemma game to study strategic interactions and their deviations from Nash equilibrium. Explanation of the deviations led to further development in game theory as well. Chamberlin (1948) conducted market experiments where individual utilities were aggregated in the context of supply and demand. He demonstrated that prices do not correspond to the equilibrium point where the supply and demand curves meet. Smith (1962) followed up on Chamberlin’s work to focus on the properties of different market institutions that converge toward equilibrium. His focus on the informational aspect to the attainment of equilibrium was significant. Simon (1955) introduced the concept of bounded rationality, which viewed humans as boundedly rational agents who experience limits in formulating and solving complex problems and in processing (receiving, storing, retrieving, transmitting) information. Kahneman and Tversky have studied the influence of biases in decision-making under uncertainty. Their notable work on prospect theory (Kahneman and Tversky1979) was further developed in 1992 as Tversky and Kahneman (1992). In that regard, Kahneman’s collaborations with Thaler are also significant. Kahneman has credited Thaler (1980) as being the founding text in behavioral economics.

A guiding principle in setting up experiments is simplicity (Smith 1976). The rationale behind a simple design is that, with a parsimonious setup, the researcher can attribute the differences in observations to the manipulations of the experimental environment. The need for a parsimonious design led to another salient feature of economic experiments, which is that abstractions from realistic contexts (see, for example, Ariely and Norton 2007) are permitted. For example, free-riding in piracy may be studied experimentally using a public good game.

Over a period of time, the use of economic experiments in more realistic contexts has increased. Experiments are now being conducted in the field too. Field experiments differ from the ones conducted in a laboratory in their use of “nonstandard subject pool” (Harrison and List 2004, p. 1012), actual goods rather than induced valuations, and/or real contexts rather than abstract framing with an imposed set of rules. Accordingly, Harrison and List (2004) classify a field experiment as artefactual, if it uses a nonstandard subject pool (relative to the standard student subjects used in laboratory experiments); framed, if it uses a field context along with a nonstandard subject pool; and natural, if it uses a natural environment along with a field context and a nonstandard subject pool. Independent of the field, if natural field experiments are feasible, they are arguably the best method to study a phenomenon or compare treatments. So, it would be imprudent to not include them when discussing economic experiments. However, implicitly, the focus of this commentary is on conducting laboratory experiments.

In any case, economic experiments have been recognized to generate values in the following five ways (Smith 1994): (1) testing theories and exploring the causes of their failure, (2) finding empirical regularities as a basis for new theory, (3) comparing environments and institutions, (4) evaluating policy proposals, and (5) testing new mechanisms.

## Classifying Economic Experiments in IS Research

One of the key features of IT is its ability to create information regimes, and many IS researchers are interested in related problems. In any decision-making context, there are three basic steps involving the procurement and use of information: the decision maker acquires the information, processes it, and generates value from the decision. Economic experiments in IS can be classified along similar themes: (1) acquiring information theme, which consists of experiments designed to study the manner in which economic agents seek information to make better decisions, (2) processing information theme, which includes experiments designed to investigate the manner in which the agents process information, and (3) valuing information theme, which consists of experiments designed to understand how the variations in the valuation of information by economic agents affects system outcomes. These three themes differ along the two key features of economic experiments we mentioned in the previous section (i.e., how economic incentives are presented as well as the internal validity requirements). For example, to maintain internal validity, the framing of the experiment may be different, with some themes requiring experiments conducted using abstracted models of real scenarios, others requiring simplistic setups conducive for tractably solving the game, and yet others requiring settings that reflect reality even if they are intractable from a gametheoretic perspective. In the following subsections, we offer a few examples under each theme to highlight the differences across the themes. IS researchers sometimes compare and/or evaluate economic mechanisms that are made possible due to advancement in technologies (see, for example, Bapna et al. 2001), but we do not explicitly discuss that stream because it is well established in economics literature. Note that our goal in this section is not to provide a comprehensive survey of the existing IS economics experiments literature, but rather to highlight the different strands of existing research in this area.

## Acquiring Information Theme

Boundedly-rational real-life agents may employ ad hoc heuristics and/or seek only a limited set of information. Such limitations in turn influence the performance of the economic system. Understanding how agents seek information can guide design decisions regarding the process and policies of new information systems. In this regard, we detail two specific papers: Adomavicius, Curley et al. (2012) and Savikhin et al. (2011).

In combinatorial auctions, bidders have to assess their valuations across various combinations of products. Often, because of cognitive limitations, bidders make valuation decisions amongst only a narrow set of options. However, when bidders are able to realize the valuations more effectively across broader choice sets, the social welfare (i.e., auction efficiency) increases. In that regard, Adomavicius, Curley et al. (2012) compare various information feedback schemes that enable bidders to effectively navigate a wide array of choices in combinatorial auctions. The experimental work adds to the theoretical constructs developed in Adomavicius and Gupta (2005) by investigating the design of a bidder-support infrastructure that can facilitate a continuous form of combinatorial auction, which neither limits the scope to a specific application nor imposes restrictive bidding rules (e.g., discrete bidding rounds) on its participants. A unique aspect of their analysis is the use of click-stream data from experiments to provide insights on the decision making process of the bidders.

Savikhin et al. investigate the decision process that people engage in when choosing a financial portfolio. Specifically, they study how subjects’ decision making when financial details are presented in a textual form compares to when the information is presented in an interactive visual form that helps the consumer quickly create, compare, and choose among several portfolios consisting of different financial instruments. Through an initial set of experiments conducted over different tasks, Savikhin et al. elicit risk preferences of the subjects. Subsequently, they evaluate how close their portfolio selections are to subjects’ risk preferences in the two forms of presentations (textual versus visual). They find that with visual tools, subjects make decisions that are closer to their risk tolerances, indicating the visual platform’s ability to help subjects better understand risk aspects of portfolios. The problem relates to processing of information by left-brained individuals as opposed to right-brained ones.

As the two examples illustrate, the primary interest in the acquiring information theme is to understand the implications of the technology-level design issues and to provide guidance for system implementations. Usually, the problem is studied in an artefactual or a framed context. In both Adomavicius, Curley et al. (2012) and Savikhin et al., the implication of information presentation is characterized in the artefactual context. The context itself may be related to reality or to an underlying game-theoretic formulation. For instance, as mentioned earlier, Adomavicius, Curley et al. (2012) uses theoretical constructs developed in Adomavicius and Gupta (2005).

## Processing Information Theme

When considering the acquisition of information, there was an implicit assumption that it is useful to process information and generate value from it. Otherwise, there is no need for agents to acquire the information. This second theme of research focuses on the processing of information, independent of how the information is acquired; in some cases, the information may be formally sought by agents while in the others, the information may be provided by a third party. Thus, the question that has been the focus of much of the prior research is how do boundedly rational decisions affect system outcomes? We review Bapna et al. (2010) and Cason et al. (2011) as examples of this theme.

How do bidders process incomplete information about overlapping auctions and decide on participation? This question is important because the bidders’ decisions have implications on system-level outcomes. Focusing on this key question, Bapna et al. (2010) consider imperfectly substitutable goods using competing Vickrey auctions that end simultaneously. Using formal models, they theoretically compute Bayesian Nash equilibrium of a game where bidders assess their types (based on private valuations) and assign higher probability to auctions whose attractiveness matches their types but occasionally take chances on auctions that are more attractive. In their experiments, they find, consistent with theory, bidders probabilistically choosing the auctions in which they participate. They also find that the high- and low-valued auctions have the highest concentration of bidders. Based on these observations, they offer insights regarding the design of such overlapping auctions.

In a competitive setting, where learning is involved, how are agents processing other agents’ capabilities to learn and pursue actions? This question is analyzed experimentally in Cason et al. using theory developed in Kannan (2012). Cason et al. analyze how bidders in a sequential auction undertake actions to learn about an opponent’s private information as opposed to preventing the opponent from learning information about oneself. They theoretically consider a specialized version of Kannan but with risk aversion incorporated. The introduction of risk aversion theoretically identifies a behavior that seems counterintuitive at first glance: in order to decrease variance of the payoff, the bidder has to choose an action that appears to be riskier. Using the experiment, Cason et al. find the results to be largely consistent with the theory. Specifically, they find that bidders recognized learning and preventing the opponent from learning to both occur. However, the theoretically observed counterintuitive behavior was not exhibited by human subjects, thereby pointing to their cognitive limitations.

A key experimental design feature of the processing information theme is that, in order to understand processing capabilities and their implications on outcomes, simplistic contexts are formulated and used. The parsimonious structural assumption has often led to experiments using rational choice theory models, including game theoretic ones. Thus, experiments in this theme are used to test theories and identify the behaviors that lead to the system outcomes that we observe. Additionally, papers in this theme also focus on comparing policies and institutions.

Other examples of this theme include Adomavicius, Bockstedt et al. (2013), who use anchoring effects (Tversky and Kahneman 1974) to explain consumers’ use of product recommender systems, and Bichler (2000) and Adomavicius, Gupta, and Sanyal (2012) who compare the performance of multi-attribute auctions under various information regimes.

## Valuing Information Theme

IS research has long been interested in valuing information systems, and there is a large body of literature addressing that topic. By extension, there is also interest in experiments to understand how an individual values information. Addressing this question is useful in enabling implementations of new mechanisms that facilitate transactions of information or information services. Such implementations have to take into account significant variations in how information is valued across and within the population. These types of tensions can result in irrational behaviors such as loss aversion. In that regard, we describe two studies—Acquisti et al. (2009) and Dyaberi et al. (2012)—to illustrate the benefits of experiments for research in this category.

One of the important challenges of the interconnected digital society is the protection of our privacy. On one hand, consumers want to protect their private information but, on the other, firms can often benefit if consumers part with their private information. Recent studies (e.g., Acquisti and Varian 2005) have shown that firms must offer some incentives to acquire customers’ private data. What incentives a firm should offer depends on how consumers value their private information. While addressing this question, Acquisti et al. bring to light the asymmetry in the valuation of private information. In their experimental setup, subjects are randomly assigned to two different treatments: one subset of the population is paid to share private information and the other is penalized for not sharing the same private information. Even though the expected payment is identical across the two treatments, Acquisti et al. observe differences in how the subjects react to the two treatments. From their analyses, they propose managerial insights regarding how private information can be collected through the use of appropriate incentives.

As economic systems involving transaction of information or information services become more prevalent, the implementations of such new systems have to take into consideration similar trade-offs between rewards and penalties. However, context-specific questions may arise, requiring economic experiments for investigation. Consider, for example, network congestion—a key problem facing cellular network providers. Dyaberi et al. employ an artefactual field experiment to study how to alleviate congestion using rewards to cellular users for backing-off from using services at highly congested towers or punishments for not backing-off. The authors distributed free iPhones (with unlimited data and minutes) to subjects over a six month period. Subjects received messages about rewards or punishments—similar to the ones they may see in a real congestions scenario—and their compliance behaviors were analyzed. The compliance responses were combined with Big Five psychological measures (Costa and McCrae 1992) to understand subject characteristics that led to compliance and whether the compliance was achieved with incentives or disincentives.

Note that both of the aforementioned studies deal with loss aversion issues. Furthermore, an important element of the information valuing theme is the need for the experiments to be relatively closer to reality, especially when studying other aspects such as the psychological measures related to compliance, which is related to implementation of new systems. Finally, when comparing competing policies—such as rewards versus punishments—it is critical that the theoretically expected payoffs for the experimental subjects are the same in the alternative policies being studied. That way one can ensure that the experiment is internally valid and any change in the outcomes measured can be primarily attributed to the policy change.

## Identifying Experimental Economic IS Research Problems

This section serves two purposes. First, we provide a general framework in the context of information that may be useful to identify IS research questions conducive to economic experiments. Second, we offer several examples of open IS questions that can be addressed using economic experiments.

## Frameworks for Identification of IS Research Problems

In business and economics, we have long believed that human behavior is mainly shaped through economic incentives. Thus, if a firm wants to increase productivity, we generally believe that it has to pay more. The idea of using economic incentives to shape behavior is rooted in the early 1900s when businesses started to function as organizations, and concepts such as scientific management principles were in vogue (Pink 2009). From a psychological standpoint, though, we have long known that extrinsic rewards, including economic incentives, do not always dictate human behaviors. It is even recognized that our brain is physiologically oriented toward pursuing actions—such as to compete (e.g., battles and wars), collaborate (e.g., tribes and communities), create and discover (e.g., fire)—that are not always driven by extrinsic rewards. In fact, some of those actions have helped humans survive and flourish across the ages. One way to view economic experiments is to analyze how our instincts and biases are affecting our relatively recent emphasis on monetary incentives as drivers. Note that human decision making in the context of information is the key consideration. One possible way to develop the framework is by considering the problems from the human physiological standpoint.

From the physiological perspective, the prefrontal cortex is a key component involved in analyses of information, which in turn is crucial to decision making. Thus, the prefrontal cortex is also referred to as the seat of rationality, although it is understood to be buggy (Lehrer 2009). For one, the prefrontal cortex is one of the last regions of the brain to reach maturation. Further, the amount of energy required for its operation is quite large when compared to that required for the same level of operation in other parts of the brain. Perhaps these attributes of the prefrontal cortex—the recency of maturation as well as the quantity of energy consumption—lead to humans being boundedly rational.

Consistent with the boundedly rational theme, evidence now suggests that the brain can suffer from information overload. For example, the brain is known to make suboptimal decisions or to even completely avoid decision making when the number of choices is beyond five to nine depending on the individual (Iyengar 2010). Given that pattern recognition (e.g., of faces) is hardwired into our brain, we generally prefer visual representation of information, including when we acquire and process information for decision making. The example research we discussed in the acquiring information theme relates to information overload problems. Variants of the information overload problem may also be worth pursuing. For example, when the data available in the business context is noisy, it may be worth studying how the data is interpreted as information. Such a problem can have implications in supply chain design, for example, where information provisions play a critical role. When dealing with studying information acquisition, the need for internal validity of experiments demands the setting to be as realistic as possible. Obviously, field experiments satisfy the criteria. Alternatively, artefactual experiments may also be used. The value generated from such an analyses may be useful for designing technology interfaces and also to analyze policy outcomes. Clearly, effective ways of presenting, interpreting, and analyzing information have been an interest to IS researchers; experimental economic research in these domains can provide significant insights beyond technical and system effectiveness.

Because the processing ability of the prefrontal cortex is limited, its decision-making procedure is not always optimal. When faced with a complex decision, our brain often resorts to heuristics to solve the problem. For example, stochasticity is a difficult concept for the prefrontal cortex to handle. Anecdotally, we have observed (during class interactions) that stochasticity is often substituted for periodicity (e.g., conditional on an event having occurred from a random process, subjects often place a lower probability for the same event occurring out of the same random process even when the second random process is independent of the first). Similarly, the heuristics adopted may be influenced by external factors, such as recent news events, and recollection (e.g., Kahneman et al. 1982). Note that, in games where strategic considerations are important, the system outcomes also depend on agents’ understanding of deviations from rational behaviors exhibited by other competing agents. Furthermore, the deviations are often not the same. For example, some people may be predisposed to using the left brain as opposed to the right for processing information (Sperry’s 1981 Nobel prize winning work). When subjects do not have the ability to fully understand, the system outcomes can be quite different. For example, when people with autism played the proposer role in the ultimatum game, they did not play the strategy of splitting fairly, which occurs with the general population; they played the equilibrium strategy. In fact, they complained to the experimenter that the responder in the ultimatum game did not play appropriately (Sally and Hill 2006). From an experimental standpoint, the focus of this theme should be on the simplicity, and game theoretic models often should satisfy the simplistic requirement. The usefulness of economic experiments within this realm is to compare the system-level outcomes and, in some cases, even to generate insights on the behaviors that lead to the system-level outcomes.

Our typical expectation that the brain evaluates all alternative choices alone using the prefrontal cortex is misplaced. In fact, it may be surprising to realize that purchasing decisions are also driven by the tension between the chemicals in nucleus accumbens and insula (both of which are not part of the prefrontal cortex) (Knutson et al. 2007). It also means that the manner in which the context is presented can play a role. When presenting research in the valuing information theme, the demonstration of loss aversion was done in a similar fashion. Note that some of these instincts may be more fundamental. For instance, in one experiment involving monkeys, loss aversion has been demonstrated (Silberberg 2008). Clearly, the realism of the environment is extremely important when invoking this theme and, therefore, natural or field experiments are typically preferred. The value generated from economic experiments is to understand how to frame the contexts to shape behavior. In the next subsection, we consider some possible open questions that would be of interest to IS researchers based on the theoretical frameworks discussed in this subsection.

## Open Questions

Consider RedHat Linux’s business model, which is to provide open-source product maintenance to the enterprise community. It is a billion dollar company, and yet it has no direct control over the product it serves; the Linux core is regularly updated and managed independently of RedHat. One can clearly expect mismatches between RedHat’s requirements and the developer community’s interest. So, for RedHat to thrive, it is not sufficient to simply react to the development by the Linux core; it has to nudge the developers into developing features that are a priority for its target market. Under these circumstances, building a one billion dollar business is interesting. It is a classic example of developing a system with economic outcomes that are shaped by other drivers such as indirect economic benefits and intrinsic rewards. In a broader sense, it relates to the discussion of designing open innovation platforms, which is gaining importance as more companies are pursuing crowdsourcing initiatives. Current theoretical models of crowdsourcing are limited in many ways. For example, they often involve one decision variable—effort—even though many other variables may also be involved depending on the innovation task at hand. Furthermore, sometimes, design decisions may be made sequentially but it is never captured in the theoretical model. Finally, designers’ strategies may evolve due to external factors such as additional information about competitors. Therefore, studies addressing questions of how to set up these crowdsourcing platforms are conducive for experimental research.

The previously mentioned scenario also raises questions related to open- versus closed-software production. In a typical software company, a developer’s work directly contributes to the profit of the company but in the context of Linux development, the contribution is indirect. This raises the question: How is the effort level of the developer different across the two scenarios, all else being equal? Developers in the open source community are not necessarily driven by extrinsic rewards. Then, how much better or worse is the product created by the open source community of developers as opposed to that created by the closed-sourced community of developers (such as those in Microsoft), all else being equal? When intrinsic rewards shape behavior, how should firms such as RedHat nudge developers into pursuing actions consistent with the company? These problems can be studied experimentally using an abstracted game. For example, a hierarchical public good framework may be considered to model the behaviors in the context of the developer.

Another domain where experimental research has the potential to make an impact is piracy and information security. In the piracy context, Hashim et al. (2017) study the issue of the everybody-else-does-it attitude motivated by piracy. Many organizations related to the music industry claim that piracy is extensive (IFPI claims that 95% of music was pirated in 2009<sup>3</sup>) but many pirates continue to engage in the activity, pointing to this large piracy rate as the reason. Motivated by these observations, Hashim et al. (2014) experimentally examine the role of advising in the context of public good and piracy. Both of the above articles fall in the category of understanding the agents’ information processing ability. While these interesting questions are focused on consumers of piracy, there is little prior work on what drives the producers of piracy to make the songs/movies available for others to pirate. Thus, this is another rich area of inquiry where IS researchers can focus on questions such as: (1) How is the production of piracy influenced by one’s own consumption? (2) How can producers of pirated music be nudged away from making the songs/movies available for pirating by others?

Similarly, in the information security space, managers are forced to make decisions without appreciable knowledge of the value of information. Specifically, even if the information can be valued, the uncertainty regarding the probability of events—such as loss—further complicates the expected value computations. When designing policies for such contexts, behavioral issues that arise because of the lack of precise estimates need to be taken into account. To the best of our knowledge, the amount of experimental work in these types of scenarios is limited but is worth pursuing.

## Designing Economic Experiments in IS: Suggestions for IS Researchers and Reviewers

In this section, we focus on the design aspects of experiments so as to provide guidelines regarding the use of economic experiments in IS.

When designing such experiments, a fundamental aspect that has to be clarified is the rationale for the experiment. For instance, economic experiments are valuable when there is a need to test a wide range of treatments. Further, experiments allow us to test the treatments across multiple samples of the population.

In studies where experiments test game-theoretic results, the issues are different depending on whether the focus is on measuring system outcomes (such as price, quantity, etc.) or explaining anomalies in strategic behaviors. While in the former, the effort is to capture the outcome measures accurately, the design objective in the latter is to portray the game theoretic model as closely as possible so that the focus is on the subjects’ behaviors. In some instances, actions such as eliciting beliefs can be used to offer insights on the observed behaviors. For example, the theoretical model in Cason et al. (2011) determines the Bayesian Nash equilibrium; naturally, whether subjects adhere to such equilibrium depends signifi cantly on the belief update mechanism they adopt. So, in that study, the researchers elicit belief information by offering suitable incentives to the subjects. In general, though, it may not always be possible to derive appropriate equilibrium insights or, even if it is possible, the computed equilibria may not be stable. For example, some market mechanisms such as combinatorial auctions have intractable analytical solutions. In those cases, the main focus is on developing insights regarding the outcomes to guide the design of the actual implementation.

Once the main structure of the experiment is decided, the focus shifts to the following three main issues: (1) input to the experiment (subjects and how they are engaged in the experiment); (2) activities during the experiment (treatments, etc.); and (3) conclusion of the experiments (demographic survey, etc.).

The choice of the subject pool is often raised as a concern by the IS audience. Many of the laboratory experiments in our domain often involve students from academic institutions. Would the results so obtained be representative of the actual student population? It is natural to expect the cardinal measures of outcomes to be different depending on the student population (undergraduates versus graduates), cultural differences, etc. However, when focusing on ordinal measures of policies, this issue may become less relevant. The experimental economics literature has extensively addressed these issues. In general, researchers have found that nonstudents behave much like students. For example, Lichtenstein and Slovic (1973) replicated, using casino-goers in Las Vegas, the same set of results on choices that they had identified earlier (Lichtenstein and Slovic 1971) using student subjects. Similarly, the gift-exchange game, run with nonstudent subjects, produced results similar to those obtained with student subjects (Fehr et al. 1998).

Even with ordinal insights, the key test for accepting such insights is to study whether the real- and experimentalcontexts interact differently in any explicit way with the alternate policy regimes, potentially leading to different outcomes.

If not, then such insights may indeed be carried forward. This issue of abstract versus nonabstract models has been considered in the economics literature—Abbink and Schmidt (2006) show that abstract and nonabstract models can indeed generate the same ordinal outcomes.

Next, consider issues related to the activities during the experiments. The first among them are the manner in which treatments (or policies) and sessions are set up. Issues such as order effects, which can arise in within-subject designs, need to be accounted for.

When dealing with contexts different from what has been motivated to study, the relevance of the insights may be limited due to the game form recognition problem, which Levitt and List (2009) and Chou et al. (2010) note. They point out that, if a problem is presented in a different form, the human subjects may perceive the core issue differently. Hence, their recognized optimal strategies may not be consistent across the settings and, consequently, any inference about the behaviors or the outcomes is subject to the game form recognition problem. Chou et al. provide some examples of questionnaires to rule out the game form recognition problem. Authors of experimental work have to be careful to make an argument why the game form representation may not be relevant in their case.

Another related issue that concerns the IS audience is the abstracted nature of the experiment. When considering alternate forms of presenting information or understanding the valuation of the information, the realism of the experiment is important. So, in those cases, natural, field, or artefactual experiments are preferred. However, when the issue is about the processing of information, abstraction should not be a concern so long as ordinal comparisons of alternate policies are studied, and both the treatment design and abstraction issues do not confound the behavior of the subjects.

In a group decision-making context, how should the subjects be grouped to participate within each session? This question is the same whether the experimental design involves a strangers or a partners design. In some settings, particularly involving competitions in markets, there may be potential for collusion among partners in the laboratory because of the smallness of the competition while it may not be the case in real life. In such cases, employing the same set of subjects to compete against one another can lead to insights different from that in the real world. This problem may be overcome by grouping the subjects randomly so that they are strangers. The implications of strangers versus partners have also been extensively studied in the experimental economics literature, where strangers are often used to test one-shot interactions while partners are typically used for repeated interactions.

There is a divergent view on whether demographic information collected at the conclusion of the experiments is relevant. Demographic information may be important when the experimental setting is quite representative of the real scenario. However, that may not be the case in a market setting involving competition. The other oft-raised question in the IS domain concerns asking subjects about their actions during the game. The notion of asking the subjects about their actions in the game is not considered useful. Responses, which are elicited without incentives, are often attributed to cheap talk (Farrell and Rabin 1996). Moreover, given that our focus is on understanding implicit behavior, asking questions to explicitly characterize the behavior may not make sense.

In our experience, two other concerns are raised within the IS community regarding the conduct of experiments. An oftraised concern is whether the treatment and/or the subject pool is conveniently chosen. For example, when considering the implications of having a social tie or not, the IS audience may have concerns if the experiment uses the tie with the parents (with whom the social tie is perhaps the strongest) to build insights. It is generally accepted in the experimental community that the treatments and the subject pools may be chosen so as to generate a stark contrast in the behaviors or the outcomes. The issue of external validity (i.e., to what extent can the insights gained in the laboratory be extrapolated to the world beyond, is also raised. Typically, in laboratory studies it is mostly relevant to ask whether the qualitative results are externally valid (for a detailed discussion, see Camerer 2014). That is, the emphasis of the conclusions is on the direction rather than the magnitude of the effect. Consequently, many laboratory studies use nonparametric statistical methods (which rely on qualitative differences) to infer significance (see, for example, Sanyal 2016). Still, there can be tension between internal and external validity since internal validity often requires abstraction and simplification to make the research more tractable, but these concessions come at the cost of decreasing external validity. It is important that the experimenters address the external validity concerns also. For instance, to improve experimental external validity, one may use randomized probability samples drawn from the appropriate population (e.g., Tipton et al. 2014). Further, to test the robustness of the results, researchers may use multiple samples (e.g., Amazon Mechanical Turk along with students). In general, we believe what Falk and Heckman (2009) have asserted: “Field data, survey data, and experiments, both lab and field, as well as standard econometric methods, can all improve the state of knowledge in social sciences” (p. 537). Each method has its pros and cons. For instance, while field experiments may have greater external validity, lab experiments have greater internal validity and are also more easily replicated. As with any other methodology, the researcher should choose to use laboratory experiments when they are appropriate for answering the research questions, and also should be mindful of the limitations of the method.

Traditionally, we have used economic experiments in IS to test theories, which relates to the deductive approach of enquiry. The deductive approach is top-down; it begins development of hypotheses based on theories, which are then tested using observations, and ultimately the observations are used to confirm or revise the original theories. The inductive approach is the opposite (i.e., observations are used to recognize patterns, based on which tentative hypotheses and eventually theories are developed). Experimental economics also has been recognized to serve as “a method of systematic inductive enquiry” (Sugden 2008, p. 623). Consistently, the use of economic experiments has the potential even in IS to lead to newer theories. Authors should clearly indicate the type of enquiry they plan to pursue.

Finally, we believe, IS researchers should be able to contribute to economic experiments as well. For instance, the collection and analysis of clickstream data of experiment subjects by IS researchers (e.g., Adomavicius, Curley et al. 2012) opens the door to explore the decision making process of economic agents along with the eventual outcome. In future, IS researchers can use technology to conduct experiments where subjects are allowed to program their participation—which would allow for generating insights more directly (as opposed to inferring from their actions).

## Conclusion

It has been suggested that much of the growth in economic experiments methodology has been possible only because of the developments in information technology (Bardsley et al. 2009). Yet, the IS domain has been largely lagging in the use of the methodology. Our commentary is aimed at spurring the use of this methodology among IS researchers by explaining the benefits of the methodology, and by defining its norms for the benefit of authors as well as reviewers.

Note that, although the experimental methodology offers an alternative mechanism to develop insights for our field, it is by no means presented as a panacea. Apart from problemspecific concerns, this methodology is also subject to Duhem-Quine thesis<sup>4</sup> just like any other empirics based methodology.
