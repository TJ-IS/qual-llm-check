# THE EFFECTS OF COMMUNICATION MEDIA AND CULTURE ON DECEPTION DETECTION ACCURACY<sup>1</sup>

Joey F. George Debbie and Jerry Ivy College of Business, Iowa State University, Ames, IA 50011 U.S.A. {jfgeorge@iastate.edu}

As the world “gets smaller” and more people engage in cross-cultural communications, their ability to success fully separate truth from deception can be critically important. Yet it is challenging. While deceptive communi cation has been studied for decades, some areas are not well understood. In particular, two areas that could benefit from further research concern the effects of cultural differences and communication media on deception and its detection. Building on developments in theories of deception and its detection, we examine the question: How do differences in culture between senders and receivers affect deception detection, especially where the deceptive communication occurs across different media? To address this question, stimulus materials from recorded interviews were created featuring participants from the United States, Spain, and India. Three stimulus sets were created, one each in American English, Spanish, and Indian English, and each consisting of 32 interview snippets. Half of the snippets were honest and half were dishonest. Each snippet represented one of four media: full audio-visual, video only, audio only, and text only. Veracity judges were also recruited from the same three countries as the interview participants, to independently observe and evaluate the communication both within their culture and across other cultures. Evidence was found that different combinations of cultural and media effects affected the accuracy of deception detection.

Keywords: Computer-mediated communications, deceptive communication, deception detection, culture

## Introduction

We live in a time when interaction between people of different cultures and languages is at an all-time high, due in part to an increase in the availability of information and communication technologies (ICTs). For example, in 2014, Skype alone accounted for 40% of the conventional international telecom market, up by 36% over 2013 and growing (Gara 2014). Increasingly, Skype and similar technologies are being used not just for personal communications but also for business-oriented activities: job interviews, interviews with prospective students from different parts of the world, virtual teams support, and distributed systems development teams.

Information systems (IS) researchers know from past research that both personal and business communication often contains a deceptive component (DePaulo et al. 1996; George and Robb 2008; Hancock et al. 2004). Researchers also know that people are not very good at detecting deception, being correct only about 54% of the time (Bond and DePaulo 2006). The relationship between communication media and accurate deception detection is currently not well understood. Several studies have investigated deception detection within one mediated medium, such as text (Zhou and Zhang 2007), but only a few have compared deception detection success across media (Dunbar et al. 2015). Fewer still have investigated deception detection across media and cultures (Furner and George 2012; Lewis and George 2008).

Communications between people from different cultures, regardless of media, can sometimes be problematic, and intentionally deceptive communication can cause more serious problems—for those communicating, for border guards, for law enforcement, and for local governments (Castillo 2013; Taylor et al. 2015). How can people tell if their cross-cultural communication, regardless of media, is based on honesty or deceit? Can people successfully detect deception in people from other cultures?

Some past research indicates that successful deception detection across cultures may not be possible. There are two perspectives on whether people should be able to detect deception in other cultural groups (Bond et al. 1990; Castillo 2013). One perspective, the specific discrimination perspective, argues that language and cultural differences make deception detection particularly difficult. The other view, the universal cue perspective, holds that deception detection should be equally challenging regardless of the culture of the sender (deceiver) or the receiver (person being deceived). Only three studies have explicitly investigated deception detection across cultures: Bond et al. (1990) found that people could not detect deception in those from other cultural groups, while Al-Simadi (2000) and Bond and Atoum (2000) found that people could detect deception just as well with people from other cultures as with people from their own culture. Given that past findings are contradictory, we need additional research to better understand the relationships among media, culture, and deception.

To that end, the current study examines theory and research at the intersection of media, culture, and deception detection. Its purpose is to investigate the role played by common computer-mediated communication media in the detection of deception by people from one cultural group interacting with people from another cultural group. Stimulus materials were created from recorded interviews in which students from the United States, Spain, and India defended scholarship applications they had intentionally and falsely enhanced. Three stimulus sets were created, one each in American English, Spanish, and Indian English. Each set consisted of 32 snippets from the interviews, where half were honest and half were dishonest. Each snippet represented one of four communication media: full audio-visual, video only, audio only, or text. Veracity judges were recruited from the same three countries as the interviewees: the United States, Spain, and India. We found evidence of a media effect: Judges were worse at detecting deception when viewing video without sound, compared with other media, regardless of the culture of the judge or the interviewee. We also found that judges could accurately detect deception in other cultural groups; two of the three groups of judges were just as good at detecting deception in other groups as in their own, providing support for the universal cue hypothesis. While all three groups of judges relied on the same set of cues to detect deception, they relied on them to different extents. This implies that the universal cue hypothesis is valid to a point, after which cultural differences and expectations play a role. Further, we found an interaction between culture and media, such that media combined with culture affects the accuracy of deception detection.

Media theories, such as media synchronicity theory (MST) (Dennis et al. 2008), focus on the channel over which the message is transmitted and the noise that may interfere with transmission. They devote less attention to the source of the message and its destination. On the other hand, theories of deceptive communication focus on the source of the deceptive message and the destination, that is, the person being deceived; the channel and any noise that affects it, is of relatively less interest. While these theories, such as interpersonal deception theory (IDT) (Buller and Burgoon 1996), recognize media variations in cue transmission to include media in the overall communication process, their explicit treatment of media is relatively sparse. This is because most research into deception and its detection has historically focused on real-time face-to-face communication. Yet as modern communication expands to include more channels, many of which are computer-mediated, the role of the channel has increased in importance. Different media and the channels that constitute them (Griffith and Northcraft 1994) vary in their ability to transmit cues to deceptive communication (Rao and Lim 2000). Investigating the role of media in deception and its detection would require expanding current theories of deceptive communication to include key elements of communication, such as the communication channel (and any related noise), the source and destination of the transmission, and the nature of the message. Elements of strong media theories such as MST can be used to fill this gap.

Theories of cultural differences, such as those of Hofstede (1980) and the GLOBE group (House et al. 2004), deal with neither communication nor deception. Instead, they examine why and how groups of people differ from each other. These differences affect how people communicate with and understand each other, due to differences in language and in nonverbal communication practices across groups. While MST and other media theories largely do not consider the characteristics of the source and destination of messages, theories of deceptive communication do, as part of the overall communication context. However, the universal cue hypothesis from the deception literature (Bond et al. 1990) holds that culture is not an important factor in deception and its detection. Instead, this hypothesis posits that everyone, regardless of culture, transmits the same cues to deception. This implies that everyone is able to recognize these cues, regardless of the culture of the source. This may explain why cultural differences have had a relatively minor role in studies of deceptive communication.

Given the constraints of current theories of media, deception, and culture, and the need to integrate them to study issues at the intersection of these three areas, we propose that a theory of deceptive communication be expanded to explicitly include elements of media and of culture. Starting with Shannon and Weaver’s theory of communication, we integrate MST, IDT, and Hofstede’s theories on national culture and its various dimensions, to address key research questions at the intersection of media, deception, and culture. ## Media and Deception

Over the years, research on deception and its detection has yielded interesting insights. Much of this research has been guided by interpersonal deception theory (IDT) (Buller and Burgoon 1996). IDT treats deception as a dyadic “interactive” communication event between a sender and a receiver. According to IDT, interpersonal deceptive communication takes place in three phases: (1) pre-interaction, (2) the interaction, and (3) post-interaction. The pre-interaction phase is distinguished by its focus on what the sender and receiver bring to the interaction before it begins. The context of the interaction and the preexisting relationship between the sender and receiver are given superordinate status, that is, they are the overarching factors upon which understanding the deceptive interaction depends. The communication context has five features: (1) access to social cues, (2) immediacy, (3) relational engagement, (4) conversational demands, and (5) spontaneity. While the latter three features are part of the context and primarily illustrate the differences between interactivity and lack of it, the first two features focus on communication channels. For conveying social cues, the face-to-face context is best, as it provides “access to the range of social information available in environmental, visual, auditory, and verbal channels” (Buller and Burgoon 1996, p. 212). In contrast, less interactive contexts such as audio or written media, “restrict channel and information availability, producing a limited cues environment that may alter behaviors and perceptions” (ibid., p. 212). Immediacy has both spatial and temporal components and refers to the here and now.

Spatial immediacy is created through a combination of nonverbal behaviors that engage the senses (specifically, proximity, gaze, body lean, body orientation, and touch) .… Temporal immediacy is created when interchanges occur in real time (i.e., they are synchronous) (ibid., pp. 212-213).

While face-to-face communication would be characterized as having high levels of both spatial and temporal immediacy, audio only communication would be temporally immediate but spatially distant, and written communication would be both spatially and temporally distant. Buller and Burgoon (1996) argue that sender and receiver cognitions and behavior will vary systematically as the deceptive communication contexts vary (Proposition 1, p. 214).

According to IDT, deceptive messages typically have three parts: (1) the primary message, (2) ancillary messages, whether verbal or nonverbal that support the credibility of the primary message, and (3) inadvertent behaviors that indicate the message may be deceptive. These inadvertent behaviors are referred to as “leakage,” a term first coined by Ekman and Friesen (1969). Deception is a complicated process to control, so deceivers often leak cues in the form of inadvertent verbal and nonverbal behaviors (Buller and Burgoon 1996; Ekman 1985; Ekman and Friesen 1969). Leakage allows for deception to be detected. Thus, if receivers are observant to the verbal and nonverbal behaviors of others, then deceivers stand a better chance of getting caught. Carlson et al. (2004) suggest that “successful detection of deception is determined by the participants involved, their relationship, the design and delivery of the deception, and the medium used to convey the communication” (p. 14).

Empirical research on media and deception detection largely focuses on one medium at a time. For example, Hancock et al. (2008) compared honest to dishonest chat sessions. Zhou et al. (2004b) and Zhou et al. (2004c) compared honest and dishonest e-mail exchanges. Zhou et al. (2004a) studied deceptive and nondeceptive content from a web-based messaging tool. Other IS studies investigating deception and honesty in a single medium have examined online dating (Lo et al. 2013), online games (Pak and Zhou 2013), e-commerce websites (Abbasi et al. 2010; Xiao and Benbasat 2011), phishing attempts (Wright and Marett 2010; Wright et al. 2014), and group-based tasks (Fuller et al. 2012; Zhou et al. 2014; Zhou and Zhang 2006).

Another body of work in the communication and IS disciplines has compared deception detection across two or more media in a single study to determine if the media in which the messages are exchanged affects deception detection accuracy. The findings are mixed (Table 1). Some studies have found evidence of a direct relationship between media and deception detection (e.g., Burgoon et al. 2003; Dunbar et al. 2015), while others have not (e.g., Burgoon et al. 2010). Still other studies found that the relationship between media and accurate deception detection was mediated by a third variable (e.g., Dunbar et al. 2014; George et al. 2008).

Thus, while the exact nature of the relationship between media and deception detection is still under investigation, research results (Table 1) collectively show there is a relationship. From the perspective of deception detection, the media differ by the number of cues to deception leaked by senders that each medium can convey. In IDT, the communication context is regarded as a higher level construct: Buller and Burgoon (1996) suggest its effects can be tested by operationalizing the communication context as communication media. While all cues to deception are available face-to-face, the number of available cues gets “filtered out” as the communication medium changes from synchronous to asynchronous and from more social cues to fewer cues being provided (Adkins and Brashers 1995; Daft et al. 1987; Sproull and Kiesler 1986). DePaulo et al. (2003) conducted a metaanalysis of reliable cues or indicators of deception, using 120 independent samples. They identified 88 indicators that met their criteria for further study. Table 2 shows the 23 indicators that had the largest effect sizes, listed according to which indicators would be available in audiovisual, video only, audio, or written media. Of all the computer-mediated communication media, an audiovisual medium is capable of transmitting the maximum number of cues, with comparatively fewer cues revealed in other media. Rao and Lim (2000) linked a medium’s capability to transmit a wide variety of verbal and nonverbal cues to the accuracy of identifying deception, such that the more cues available, the higher the deception detection rates, and vice versa. Table 2 shows that audiovisual media can convey all 23 cues, while audio, text, and video only can convey 20, 12, and 6 cues respectively. ## H1: The more cues to deception that are available in a communication medium, the more accurate the detection of deception.

While simply counting the number of cues that a medium can convey is one way to predict successful deception detection for that medium, media theories show media to be more complex. According to MST (Dennis et al. 2008), communication media can be described in terms of five media capabilities: symbol sets, parallelism, transmission velocity, rehearsability, and reprocessibility. Symbol sets are the number of ways in which a medium allows information to be encoded for communication. People can communicate through many different types of symbols, ranging from physical to oral/verbal to written to nonverbal. Parallelism reflects the number of simultaneous transmissions that can effectively take place. Specifically, Dennis et al. (2008) define parallelism as “the extent to which signals from multiple senders can be transmitted over the medium simultaneously” (p. 585). Parallelism is more easily facilitated in newer media, such as synchronous electronic conferencing. Transmission velocity is the speed at which a medium can deliver a message to intended recipients. For media high in transmission velocity, messages spend little time in transmission, more readily supporting synchronous communication. Rehearsability is the extent to which the medium enables the sender to rehearse or fine-tune a message during encoding, before sending. Rehearsability is of greater importance for the transmission of new or complex information and less important when communicating with others who share common experiences and an understanding of the context of the communication.

Reprocessibility is the extent to which the medium enables a message to be reexamined or processed again, during decoding, either within the context of the communication event or after the event has passed (Dennis et al. 2008, p. 587, emphasis added).

Whereas rehearsability may slow the encoding of a message, reprocessibility may slow its decoding.

In their Table 2 (p. 589), Dennis et al. (2008) provided ratings of media capabilities across several media. Table 3 shows the results of matching the media from MST and our study. Except for the video only mode, all values are from MST. A given communication medium can be considered high on some capabilities and low on others. For example, face-toface communication provides for multiple symbol sets (since it can simultaneously transmit physical, oral, verbal, and nonverbal symbols), has limited ability to support multiple parallel messages (possible but difficult if more than two people are conversing simultaneously), has rapid transmission velocity, and supports very limited if any rehearsability or reprocessibility. At the other extreme, written media typically support only written symbol sets, can support parallel communication efforts, can offer rapid (instant messaging) or slow (e-mail) transmission velocity, and can easily provide for both rehearsability and reprocessibility.<sup>3</sup>

Extending IDT beyond its current perspective on media, we can hypothesize that each media capability plays its own role in sending and receiving cues to deception, which in turn affects deception detection. Multiple symbol sets imply the transmission and reception of more cues, since each symbol set would convey its own unique set of cues to deception. If more cues result in better detection (as predicted by H1), then media that could convey more symbol sets would enhance detection efforts. Reprocessibility would also aid deception detection, regardless of the number of sets available, as receivers could study the cues more carefully, leading to better detection (Jensen and Chidambaram 2015). The other three media capabilities appear to hamper deception detection. High transmission velocity would impose a heavier burden on the receiver, as exchange would be instantaneous, leaving limited opportunity for the receiver to observe and digest all of the cues being transmitted. Similarly, high parallelism could overwhelm the receiver, as multiple messages and the cues they contain would require too much cognitive effort to notice and process them all. Rehearsability would help the sender but not the receiver, as the sender would have the opportunity to carefully craft a deceptive message and control multiple aspects of it (Jensen and Chidambaram 2015).

Given our study design, where interactive and unrehearsed interviews were recorded for later judgment by third-party judges, we can test some of these ideas about media capabilities but not others. Rehearsabilty is not relevant here, as it typically aids the sender, and the focus here is on the receiver. Parallelism would be applicable under conditions that facilitated it, for example, if communication occurred over synchronous electronic conferencing. However, we limited our design to the four modes of communication listed in Table 2, so testing hypotheses about parallelism is not possible. One could posit, however, that successful deception detection would be difficult for media high in parallelism, as the types of cues, their volume, and their simultaneous transmission could easily distract the receiver, leading to difficulties in noticing some cues and in determining what parts of the transmission to attend to.

We therefore test hypotheses about the other three media capabilities.

H2a: Judges will be better at deception detection with media that are capable of transmitting multiple symbol sets rather than fewer symbol sets.

H2b: Judges will be better at deception detection with media that provide high levels of reprocessibility rather than low levels of reprocessibility.

H2c: Judges will be worse at deception detection with media characterized by high levels of transmission velocity rather than low levels of transmission velocity.

## Deception and Culture

IDT is silent on the topic of deception and culture. The closest IDT comes to a treatment of culture is in its discussion of familiarity (Buller and Burgoon 1996). IDT splits familiarity into two aspects: shared history and valence. A shared history provides territory the deceiver knows not to cross, as a deception about some past shared event would be readily seen as untrue by the receiver. For valence, a positive relationship leads both parties to expect truthful exchanges, while a negative relationship would begin with a certain amount of distrust and suspicion. While the interaction of context and familiarity influence the pre-interaction phase, IDT assumes that all individuals come to the conversation with similar expectations:

People in normal (truthful) face-to-face interactions are expected to display, and in actuality do typically display, moderately high immediacy and involvement; moderate arousal; positive affect; and relatively fluent, smooth performances that mesh with those of cointeractants (Buller and Burgoon 1996, p. 219).

However, there is a growing literature on cultural differences and deception. An explicit treatment of culture and its relationship to deception and its detection would complement IDT and add a new dimension to its treatment of familiarity.

## Culture

Of the many conceptualizations of national culture, Hofstede’s is the most cited and well known (Srite and Karahanna 2006). Hofstede defined national culture as “the collective programming of the mind which distinguishes the members of one human group from another” (1980, p. 260). He used the shared values approach to suggest that national culture differences are due to differences in values and practices, with value differences having the greater impact.

In his theory of cultural differences, Hofstede (1980) initially divided culture into four dimensions: individualism/collectivism, power distance, uncertainty avoidance, and masculinity/ femininity. These four dimensions were first measured in a study with IBM, where individuals in 64 countries were surveyed. A fifth dimension, long-term orientation, was added later (Hofstede and Bond 1988). Some have questioned the validity of the fifth dimension (Fang 2003); however, it has been shown to be especially useful in distinguishing differences between value systems of the East and the West (Hofstede 1991).<sup>4</sup>

## Detection across Cultures

The first study of culture and deception detection demonstrated that people could detect deception within their own cultural group, but they could not do so across groups (Bond et al. 1990). By contrast, Bond and Atoum (2000) found that deception could be successfully detected across cultures; Al-Simadi (2000) supported these findings. Across these three studies, relatively few cultural groups were studied: Bond et al. (1990) compared Jordanians to Americans; Bond and Atoum compared Jordanians, Americans, and Indians; and Al-Simadi compared Jordanians and Malaysians. Since 2000, no other studies have explicitly investigated successful deception detection across cultures. The focus has instead shifted to language and to cross-cultural biases (see Castillo and

Mallard 2012; Cheng and Broadhurst 2005; Da Silva and Leach 2013; Evans and Michael 2013).

Although the evidence is contradictory and limited to a few cultural groups, the findings suggest people can successfully detect deception in different cultures. This seems to be so even if the other cultural group is speaking a different language from the observer. To explain these observations, Bond et al. posed two rival hypotheses regarding peoples cross-cultural deception detection abilities. Their “universal cue hypothesis” held that deception was universal, that the burden of successfully deceiving someone was felt the same by everyone, so the ability to detect deception would be about the same for everyone, even across cultures.

The opposing hypothesis, the “specific-discrimination hypothesis,” holds that deception and its detection are both learned and are specific to communication patterns that vary across cultures. So someone from one culture would have problems detecting deception in other cultures, as communication patterns and related cues to deception would vary. Bond et al. found that people could not detect deception across cultures, supporting their specific-discrimination hypothesis. However, in their study, participants viewed deceivers in video only mode. When Bond and Atoum conducted their study 10 years later, they found that people could successfully detect deception across cultures. The primary difference between the two studies was that participants in the latter study viewed full audiovisual recordings of deceivers; having both sound and video available changed the outcome of the latter study. Bond and Atoum then concluded that

Perhaps liars throughout the world have common experiences .… Perhaps in all cultures, liars’ experiences give rise to the same behaviors, or to behaviors that convey the same impression. These function as pan-cultural detection cues (p. 394).

This interpretation essentially adopted the universal cue hypothesis. Consistent with Bond and Atoum, Al-Simadi also cited pan-cultural deception cues in support of his findings. Therefore, based on the universal cue hypothesis, we expect that participants in our study can detect deception across cultures:

## H3: Judges will be able to successfully detect deception across different cultural groups.

According to IDT, a key assumption of interpersonal communication is that people enter into all interpersonal encounters with a set of expectations, grounded in social norms, about how others will behave and why. One basic expectation is that our communication partners will be truthful; another is that they will follow the norm of reciprocity, that is, they will respond in like kind to the way others communicate with them. Violations of these expectations will be obvious. Once noticed, “violations prompt an attentional shift to the communicator and the violative act, and violations activate an interpretive and evaluative appraisal process” (Buller and Burgoon 1996, p. 209). From the perspective of the universal cue hypothesis, individuals would tend to interpret these violations of expectations as signs of possible deception, regardless of the culture of the person with whom they were communicating. However, although IDT is silent on the topic, cultures differ on the display rules they adhere to when they communicate (Akechi et al. 2013; Graham and Argyle 1975). What may be a normal display for one culture during an interaction, for example, extensive gesturing on the part of Italians, would be seen as inappropriate in another culture (such as the British). For the latter, the display would constitute a violation of expectations that would result in heightened suspicion. At the least, such suspicions might result in misunderstandings; at the most, they might be seen as evidence of attempts to deceive. Due to heightened sensitivity from observing apparent display rule violations, members of one cultural group may be better in detecting deception in another group, compared to their own group.

Empirical findings in this regard are mixed. Bond and Atoum found that American and Jordanian deceptions, while successfully detected by both groups, were better detected by members of the deceiver’s culture than by judges from other cultures. For Indian deceptions, while detectable by all groups of judges, Indian judges were no more successful in their detection than were other judges. While the universal cues hypothesis, which holds that cultural differences are not important to detection, explains the latter findings, it does not explain the former, where deception was more easily detected within one’s own culture. American and Jordanian judges were more attuned to cues to deception from members of their own group than they were to those from other groups. Al-Simadi’s findings differed in the other direction: study participants were better able to detect deception across cultures (with 57% accuracy) than within cultures (with 52% accuracy). The result was unexpected. Al-Simadi suggested the outcome was due to violation of local display rules, maintaining that, “in general, people of different races have different display rules from those which other races interpret as expectancy of violations” (2000, p. 461). The universal cue hypothesis cannot explain these findings either, as they demonstrate that judges are attuned enough to cultural differences to be able to detect deception more accurately in members of other groups than in their own group. Given the mixed empirical findings, and to be consistent with H3, based on the universal cue hypothesis, we predict that

H4: Judges will not be able to more successfully detect deception in members of other cultural groups than they will in members of their own cultural groups.

According to both leakage theory and IDT, senders emit cues to deception and receivers become aware of those cues as they continue the communicative interaction. Table 2 provided a list of reliable cues from meta-analyses of senders and their behavior during deception. This list is based on sender behavior in a North American context, but the universal cue hypothesis implies that these cues to deception are experienced in the same way across cultures. The cues emitted by senders should not vary across cultures, nor should the cues receivers recognize vary during deceptive encounters. Therefore, we hypothesize that

H5: Judges will rely on the same set of cues to deception in their detection efforts, regardless of the cultural group to which the judges belong.

To extend further our understanding of IDT and the relationship between culture and deception detection, we introduce Hofstede’s (1984) dimensions of culture. A few studies have examined the link between deception and Hofstede’s cultural dimensions (see Li et al. 2006), but the literature, like IDT, is largely silent on the effect of culture on deception detection. Much of what follows is based on works by Hofstede (1980, 1984; Hofstede et al. 2008; Hofstede et al. 2009) and a metaanalysis of communication patterns and dimensions of culture (Merkin et al. 2014).

In terms of the individualist/collectivist dimension, we argue that individualists would be better at detecting deception than collectivists. The individualist/collectivist dimension captures the extent to which individuals are affiliated with their groups. Collectivists consider themselves to be integrated into strong, cohesive groups. Group interests, regardless of whether the group is the family, tribe, village, religious community, nation, or work group (Hofstede 1984) motivate them. As a result, collectivists have a strong sense of loyalty, respect, and trust toward others. Accordingly, collectivists are less suspicious of others, so they are less likely to pay attention to leaked deceptive cues while communicating with others (McCornack and Levine 1990). By comparison, individualists operate in a world of loose ties, and they are not culturally obligated to trust and respect others. Consequently, individualists are more prone to suspicion, which makes them better detectors of deception (Lowry et al. 2010, McCornack and Levine 1990). We hypothesize

## H6a: Individualists are better at deception detection than collectivists.

Power distance captures how a society deals with inequality (Hofstede 1980). Social inequality is apparent in the presence of different social classes. Societies also have stratifications based on prestige, intellectual and education levels, wealth, occupations, and political ranks (Bochner and Hesketh 1994). Due to their (higher) social class or stratification, some individuals may have more power (i.e., status and respect) than others. We suggest that those more accepting of large differences in power and authority would be less able to detect deception. Individuals with high power distance values would be less likely to question communications from the elderly, as they tend to be obedient to old age, both within the workplace and at home with family. Subordinates in high power distance cultures would also be less likely to question decisions and justifications from superiors, whether the justifications were honest or not (Hofstede et al. 2009). The expectations of accepting the communications of others with more power (i.e., due to age, education, social, political, or organizational title) out of respect or fear will lead to ignoring cues to deception (Hofstede 1980). On the other hand, those who expect low power distances expect their leaders and peers to communicate in a “supportive, assured, and precise” manner (Merkin et al. 2014, p. 5). Regardless of the other party’s power, they would be suspicious of communications that did not fit expectations, which could lead to better deception detection.

## H6b: Those with expectations of small power distances are better at deception detection than those with expectations of large power distances.

Uncertainty avoidance refers to the extent to which individuals feel comfortable or uncomfortable in uncertain and ambiguous situations (Hofstede 1980, 1984). High uncertainty avoidance societies are characterized by resistance to change and risk. In contrast, low uncertainty avoidance cultures have a lesser need for rules to guide behavior and are more comfortable with uncertainty and ambiguity. Rules exist, but they are more for convenience. Suspected deception implies uncertainty, given that the truth is glossed over and hidden. Individuals with high uncertainty avoidance cultural values are risk-averse, so have a strong need for clarity and structure. They also tend to be highly intolerant of “deviant persons and ideas” (Hofstede 2011, p. 10). Therefore, they are likely to be extremely vigilant of deceptive cues while communicating with others. Identifying deception early will help them moderate unexpected incidents and minimize ambiguity. On the other hand, those low on uncertainty avoidance tend to reflect risk-taking behaviors, so they are less likely to scrutinize communications with others, compared to those with high uncertainty avoidance values.

H6c: People who have a hard time tolerating uncertainty are better at detecting deception than people who tend to tolerate uncertainty.

The fourth cultural dimension is masculinity/femininity. Masculine cultures are characterized as assertive and competitive, placing a strong emphasis on performance, while feminine cultures display more modest and caring values, emphasizing public welfare (Hofstede 1980). Past research suggests that masculine cultures have a low-context communication style (Merkin et al. 2014). This form of communication is direct and explicit, as individuals rely more on verbal than nonverbal behavior. In contrast, feminine cultures tend to have a high-context communication style, in which nonverbal behavior plays an important part in deciphering communication. We know from prior research that, while both verbal and nonverbal deceptive cues can lead to successful detection of deception, those who can read nonverbal cues are likely to be significantly better at detecting deception than those who rely on verbal cues (Van Edwards 2013). Given that individuals in feminine cultures actively use nonverbal behavior in their communication, they are also more likely to identify deceptive nonverbal cues than those from masculine cultures, which rely primarily on verbal behavior. Therefore, we hypothesize

## H6d: People from feminine cultures are better at detecting deception than people from masculine cultures.

Long-term orientation refers to future-oriented values such as savings, persistence, and planning, while a short-term orientation is associated with past and present values like personal stability, respect for tradition, and reciprocation (Hofstede et al. 2009). Individuals with a long-term orientation tend to be forward looking such that they do not mind adapting to circumstances (Hofstede 2011). They also do not have a universal definition of what is good and evil, as this depends on the circumstances (Hofstede 2011). Therefore, those with long-term oriented values will likely give a deceiver the benefit of the doubt, making them poor detectors of deception. On the contrary, those with short-term oriented values have an absolute definition of good and evil, so they are quick to classify communications as either honest or dishonest. Additionally, those with a long-term orientation are inclined to invest in relationships, much in the same way that they invest in business transactions (Hofstede et al. 2009). If they foresee long-term economic benefits in fostering a relationship with someone, then they may not pay much attention to leaked cues to deception. By comparison, short-term oriented individuals value personal steadiness and stability and are suspicious of societal change. Deception would be more of a threat for these short-term values, as it could potentially disrupt personal stability and threaten the status quo. Thus, those with a short-term orientation would be motivated to identify cues to deception so they can find and eliminate such threats.

## Research Method

To gain further insight into the relationships between culture, media, and deception detection, we prepared three stimulus sets from recorded interviews involving three cultural groups and two languages (i.e., with American English, Spanish (Castilian), and Indians speaking English, which we call Indian English). Each stimulus set consisted of 32 snippets from the interviews (8 each in full audiovisual, audio only, video only, and text) that were incorporated into a Qualtrics survey—one for each set. These were then used in a series of experimental studies in which participants judged the veracity of the stimuli to which they were exposed. Details on how the stimulus sets were created are found in Appendix A.

Neutral third-parties were recruited to judge the veracity of the stimulus materials. Three groups of judges were recruited: Americans, Spaniards, and Indians. The Spanish data was collected in 2009, American data in 2012–2013, and Indian data in 2015. All the studies followed the same protocols for the veracity judgment task. Undergraduate students, who did not know the participants in the recorded interviews, evaluated the veracity of each snippet shown in a stimulus set. Each participant was exposed to all 32 snippets in only one of the stimulus sets. They were asked to read, view, and/or listen to each snippet and to determine its veracity on a seven-point scale, from 1 for “very honest” to 7 for “very dishonest.” If the participant selected 5, 6, or 7 on the scale signaling dishonesty, she was asked to explain what she had observed in the snippet that led her to judge it as dishonest. Once veracity judgments were completed, the participant then completed questions for the remaining constructs (i.e., Hofstede’s cultural dimensions) and demographics (e.g., age, gender). On average, it took each participant about 1 hour to complete the veracity judgment tasks and questionnaire. A total of 279 judges participated (106 from Spain, 129 from America (63 from Iowa and 69 from Ohio), and 41 from India). For those participants who provided information about gender, 65% were male and 35% were female.

Even so, the groups of judges differed from each other on Hofstede’s cultural dimensions, and this is expected to affect their evaluations.

## Analysis and Results

To analyze the relationships between culture, media, and deception detection accuracy, the data were analyzed using repeated measures logistic regression, in SPSS Version 21. The GENLIN command was used, with a binomial distribution and logit as the link function. Repeated measures were used, as each participant answered 32 different questions. Given that each of the 279 judges was asked to respond to 32 snippets, 8,928 responses were generated.

Logistic regression was used since the dependent variable, whether the veracity judgment was correct or incorrect, was binomial. The seven-point scale on which veracity was judged was collapsed into a discrete variable, where scores of 1 to 3 were considered a judgment of truth, and scores of 5 to 7 were considered a judgment of dishonesty. A total of 1,093 responses (12.24%) were judged as 4 (i.e., undecided), so they were omitted from the analyses. Accuracy rates for all four media<sup>5</sup> and stimulus sets<sup>6</sup> were statistically better than chance.

## Hypothesis 2

Hypothesis 2 dealt with the relationships between different media capabilities and deception detection. We predicted that the most successful deception detection would occur for media with multiple symbol sets (H2a), high levels of reprocessibility (H2b), and low levels of transmission velocity (H2c). Using media ratings provided by Dennis et al. (2008), we created three new variables for the four media used: transmission velocity, symbol sets, and reprocessibility (see Appendix C). The data were analyzed using repeated measures logistic regression with correct judgment as the dependent variable. We ran a separate regression for each media capability. The regression models for transmission velocity (Wald chi-square = 1.572, df = 1, n.s.) and reprocessibility (Wald chi-square = 1.572, df = 1, n.s.) were not statistically significant. Hypotheses 2b and 2c were not supported. The regression model for symbol sets was statistically significant (Wald chi-square = 37.648, df = 3, p # 0.001). Using the Bonferroni procedure (α # 0.05), we found that media with very high support for multiple symbol sets were associated with better deception detection than media with moderate or low levels of support (Table 6). Also, high levels of support for multiple symbol sets were better for deception detection than were moderate levels. Hypothesis 2a was supported.

## Hypothesis 3

Hypothesis 3 predicted that judges would be able to successfully detect deception across different cultural groups. Three different analyses were run based on the cultural affiliation of the veracity judges (see Table 7). For each analysis, there were main effects found for stimulus set and media. There were interaction effects only for the American judges. All judges were better than chance at detecting deception, although there was some variance across the groups of judges. The best deception detectors were the Spanish judges, with an accuracy rate of 61.2%, followed by American judges at 58.1%, and Indian judges at 57.7%. All accuracy rates were better than the 54% average found in the deception literature (Bond and DePaulo 2006), and they were all statistically different from chance.<sup>7</sup> Hypothesis 3, which predicted that individuals would be able to successfully detect deception across different cultures, was supported. Every group of judges was able to successfully detect deception in another group, as all cross-cultural detection accuracy rates exceeded 50%.

## Hypothesis 4

Hypothesis 4 predicted that judges would not be able to detect deception in members of other cultural groups more successfully than they would in their own cultural group. Spanish judges were more accurate at detecting deception in the Spanish stimulus set than for American English. ## Hypothesis 5

Hypothesis 5 examined the cues that judges cited as important to their determination that a snippet was dishonest. Based on the universal cue theory, we predicted that each group would report using the same set of cues. To test this hypothesis, we identified and coded the cues cited in the 1,807 snippets that were correctly identified as dishonest. A description of the coding process, the resulting 23 codes, their raw counts, and incidence for each group of judges are detailed in Appendix D.

Cues citations by the three groups of judges tended to support the universal cue hypothesis. Nine cues out of 23 (i.e., tone/pitch, fidgeting, vague reply, no eye contact, pauses, stuttering, repetitive answers, contradicting oneself, and bragging) accounted for 69% of the cues cited by the American judges, 92% of those cited by the Spanish judges, and 80% of those cited by the Indian judges. American judges cited all 23 cues, Spanish judges cited 16, and Indian judges cited 17. Yet the different groups relied on the cues to different extents. For Americans, the most cited cues were vague reply (18%), fidgeting (16%), and lack of eye contact (12%). For the Spanish judges, tone/pitch alone accounted for 32% of the cues cited, while for the Indian judges, vague reply was cited 44% of the time. So while members of all groups were able to explain the presence of the deception they correctly detected by relying on similar cues, groups differed in what other cues they cited and the extent to which they relied on them.

## Hypothesis 6

Hypothesis 6 predicted there would be a relationship between cultural dimensions and deception detection. In particular, we predicted the best deception detection would occur among individualists, those who believed in small power distances, people who disliked uncertainty, those from feminine cultures, and people with short-term orientations. We tested the five hypotheses (H6a–H6e), using repeated measures logistic regression with correct judgment as the dependent variable. We ran five separate regressions, one for each cultural dimension. To do this, we converted the continuous dimension scores to categories. For each dimension, we created two categories (see Appendix B). We ran five separate regressions, one for each cultural dimension. Only one of the regression models was statistically significant and in the expected direction: uncertainty avoidance (Wald chi-square = 6.799, df = 1, p # 0.009). Those with a high degree of uncertainty avoidance were the most accurate at detecting deception, supporting H6c.

Given that the other models were not significant, and that no one previously has investigated the link between deception detection and cultural dimensions, we conducted additional analyses. We ran regression models for each dimension, but separately for the honest snippets and for the dishonest snippets (Table 9). With the exception of the individualist/ collectivist dimension (H6a), there were differences by dimension for successful detection of truth and of dishonesty. When looking at each entire set of experimental stimuli, these differences cancelled each other out.

For power distance (H6b), those with high power distance ratings were better than those with low power distance ratings for discerning the truth (Wald chi-square = 13.970, df =1, p # 0.001), and those with low power distance ratings were better at discerning dishonesty (Wald chi-square = 14.540, df =1, p # 0.001). For uncertainty avoidance (H6c), those with low levels of uncertainty avoidance (i.e., those who can better tolerate uncertainty) were better at discerning truth (Wald chisquare = 27.634, df =1, p # .001); those with high levels of uncertainty avoidance (i.e., those who cannot tolerate uncertainty) were better at discerning dishonesty (Wald chi-square = 47.324, df =1, p # 0.001). For masculinity/femininity (H6d), those from masculine cultures were better at discerning truth (Wald chi-square = 32.139, df =1, p # 0.001), while those from feminine cultures were better at discerning dishonesty (Wald chi-square = 26.527, df =1, p # 0.001). For long-term/ short-term orientation, those with a short-term orientation (H6e) were better at discerning honesty (Wald chi-square = 16.630, df = 1, p # 0.001), while those with a long-term orientation were better at discerning dishonesty (Wald chi-square = 6.527, df =1, p # 0.011), the opposite of what was expected. When honest and dishonest snippets were separated, the findings support H6b (power distance), H6d (masculinity/ femininity), and H6c (uncertainty avoidance), but not H6e (long-term/short-term orientation).

Although we do not have any hypothetical reasons to think there would be interactions between cultural dimensions and media capabilities on the accurate detection of deception, the existence of such interactions seem likely, given the interactions we found between media and stimulus set. We tested for interactions between the five cultural dimensions and the three media capabilities. We found one statistically significant interaction, between symbol sets and individualism/ collectivism.<sup>8</sup> Individualists viewing media with very high symbol sets were more accurate (66%) than were individualists viewing media with any other level of symbol sets. Their accuracy rates were also better than those of any collectivists, regardless of symbol sets exposure. Collectivists viewing media with low levels of symbol sets were worse at detection accuracy than were all other collectivists. Finally, collectivists exposed to moderate symbol sets were more accurate than individualists with moderate symbol sets. It is interesting to note that the individualism/collectivism dimension was the only one where there were no findings of direct effects. Only the interaction between the dimension and media capabilities was related to detection accuracy. It is remarkable that the combination of individualists and media with high levels of symbol sets dominated. Perhaps due to their tendency to distrust, individualists were better able to make use of their exposure to a large number of cues to deception, compared to collectivists, who were equally accurate with any level of symbol sets except the lowest.

## Discussion

In today’s world, people from different cultures interact, in person and across various communication media, like never before, due to the ready availability of inexpensive ICTs, mass migrations, and increases in global travel. These movements have implications for personal relationships, for governmental functions such as immigration and law enforcement, and for business processes ranging from online interviews to support for distributed systems development teams. Can people tell when those from other cultures are being dishonest? Do the media they communicate through play a role? In this study, using stimulus sets and judges from three different national cultures, we found that people from different cultures can successfully tell when people from cultures outside their own are being dishonest. We found that media play a role in successful deception detection: Video-only exchanges are the worst for successfully detecting deception, while media that support a large number of symbol sets are better for deception detection than media that support fewer sets.

As predicted, judges were the least accurate at detecting deception when viewing the medium that could convey the fewest cues to deception, that is, video only. There was a clear differentiation between video only and the other three media (i.e., audiovisual, audio only, and text), but there was no differentiation among those three. Video-only stimuli could convey 6 of 23 cues, while audiovisual and audio could convey 23 and 20 cues, respectively. So perhaps it is not surprising that there were no significant differences between audiovisual and audio-only communications in terms of detec-

Perhaps there is a threshold of a viable number of cues conveyed, which falls somewhere between six and 12, above which detection is facilitated, and below which, detection is more difficult. It may also be the case that not all cues are equal in their intensity or signaling power. While it seems clear that more cues are associated with better detection, it may well be the case that one or two powerful cues could be just as helpful as a handful of less useful ones. It is also possible that some cues were enacted differently across the stimulus sets. For example, fidgeting within one group of interviewees may have been more pronounced than fidgeting within another group. More research is called for to investigate the signaling power of cues and their variation across cultures.<sup>9</sup>

When testing the relationships between media capabilities identified by MST (Dennis et al. 2008) and deception detection, we found evidence of a relationship between symbol sets and detection success: a very high level of support for symbol sets facilitated better deception detection than moderate or low levels of support. There was also an interaction between medium and stimulus set. It was more difficult to detect deception in the text snippets for the American English stimulus set than for Indian English or Spanish. It was easier to detect deception in Spanish audio only snippets than the other two stimulus sets. Detection was also easier in Indian English video only compared to American English and Spanish video only, but there were no differences in detection success for audiovisual snippets, regardless of stimulus set. These findings provide more nuance to the overall picture of the role of media in deception detection. For example, while the American English stimulus set was in general the most difficult for successful deception detection, compared to the other sets, there were no differences between American English and the other sets for audiovisual snippets. Deception detection success rates, which were high at 62% to 67%, did not vary by the culture of the person viewed.

The universal cue hypothesis holds that deception is similar across cultures, placing similar emotional, cognitive, and physiological demands on deceivers. It is because of these demands and how they affect deceivers that deception is detectable in the first place. The cues to deception that deceivers emit, then, are similar across cultures, implying that it would be as easy (or as difficult) to detect deception in other cultural groups as in your own. The findings for Hypotheses 3, 4, and 5 support the universal cue hypothesis (Bond and Atoum 2000; Castillo 2013; Taylor et al. 2015). All three groups of judges were able to detect deception in other cultures, even when the language was different. In two of the three cases, judges did not do a better job of detecting deception in groups outside their own, supporting the idea that detection success rates are roughly the same, regardless of the sender’s or receiver’s culture.

Judges from all three cultural groups converged on a basic set of deception cues they found to be important, again supporting the universal cue hypothesis. However, each group relied on the same cues to different extents. Each group had favored cues on which they relied, and each group had a few cues they observed that other groups did not. The differences in cue identification across groups of judges cannot be explained by differences in the stimulus sets they observed. Within each group of judges, members were exposed to two or three different stimulus sets. For example, the set of cues reported by the Spanish judges were compiled from participants who observed the American English and Spanish stimuli. The implication is that the cues reported by each cultural group were influenced by their own culture. We found that American judges did better at detecting deception in other groups than when judging other Americans. Al-Simadi (2000), in addressing similar but unexpected findings in his study, suggested that the differences were due to violations of expected display rules. Different cultures have their own expectations about appropriate behavior. During a communication event, inappropriate behavior on the part of those being communicated with could lead to suspicion about their truthfulness. Increased suspicion tends to promote deception detection success. Castillo and Mallard (2012) found additional support for the expectancy-violation model. In their study, Australian judges were more suspicious of the veracity of actors displaying norm-inconsistent behavior than they were of actors displaying norm-consistent behavior.

The findings from American judges violate universal cue hypothesis expectations, as they point to an enhanced focus on cues particular to other cultures, compared to cues noticed in one’s own cultural group. Taken with our findings from Hypothesis 5, where each group of judges relied on the same cues to different extents, these results point to a more nuanced view than the universal cue hypothesis would predict. Yes, individuals can detect deception outside their own culture just as well as they can within their own group, and yes, they rely on the same set of cues to detect deception as people from other cultures. However, which cues they rely on seems to vary by culture, and sometimes they use the cues in such a way that they notice deception outside their culture even better than in their own cultural group. Across the available empirical evidence, we see three patterns. Some groups of judges have been better at detection deception in their own group (Americans and Jordanians in Bond and Atoum, and Indians and Spaniards in this study); others have been better with other cultures (Jordanians and Malaysians in Al-Simadi, and Americans in this study); and one group was equally successful with all cultural groups (Indians in Bond and Atoum). In general, the universal cue hypothesis holds: people can successfully detect deception in their own group and in other cultures using similar cues, but sometimes they do better with their own culture, and sometimes they do better with others. Based on the available evidence, these comparative differences are not due to the abilities of any one particular culture. Depending on the study, Americans and Jordanians did better with their own group (e.g., Bond and Atoum 2000) or with outside groups (e.g., Al Simadi 2000; Table 8 in this study).

Finally, we found that dimensions of culture influence successful deception detection, but the relationships are more nuanced than we envisioned. The only exception was the individualist/collectivist dimension, where the majority of judges self-identified as individualist. The lack of findings could be due to the psychometric properties of the scale or to the particular groups of judges who participated in the study (Srite and Karahanna 2006; Zhang and Lowry 2008). For the other dimensions, there were relationships between culture and successful detection. For the honest and dishonest stimulus materials, those with a low tolerance for uncertainty were better at detecting deception than those who tolerate uncertainty. For the three remaining cultural dimensions, the findings varied for honest and dishonest stimuli. For honest snippets, judges with high-power distance ratings, from masculine cultures, or with short-term orientations were the best at detecting truth. For dishonest snippets, judges with low-power distance ratings, from feminine cultures, or with long-term orientations were the best at detecting deception.

## Implications

Our findings have implications for both research and practice. From a research perspective, we recognized there was no single extant theory that addressed the research questions we were asking about media, culture, and deception detection. Starting with Shannon and Weaver’s (1949) model of communication, we illustrated the similarities between theories of media and theories of deceptive communication. Media theories tend to focus on the communication channels while theories of deceptive communication focus on the source and destination of the message. Based on these similarities, we combined MST and IDT to provide a theoretical basis for testing the relationships between media and deception detection. However, to consider the cultural encoding and decoding of the message in the Shannon and Weaver model, we included a cultural component by incorporating Hofstede’s cultural dimensions. The relationships between culture and deception detection were further informed by the universal cues hypothesis (Bond and Atoum 2000). In essence, we combined aspects of MST, IDT, and Hofstede’s work with universal cue hypotheses, to frame the theoretical basis for investigating our research questions.

The original IDT, now 20 years old, included the communication channel as part of the basic structure of the theory, but the channel was not as central to the theory as were the sender and receiver of the deception. We expanded IDT to explicitly consider the media capabilities identified in MST. Our findings showed that the symbol set media capability was related to successful detection. Dennis et al. state that the MST theory was not created to deal with deceptive communication (2008, p. 592). However, George et al. (2013) demonstrated that the MST ideas of conveyance and convergence explained well the media choices deceivers made for deceiving. MST and IDT, therefore, complement each other well when focused on deceptive communication. This suggests that future research using IDT should consider not only the channel used by sender and receiver; it should also consider the media capabilities of the channel. Analysis at the media capability level may be more revealing than the channel as a whole.

We also expanded IDT to explicitly consider culture and its role in deception detection. Culture is implied in IDT as part of the familiarity construct but its treatment is sparse. By incorporating elements of Hofstede’s work along with the universal cue hypothesis, we were able to examine the role of culture in deception detection. Although there has been some work on the cultural dimensions and the act of deceiving (e.g., Li et al. 2006; Merkin et al. 2014), we are aware of no work outside this study that explicitly considered the relationship between Hofstede’s cultural dimensions and deception detection. IDT is strengthened by making culture, and specific cultural dimensions, an explicit aspect of investigations of deception and its detection. Similarly, research on culture is strengthened and expanded with its introduction into a new domain: deceptive communication.

We also explicitly incorporated the universal cue hypothesis into our expanded version of IDT. Our findings aligned with the universal cue hypothesis: people can successfully detect deception both inside and outside their own cultural groups. However, some of our specific findings, such as the differences in how different groups of judges use indicators and why some groups are better at detecting deception in outside groups, cannot be explained through a strict reading of the universal cue hypothesis. Our findings implied the existence of boundary conditions for the hypothesis. Although the universal cue hypothesis seems to be dominant, the extent to which it holds, and under what circumstances, is unclear. Future research can focus on determining why some cultural groups appear to be better at detecting deception in other groups, compared to their own, as well as on similarities and differences in the cues to deception each cultural group prefers to use for detection.

Our findings also have practical implications. With increased globalization and advances in ICTs, most of us today will be exposed to and operate in a more heterogeneous cultural environment than ever before. Almost on a daily basis, people interact with others from very different cultural and language groups other than their own, and they may have different attitudes, beliefs, norms, and personal motivations regarding deception. How people communicate has changed rapidly in the past decades, with a large proportion of our communications being mediated by ICTs, whether e-mail, text message, audio/voice, or video. As technology and social media continue to evolve, using just one device such as the smart phone, people can send text-only messages (e.g., IM, e-mail), voice-messages, make full audiovisual calls, and send (video only) “live pictures.” Understanding the affordances that different media provide can help people and organizations make better choices to determine if deception is present.

People acquire their knowledge of deception detection and their detection abilities from various sources including their interactions with peers and observation of others. So it should be heartening to realize that, as the world embraces more computer-mediated communication, people have the ability to successfully detect deception in cultural and language groups that differ from their own. They can do this even without training about cues to deception and how they operate, as many of the cues to deception seem to be universal, irrespective of cultural or language group. In fact, some people are better able to detect deception in other groups than in their own cultural groups. Yet, a key point here is that, for this study, judges were instructed to look for deception, so they were motivated to look for cues that might be operating in a deceptive message. Most people are not necessarily looking for deception in their cross-cultural communications. Yet people can be easily motivated to detect deception: simple warnings about possible deception have been shown to make people better detectors (Biros et al. 2002; George et al. 2008). With the right motivation, people can use the abilities they already possess to detect deception both in people from within and outside their cultural group.

If an organization wants to maximize individuals’ deception detection potential (e.g., for online interviews of prospective graduate students or employees), our findings imply that much of the training can be universal across cultures. In many cases, people will deal with only a few cultural groups, such as cross-cultural virtual teams. Having a good understanding of the norms and nuances of the other cultural groups may help improve communication in general and aid in detecting deception. However, attention should be paid also to differences in accuracy across media on areas where individuals have more difficulty with detection. For example, if another cultural group is working with Americans, they would want to emphasize detection training in media types other than audiovisual, since successful deception detection in audiovisual does not differ in difficulty across Americans, Spaniards, and Indians. With Spanish individuals, the extra emphasis would be on media other than audio where detection rates were lower.

How far we need to go to improve our ability to detect deception will depend on the context. In less formal contexts, such as with most social media fora, simple cautions may be enough to heighten awareness. For law enforcement or intelligence gathering, where errors can have serious impacts on national security, in addition to formal training, organizations may want to use automated tools to boost detection accuracy. For example, cross-referencing information and natural language processing can help uncover features in textbased communications that suggest deception, from which people could then learn. In high stake situations, more sophisticated mechanisms for deception detection may include interpersonal screening techniques, such as the behavioral analysis interview and technology-based approaches using computational analyses such as scientific content analysis and video-analysis.

Finally, some low-cue media, such as security cameras, are difficult to avoid completely. This study showed that, although accuracy rates across cultural groups were significantly better than chance, the judges performed poorest with video only media. A key difficulty with nonverbal communications only (e.g., gestures, gaze, timing, facial expressions, posture) is that they may have quite precise symbolic meanings which differ across cultures, making them harder to interpret in the absence of other cues. For example, there are differences in how some cultures perceive eye contact, with Western cultures viewing it as a sign of honesty and attention, while Hispanic and Asian cultures view it as disrespectful, leading to gaze aversion. At the same time, low-cue media may be the most efficient or best available way of unearthing potential issues. In this case, organizations would be best advised to adopt a tiered approach to deception detection, complementing detection via low-cue media with detection efforts based on media that transmit more cues and have more sophisticated media capabilities.

## Limitations and Future Research

Our study has several limitations. We used students as both the sources of deception and as judges of veracity. In this study, students falsely enhanced their “scholarship applications,” and other students were able to successfully detect their deceptions. There may have been better ways to introduce deception, or to employ both better liars and better judges. However, students are people, and people both deceive and detect deception, so generalizing from our findings to the wider population is feasible. Generalizability is still limited, however, as we used stimulus materials in only two languages, and judges from only three countries; there are many more languages and cultures to study. Also, our measures of national culture were derived from Hofstede’s studies via the work of Srite and Karahanna (2006). While some scales performed as expected in our context (e.g., UA, MF, PD), others did not (e.g., IC). In addition, the results from our measures did not always accord with Hofstede’s findings on national culture. However, for our purposes, the exact ordering of the differences was not as important as the existence of cultural differences across the groups. We found there were differences, so we were able to address our measurement objectives. Still, future research may want to use other measures based on Hofstede’s scales or use measures developed by other researchers such as the GLOBE studies group (House et al. 2004).

Two of our hypotheses about media capabilities were not supported. To recode our media type data into measures of transmission velocity, symbol sets, and reprocessibility, we relied on the ratings of media capabilities in MST (Dennis et al. 2008). However, the new variables that were created may have lacked the variance needed for more useful analyses. Three out of our four media had high ratings for transmission velocity, and three had low ratings for reprocessibility (Table 3). The ratings we used for these media, taken from MST, may have been too broad for this type of study. For example, audiovisual and audio only may not have the same ratings for transmission velocity, as implied by MST. Instead, it may have been useful to use a scale for measuring perceived media capabilities (e.g., Harrison 2014).

American judges were the only ones who were better at detecting deception in cultures other than their own. They were only 51% accurate with the American English stimulus set. However, the other groups also had difficulty with this set: Indians were 54% correct and Spanish judges were 57% correct. The Spanish and Indian English stimulus sets had better success rates (from 61% to 66%). It may be that the American English stimulus was not equivalent to the other two in its level of detection difficulty. Further testing or adjustment may be needed.

The findings and limitations of this study give rise to a number of avenues for future work. For example, future research into the relationships among culture, media, and deception could focus on different cultural groups and languages, media, and types of deception other than those in our studies. Research has only just touched on the variety of languages and cultures in the world that could be used for comparative work. There are also other media that could be studied, such as SMS-based texting, an increasingly pervasive and important communication medium worldwide. Future studies could also examine deception by omission, by exaggeration, and through ambiguity. Additional research could be conducted into the workings of violations of expectations in communication and how those violations lead to deception detection, especially with differing language and cultural groups.

## Conclusions

Our research was aimed at understanding how various ICTs affect deception detection where the sender is from one culture and the receiver is from another. Our results showed a relationship between media and deception detection success. Detection rates were worse when judges were exposed to video only exchanges, compared to other media. But we also found that culture and media interacted, such that successful detection rates varied across combinations of media and culture. Our findings support the universal cue hypothesis but point toward boundary conditions where it does not hold. These results have implications for the computer-mediated cross-cultural communications that take place each day, but research has only just begun to investigate and understand the differences.

## Appendix A

## Creating the Stimulus Sets

All three stimulus sets were prepared from recorded interviews. The first set, in American English, was a by-product of an experiment (Tilley 2005) in which students were interviewed about a scholarship application, which they had enhanced in their favor. The interviews were done via either VoIP or e-mail, and those done via VoIP were recorded with the camera recording only the interviewee. The second stimulus set, in Spanish, was created for another experiment (Lewis 2009). Spain was chosen because of the language difference (i.e., Spanish) and because Hofstede’s measures indicate that Spaniards substantially differ from Americans across all five cultural dimensions (geert-hofstede.com dimensions.html). MBA students in Barcelona were asked to prepare a scholarship application and were then interviewed about it. The third stimulus set was created in 2011–2012. Indian students were asked to prepare scholarship applications and were then interviewed about them. Indians were chosen for the third stimulus set because Indians differ substantially from Americans on four of Hofstede’s five cultural dimensions (all but the masculinity/femininity scale), and from Spaniards on two dimensions (i.e., power distance and uncertainty avoidance).

To create each stimulus set, applicants were asked to provide their résumés, in order to establish ground truth in each case. Researchers then compared the résumé to the enhanced application in order to determine what was truthful and what was dishonest on the application. In all cases, the participants were encouraged to complete the application form so as to make themselves appear as top candidates for the scholarship. The participants were not told to lie explicitly, but they were told that they could enhance their information if they wanted to. In all cases, each student was interviewed by a third party who was ignorant of the design of the study and of the study hypotheses. As such, both the interviewers and interviewees were independent of the researchers and were equally naïve as to the research purpose of the interviews. Interviewers were told to ask any questions they wanted about the scholarship application, and interviewees were asked to defend anything on the application that the interviewer asked about. In total, 20 unique students were interviewed for each set of stimulus materials (20 Americans, 20 Spaniards, 20 Indians). The résumé task had been used in previous studies (George et al. 2008; George et al. 2014), where it was shown to successfully elicit genuine non-rehearsed deception from participants who were asked to prepare and defend their applications. As the task involved interactions between the interviewer and interviewee, following the process outlined in IDT, interviewees generated additional deceptions during the interviews in order to defend their initial enhancements.

The recorded interviews were used to create each stimulus set. For each set (one for American English, one for Spanish, and one for Indian English), the researchers carefully reviewed the full interviews and chose 32 brief snippets to be included in the stimulus set. The snippets fo the Spanish and American English sets were chosen (and edited) by one author; the snippets for the Indian English set were chosen (and edited) by another author. For both, the challenge was to select snippets that were representative of honesty and dishonesty across each set of interviews. The snippets could not be too easy or too hard to evaluate. Each author also made sure that no single interviewee appeared in more than one video snippet (sound or not) within a stimulus set (where the face would be the same) and that no interviewee appeared in more than one audio only or full audiovisual snippet within a stimulus set (where the voice would be the same). Half of the chosen snippets were honest, and half were dishonest. Further, the snippets were edited so that each one appeared in one of four media formats: (1) full audiovisual, (2) video only, (3) audio only, and (4) text only.

For each stimulus set, eight snippets were in full audiovisual; eight were video only; eight were audio only; and eight were text only. Text snippets were transcribed from the interview recordings and translated into the language of the judge—into English for the English-speaking judges, and into Spanish for the Spanish-speaking judges. For snippets where gender was obvious (i.e., all but the written snippets), 60.42% of the interviewees were male, and 39.58% were female.

For the four simulated media to which the judges were exposed, the media capabilities associated with each medium (Dennis et al. 2008) were determined. For full audiovisual, transmission velocity was high, there were a moderate number of symbol sets (using the Dennis et al. assessment terms), and reprocessibility was low. For audio only, transmission velocity was high, the number of symbol sets was moderate but fewer than for full audiovisual, and reprocessibility was low. For text, transmission velocity was low, the number of symbol sets was low, and reprocessibility was high. Finally, for video only, transmission velocity was high, reprocessibility was low, and the number of symbol sets was low but not as low as that of text, given that visual symbols have the capacity to transmit more information that written words alone. It is interesting to note that, since all of the stimuli were recorded, all of the media had enhanced reprocessibility capabilities. Judges could replay all of the stimuli, giving them the ability to view and study the stimuli over and over. However, for whatever reasons, maybe because of their desire to finish the task in a timely manner, we observed hardly any judge “rewinding” a snippet. The “natural” level of reprocessibility for text was not affected, as judges could read the text snippet several times, just by looking at the text on the screen.

The Cronbach alphas for the scales were as follows: PD: 0.831; UA: 0.385; MF: 0.930; IC: 0.703. All of these reliability measures are above standard cutoff levels (of 0.70) except for UA. The SPSS output suggested that the reliability would improve to 0.708 if UA6 were removed. An additional reliability test of the remaining UA scale items showed that the Cronbach alpha would increase to 0.862 if UA5 were dropped. Considering that both UA5 and UA6 had negative standard regression weights, and that the reliability of the scale increased without them, we decided to drop both items from the scale. For a CFA test in AMOS without UA5 and UA6, the chi-square was 1.54 (df = 2), with a p-value of 0.4634. The RMSEA value was 0.000, indicating the model was a good fit to the data.

Finally, we explored the cross-cultural equivalence of our instrumentation and its results. Cross-cultural equivalence is the extent to which research designs across different cross-cultural contexts can be employed in the same fashion. Essentially, cultural differences create challenges in surveys being utilized in their same form among cultures. Thus, it was necessary to take steps to ensure data equivalence so as to be sure that any significant differences among cultures are truly a reflection of cross-cultural distinctions, and not due to construct operationalizations and types of scales used (Hult et al. 2008). To this end, this study examined three aspects of equivalence: construct, measurement, and sample. Construct equivalence, which determines whether a concept has the same meaning among cultures (DeCarlo et al. 2007; Kumar 2000), was achieved through a variety of methods that provide typical reliability and construct validity for the study measures reported previously in this appendix. Measurement equivalence was ascertained by using Hofstede’s measures, which have been used and validated in 50 countries and translated into 20 different languages (Hofstede 1983). Regarding sample equivalence, appropriate measures were taken to ensure that the veracity judges were similar. We therefore limited our sample to students. Further evidence of limited variability among the sample was exhibited by the commonality of language. Of the three groups of judges, all of the American and Indian judges spoke English, as well as 72% of the Spanish group. It is also important to note that the three groups are comparable demographically. The mean age of the Americans, Spaniards, and Indians was approximately 23, 22, and 21 years, respectively. Regarding gender, the American (64%), Spanish (66%), and Indian (66%) judges were primarily represented by males. ANOVA tests among the groups for age and gender were not significant.

## Appendix C

## Creation of Media Capabilities Variables

In this study, medium was a treatment, as participants were exposed to four different media: (1) full audiovisual (AV), (2) video only, (3) audio only, and (4) text. In order to recode the media categories into variables based on media capabilities, we relied on the study by Dennis et al. (2008), which provided ratings for five media capabilities in their media synchronicity theory (MST): (1) transmission velocity, (2) parallelism, (3) symbol sets, (4) rehearsability, and (5) reprocessability. Neither parallelism nor rehearsability were appropriate capabilities for our study

In their Table 2 (p. 589), Dennis et al. provided ratings for media capabilities across several media. Their “video conference” compares most favorably to our full audiovisual mode; “telephone conference” matched our audio-only mode; and “asynchronous electronic mail” best approximated to our text-only mode. There was no medium in their table that matched our video-only treatment, so we made some conjectures about this medium in order to classify it. Table 3 in the main paper shows the results of matching the media from MST to those in our study. All values are from Dennis et al. except those for the video-only mode.

We created three new variables, one for each media capability that was identifiable in our study. For “transmission velocity” and “reprocessibility,” we coded high as 4 and low as 1. For symbol sets, we used four codes: 4 for very high; 3 for high; 2 for moderate; and 1 for low. Dennis et al. list eight different categories of symbol sets: physical, visual, verbal, written words, tables, images, videos and mathematical models (p. 584). Another way to look at symbol sets is to consider nonverbal behavior, which we can break down into subcategories such as kinesics (body language), proxemics (the use of space in communication), vocalics (the use of pitch, volume, intonation and so on), and haptics (touch) (Burgoon et al. 2016). Dennis et al. cover proxemics and haptics under physical symbol sets and kinesics under visual but they do not explicitly consider vocalics. We consider vocalics to be a separate symbol set that we added to their list.

We reasoned that our full audiovisual snippets included three of these symbol sets: visual, verbal, and vocalics. We also reasoned that audioonly snippets contained two symbol sets, verbal and vocalics. Text featured only one, written words. Video-only snippets also only featured one symbol set, visual, but we considered visual symbol sets to be capable of conveying more information that written words alone. Therefore, we assigned very high (4) to full audiovisual, high (3) to audio, moderate (2) for video only, and low (1) for text. In MST terms (Dennis et al. Table 2), the labels could have been medium, less than medium, few, and less than few, but the distinctions between the categories are equivalent.

For transmission velocity, 75% of the values were high; for reprocessibility, 75% of the values were low. The values for symbol sets were evenly distributed, at 25% each, across the four categories. (See Appendix A for additional information.)
