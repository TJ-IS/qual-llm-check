# STATISTICAL INFERENCE WITH PLSC USING BOOTSTRAP CONFIDENCE INTERVALS<sup>1</sup>

Miguel I. Aguirre-Urreta Florida International University, RB 258A, 11200 SW 8<sup>th</sup> Street, Miami, FL 33199 U.S.A. {miguel.aguirreurreta@fiu.edu}

Mikko Rönkkö Department of Computer Science and Information Systems, University of Jyväskylä, P.O. Box 35, FI-40014 Jyväskylä FINLAND and Department of Industrial Engineering and Management, Aalto University School of Science, P.O. Box 11000, FI-0076 Aalto FINLAND {mikko.ronkko@jyu.fi}

Partial least squares (PLS) is one of the most popular statistical techniques in use in the Information Systems field. When applied to data originating from a common factor model, as is often the case in the discipline, PLS will produce biased estimates. A recent development, consistent PLS (PLSc), has been introduced to correct for this bias. In addition, the common practice in PLS of comparing the ratio of an estimate to its standard error to a t distribution for the purposes of statistical inference has also been challenged. We contribute to the practice of research in the IS discipline by providing evidence of the value of employing bootstrap confidence intervals in conjunction with PLSc, which is a more appropriate alternative than PLS for many of the research scenarios that are of interest to the field. Such evidence is direly needed before a complete approach to the estimation of SEM that relies on both PLSc and bootstrap CIs can be widely adopted. We also provide recommendations for researchers on the use of confidence intervals with PLSc.

Keywords: Partial least squares, significance testing, bootstrap, confidence intervals, statistical inference, disattenuation, consistent partial least squares

## Introduction

Information Systems (IS) researchers commonly use structural equation models (SEMs) for theory testing. In such models, theoretical constructs are typically represented by latent variables, each measured with multiple indicators, and the statistical relationships between the latent variables are used for assessing the proposed causal relationships between the constructs (see Kline 2011, Chapters 1 and 5). To argue causality from quantitative data, researchers must demonstrate that the cause precedes the effect, that the association between the cause and effect is beyond chance, and that the association cannot be explained by third variables (e.g., Antonakis et al. 2010, p. 1087). The second of these conditions, elimination of chance as an explanation, is what we refer to as statistical inference. In IS research, statistical inference is most commonly done by calculating the ratio of an estimate to its standard error and comparing this test statistic against a probability distribution derived under the condition that there is no effect in the population (the null hypothesis). If the p value resulting from this comparison is small, we reject chance as an explanation for the observed association. This type of inference is referred to as null hypothesis significance testing (NHST) in the literature. When applied with the commonly used partial least squares (PLS) estimation technique, the p value is calculated by comparing the ratio of an estimate to its bootstrap standard error against a t distribution (see Chin 1998).

In recent years, however, this practice has been challenged in a number of different ways. First, when applied to data originating from a common factor model, as is commonly the case in the discipline, PLS will produce biased and inconsistent estimates (e.g., Dijkstra 1983; Schneeweiss 1993).<sup>2</sup> As a recent development, consistent PLS (PLSc) (Dijkstra and Henseler 2015) has been introduced to correct for this bias. Second, the common practice of comparing the ratio of a PLS estimate to its standard error to a t distribution has been challenged (Rönkkö and Evermann 2013; Rönkkö et al. 2016). Most importantly, this test assumes that the underlying statistic is normally distributed, which is not necessarily the case with PLS. Though the fact that the test statistic may not follow the t distribution has been recognized before (Goodhue et al. 2007; Sarstedt et al. 2011), the challenges that it presents for statistical inference have only recently begun to be explored (Henseler et al. 2014; McIntosh et al. 2014; Rönkkö and Evermann 2013; Rönkkö et al. 2015). Finally, the general practice of NHST has also been heavily criticized in recent years (Balluerka et al. 2005; Nickerson 2000) (see Appendix B for a more comprehensive discussion of NHST). Taking all of these together, it seems evident there is value in exploring alternative approaches to statistical inference when estimating SEMs with composite-based techniques, as well as in providing evidence of their validity and applicability to the research scenarios that are of interest to the discipline.

The use of confidence intervals (CIs) has often been suggested as an alternative that can address the latter two issues just discussed. For example, Chin (1998) stated that “Various approaches for estimating confidence intervals have been developed” (p. 320) and more recently added that “both a percentile or BCA [sic] approach would be completely distribution free” (Chin 2010, p. 675). Similar recommendations were made by Ringle et al. (2012) who stated that “Reporting (bias corrected) bootstrap confidence intervals … should be more strongly considered in future studies,” but also noted that “no study in our review made use of this useful significance reporting option” (p. viii). Similar suggestions have also been made more recently, particularly with regards to the potential usefulness of bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) bootstrap CIs (Hair et al. 2017; Henseler et al. 2014; Henseler et al. 2009). Despite all of these, the use of CIs remains scarce in the discipline. Moreover, the presentation of PLSc only used the percentile approach and not the most recommended BCa (Dijkstra and Henseler 2015). CIs have also been suggested by researchers who are otherwise critical of PLS approaches, but with the caveat that evidence that demonstrates their usefulness should be provided before their widespread adoption (McIntosh et al. 2014; Rönkkö and Evermann 2013; Rönkkö et. al. 2016). At this moment, however, such evidence is limited to only a couple of studies with conflicting findings (e.g., Henseler et al. 2014; Lu et al. 2011), none of which examined the performance of PLSc, making it difficult for authors who want to use CIs in their PLS-based research to justify their choice of methods beyond stating that someone has recommended their use. This concern is highlighted by Guide and Ketokivi (2015) in a recent Journal of Operations Management editorial: “Authors should always avoid rhetoric such as ‘expert X has suggested that estimator Y be used.’ Such rhetorical appeals must be replaced with methodological justification” (p. vii).

We therefore contribute to the practice of research in the IS discipline by providing evidence that bootstrap CIs, in conjunction with PLSc, can produce valid confidence intervals. Such evidence is direly needed before a complete approach to the estimation of SEMs that relies on both PLSc and bootstrapped CIs can be widely adopted. We start by reviewing some of the recent challenges to the reliance on NHST for statistical inference. We then highlight specific issues in current practices for statistical inference with PLS, which we seek to prevent from also affecting PLSc in the future.<sup>3</sup>

Thereafter, we introduce bootstrap CIs as a potentially valuable alternative, and examine their performance when applied to PLSc (in terms of balance and coverage, which had been omitted from consideration in the few studies previously noted) by means of two simulation studies. We conclude our research with recommendations for researchers on the use of CIs with PLSc.

## Statistical Inferences in PLS: Issues and Alternatives

We begin this section with a review of the current practice of statistical inference with PLS, which follows the NHST approach. The dominant approach for statistical inference in PLS is to first obtain an estimate for a parameter of interest from a PLS analysis, then estimate the standard error for that parameter by the standard deviation of a large number of bootstrap replications, and finally compare the ratio of the original parameter estimate to the standard error against a t distribution. If the p value obtained from the t distribution is smaller than an a priori determined cutoff—typically 5%— the original estimate is taken to be statistically significantly different from zero. In our review of PLS applications in the IS discipline this approach is not just the dominant one, but the only approach that has been used in the literature. This same conclusion was also reached by Ringle et al. (2012). Unfortunately, there are several issues with the process just described, some which are related to the overall approach to significance testing, and some of which are specific to PLS. We review those here in some detail in order to avoid the same issues affecting future research employing PLSc. In what follows, we focus specifically on those issues that are of particular interest for the assessment of significance with regard to PLS and, moving forward, to PLSc. A summary of the general criticism toward NHST (e.g., Balluerka et al. 2005; Nickerson 2000) is provided in Appendix B.

## Issues with Current Practice

Perhaps the most important issue to recognize moving forward is that PLS may not be the most appropriate approach for estimating SEMs when the data are assumed to conform to an underlying common factor model because the estimates produced by this approach are inconsistent and biased in that scenario. The recent development of PLSc (e.g., Dijkstra and Henseler 2015) addresses this problem by disattenuating the correlation matrix of the composites in order to remove the effects of random measurement error from the relationships. While these corrections address the concern that “bootstrapping an inconsistent estimate will not make inferential tests consistent” (McIntosh et al. 2014, p. 229), simply moving from PLS to PLSc will not address all the problems associated with the use of NHST with PLS.

Bias due to random measurement error is not the only problematic issue in the current use of the t statistic with PLS. First, the current literature is ambiguous about the degrees of freedom for the test; while degrees of freedom normally depend on sample size, some authors argue that, with PLS, degrees of freedom should be based on the number of bootstrap replications instead (see Rönkkö et al. 2016). Second, using a t distribution as a reference for assessing the significance of an estimate relies on the assumption that the underlying estimate is normally distributed when the null hypothesis of no effect holds true (Wooldridge 2009, Chapter 4). Not only this is not necessarily the case with PLS estimates, but it also goes against the longstanding position in the PLS literature that the technique does not make any “hard” distributional assumptions (see Chin 1998), though this claim has recently been disputed by Dijkstra (2015).

In addition to the conceptually problematic need to assume underlying normality, we cannot in fact always expect that the test statistic will indeed follow a t distribution when PLS is employed. The reason for this is that the distribution of parameter estimates depends on the distribution of the PLS weights, which remains unknown (Dijkstra 1983). Because the PLS weights are used as the starting point for the PLSc procedure, this issue remains the same for both techniques (Rönkkö, McIntosh, and Aguirre-Urreta 2016). In more extreme cases, when a latent variable of interest is not related to any others in the population, the distribution of the estimates can be bimodal (i.e., two-peaked). This occurs because a latent variable that is uncorrelated with all other latent variables in a population of interest represents a degenerate case where there is not enough information—as there is no relationship between some of the latent variables—for the algorithm to obtain a stable estimate of the relationship (Dijkstra and Schermelleh-Engel 2014). This has also led to the recent recognition that not all SEMs are amenable to estimation with PLS (or PLSc moving forward). Rather, all constructs included in the research model need to have a nonzero relationship to at least another construct (i.e., a nomological net; Henseler et al. 2014; Henseler et al. 2016). This is an important, if overlooked in the past, feature of PLS and PLSc that poses a constraint on the types of models that can be examined. Indeed, a review by Goodhue et al. (2015) indicated that models with weak nomological networks are common in PLS-based IS studies. Even when a construct is embedded in a nomological network, estimates can exhibit long tails (skewness) and therefore be non-normally distributed (Henseler et al. 2014, Figure 5). Therefore, a move away from an explicit assumption of underlying normality of estimates, which the bootstrap CIs examined in this research do not make, is worth exploring.

## Bootstrap Confidence Intervals as an Alternative to NHST

CIs have repeatedly been presented as a desirable alternative to the current reliance on NHST and p values, as they provide the same ability to indicate whether zero would be a possibly reasonable estimate of the relationship under examination— that is, whether zero is inside or outside of the CI—as the current practice of NHST, while also providing information about the accuracy of the parameter estimate, thus reflecting a degree of uncertainty about the results that is absent from NHST and its reject/not-reject dichotomous decisions (Balluerka et al. 2005; Nickerson 2000). As noted by the Task Force on Statistical Inference of the American Psychological Association (Wilkinson 1999): “it is hard to imagine a situation in which a dichotomous accept-reject decision is better than reporting an actual p value or, better still, a confidence interval” (p. 599). That is, providing a range indicating the precision of the estimates is much more informative to researchers than informing solely on “the presence of something or the absence of nothing” (Edwards and Berry 2010). While the move to using CIs in place of simple significance testing will not address all of the challenges of NHST (Cortina and Dunlap 1997), some of which are not statistical in nature, they certainly represent a step in the direction of more informative research results.

A CI is an interval with an upper and lower endpoint corresponding to a predefined confidence level. The two most important characteristics of CIs are coverage and balance. The coverage of a CI is the proportion of times the population value of the parameter is included in the CI in repeated samples. This value should ideally be close to the stated confidence level of the interval—that is, a procedure for constructing 95% CIs should include the population value of the parameter of interest 95% of the time across multiple replications. This means that in 5% of the cases, the population value does not fall within the interval. The balance of a CI refers to how these (ideally) 5% of cases fall to the right of the interval (that is, the population value is greater than the upper boundary of the interval) or to the left (where the population value is smaller than the lower boundary of the interval). In an ideal scenario, CIs should be balanced, with the population value falling to the right or left of the interval the same number of times across all replications, and while achieving the desired level of coverage.

There are several different ways in which CIs can be constructed, of which some require explicit assumptions about the distribution of the underlying statistic or parameter of interest, whereas others do not. Among these we have normal approximation CIs and empirical CIs, which rely on bootstrapping to obtain replicates and then use those to determine the endpoints of the interval (see Wood 2005). The literature presents several different approaches for choosing the replications that are used as endpoints; three methods for doing so are the percentile, bias-corrected (BC), and bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) approaches. Given that all these alternatives are well-known in other fields we leave their formal definition and calculation procedures to Appendix A, and provide only a short overview here.

Normal approximation CIs assume that the underlying statistic is normally distributed, in which case the CIs are completely specified by summary statistics (estimate and standard error). While PLS is often presented as a distribution-free technique, the traditional approach to statistical inference in PLS is in fact equivalent to using normal approximation intervals.<sup>4</sup> Unfortunately, as noted before, there are a number of conceptual issues with the application of a procedure that assumes normality of the estimates, which is neither applicable nor desirable in the case of PLS.

Compared to the normal approximation, empirical approaches have two desirable features, particularly considering their applicability to the case of PLSc. First, empirical approaches avoid the explicit normality assumption that underlies the normal approximation CIs. Second, these techniques use information from the full set of bootstrap estimates instead of simple summary statistics, therefore taking advantage of the full information available from the bootstrap replicates in the construction of the CIs.

The percentile approach is the simplest empirical technique. Percentile CIs are calculated by taking B bootstrap replicates of the estimate, sorting them, and then selecting appropriate replicates (based on the desired confidence level) as the endpoints of the interval. This simple approach works best when the distribution is symmetric and centered on the original estimate. However, bootstrap distributions are generally biased in small samples, thus violating the second assumption. Bias-corrected CIs were developed to correct for this bias, and bias-corrected and accelerated CIs apply an additional adjustment to compensate for skewness of the original statistic. Both of these intervals are obtained by choosing replicates from the bootstrap distribution as the boundaries of the CIs—as was the case before in the percentile method—but instead of choosing fixed replicates, the particular replications used as boundaries are determined by calculating a biascorrection statistic and, in the case of BCa intervals, also an acceleration statistic, both of which are explained in detail in Appendix A. Bias correction has been shown to significantly improve interval coverage, and the use of the acceleration statistic further improves the balance of the intervals (DiCiccio and Efron 1996; Efron and Tibshirani 1993). The latter may be required as many studies show that PLS estimates are indeed skewed, having a longer and heavier negative tail (Goodhue et al. 2012; Henseler et al. 2014), and this can be expected to carry over to PLSc as well.

## Bootstrap Confidence Intervals in PLSc

We conducted two simulation studies in order to examine the performance of the various approaches to the construction of CIs when applied to PLSc. In both studies, all data were generated conforming to an underlying common factor model, as that is the common assumption in empirical applications of PLS in the IS discipline, as well as the basis for the correction introduced by Dijkstra in the development of PLSc (Dijkstra and Henseler 2015). For Study 1, we employed the structure of the simpler model by Goodhue et al. (2012, p. 985; see Figure 1), as depicted in Figure 1. We designed the simulation conditions following previously published research. First, the number of indicators measuring each latent variable in the model was either 3, 6, or 9. Second, the loadings relating each indicator to its latent variable were either all 0.7, 0.8, 0.9, or mixed with one third of the loadings at 0.7, one third at 0.8, and the remaining third of the loadings in each condition at 0.9. Third, data were generated from either a multivariate normal distribution or a non-normal distribution with skewness of 1.8 and excess kurtosis of 0.8; these values correspond to the “high skew” scenario in Goodhue et al. (2012, p. A13). Finally, sample sizes were 50, 150, 250, 500, or 1,000. All of these were combined in a full factorial design, for a total of 480 different conditions. Two variants of this model were employed: As discussed earlier, a latent variable that is uncorrelated with all other latent variables presents a boundary condition to the application of PLSc. To study this boundary condition, the first variant had all exogenous variables uncorrelated, with four alternatives for the strength of the structural paths: [0.480, 0.480, 0.480, 0], [0.314, 0.314, 0.314, 0], [0.114, 0.114, 0.114, 0], or [0.480, 0.314, 0.114, 0] for the $\gamma _ { 1 - 4 }$ parameters; the last scenario corresponds to the values employed by Goodhue et al. (2012). These parameter values correspond to R<sup>2</sup> in the η variable of 0.691, 0.296, 0.039, and 0.342, respectively. In this variant, one of the exogenous variables $( \xi _ { 4 } )$ is completely uncorrelated with all other latent variables in the population. In the second variant, all exogenous variables were correlated at 0.3, and a similar set of paths was employed, modified such that the variance explained in the dependent variable was the same as in the first variant: [0.379, 0.379, 0.379, 0], [0.248, 0.248, 0.248, 0], [0.090, 0.090, 0.090, 0], or [0.379, 0.248, 0.090, 0] for the γ<sub>1-4</sub> parameters.

For Study 2, we employed the more complex model from Goodhue et al. (2012), which includes a more complex web of relationships with no, partial, and full mediation. This model is shown in Figure 2. All other aspects of the simulation design (e.g., number of indicators, loadings, normal or non-normal distribution, etc.) were the same as in Study 1. Given the additional structural complexity of the model employed for Study 2, the values of the structural parameters were kept constant throughout the simulation; these are shown in Figure 2 as well, with the corresponding values of the R<sup>2</sup> for each endogenous latent variable. The total number of conditions in this second simulation study was 120. For each combination of these conditions, 1,000 replications were generated with 10,000 bootstrap replications for each, following recent recommendations about the number of bootstrap replications that should be employed (Streukens and Leroi-Werelds 2016).<sup>5</sup> Both simulations were executed in the R Statistical Environment, using the matrixpls package (Rönkkö 2017).<sup>6</sup> In all simulations, when the original run did not converge, that replication was discarded and a new set of data were drawn.

In what follows we present the results of both simulations with a series of plots showing the performance of the various approaches to the construction of CIs discussed here, for each of the different sample sizes employed, and over the other simulation design factors. In these plots, the performance of all paths over all conditions are collapsed as a single plot, for ease of exposition. The x axis indicates sample size and the y axis the proportion of times the population value of a path was above (below) the upper (lower) limit of a CI. For a 95% CI, on which we focus, this should not occur more than 5% of the time. Moreover, the population value of a path should be above (below) the upper (lower) limit of the CI in a balanced way (that is, no more than 2.5% in each case). Horizontal dashed lines thus indicate the theoretical coverage range of a perfectly balanced 95% CI. Therefore, the closer the lines representing each approach to the construction of a CI are to the dashed lines included in the plots, the closer that approach is to the ideal coverage and balance that the CI should exhibit. To improve the resolution of the plots in the part where a majority of the CIs fall, the scale of the y axis is piecewise linear, such that the ranges between 0%-80%, 80%-95%, and 95%-100% are individually linear.<sup>7</sup>

## Results: Simple Model

We start by focusing on the first variant of the simple model, which represents a boundary condition for the application of PLSc (recall that, in this variant, the latent variable $\xi _ { 4 }$ is completely uncorrelated with all other latent variables). Figure 3 presents the results by sample size and over the four different sets of path values included in the simulation design (labeled strong, medium, weak, and mixed), and collapsed across all other simulation conditions. Figure 4 presents the same results by sample size and number of indicators used to measure each latent variable, and collapsed across other simulation conditions. Figure 5 does the same for the different values assigned to the loadings of each item (all at 0.7, 0.8, 0.9, or a mixed condition, as previously described). Finally, Figure 6 shows the results by sample size and normality of the data, and again collapsed across the remaining simulation conditions.

The figures show five different approaches to the construction of CIs. We constructed CIs following the normal approximation approach and using PLS to obtain parameter estimates (“PLS, norm” in the figures). This is equivalent to the approach to statistical inference that has been employed with PLS in the IS literature for over two decades, and is included here largely as a benchmark. The results show that this technique does not perform well with regard to the desired coverage of the CIs. Though noticeable for both endpoints, the problems are more striking in the upper bounds of the CIs, where the population value of the paths falls outside the CI much more often than would be desirable. This is a result of the biases resulting from using PLS with common factor data, which lead to CIs constructed around biased estimates, and shifting those CIs downward. Counterintuitively, these problems become more severe as sample size increases. The reason for this effect is that smaller samples have a higher degree of sampling variability and this allows the PLS technique to produce higher—that is, in this case less biased— estimates, but as sample size increases and sampling variability correspondingly decreases, the results approximate those that would be expected given the reliability of the composites used to represent the latent variables of interest (Aguirre-Urreta et al. 2013; Goodhue et al. 2012, 2015; Rönkkö 2014).

Moving forward to the CIs based on PLSc estimates, which corrects for attenuation due to the presence of random measurement error, the figures show that, even with disattenuation, the normal approximation (“PLSc, norm” in the figures) does not work well. In particular, the CIs constructed in this manner are too wide compared to their theoretical coverage. Even though their coverage improves slightly as sample size increases, they are not at all close to their theoretical coverage even for samples of 1,000, which are much larger than those commonly used in the discipline.

Turning to the empirical bootstrap approaches, we first consider the percentile approach (“PLSc, perc” in the figures), which is the same used by Dijkstra and Henseler (2015) in their presentation of PLSc. As the results show, the performance of this approach is similar to that of the normal approximation with PLSc: CIs that are too wide but improve as sample size increases (and do so much faster than those constructed with the normal approximation). The BC and BCa intervals are on average slightly closer to the nominal coverage than normal or percentile intervals and improve markedly as sample size increases, but produce intervals that are too narrow. The effect of the acceleration coefficient is small, but on average the BCa intervals are slightly better balanced than the BC intervals.

We now focus on the second variant of the simple model, where all exogenous latent variables are correlated, thus satisfying the need for a nomological net relating all latent variables in the research model to at least one other, as required by both PLS and PLSc (e.g., Dijkstra and Henseler 2015). As before, the results are presented graphically by sample size and focal design factor, and collapsed across all others in the simulation design: path strength (Figure 7), number of indicators (Figure 8), loading strength (Figure 9), and normality of the data (Figure 10).

The performance of PLS together with normal approximation CIs is again quite suboptimal, and does not provide appropriate coverage in any of the simulation conditions. Normal approximation and percentile CIs constructed from PLSc estimates are too wide at the lower end of the range of sample sizes, and only approach their theoretical coverage as sample size increases. Although this is in evidence for both endpoints of the CIs, it is clearer in the case of the upper bound (see, for example, Figure 9). However, these CIs only approach their theoretical coverage for samples of 1,000 or greater, which is much more than the commonly used sample sizes in IS research.<sup>8</sup> The BC and BCa CIs are closer to their theoretical coverage even for reasonably small samples, but are again too narrow and contain the population value less than the nominal 95% of the time.

The simulation condition where the exogenous variables are only weakly related to the endogenous variable does again present a challenge to all approaches to the construction of CIs, although less so for the BC and BCa alternatives as before. See the “weak” condition in Figure 7. In this simulation condition, the four exogenous variables, taken together, explain slightly less than 4% of the variance in the dependent variable. This is a result of very weak paths relating the exogenous and endogenous variables ([0.090, 0.090, 0.090, 0] for the four paths). As a result, though not strictly violating the requirement of a nomological network relating all variables to at least one other in the research model, the presence of a weak one does lead to unstable results which should be interpreted with caution.

To summarize the results of this first simulation, the empirical approaches to the construction of CIs using results from PLSc show substantial superior performance over the normal approximation intervals, which have traditionally received the most focus in the PLS literature (e.g., Hair et al. 2014).<sup>9</sup> Our results, presented over the various conditions of the simulations, demonstrate that while the coverage of all three empirical CIs deviates from the nominal levels in small samples, their performance increases with increasing sample size (except for the anomalous performance of BC/BCa with weak paths noted above). While the BC/BCa intevals are slightly closer to the nominal coverage levels than the percentile intervals, the key difference between these two sets of techniques is that the BC/BCa are too narrow whereas the percentile intervals are overly wide. This is an important consideration because if confidence intervals deviate from the nominal coverage, they should be biased toward being conservative (Meeker et al. 2017, Chapter 5; Smithson 2003, Chapter 2). That is, if we cannot estimate intervals that have the exact nominal coverage, we should err on the side of caution and choose an interval that is overly wide over choosing an interval that is too narrow. Therefore, the results from the small model suggest always choosing the percentile intervals, which have a coverage greater than the nominal 95% but always approach the nominal coverage as sample size increases.

## Results: Complex Model

The results for the complex model, shown in Figure 2, are presented graphically in Figure 11, Figure 12, and Figure 13. The issues related to the performance of CIs built with the normal approximation have been extensively discussed when the results for the simple model were presented above and will thus not be considered here in any further detail. For all other CI approaches, the resulting intervals are substantially similar to those examined in the previous model except that with this model the coverage error of both percentile and BC/BCa intervals is always in the same direction. Sample size has again a marked effect in that, particularly for the upper bound, the CIs approximate theoretical coverage more closely as sample size increases. This effect is stronger when there are more indicators in the model. Both the BC and BCa approaches perform quite close to the theoretical expectations in terms of balance and coverage when sample size is above 150, with a small advantage for BCa.

Figure 12 presents the results across the different levels of indicator loadings and Figure 13 show the results by the normality condition. As before, there is a marked advantage for both the BC and BCa over the other approaches (and the same smaller advantage of BCa over BC as noted before). In all cases, when sample sizes are at 150 or above, the BC and BCa approaches perform close to theoretical coverage of a 95% CI and the loadings and non-normality have little impact on this result. Other alternatives, such as the normal or percentile approaches, only approximate the theoretical coverage with samples at or above 500 when applied to non-normal data or weak loadings scenario, which largely exceed the common sample sizes employed in IS research.

To summarize, the results of our simulation study employing the complex model—which may be more representative of the models commonly employed in IS research practice that include multiple chained latent variables—underscore the poor performance of the normal approximation CIs when applied to traditional PLS without the disattenuation correction. The key difference between the performance of the empirical CIs for the PLSc estimates between the simple model and the complex model is that with the complex model, the coverage error of the CIs is always to the same direction, which would suggest preferring the BCa intervals in this model because these intervals converged toward the nominal coverage faster than the alternatives as sample size increases. However, a closer inspection of the results on an individual path level<sup>10</sup> reveals that this conclusion may not be warranted.

As before, we emphasize that if confidence intervals cannot achieve the nominal coverage and balance, we should choose the most accurate conservative interval and avoid techniques where the population value falls either above or below the interval too frequently. On individual path level, the population value fell below the lower limit too frequently for all techniques for the simple regression paths $\gamma _ { 1 } , \gamma _ { 3 }$ , and $\gamma _ { 5 } .$ For all other paths, the performance of the empirical techniques was nearly identical as long as sample size was at 150 or more.

## Discussion, Recommendations, and Conclusion

We began this research by noting the existence of a number of issues with what has been the standard practice with regard to statistical inference when applying PLS to data arising from common factor models, as is commonly the case in the discipline. Given that it is now clear that PLSc is a more appropriate approach for this purpose, we have sought to provide evidence of the adequacy of using empirical bootstrap CIs to perform statistical inference with PLSc, and thus avoid facing the same issues that were criticized for the case of PLS (e.g., biased estimates, underlying normality assumptions, excessive focus on binary yes/no decisions). By using empirical CIs in conjunction with PLSc it is possible to overcome these problems—and therefore adequate statistical inference—without invoking any explicit distributional assumptions as part of the process. In our simulations, all empirical intervals generally improved with sample size. This is consistent with the broader literature on bootstrapping, which has always presented bootstrapping as a large sample technique (Davison and Hinkley 1997; Efron 1992; Efron and Tibshirani 1993). While the position that bootstrapping is applicable to small samples can often be found in the empirical literature, the tenability of these claims is questionable (Koopman et al. 2014).

In choosing which interval to report, we emphasize that when a confidence interval with the exact nominal coverage cannot be calculated, a conservative interval with over-coverage is preferable over intervals that are too narrow (see Meeker et al. 2017, Chapter 5; Smithson 2003, Chapter 2). While the BC and BCa approaches often produced valid CIs with sample sizes of 150 or more, these techniques also produced intervals that suffered from under-coverage. We therefore recommend that the percentile CIs should be reported as standard practice.

Practicing researchers interested in applying empirical bootstrap CIs in their PLS-based research should proceed as follows.<sup>11</sup> First, after collecting data and specifying their theoretical research model, they should obtain estimates for all parameters of interest from a run of PLS. Second, the corrections implemented in PLSc (e.g., Dijkstra and Henseler 2015) should be applied to the original PLS estimates in order to correct those for the effects of attenuation due to random measurement error. Third, a large number of bootstrap resamples (e.g., 10,000, as recently recommended in the literature, see Streukens and Leroi-Werelds 2016) of the same sample size as the original research and with the same research model specification should be obtained, and the same process—obtaining estimates from PLS and then correcting them with PLSc—should be performed on each of the bootstrap replicates. These bootstrap replications should be performed without any sign-change corrections (Henseler et al. 2016; Rönkkö et al. 2015) and that no sign-change corrections were used should be explicitly reported. Instead, researchers should fix the signs of the PLS weights by either fixing the sign of the “dominant indicator” (Henseler et al. 2016, p. 7) or by choosing the signs so that a majority of the indicators are positively correlated with the composite (Wold 1985, p. 585; used in our study); if using the latter procedure, all negatively coded items should be reversed prior to weight calculation.<sup>12</sup> The bootstrap replications are then used to calculate the endpoints of a percentile CI with the desired level of confidence (e.g., 95%) for each of the estimated parameters. The resulting CIs provide researchers with the range of plausible values for the estimate of interest, given the desired confidence level, thus also showcasing the uncertainty of the estimate (depending on the width of the resulting CI). BCa intervals should be reported as supplementary results along with the indices of the bootstrap replicates that were chosen as the end points if space permits so that future research can compare how these two kinds of intervals behave in various empirical applications.

Models that contain uncorrelated or only weakly correlated latent variables present a boundary condition for the applicability of PLSc. Because researchers cannot generally assume a priori that all relationships in their models are sufficiently strong, this must be diagnosed. First, CIs that either produce inadmissible endpoints or do not include the original estimate should not be trusted. Second, the indices used to select the end points for the BC and BCa should be inspected for unusual values (e.g., using the 100<sup>th</sup> observation out of 10,000 replications as the upper limit). These occur in scenarios where the data does not provide sufficient information for accurate estimation (see Appendix C). Additionally, Goodhue et al. (2015) further point out that variables with just one incoming or outgoing path may be problematic. While our simple model had four exogenous variables each with just one outgoing path, the percentile intervals were always conservative, leading a researcher at worst to conclude that there is great uncertainty in the results. However, our results for the complex model support the claim that variables with a single incoming path $( \gamma _ { 1 } , \gamma _ { 3 } , \mathrm { a n d } \gamma _ { 5 } )$ are problematic because, with all techniques, the population value fell below the lower limit more often than the nominal 2.5% of the case unless the sample size was very large.

In these problematic cases, researchers are faced with two alternatives. First, assuming all other requirements are met, they can estimate their research models by fitting covariance matrices (CBSEM), which does not have the same requirement of a strong nomological network as does PLSc (provided that the model is otherwise identified). If wanting to remain with composite-based approaches, rather than relying on unstable weights in this scenario, we recommend that researchers use fixed weights for the problematic latent variables.<sup>13</sup> The most straightforward alternative is to fix the weights to be equal (Rigdon 2012) and a large number of studies support the robustness of this alternative (Graefe 2015) over more complex weighting systems. Moreover, when an underlying common factor model holds, as is the case throughout this research, the difference between unit weights and PLS weights should be small unless there is a theoretical reason to expect the weights to differ (Gefen et al. 2011, p. viii). This is even more so the case when researchers retain only those items that are strongly related to the construct of interest (e.g., loadings greater than 0.7 or a similar cutoff), which also leads to reduced variability in the underlying weights of the retained items. At first sight, it may appear that using fixed weights within the PLSc procedure is problematic because the disattenuation correction is based on the assumption that the indicator weights are proportional to factor loadings. But this may not be as severe a problem as it seems: the $\rho _ { A }$ coefficient used as reliability estimate in PLSc is equivalent to Cronbach’s alpha if the weights are equal.<sup>14</sup> While Cronbach’s alpha is a biased estimator for the reliability of equally weighted composites unless all indicators are equally reliable, in practice the differences between alphas and composite reliability statistics, which do not assume equal reliabilities, are very small (Peterson and Kim 2013) and there is some simulation evidence that the bias of Cronbach’s alpha is small (Rönkkö, McIntosh, and Aguirre-Urreta 2016). Another alternative would be to factor analyze the data for each construct separately (e.g., by using Stata or SPSS) and using the loadings of the first unrotated factor as the weights in the analysis. Because the PLSc correction relies on the fact that weights are asymptotically proportional to factor loadings, this approach would have exactly the same asymptotic properties as PLSc.

One recurrent criticism of NHST is that it leads researchers to focus on whether an estimate is significantly different from zero without considering the practical magnitude of the results. Particularly for large sample sizes (Lin et al. 2013) even minor deviations from the hypothesis of no effect will reach significance. A significant estimate, however, is not necessarily of practical relevance. One of the key advantages of CIs is that they steer researchers away from the dichotomous thinking used in NHST. Therefore, when interpreting their results, researchers should also be mindful of the magni tude of the effect under consideration and the endpoints of the interval and not only whether zero is included in or excluded from the CI. As is the case with any other research that relies on simulations, there are some limitations that researchers should take into consideration, which also provide avenues for future research in this area. First, our results arise from consideration of two different population models, which had been used in previous research by Goodhue et al. (2012). Although those are complex enough to be representative of applied research in the IS discipline, certainly other models with a different number of latent variables, pattern of relationships, and strength of those, could be considered. Second, we varied several conditions (sample size, number of indicators, strength and pattern of loadings) in our simulations using values that we believe to be representative of IS research practice. Different values could be chosen to understand the performance of the CIs under more extreme conditions. Third, we examined the performance of the most common and wellunderstood approaches to the construction of bootstrap CIs, which also enjoy widespread software support. Certainly other, more sophisticated, approaches, such as the double bootstrap (e.g., McCullough and Vinod 1998) could be examined to see if the increase in computational demands that is required translates into improved performance. Fourth, our focus here was solely on the PLS family of approaches. There are, however, other approaches based on composites, such as GSCA (Hwang and Takane 2004) or even summed scales (Rigdon 2012) that could be employed. We should note, however, that these would suffer from the same issues with regard to attenuation due to random measurement error as is the case for PLS, and which PLSc seeks to correct. Fortunately, the disattenuation correction does not depend on any particular weights employed (Rönkkö, McIntosh, and Aguirre-Urreta 2016), and the literature on reliability estimation provides several techniques that can be used (Aguirre-Urreta et al. 2013). Future research should focus on ascertaining which approach to statistical inference works best depending on which estimator is chosen.

To summarize, this research examined the performance of various approaches for statistical inference using PLSc. We examined alternative empirical approaches to statistical inference based on bootstrap CIs, and constructed those with estimates obtained from the new PLSc procedure, which corrects for the presence of random measurement error. Of these alternative approaches, the percentile interval provides a conservative alternative and is thus our recommended approach for practice moving forward, providing users with an effective way to counter the recent criticism that PLS methods cannot be used for null hypothesis significance testing (McIntosh et al. 2014; Rönkkö and Evermann 2013). This research presents much needed evidence to support the use of a practice that is already being recommended for the field by leading scholars (Chin 2010; Hair et al. 2017; Ringle et al. 2012),<sup>15</sup> but for which research supporting its effectiveness was severely lacking, thus leaving researchers open to challenges as to its validity (Rönkkö et al. 2016). We believe this research clearly shows that the proposed approach is not only a valid alternative for statistical inference with PLSc, but also a substantial improvement over current practice.

## Bootstrapped Confidence Intervals

Confidence intervals (CIs) are an alternative to null hypothesis significance testing (NHST) (Nickerson 2000; Wood 2005); both techniques essentially convey information about how certain we are of an estimate. A CI consists of two values, upper and lower confidence limits, associated with a confidence level, typically 95%. Valid CIs should satisfy two important conditions (DiCiccio and Efron 1996). First, a C should contain the population value of the parameter under estimation with the stated degree of confidence over a large number of repeated samples. For example, a 95% CI should contain the population value of a parameter 95% of the time, with the true value of the parameter falling outside of the interval only in 5% of the cases. Second, in those cases where the true value of the parameter falls beyond the boundaries of the interval, it should do so in a balanced way. Using again the 95% CI as an example, the population value should be higher than the upper boundary in 2.5% of the samples and lower than the lower boundary of the interval 2.5% of the time.

We illustrate these two properties of CIs in Figure A1. The figure shows 250 correlation estimates drawn from three different populations (where the true value of the correlation is 0, 0.3, or 0.6, respectively), ordered from smallest to largest, and their 95% CIs. In this scenario, the population value falls outside the CI only about 5% of the time and does so in a balanced way, such that the population value lies above the CI 2.5% of the time and below the CI 2.5% of the time. The figure also shows that both the variance of the estimates and the width of the CIs depend on the population value of the correlation; when the population correlation is zero, the difference between the largest and smallest estimate is close to 0.5, but when the population value is 0.6 this difference decreases to about 0.35. Similarly, the CIs are narrower for larger estimates. This is an important feature of CIs that unfortunately complicates their calculation, as we discuss later.

The CI of a correlation has a valid closed form solution, but estimating CIs for more complex scenarios is a non-trivial problem. The most straightforward way to estimate CIs is to use a known theoretical distribution. We refer to these as parametric approaches. When the distribution of the estimates is not known, as is the case with those obtained from PLSc, CIs based on bootstrapping provide an attractive alternative (Wood 2005). In these approaches, which we refer to as empirical, the endpoints of the CIs are not taken from a known statistical distribution, but rather the values are obtained from the empirically approximated bootstrap distribution.

Bootstrapping means that we draw a large number of samples from our original data and calculate the statistic for each sample. The samples are drawn with replacement, which means that each observation in the original sample can be included in each bootstrap sample multiple times. While bootstrapping can be useful when working with statistics whose sampling distributions are unknown, it is not a silver bullet. A key assumption behind bootstrapping is that the bootstrap estimates follow the same distribution as the original statistic, but this is not always the case, and can lead to incorrect inference unless corrections are applied. Generally, many properties of bootstrap estimators have been proven only for the asymptotic case (Davison and Hinkley 1997, pp. 37-41) and may therefore work well only with large samples.

Empirical CIs can be calculated from bootstrapped estimates in many different ways. Three such approaches are the percentile, bias-corrected, and bias-corrected and accelerated CIs, which we review next. More technical discussions can be found in DiCiccio and Efron (1996), Efron and Tibshirani (1993), or Davidson and Hinkley (1997). All three types of CIs are calculated from the same bootstrap replication data. In the percentile approach, all bootstrap estimates of a parameter of interest are ordered from smallest to largest. Then, two particular estimates are selected to represent the boundaries of the CI with a desired coverage (e.g., the 250<sup>th</sup> and 9750<sup>th</sup> replicates for a 95% interval based on 10,000 bootstrap replications). This is the simplest way of creating an empirical CI from the bootstrap replications, and works best when the distribution is symmetrical and centered on the original estimate. While simple, this approach is often suboptimal because of two problems.

First, bootstrap estimates are generally biased in small samples. The bootstrap bias is illustrated in Figure A2, which shows the sampling distribution of the mean from a normally distributed population and three bootstrapped distributions for four different sample sizes. The bootstrapped distributions are roughly the right shape and width, but are off sideways.

Second, the sampling variance of a statistic can depend on the population value, as briefly mentioned in reference to Figure A1 earlier. In bootstrapping, the original sample is essentially treated as a population from which samples are drawn, and this makes the variance of the bootstrap replications dependent on the value of the original estimate, particularly if the original statistic is itself skewed. The problem of uneven variance is illustrated in Figure A3, which shows PLS estimates from a simple example with two exogenous latent variables, which are uncorrelated, and one endogenous latent variable. All latent variables are measured with three indicators with loadings of 0.7 and errors of 0.51. The paths relating the latent variables are 0.4 and 0, thus explaining 16% of the variance in the endogenous latent variable. The sample size for this example is 100 and we replicated the analysis 1,000 times. For this example, we are focusing on the nonzero path coefficient (population value of 0.4) for the case of normal data.

Using these data in PLS estimation resulted in a substantially left-skewed sampling distribution of the estimates. To demonstrate the effects that a skewed distribution has on the bootstrap, we chose six replications producing a wide range of estimates. These six selected samples were each bootstrapped with 1,000 replications and we estimated the distribution of the PLS estimates with these six sets of resamples. We then centered the six resulting bootstrap distributions to the mean of the original distribution, thus completely eliminating bootstrap bias, and plotted the unbiased distributions over the original distribution in Figure A3. The figure shows a clear skew in the original estimates and consequently the variance of the bootstrap estimates is uneven depending on the value of the original estimate. As a result, the 95% intervals of the distributions calculated from the bootstrap replications do not always match those calculated from the original distribution, and any CIs based on these distributions would be problematic

Bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) CIs were developed to simultaneously address both of these problems, and have been shown to significantly improve interval coverage and balance (DiCiccio and Efron 1996; Efron and Tibshirani 1993). Like percentile intervals, these are obtained by choosing replicates from the bootstrap distribution as the boundaries of the CIs, but instead of using fixed replicates the choice is based on two statistics that correct for the median bias and uneven variance of the bootstrap distribution.

We will now explain these corrections in more detail. The bias-corrected (BC) CI is best understood as a special case of the more general biascorrected and accelerated interval. Therefore, we explain the more complex BCa correction first. Let θ be the original estimate, $\theta ^ { * } ( b )$ the estimate for the same parameter obtained from the $b ^ { t h }$ bootstrap sample, and B the number of bootstrap samples. The bias correction adjustment is calculated as (DiCiccio and Efron 1996, eq. 2.8, p. 193):

$$
z = \phi^ {- 1} \left(\frac {\# \left\{\theta * (b) <   \theta \right\}}{B}\right)
$$

Where #{condition} is the number of samples that meet the condition in parentheses, in this case being lower than the original estimate, and $\phi ^ { - 1 }$ <sup>1</sup> is the inverse function of the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution.

To address the issue of uneven variance, an acceleration coefficient is calculated to measure how quickly the standard error of the estimate is changing on a normalized scale. Efron and Tibshirani (1993) suggested that this acceleration coefficient be calculated using the jackknife (“keep one out”) estimates of the parameter of interest. Let $\theta _ { ( i ) }$ be the estimate obtained on the sample with the $i ^ { t h }$ case removed, n the number of jackknife samples, and $\theta _ { \scriptscriptstyle ( ^ { * } ) }$ be the mean of the estimates from the jackknife samples. The acceleration coefficient is calculated as (DiCiccio and Efron 1996, eq. 6.6):<sup>1</sup>

$$
a = \frac {\sum_ {i = i} ^ {n} \left(\theta_ {(*)} - \theta_ {(i)}\right) ^ {3}}{6 \left\{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left(\theta_ {(*)} - \theta_ {(i)}\right) ^ {2} \right\} ^ {3 / 2}}
$$

The bias-correction and acceleration constants are then used to select which bootstrap replicates will be used as the boundaries of the CI. Let z be the bias-correction coefficient and a the acceleration coefficient (as defined above), B the number of bootstrap replications,  the standard normal cumulative distribution, $\phi ^ { - 1 }$ the inverse function of the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution, and α the chosen significance level for the CI (5% throughout this research). We can then calculate coefficients ${ \bf { q } } _ { 1 }$ and ${ \bf { a } } _ { 2 }$ as (Davison and Hinkley 1997, eq. 5.21, p. 204):

$$
\alpha_ {1} = \phi \left(z + \frac {z + \phi^ {- 1} (\alpha / 2)}{1 - a (z + \phi^ {- 1} (\alpha / 2))}\right)
$$

$$
\alpha_ {2} = \phi \left(z + \frac {z + \phi^ {- 1} (1 - \alpha / 2)}{1 - a (z + \phi^ {- 1} (1 - \alpha / 2))}\right)
$$

These two coefficients are then multiplied by the number of bootstrap replications to identify the two replicates to be used as the lower and upper boundaries for the CI (LB = Lower Boundary, UB = Upper Boundary) (Davison and Hinkley 1997):

$$
L B = B * \alpha_ {1}
$$

$$
U B = B * \alpha_ {2}
$$

When the acceleration statistic is zero, this simplifies to the bias-corrected interval. When both the acceleration and bias-correction statistics are zero, the chosen replications will be the same as in the simpler case of the percentile interval discussed before.

## Null Hypothesis Significance Testing

## Criticisms of Null Hypothesis Significance Testing (NHST)

The practice of NHST, even though commonplace in the social and behavioral sciences, has been the subject of much criticism for more than 40 years (Balluerka et al. 2005); for comprehensive treatments of these discussions, see Oakes (1986), Kline (2004), or Ziliak and McCloskey (2008); for a recent commentary, see Antonakis (2017). In order to ground our discussion, the NHST as performed by applied researchers can be summarized in the following sequence of activities (Balluerka et al. 2005; Gigerenzer 2004; Levine et al. 2008). First, a null hypothesis is proposed regarding the value of a parameter in the population. Although not strictly necessary, this hypothesis is most commonly that of no effect, also labeled the null-nil hypothesis. Second, a test statistic—typically the ratio of an estimate and its standard error—is chosen. Third, a random sample of data is obtained and the statistic is calculated in this sample. Fourth, the probability of obtaining a test statistic, which is as large as or larger than the value actually obtained, given the sample size and assuming that the null hypothesis is true in the population, known as the p value, is calculated. Finally, the obtained probability is compared against a predefined criterion (most commonly ${ \mathfrak { a } } = 0 . 0 5 )$ . If the obtained probability is less than the criterion, the null hypothesis is rejected, and this is taken to provide support for the alternative hypothesis (though this is potentially problematic, as discussed later). There are two sets of criticisms commonly levelled against NHST, which we cover in more detail in the next two sections.

## Misinterpretations of NHST or Misconceptions about the Process

The first set of issues has to do with the presence of major misconceptions about how NHST works and how its results should be interpreted. Our discussion is based on Nickerson (2000), who covers the issues in great detail. For additional discussion on these see Hubbard and Lindsay (2008), Gliner et al. (2002), Gigerenzer (2004), Gigerenzer et al. (2004), or Greenwald et al. (1996). These problems include (page references are to the work of Nickerson (2000), which discusses them in detail) the belief that p is the probability that the null is true and that, by extension, 1 – p is the probability that the alternative hypothesis is true (p. 246), the belief that rejection of the null hypothesis establishes the truth of a theory that predicts it to be false in the first place (p. 254), the belief that a small p value is evidence that results are replicable (p. 256), th belief that a small $p$ value indicates an effect size of large magnitude (p. 257), the belief that statistical significance implies theoretical or practical significance (or relevance) (p. 257), the belief that α is the probability of committing a type I error if the null hypothesis has been rejected (p. 258), the belief that the value at which α is set for a given experiment or a large set of experiments is the probability of type I erro for those (p. 259), the belief that failing to reject the null hypothesis is tantamount to demonstrating it to be true (p. 260), or the belief tha failing to reject the null hypothesis is evidence of poorly conducted research (p. 261).

All these represent misunderstandings or misconceptions about NHST and therefore do not, as such, present any fundamental challenges to practice (unlike those reviewed next). However, these are still worrisome, in that they reflect long-standing misunderstandings of a very widespread approach in the social and behavioral sciences, and should be cause of concern for the different disciplines that make extensive use of NHST.

## Fundamental Challenges to NHST

There are also a number of issues that present fundamental challenges to the practice of NHST and which highlight the need for alternative approaches to the assessment of statistical estimates, with the use of CIs being one of the central recommendations arising from this literature. These challenges are reviewed here in more detail below; for an accessible introduction see Nuzzo (2014).

First, several researchers have challenged the practice of NHST—particularly when coupled with null-nil hypotheses of no effect—on the grounds that the null hypothesis is never strictly true, at least in the social and behavioral sciences (Balluerka et al. 2005, p. 58; Levine et al. 2008, p. 176; Nickerson 2000, p. 263; Schmidt and Hunter 2002). From this position, rejecting something that is not true in the first place is neither informative nor useful (Abelson 1997; Cohen 1994). A direct corollary is that all that is required to obtain significance is a large enough sample size, as there is always some underlying effect to detect (the issue of whether these effects are of any relevant magnitude is a different problem). In most cases, researchers are not strictly interested in whether an effect is precisely zero, but rather in whether it is close enough to zero to be of no practical interest.

Second, NHST has been heavily criticized for its sensitivity to sample size (Levine et al. 2008, p. 176; Nickerson 2000, p. 265). When the sample size is small, even strong—and arguably practically relevant—effects will fail to reach significance. Alternatively, large samples can make even smaller— and inconsequential—effects be significant. As a result, the p values obtained from NHST reflect not only the magnitude of the effect but also the investment in resources expended by researchers in collecting data. This can lead to ignoring important effects arising from small samples and embracing minor ones solely because a large enough sample was collected.<sup>2</sup> This is not a desirable property for the scientific enterprise (Levine et al. 2008).

Third, NHST has been criticized on the grounds that it is illogical (Balluerka et al. $2 0 0 5 , { \mathfrak { p } } . 5 7 ;$ Nickerson 2000, p. 267). This refers to a rule of logic called modus tollens, which is valid when statements are categorical, but not so when those are probabilistic, as is the case when dealing with empirical tests of a theory (Cohen 1994; Falk and Greenbaum 1995; Macdonald 1997). The general form of the statement “If P then Q; not $\mathcal { Q } ,$ therefore not $P ^ { * }$ is not necessarily valid when the premises are probabilistic, that is $ { \mathrm { ~  ~ \omega ~ } } _ { I f P }$ then probably Q; not Q, therefore probably not $P _ { \mathrm { } } ^ { \prime \prime } , 0 \Gamma ,$ in NHST terms, ${ } ^ { \sc } { H H _ { o } }$ is true then probabl $p > . 0 5 ; p < . 0 5 ,$ therefore probably $H _ { o }$ is false” (Nickerson 2000). Carver (1978) labeled this the “odds-against-chance fantasy” (p. 382).

Finally, other challenges to NHST focus on its value, or usefulness, for the practice of theory testing and, more generally, for the advancement of scientific knowledge. Among these, the following are likely those of most importance. First, NSHT tests provide relatively little information about the outcomes of a research study (Balluerka et al. 2005, p. 58; Nickerson 2000, p. 268). One such position is that the aims of NHST and that of statistical inference—the latter more in line with the aims of researchers—are substantially different. That is, researchers are interested in the probability that the null hypothesis is true based on the observed results [more formally, $p ( H _ { \boldsymbol { o } } | D ) ]$ , whereas NHST only informs the probability of obtaining observed results if the case of the null hypothesis being true [that is, $p ( D _ { \theta } | H _ { \theta } ) ]$ , which is not the same thing. Second, the “all or nothing” nature of NHST means that a p value that is only slightly greater than the α level is lumped together with those where they value is much greater. Many researchers find this practice problematic, which is generally expressed in statements about “marginally significant,” “borderline significant,” or “significance at the more liberal level of (for example) ${ | p < . 0 2 } ^ { \prime }$ Moreover, nonsignificant results are largely ignored which, coupled with a strong bias toward the publication of significant results, inhibits the accumulation of knowledge. Closely related to this is the reification of an arbitrary decision criterion as the arbiter of significance $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e } } . \boldsymbol { \mathrm { g } } . , \boldsymbol { \mathrm { a } } = . 0 5 )$ (Nickerson 2000, p. 269). Finally, but of central importance, is the argument that NHST does not actually enable the testing of our theories (Balluerka et al. 2005, p. 58). In current practice, a researcher develops a set of arguments for why a relationship would be expected to be observed between certain variables (or, in experimental designs, why a difference between groups would be expected). Null and alternative hypotheses are presented as competing and mutually exclusive, and rejection of the null hypothesis is seen as providing support for the alternative one, which in turn provides evidence in support of the theory. There is, however, an important disconnect between these last two steps. That is, the rejection of the null-nil hypothesis of no effect does not automatically imply that the observed effects are due to the reasons postulated by researchers in their theoretical developments. Rather, this inference from observed results to theory can only be made when care has been taken to design and execute a research design that excludes other competing theoretical reasons that could explain the observed results, as well as any methodological confounds that could be in operation. None of this, however, can be ascertained solely by considering the significance (or not) of the results.

## Moving Forward

Given these extensive criticisms of past research practice with regard to NHST, recent years have witnessed the emergence of a number of published recommendations for the reporting and interpretation of research findings that seek to ameliorate many of these issues; the spirit of much of this renewed emphasis on interpretation of research findings in a more extensive light has been captured in the recommendations provided by Cumming (2014, see Table 1). To be fair, some of these criticisms of NHST have also been challenged in the literature. It could be argued that we need some criteria on what can be published and p <.05 presents a fairly low bar that is necessary for ruling out chance as an explanation, but should not be interpreted as being sufficient evidence of a meaningful finding (Cortina and Landis 2011). However, most importantly, there is no reason to believe that a researcher who misused or misinterpreted the results of NHST would not misuse or misinterpret CIs as well (Cortina and Landis 2011) and CIs and NHST are both based on the same statistical theory, so therefore CIs cannot solve all issues with NHST (Trafimow and Marks 2015). Thompson (2001, pp. 82-83), in an often cited quote, summarizes the discussion bluntly “if people interpret effect sizes with the same rigidity with which α = .05 has been used in statistical testing, we would merely be being stupid in another metric.” Nevertheless, we argue that, for example, when a statistic that is normally distributed in the sample has a point estimate of 0.2, stating that the p value is less than 0.05 is more likely to lead to false conclusions about the accuracy of the estimate than stating that the 95% CI for the estimate is 0.004–0.396. If one wants to make a dichotomous conclusion about a theoretical hypothesis based on a statistical analysis, the choice between NHST and normal approximation CIs is in many cases largely a personal preference, as both lead to the same conclusion. However, the same does not apply to the empirical CIs that we discuss in this research.

To address the issues presented above, a number of different approaches have been taken in the literature. First, some journals, such as Epidemiology or Basic and Applied Social Psychology, have taken an extreme view on the issue and instituted editorial guidelines outright banning the use of NHST in their published research. For the latter case, for example, the editors have noted that, while submissions that include mention of p values and the like will not be subject to automatic desk rejection, all such indications of the use of NHST (such as p values, t values, F values, statement about “significant” results, etc.) will need to be removed prior to publication. The ban is also extended to the use of CIs as well (Trafimow and Marks 2015).

Second, while not going to the same lengths, there have been renewed calls to move away from the binary decisions (significant or not) underlying the practice of NHST and to focus more on providing estimates of the magnitude of an effect that consider both the magnitude of the estimate as well as its precision. The use of effect sizes, together with CIs, has been strongly advocated for that purpose. CIs are seen as accommodating a more varied gamut of interpretations of the results, can potentially provide more insight into the findings as they provide a much more clear perspective on the range of possible outcomes that are implied by the collected data and theoretical model tested, and more heavily encourage replication in pursuit of subsequent narrowing of that range, thus leading to potentially greater insight into the research question (Balluerka et al. 2005; Kline 2004). At the very least, and taking into consideration the caveats discussed above, CIs provide no less information than what is made available under current reporting practices (whether an estimate is different from zero) but, by making clear the range of possible outcomes that go together with that binary assessment, give a more complete picture of the results

Third, researchers have also started to advocate that, together with the reporting of CIs, the estimates obtained from the analysis of the data and research model are expressed in the form of effect sizes as well as in the original metric of the study (Thompson 1999). Likely even more important than publishing CIs for the original results, however, is also the inclusion of CIs for the effect sizes as well (Thompson 2007). This practice has the distinct advantage of moving researchers away from dichotomous assessments and into discussions of the magnitude of reported effects (though it could be argued most research practice in this area is rudimentary, still relying on rules of thumb as to what constitutes a large, medium, or small effect). This, in turn, leads naturally into considerations of the practical relevance of research findings, as opposed to its statistical significance. With the increasing availability of very large datasets, that distinction between results that are statistically significant but practically negligible, and those that are both statistically significant and practically relevant is more likely to take on a central role in our discussion of the implications of our research (Lin et al. 2013).

Fourth, together with a renewed emphasis on reporting more as well as different information about the research findings (e.g., effect sizes and CIs), new recommendations have arisen for how to best describe and present data (in both tabular and graphical form) in order to enhance our understanding of the findings (Valentine et al. 2015). These include the usage of a number of alternative ways in which results can be presented graphically, in terms of the types of results and variables being depicted, as well as the embodiment of basic principles such as “never a center without a spread,” which captures the notion that any information presented about a distribution should be accompanied by information about the variability of such distribution (Fidler and Loftus 2009; Valentine et al. 2015) (which is notably absent from the plots and figures currently employed in research in the IS discipline).

Finally, and perhaps of greatest importance for the conceptual and theoretical development of a discipline, it can be argued that the research practice of assessing the outcome of a study by means of binary significant/nonsignificant decisions has been developed in parallel with the equally problematic practice of stating research hypotheses also in binary terms, for example, the presence or absence of an effect (the direction of causality between both practices, in historical terms, may be debatable; that the practice of NHST lead to hypotheses that reflect their testing may seem somewhat more likely). This, however, need not be the case. Edwards and Berry (2010, see Table 1 for a summary) recently sought to call attention to a number of practices designed to improve theoretical precision in social science research. Their main contention is that increased methodological rigor leads to hypotheses—in their simple, directional form—that are not likely to be seriously challenged, therefore putting theories at a lower risk of falsification, which is an undesirable side-effect. This argument also has implications for the practice of NHST (indeed, the authors note NHST as a barrier to increased precision in theoretical developments). In practice this means postulating more carefully crafted hypotheses, which not only take into account the presence or absence of an effect or relationship, or its simple directionality, but also the specific functional form relating the variables in question or the expected upper and lower boundaries of the effect (to name two examples). While this kind of refined hypotheses are also arguably more interesting from a theoretical standpoint, it also shifts the focus to a wider range of statistical tests and models in order to ascertain the support for their theory and away from the simple binary decisions that currently characterize the practice of NHST. Doing so would lead not only to more precise theories, the main point of Edwards and Berry, but arguably also to more interesting ones.

## Detailed Discussion of Results: Simple Model

There is an important anomaly in the results of the first variant (e.g., uncorrelated LVs) of the simple model, which influences all results plots for the model as all figures are calculated using all data and differ only in terms of how this data were divided between the panels. Understanding the causes of this anomaly helps understanding the differences among the techniques and presents a boundary conditions for applicability of the results. This anomaly occurs in the case of the weak path configuration, as shown in the third plot in Figure 3, and manifests as a marked downward angle in the lines corresponding to the upper boundaries of the BC and BCa CIs. The results show that in these conditions the proportion of CIs with upper boundaries that are smaller than the population values is much larger than expected. This effect is the result of a combination of a less well-known feature of PLS and the bias correction implemented in the BC and BCa approaches to CI construction. First, PLS weights have been shown to be susceptible to capitalization on chance, such that the regression paths are larger than would otherwise be the case (Goodhue et al. 2015; Rönkkö 2014; Rönkkö et al. 2015) and this effect carries over to PLSc as well (Rönkkö et al. 2016). While this effect is not yet fully understood, it seems to be related to sample size (negatively), number of indicators (positively), strength of factor loadings (negatively), and the strength of the paths between the latent variables in the population (negatively). When sample size is small and the effects between two latent variables are weak (as is the case here), the PLS estimates are clearly bimodal such that small negative (e.g., -0.1) or small positive (e.g. 0.1) values are much more likely than zero. This is consistent with the idea that PLS weights attempt to maximize the inner model R<sup>2</sup> (Hair et al. 2014), because paths with zero coefficients do not increase R<sup>2</sup> at all. In the weak path values scenario, the three paths that had an effect on the dependent latent variable were just 0.114, representing only about 1% of shared variance between the endogenous and each exogenous latent variable. Consequently, the estimates were strongly bimodal in small samples. This effect decreases with increasing sample size and strength of the effect between the latent variables so that the secondary mode on the negative side of the y axis first decreases up to a point where it appears just a long negative tail right before all estimates become positive (see Henseler et al. 2014, Figure 5)

When sample size increases, the accuracy of the bootstrap distribution as an estimate of the true distribution improves accordingly. In these scenarios, a small number of replications contained the original value in the negative tail and in this case the majority of the bootstrap replications were larger than the original replication. Because the bias correction factor is calculated based on the count of bootstrap replications that exceed the original replication, this results in very large negative bias correction values. The large bias correction values then result in rather extreme intervals. For example if the original estimate is in the negative tail (which is the more common case because the tail is heavier) it is possible to observe that 99% of bootstrap replications (i.e., 9,900 out of 10,000) are larger than the original estimate. In this scenario the bias correction coefficient receives the value of -2.33, leading to choosing the smallest and 35<sup>th</sup> smallest replication (out of 10,000) as the confidence limits of the resulting CI. In this extreme scenario even the original estimate, let alone the population value of the parameter, fall outside the limits of the CI, which creates a bias in the upper limit. We examined a small subset of conditions using sample sizes larger than 1,000 and concluded that the effect was present when the sampling distribution of the original estimates crossed zero, but disappeared when sample size grew large enough so that all estimates were always positive. This represents an important boundary condition for the application of CIs to results obtained from PLSc. Fortunately, identifying these occurrences is straightforward by inspecting the indices used for selecting the replications to be used as confidence limits. While these indices are not directly reported in any of the commercial PLS software, they can be calculated by comparing the reported values of bootstrap replications against the confidence limits counting how many replications fall below the upper and lower limits
