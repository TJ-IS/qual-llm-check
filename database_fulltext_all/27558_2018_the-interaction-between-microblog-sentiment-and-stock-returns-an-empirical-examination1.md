# THE INTERACTION BETWEEN MICROBLOG SENTIMENT ANDSTOCK RETURNS: AN EMPIRICAL EXAMINATION<sup>1</sup>

Shuyuan Deng Seidman College of Business, Grand Valley State University, Grand Rapids, MI 49504 U.S.A. {dengshuy@gvsu.edu}

Zhijian (James) Huang Saunders College of Business, Rochester Institute of Technology, Rochester, NY 14623 U.S.A. {zhuang@saunders.rit.edu}

Atish P. Sinha and Huimin Zhao Sheldon B. Lubar School of Business, University of Wisconsin–Milwaukee, Milwaukee, WI 53201 U.S.A. {sinha@uwm.edu} {hzhao@uwm.edu}

Opinion mining of microblog messages has become a popular application of business analytics in recent times. Opinions reflected in microblogs have provided businesses with great opportunities to acquire insights into their operating environments in real time. In particular, the relationship between microblog sentiment and stock returns is of great interest to investment professionals and academic researchers across multiple disciplines. We empirically test this complex relationship in a comprehensive study. We perform vector autoregression on a data set containing close to 18 million microblog messages spanning 4 years at the market and the individual stock levels, and at the daily and the hourly frequencies. The results show that the influence of microblog sentiment on stock returns is both statistically and economically significant at the hour level. Microblog sentiment is also largely driven by movements in the market. Moreover, stock returns have a stronger influence on negative sentiment than on positive sentiment. These findings have important implications for both research and practice.

Keywords: Social media, microblog, stock returns, vector autoregression, big data, sentiment analysis

## Introduction

Social media messages, as a major type of big data, are believed to be the largest data source for public opinion (Bifet and Frank 2010; O'Leary 2011; Tsytsarau and Palpanas 2012; Yu et al. 2013). Individuals are driven to express their emotions and opinions on social media (Aggarwal et al. 2012b).

The proliferation of mobile platforms in recent years has removed most limits on where and when social media users can post a message. The tremendous volume and velocity of social media messages have given organizations an unprecedented opportunity to monitor their operating environments in real time (Jacobs 2009). Moreover, it has become promising to exploit the “crowd wisdom” on social media for predicting social, political, and economic activities. For instance, Tumasjan et al. (2011) found that party mentions on Twitter predicted the German federal election results. Rui et al. (2013) showed that the sentiment of influential Twitter users significantly influences movie sales. Dewan and Ramaprasad (2014) revealed that blog buzz has negative effect on music sales.

In recent years, Information Systems (IS) researchers have studied social media for its predictive power in the stock market. The most commonly used data are microblog messages (e.g., Twitter messages), especially because most of those messages can be viewed without an established connection with the author. One particular type of information extracted from microblogs, sentiment, has received a lot of attention. Sentiment refers to the emotional state of users, such as happy, angry, or nervous. Most of these states can be classified into one of two categories: positive or negative. Sentiment analysis, also known as opinion mining, has been used to extract and assess sentiment from text data (Abbasi et al. 2011; Chen and Zimbra 2010). It has become a primary application of social media analytics. Institutional investors are now looking at sentiment reports of microblogs to support their investment decisions (Fan and Gordon 2014; Philips 2013; Rogow and Demos 2013; Rose 2013). Bollen et al. (2011) were among the first researchers to explore the predictive power of microblog sentiment on stock returns. In particular, their finding that Twitter sentiment predicts the Dow Jones Industrial Average (DJIA) returns at a directional accuracy of up to 86.7% has inspired a number of institutional traders and analytics professionals. Other related studies have also emerged in this high-impact area. Some of them have developed predictive models to forecast stock returns using microblog sentiment. Others have used econometric models to test if the predictive power of microblog sentiment exists in the stock market.

Despite recent attempts to predict stock returns using microblog sentiment, discussions on this topic are fragmented and the results are inconsistent. Different data sets spanning different periods have been used. Some of the studies achieved high prediction accuracy (Bollen et al. 2011; Oh and Sheng 2011), while others found the predictive power of microblog sentiment to be insignificant (Oliveira et al. 2013; Yu et al. 2013). Moreover, there has been a lack of consensus on how social media sentiment and stock returns are associated with each other. For instance, Bollen et al. (2011) acknowledged that the mechanisms that connect Twitter sentiment and DJIA return are still not clear. Nann et al. (2013) cautiously pointed out that the causality between microblog sentiment and stock returns is still uncertain, even after they obtained satisfactory results from a predictive model. To advance our knowledge of the important, yet complex, relationship between microblog sentiment and stock returns, we empirically study this relationship using a large and comprehensive data set spanning multiple years.

Despite the previous efforts on testing the predictive power of microblog sentiment, an important issue that has been overlooked is the endogeneity of social media sentiment. It is likely that stock returns also affects social media sentiment. There has been considerable work in finance studying how investors’ subjective expectations about future stock returns are influenced by past returns (e.g., De Long et al. 1990b; Grossman 1976; Grossman and Stiglitz 1976). Thus, the sentiment associated with such expectation is also likely to be a result of stock returns. In other words, a causality loop (i.e., bidirectional causality) could exist between microblog sentiment and stock returns. Omitting this important effect undermines the reliability of the empirical findings in prior research. In this study, we rigorously examine the bidirectional relationship between microblog sentiment and stock returns, treating microblog sentiment and stock returns as jointly endogenous.

Regarding the effect of microblog sentiment on returns, most previous studies did not address the difference between positive sentiment and negative sentiment. The different roles of positive emotions and negative emotions in influencing human behavior have been comprehensively discussed in the IS literature (Zhang 2013) and supported by empirical findings (Luo et al. 2013). A further understanding of the different mechanisms of the positive sentiment and the negative sentiment on microblogs is crucial in explaining the interaction between microblog sentiment and stock returns. In this study, we empirically examine the differential effects of positive sentiment and negative sentiment, captured in microblog messages, on stock returns.

Prior studies investigating the relationship between microblog sentiment and stock returns have exclusively focused on the day level (i.e., how daily aggregate sentiment influences daily returns). As microblogs are updated at a high velocity within a day, analysis at a finer granularity of observation frequency could be critical in helping reveal the instantaneous effect of microblog sentiment on the stock market. This is apparently of great value to intraday and high-frequency traders, and could have a huge impact on the amount of dollars traded on a daily basis. In this study, we examine the relationship between microblog sentiment and stock returns, at both the day level and the hour level, to identify how the relationship varies by frequency. Specifically, we pose the following research questions:

(1) How does microblog sentiment interact with stock returns?

(2) Do positive sentiment and negative sentiment influence and react to stock returns in the same way?

(3) Does the relationship between microblog sentiment and stock returns differ at the day and hour levels?

To address these questions, we tested the interaction between microblog sentiment and stock returns using a data set containing 17,835,174 StockTwits messages spanning four years. StockTwits is similar to Twitter but it exclusively focuses on investors. Each StockTwits message that mentions a stock also contains a symbol entity, known as Cashtag, which allows the message to be accurately mapped to the mentioned stock. This nature of StockTwits message makes it more suitable than Twitter data for our study, rendering our results more reliable than many related studies in terms of data quality. We performed vector autoregression (VAR) to analyze the data. VAR allows us to treat the time series of microblog sentiment and stock returns as jointly endogenous.

Through a series of rigorously designed analyses, we found that (1) microblog sentiment, especially negative sentiment, has an influence on stock returns at the hourly frequency but not at the daily frequency; (2) stock returns significantly impact microblog sentiment, and the effect is larger on negative sentiment than on positive sentiment; and (3) the interaction between microblog sentiment and stock returns is stronger and longer at the market level than at the individual stock level.

Our research makes several important contributions to the literature on microblog sentiment and stock returns. First, ours is among the first few studies to examine the relationship between microblog sentiment and stock returns at different levels (viz., market and stock levels, daily and hourly levels). The findings from our study provide a comprehensive understanding of the interaction between microblog sentiment and stock returns, and advance the knowledge not only for IS and finance researchers, but for investment and social media analytics professionals as well. Ours is also the first study to reveal the influence of microblog sentiment on stock returns at the hourly frequency. The effect is both statistically and economically significant. This finding not only adds to our understanding of the economic impact of IS, but is also particularly relevant to intraday and high-frequency traders.

Second, we address the endogeneity issue of microblog sentiment. The finding on how microblog sentiment reacts to the stock market extends our knowledge on microblog users from the sociopsychological perspective. It also adds to our understanding on the behavioral bias of investors.

Third, our study has found that positive sentiment and negative sentiment on StockTwits influence and react to stock returns differently. In particular, the interaction between negative sentiment and stock returns is stronger. Although the extant literature has shown that positive sentiment and negative sentiment work differently, no study has so far reported, based on empirical analysis, the patterns of how negative sentiment and positive sentiment on microblogs differ in interacting with stock returns. This finding has important implications for multiple disciplines.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we review related prior work on microblog sentiment and stock returns. Next, we present our research objectives and discuss in detail the motivation for our empirical analysis. We then develop our empirical methodology, describing the data set, the variables, and the empirical models. Next, we report the results and supplement them with robustness checks, followed by a discussion of the main findings and their implications for research and practice. Finally, we conclude the paper with a discussion of the limitations of our study and outline a set of future research directions.

## Related Prior Work

With the growing popularity of the Internet, the finance literature has witnessed a lot of interest in the online behavior of investors. For instance, Antweiler and Frank (2004) found that the recommendations (buy/sell) on stock message boards are associated with stock returns contemporaneously. But they did not find the predictive relationship to be statistically significant. Although both stock message boards and microblogs contain user opinion and sentiment about the stock market, microblogs generate a much larger number of messages per day. Such large volumes of data, generated at such high velocities, capture more effectively the “wisdom of the crowd” for performing real-time predictive analytics.

Bollen et al. (2011) conducted one of the earliest studies exploring the predictive power of microblog sentiment on stock returns. They used OpinionFinder (Wilson et al. 2005) to classify Twitter messages as either positive or negative. The messages were then aggregated at the daily frequency level and were used to correlate with daily DJIA returns. Based on a Granger causality test on a sample of 342,255 Twitter messages, they did not find any significant relationship between the polarized (i.e., positive and negative) sentiment and DJIA returns. In another experiment in this study, Bollen et al. classified Twitter message sentiment into six emotion dimensions: calm, alert, sure, vital, kind, and happy. They found that the third lag of the calm dimension significantly predicts DJIA returns. However, they did not provide an explanation as to why this relationship exists. When cross validating the prediction model they created, they achieved an accuracy of 86.7% in forecasting the daily price movement direction of the DJIA. This accuracy is quite high, considering that finance researchers have been constantly debating if stock returns are predictable at all (Ang and Bekaert 2007; Campbell and Thompson 2008). Despite the innovativeness of the idea and the exciting results, there is a lot of room to explore how microblog sentiment and stock returns are related.

Oh and Sheng (2011) proposed a predictive model to forecast individual stock returns using sentiment extracted from StockTwits. They achieved over 80% in F-measure (the harmonic mean of precision and recall) when predicting the direction of stock price change. However, their results suffer from small sample bias. The data set they used contains 72,221 messages for 1,909 assets over a 3-month period. This indicates that there were a very limited number of messages for each stock per day. Since the aggregate opinion on social media represents the wisdom of the crowd, a larger sample is needed to show the robustness of the predictive power.

Nann et al. (2013) developed a predictive model by aggregating sentiment from multiple social media sources (including Twitter) and achieved over 60% directional accuracy in forecasting the returns of the Standard and Poor 500 index. They also realized positive profit in a simulated trading experiment. Despite the seemingly promising results, they acknowledged that they were uncertain about the causality between social media sentiment and stock returns, and their results also suffered from small sample bias.

Inconsistent findings have also been reported in more recent studies. For instance, using econometric models on a panel data set over a 3-month period, Yu et al. (2013) found that neither positive nor negative sentiment on Twitter significantly predicts the return of individual stocks. However, they reported that the effect of forum sentiment is significant. This finding is contradictory to Das and Chen (2007), who did not find the relationship between forum sentiment and stock returns to be significant. Oliveira et al. (2013) developed a predictive model using StockTwits sentiment and compared it with traditional equity models that use fundamental and technical indicators in forecasting individual stock returns. They found no evidence to support the predictive power of StockTwits sentiment. Sprenger et al. (2014) found that sentiment in Twitter messages has a contemporaneous association, but not a predictive relationship, with individual stock returns.

Tirunillai and Tellis (2012) studied the influence of online reviews on stock returns. They found that both positive and negative reviews influence subsequent daily returns, with the effect of negative reviews being stronger. However, microblog messages differ from online reviews significantly in terms of content and message volume. Luo et al. (2013) empirically tested the relationship between blog sentiment and individual stock returns. They found the influence of blog sentiment on stock returns to be statistically significant. They also provided theoretical support for this result. However, the blogs used in their study were retrieved from such websites as

Engadget and Mashable, whose authors are usually professional writers, rather than average social media users. While these blogs are updated by a small group of professional writers a couple of times per week, microblogs are constantly refreshed by a much larger demographic population of users in real time. Hence, these blogs share more similarity with traditional news media than with the crowd wisdom on microblogs. Their finding is consistent with the finance literature on the relationship between news sentiment/information and stock returns (Tetlock 2007; Tetlock et al. 2008). However, the relationship between microblog sentiment and stock returns still remains to be explored.

In this section, we reviewed studies that employ predictive models (e.g., Nann et al. 2013; Oh and Sheng 2011), as well as those that employ explanatory models (e.g., Luo et al. 2013; Sprenger et al. 2014). Predictive models in general provide stronger practical implications, whereas explanatory models are suitable for testing the statistical significance of effects (Shmueli and Koppius 2011).

## Motivation and Research Objectives

Our review of related prior work reveals several research gaps in the extant literature:

(1) Due to the inconsistent findings and the lack of explanation in prior work, our understanding of the relationship between microblog sentiment and stock returns is quite limited. Findings from past studies favoring the use of social media sentiment to predict stock returns were often based on small test samples and, therefore, lacked reliability. For example, the impressive prediction accuracy (86.7% in predicting the daily price movement direction of the DJIA) reported by Bollen et al. (2011) was based on a single test with only 15 trading days.

(2) Existing studies treated microblog sentiment as exogenous when using it to predict stock returns. This assumption is questionable since microblog users post not only their opinions about the future, but also their response to past social and economic activities, such as historical stock returns. Thus, the endogeneity of microblog sentiment needs to be investigated.

(3) Most related predictive models and empirical studies have treated positive sentiment and negative sentiment equally. Oh and Sheng (2011) found that the predictive model using negative sentiment outperformed that using positive sentiment. The difference between positive sentiment and negative sentiment in terms of how they interact with stock returns needs to be further investigated.

(4) Most importantly, all prior studies have been done at the day level. Analysis at a finer granularity of time—for example, at the hour level—is needed to reveal if a smaller time window for examination of high-velocity big data is more appropriate for revealing the relationship between microblog sentiment and stock returns.

Our goal is to address these gaps through a comprehensive empirical study. We use econometric models to investigate the bidirectional causality between microblog sentiment and stock returns, the difference between positive sentiment and negative sentiment in this relationship, and the difference at the day level and the hour level in this relationship.

## Influence of Microblog Sentiment on Stock Returns

It is widely believed that stock prices react to new information (Fama 1970; Luo et al. 2013). Online word-of-mouth (WOM), reflected in blogs and customer reviews, provides useful cues to a firm’s future performance (Luo and Zhang 2013). A few studies have found that the online WOM of a product influences its sales positively (Dewan and Ramaprasad 2014; Rui et al. 2013). Therefore, sentiment expressed on social media could also be a reflection of consumer satisfaction and create a positive image for investors (Luo et al. 2013). Given that customer satisfaction has been found to increase firm value (Anderson et al. 2004), it seems reasonable to expect that social media sentiment predicts firm value in the form of stock returns. However, such reasoning is not quite enough to explain how such WOM effect can explain the daily (or hourly) variation of stock prices. A public firm’s product and service quality is a relatively stable characteristic of the firm and is not expected to vary on a daily (hourly) basis. So is the perception of this characteristic by consumers. For a fixed group of consumers, their opinion toward a company is not likely to vary from day to day.

From another perspective, there are heterogeneous user groups on social media. It could be too strong an assumption that the sentiments of different groups are associated with stock returns in the same manner. While many social media users are consumers, there are also many investors and other types of stock market participants. We argue that it is important to distinguish between consumer sentiment and sentiment of market participants. As discussed in related work, consumer sentiment mainly reflects the satisfaction of consumers with products and services. The sentiment of market participants depicts their direct emotional response to market events and their forward-looking opinion (Mayew and Venkatachalam 2012; Tetlock 2007).

It is also important to further distinguish between the different types of market participants. Investors are generally divided into noise traders, who hold random beliefs about future returns, and rational arbitrageurs, who hold Bayesian beliefs (De Long et al. 1990a; Park et al. 2013; Tetlock 2007). Noise traders do not make investment decisions by rationally evaluating firm fundamentals. Instead, they constantly seek information from unofficial channels and share that information. They hold beliefs on future returns and risks that are not based on facts, and irrationally act on noise (Baker and Wurgler 2006; De Long et al. 1990a). Oh and Sheng (2011) believe that noise traders are prevalent on investor-focused social media sites. Through a field experiment, Park et al. (2013) found that the users in an online stock message board do not follow the Bayesian decision process and exhibit confirmation bias. This suggests that noise traders are common in online investor communities.

Noise traders cause overreaction in stock price movements, which can explain short-run return anomalies (Hirshleifer 2001). After receiving new information, noise traders do not update their beliefs about future returns in a Bayesian fashion. Instead, they assign more weight to new information but less weight to prior information. As a consequence, they temporarily move stock prices away from the fundamental values (De Bondt and Thaler 1985; Lo and MacKinlay 1990).

Social media sentiment may also incorporate some effects of traditional news media. News agencies may post news updates using their social media accounts. Thus, a certain proportion of microblog messages could be considered to be news items. Such messages usually contain the title of a news article and a link to it. Any examination of the relationship between microblog sentiment and stock returns should therefore try to effectively control for the effect of news sentiment on returns.

Tetlock (2007) hypothesized that sentiment reflected in news can be considered as a proxy for the fundamental values of a company’s prospect that have not been incorporated into prices. If this were true, news sentiment would impact stock returns and the impact would persist. However, Tetlock found that news sentiment has a short-run effect on stock returns, but this effect gets reversed within a few days. This finding is consistent with the short-run overreaction caused by noise traders. On microblog platforms, where noise traders are prevalent, the sentiment reflected in stock-related messages is likely to have an effect on stock returns through noise traders. This effect is also expected to reverse because of the overreaction bias. To verify this belief and reveal the dynamics, empirical tests need to be conducted.

## Influence of Stock Returns on Microblog Sentiment

The appraisal theory of emotion posits that emotions arise from evaluations (appraisals) of events and situations (Roseman and Smith 2001). Lazarus (1991, p. 827) suggests that

if an appraisal is made that harm or threat has occurred, then the emotion will be negative; if the appraisal is that benefit has occurred, then the emotion will be positive.

Drawing on the appraisal theory, Mayew and Venkatachalam (2012) argued that affective states expressed in communication are based on individuals’ cognitive evaluation of stimulus. Although human emotions can exist without external stimuli, most emotions can be attributed to certain triggering events (Andersen and Guerrero 1998). Such affective states also allow us to infer what the stimuli are. Prior studies in sentiment analysis and opinion mining suggested that social media messages are rich in emotions (e.g., Chen and Zimbra 2010; Liu 2012). In our research context, the fluctuations of the stock market can be considered as stimuli, while microblog sentiment can be considered as the affective states expressed in communication.

The extant literature in finance shows that trader emotion is heavily affected by stock market performance. Lo et al. (2005) found that traders have strong emotional response to stock returns and other market events. Human emotional response has at least two components: arousal and valence (Deng and Poole 2010). The former refers to the nonspecific and nondirectional component, while the latter refers to the directional component, which ranges from positive to negative. Valence is a result of the cognitive appraisal to an external stimulus. Investors and investing professionals, whose economic prospects are closely associated with the financial market, are likely to be consistently influenced by stock returns. By studying consumer sentiment survey and stock prices, Otoo (1999) found that an increase in stock price signals a good economic trend, which in turn boosts public sentiment. Emotions are not just feelings. They also create an impulse to act (Frijda 1986; Gross and Thompson 2007). One action commonly associated with emotion is to post messages on social media (Aggarwal et al. 2012a).

From another perspective, the positive feedback trading theory posits that noise investors’ beliefs about future stock returns are heavily influenced by the returns in the previous period (De Long et al. 1990b; Nofsinger and Sias 1999). They follow the “trend” of the market. Specifically, feedback traders believe that stock prices will continue to increase (or decrease) within a certain period after a prior increase (or decrease) (Hu et al. 2015). Herding is also likely to happen after increased return and is observed for both institutional and individual investors (Nofsinger and Sias 1999).

In the classic information economics setting of Grossman (1976), investors form their beliefs about future stock returns based on their private information and the public information reflected in past stock returns. Herding will be high if (1) investors see a strong public signal or (2) investors private signals are highly correlated (Graham 1999). With the use of social media, past stock returns will not only shape the public signal but also affect the process of producing private information, thus affecting the herding behavior of investors. Moreover, prior IS research found that communicating opinions accompanies herding behavior (Duan et al. 2009). In the context of microblogs, such communication is likely to be reflected as posting similar opinions toward the stock market by individual investors. Thus, increased return will push the collective sentiment of market participants in one direction. Such investor behavior is also referred to as extrapolative expectation (De Long et al. 1990b). As a result, the latest stock market movement constantly updates investors forward-looking opinions. We therefore want to examine the impact of stock returns on microblog sentiment.

## Positive Sentiment Versus Negative Sentiment

Previous research on the social psychology of microblog users found that users respond to positive and negative stimuli differently (Stieglitz and Dang-Xuan 2013). In particular, the emotional response to negative stimuli is stronger than that to positive stimuli. This “negativity bias” has been observed in a variety of domains. For example, negative online WOM receives more attention than positive WOM (Luo 2007); negative blogs attract more readers (Aggarwal et al. 2012b); and negative sentiment (but not positive sentiment) expressed during the earnings conference call significantly predicts the abnormal returns for the subsequent 180 trading days (Mayew and Venkatachalam 2012).

Positive forward-looking opinion about the stock market implies potential increase in stock returns, which is a positive outcome. Similarly, negative opinion implies potential loss. If investors are rational, they should act symmetrically after receiving positive information and negative information. Based on prospect theory (Kahneman and Tversky 1979), however, individuals assign more weight to potential loss when making decisions, leading to the loss aversion bias. The negative feelings associated with loss outweigh the positive feelings associated with the same amount of gain. Such individual behavioral bias was also supported by neuroimaging research (Tom et al. 2007). Prior research in behavioral finance characterized noise traders as risk-averse and capital constrained (De Long et al. 1990a). Thus, negative sentiment on microblogs may have a larger impact on stock returns than positive sentiment.

When negative forward-looking opinion is perceived, noise traders holding the underlying stocks have more impulse to cut potential loss and change positions to minimize further loss and liquidity risk. This is likely to drive prices down and lead to more reduced returns. When positive forward-looking opinion is observed, noise traders do not always have the resources to take a long position on more stocks because of constrained capital. Hence, the influence of negative sentiment on stock returns may be larger than that of positive sentiment. In fact, Oh and Sheng (2011) found that the predictive model using negative sentiment on microblog outperformed the model using positive sentiment in predicting individual stock returns.

In the same vein, research in behavioral finance has found that investors react to good news and bad news differently, especially for popular stocks (Barberis et al. 1998; Conrad et al. 2002). In particular, the stock market responds to bad news in a much stronger fashion than to good news (Conrad et al. 2002). Specifically, due to extrapolative expectation (De Long et al. 1990b), investors, in general, infer a stock’s future based on its past performance. Popular stocks typically have superior historical performance. This forms investors prior beliefs about their future returns. As a result, when good returns of popular stocks are realized, investors take it for granted and show little reaction. However, if a popular stock realizes negative return, strongly contradicting prior beliefs, investors exhibit greater reaction.

Investors’ asymmetric reaction has been supported in a number of empirical studies. For instance, Tetlock (2007) observed that negative stock returns lead to more pessimistic sentiment in The Wall Street Journal the next day than what increased returns do to positive sentiment. Garcia (2013) found that investors are more sensitive to economic downturns. In a controlled experiment, Ko and Huang (2012) also observed that investors do not pay attention to news that conforms to their prior beliefs. On social media platforms, discussions on popular stocks are dominant. When investors on social media observe negative stock returns, they are likely to exhibit much stronger (negative) sentiment than the (positive) sentiment they experience after observing positive returns. Such investor behavior is also explained by the cognitive dissonance theory (Festinger 1962), which states that discomfort and anxiety (i.e., negative feelings) arise when an individual holds contradictory beliefs (e.g., the superior return of a stock in the past and the recent negative return).

Hence, microblog sentiment is likely to respond to stock returns and the response is likely to be larger for negative sentiment than for positive sentiment.

## Day Versus Hour

Prior research has found significant correlations between news sentiment and stock returns at the day level (Lillo et al. 2015). Based on that, the extant literature studying microblog sentiment and stock returns focused on the relationship at the day level. However, microblogs are different from traditional media such as news, because microblogs are updated at a much faster pace. That is, they have to handle data not only with very high volume but also with very high velocity. Analysis conducted at the day level, in which stock returns are calculated using the daily closing prices, would not be able to capture the instantaneous effect of microblog messages on the stock market. Nonetheless, trading at the intraday frequency is of wide interest among hedge funds, which have also been known to be exploring opportunities for incorporating social media sentiment into their trading models. Given that few related theories have discussed the difference of social psychological behavior at the daily frequency and the hourly frequency, revealing such difference of the relationships between microblog sentiment and stock returns calls for a serious empirical investigation across those time frames.

Another motivation behind looking into the hour level is the rapid growth of high-frequency trading (HFT) in recent years. With HFT, numerous automated trading systems respond to stock market fluctuations immediately, which in turn further moves stock prices (Brogaard et al. 2014). As a result, stock prices could experience dramatic intraday fluctuations, even without much news. An epic example is the May 6, 2010, flash crash (Easley et al. 2011), when the DJIA index plummeted more than 5% within a few minutes without any news, then quickly recovered. It is therefore important to look into the hourly frequency to capture the relationship between investor sentiment and intraday stock returns, especially when there are no major news events.

## Empirical Methodology

We used a multiple time series technique, vector autoregression (VAR) (Lütkepohl 2007), to model the dynamic interactions between stock returns and microblog sentiment, as well as the difference between positive sentiment and negative sentiment and the difference between daily frequency and hourly frequency in this context, at both the market (stock index) level and the individual stock level. Compared to alternative econometric models, VAR has the advantage of being able to address biases, such as endogeneity, autocorrelations, and causality loops (Luo et al. 2013). It has been used in prior studies to examine the potential bidirectional relationship between stock returns and other variables, such as news sentiment (Tetlock 2007) and search attention (Da et al. 2011).

Figure 1. Framework for Empirical Analysis

The framework highlighting the VAR models used in this study is illustrated in Figure 1. Both stock returns and microblog sentiment are treated as endogenous (i.e., each variable is predicted by lagged values of itself and the other variables). By doing so, the model is able to capture potential feedback loops among the endogenous variables (Dewan and Ramaprasad 2014; Love and Zicchino 2006). For instance, return in the current period may influence sentiment in the next period, which, in turn, may cause change in the return after the next period. For the index-level models, we used VAR with exogenous variables (VARX). The exogenous variables include weekday and hour (for the hour-level model only). For the stock-level models, we used panel VAR with exogenous variables (PVARX).

VAR can be considered as a generalization of Granger causality test for multiple time series (Lütkepohl 2007). Once the VAR models were estimated, we examined the Granger causality test results for the potential bidirectional relationship between stock returns and microblog sentiment. The Granger causality test we used first estimates an equation including all of the relevant independent variables. Next, it estimates a nested equation by removing (all lags of) an independent variable of interest. If the Chi-squared value of the nested equation is significantly worse based on the Wald test, we can conclude that the removed variable significantly Granger causes the dependent variable in this equation.

We also plotted orthogonalized cumulative impulse response functions (IRF, hereafter) to analyze how each endogenous variable responds to one standard deviation of unexpected shock (one shock in short, hereafter) from another endogenous variable, holding other effects constant (Love and Zicchino 2006). On one hand, this shows the effect size of each coefficient. On the other hand, this allows us to understand the dynamics between each pair of variables (Dewan and Ramaprasad 2014; Luo et al. 2013). We also examined the forecast error variance decomposition (FEVD) of the effects modeled in VAR. FEVD attributes proportions of the forecast errors on a dependent variable to the predictors. It allows us to understand the relative importance of each predictor in forecasting the dependent variable.

## Data

We used a data set containing all messages on StockTwits. com between September 2010 and September 2014. StockTwits is a microblog website for investors and investment professionals to share information and ideas about financial markets (StockTwits 2014). Similar to Twitter messages (prior to November 2017), each posting on StockTwits is limited to 140 characters. The data set contains 17,835,174 messages. Although Twitter generates more data than StockTwits, data from StockTwits are more suitable for this study. Historical Twitter data spanning multiple years are not available for academic research. The StockTwits sample used in our study is much larger than the sample employed by any of the related studies using Twitter data. Compared to Twitter data, messages on StockTwits are closely related to the stock market and contain much less noise. The Cashtag used in StockTwits also allows accurate mapping of a message to the stock it mentions. This data set is also much larger than those used in prior related studies. For the news data, we downloaded all Reuters news articles for the same period from the news archives of reuters.com.

## Key Variables

We measured investor sentiment at both the day level and the hour level. We first used SentiStrength (Thelwall et al. 2010) to classify the sentiment of each message as positive, negative, or neutral. While some previous studies manually classified sentiment (Aggarwal et al. 2012b), it is more practical to use a computational method for this task given the volume of the data (Luo et al. 2013). Although prior studies in finance used Harvard General Inquirer (GI) word lists (Stone et al. 1966) to classify the tone in financial reports and news (Garcia 2013; Tetlock 2007), this simple method would not be able to classify StockTwits messages accurately since the language styles used in microblogs and in formal documents are vastly different (Deng et al. 2013). A recent study benchmarked 20 popular Twitter sentiment analysis tools and showed that SentiStrength is among the best in terms of classification accuracy (Abbasi et al. 2014). Given the similarity between StockTwits and Twitter, SentiStrength is wellsuited for the sentiment classification task in this study.

We further validated the accuracy of SentiStrength using a subsample of 9,997 messages from our data. Similar to Aggarwal et al. (2012b), we asked two graduate students to label each of these messages as positive, negative, or neutral. The two students agreed on 89% of the messages. The Cohen’s Kappa is 0.82, indicating almost perfect agreement (Landis and Koch 1977). Following Aggarwal et al. (2012b), when the two raters disagreed on the sentiment of a message, a third person’s judgement was used. We used SentiStrength to classify the sentiment of these messages. The accuracy achieved is 70.15%, which is comparable to related studies. For instance, Das and Chen (2007) developed a variety of classifiers to classify the sentiment of forum messages. The classifier they eventually used achieved up to 66.79% accuracy on a sample of 913 messages. They believed that an accuracy between 60% and 70% is acceptable for sentiment classification. Aggarwal et al. (2012a) employed a computational method to classify the sentiment of blog posts, which were subsequently used in an econometric model. The method achieved an accuracy of 72.86%. We thus believe that the sentiment classification results using SentiStrength are acceptable.

We aggregated the number of positive and negative messages by day and by hour. The overall positive sentiment within a specific day (hour) was measured as the ratio of the number of positive messages to the total number of messages on that day (hour). Using ratio instead of count allows us to minimize heteroskedasticity across time, as we know that the number of StockTwits messages has increased explosively in recent years. The overall negative sentiment at the day or hour level was calculated in a similar fashion. If there was no positive (or negative) message during a day (or hour), the value of positive sentiment (or negative sentiment) was set to zero. This is consistent with Antweiler and Frank (2004).

We calculated the index-level return using the hourly and daily closing prices of DJIA. All time stamps of the StockTwits messages were converted to U.S. Eastern Time to match the time stamps of the historical prices. For the daylevel models, we combined the microblog sentiment (and the news sentiment) during the weekends with those on Mondays, since no stock prices are available during weekends. We used weekday as an exogenous variable to minimize any bias resulting from this procedure. For holidays when the stock exchanges were closed, we imputed the missing prices using the average of the prices for the previous seven days. Since holidays represent a very small portion of observations in our data set, this should not have significant impact on our results.

For the hour-level models, we also followed the same procedure to process the microblog sentiment and the news sentiment during weekends and non-trading hours. For hourly return, we calculated seven returns for each trading day: at 10 a.m., 11 a.m., 12 p.m., 1 p.m., 2 p.m., 3 p.m., and 4 p.m. The return at 10 a.m. was calculated using the price at 10 a.m. and the price at 4 p.m. on the previous day. Although the return at 10 a.m. was calculated differently than those for other hours, we used hour as an exogenous variable to capture any effects that were not accounted for by the main effects and other control variables.

## Control Variables

News sentiment has been shown to influence stock returns (Garcia 2013; Tetlock 2007). Since information from news is universally employed for investment decisions, it is also likely to influence the sentiment of investors (Shiller 2000). On one hand, investors are likely to post messages about news on microblogs. On the other hand, news agencies also post news using their own microblog accounts. To more effectively reveal the relationship between microblog sentiment and stock returns, it is crucial to control for the effect of news sentiment in our models. This also allows us to see if microblog sentiment has predictive power, in addition to news sentiment.

We downloaded all archived news articles from Reuters.com for the same period spanning the StockTwits data. The total number of news articles is 3,257,797. This is on average over

3,132 articles per day, or 131 articles per hour. With such a high update frequency, Reuters news serves as an effective control for emerging news information. Following Tetlock (2007), we used the positive and negative sentiment words in the Harvard GI word lists (Stone et al. 1966) to measure the sentiment in the news. We used the sum of word counts across all articles in each emotional category, instead of first classifying each article’s polarity and then counting the number of positive and negative articles.

A single news article typically contains many more words than a single microblogging message. If a news article contains 20 positive words and 21 negative words, for instance, considering the overall sentiment of the article to be negative is overly simplistic and is likely to lose important information. Both the positive and negative word counts were aggregated at the day (hour) level and then normalized by the number of articles. We examined the descriptive statistics of the word counts and found that the positive and negative word counts are highly correlated. To avoid multicollinearity, we created a variable, news sentiment, using the positive word count minus the negative word count. This calculation is similar to that used by Garcia (2013). We used this variable in all of our models. Besides sentiment in news, we also controlled for the number of news articles since it may also impact stock returns (Fang and Peress 2009).

Antweiler and Frank (2004) observed that volatile periods are associated with more bullish forum messages. Thus, we controlled for volatility in our models. Following Antweiler and Frank, we first calculated realized volatility (i.e., the actual variation of returns within a period). Our approach for calculating day-level volatility is similar to Andersen et al. (2001). We first estimated a moving average model, MA(1), for the return of each stock using the full sample. We then removed the MA(1) component for each stock to obtain a filtered series, denoted by $r _ { i , j , t } ,$ for stock i at the $j ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ trading hour on day t. Let n denote the number of observed returns in a given day. The volatility is calculated as $\nu _ { i , t } = \ln \left( \sqrt { \frac { 1 } { n } \sum _ { j = 1 } ^ { n } r _ { i , j , t } ^ { 2 } } \right)$ . Similar to Antweiler and Frank, hourly volatility is calculated as the standard deviation of minute-level ticks.

We also controlled for market volatility. At the index level, we used the dates of the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) meetings. At the stock level, we used the postearnings announcement dates (PEAD). To control for implied volatility, we also used the Chicago Board Options Exchange Volatility Index (symbol: VIX) for all the models. Antweiler and Frank proposed an agreement index based on the buy and sell recommendations on stock message boards. We controlled for agreement index in our model. Specifically, let $M _ { t } ^ { B U Y }$ and $\boldsymbol { M } _ { t } ^ { S E L L }$ denote the number of bullish and bearish messages at time t, respectively. Let $\begin{array} { r } { B _ { t } = \frac { M _ { t } ^ { B U Y } - M _ { t } ^ { S E L L } } { M _ { t } ^ { B U Y } + M _ { t } ^ { S E L L } } } \end{array}$ Agreement index is calculated as $A _ { _ t } = 1 - \sqrt { 1 - B _ { _ t } ^ { 2 } }$

Antweiler and Frank also found that the volume of forum postings influences return. Thus, we controlled for message volume on StockTwits. We also controlled for trading volume in all of the models. We treated all of the control variables discussed in this section, except FOMC and PEAD, as endogenous in our models since these variables are likely to be affected by stock returns and/or investor sentiment. The number of lags for all of the control variables was the same as the optimal lag order of each model.

## Results

The summary statistics and correlations among the key variables are reported in Appendix A. For the index models, all 17.8 million messages and 3.2 million news articles were used. For the 44-stock panel models, 3,121,767 messages and 62,495 news articles related to the stocks were used. Note that the message volume for individual stocks is much larger than that in any of the related prior studies.

The four VAR models were estimated using Stata and R. The panel VAR program for Stata is described in Abrigo and Love (2015). For the index level models, we used the augmented Dickey-Fuller unit-root test (Said and Dickey 1984) to assess the stationarity of return, positive sentiment, and negative sentiment. For the panel level models, we performed the Harris-Tzavalis unit root test for panel data (Harris and Tzavalis 1999) on the three key variables. All of the p values were less than 0.001, indicating the stability of the estimations. The significance of the coefficient estimates is reported based on heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors. The results for the effect of sentiment on return are shown in Table 1. The results for the effect of return on sentiment are shown in Table 2. The coefficient estimates of these models are reported in Appendix B. Next, we report the results for each of the four models individually.

## Results of Model 1 (Index-Day)

The optimal lag for Model 1 based on BIC was five (days). For the equation using return (Return) as the dependent variable (i.e., the return equation), none of the lags of positive sentiment (Positive) and negative sentiment (Negative) are statistically significant at the 5% level (Table 1). Granger causality tests also show that neither positive sentiment nor negative sentiment Granger causes return. Figure 4 shows the IRF plots of the responses of return to negative sentiment (left) and positive sentiment (right). The curves show the accumulated orthogonalized responses of return by the number of days. The grey bands are the 95% confidence intervals. The intervals were obtained from 500 runs of Monte Carlo simulation. The plots (in Figure 4) show that return does not significantly respond to shocks of positive sentiment and negative sentiment.

For the positive sentiment equation (Table 2), the first lag of return is positive and statistically significant at the 5% level. For the negative sentiment equation (Table 2), the first and fifth lags of return are statistically significant. The signs of the two lags are different, suggesting an overreaction pattern. In other words, an increase in return is associated with a decrease in negative sentiment on the next day but the effect reverses five days later. Based on the results from Granger causality test, we find that return significantly Granger causes negative sentiment $( \chi ^ { 2 } = 4 7 . 2 1 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , but does not significantly Granger cause positive sentiment.

In Figure 5, the left IRF plot shows that one positive shock to DJIA’s daily return is followed by approximately -0.9% change in the negative sentiment on StockTwits on the next day. But one such shock is only expected to increase the positive sentiment by less than 0.2% on the next day (see the right plot). This shows that negative sentiment on microblog has a much larger reaction to index return than positive sentiment. This effect continues to exist but decreases quickly in the following days. We further examined the FEVD of negative sentiment. Apparently, autocorrelation plays a large role in forecasting all of the three variables. Compared to selflags, return accounts for up to 26.8% (on Day 5) of the forecast error of negative sentiment, indicating that the negative sentiment on StockTwits is largely influenced by the daily DJIA returns in the past five days.

## Results of Model 2 (Index-Hour)

For Model 2, the optimal lag selected was seven (hours). This corresponds to a whole trading day. For the return equation (Table 1), the first and sixth lags of negative sentiment are statistically significant, but with different signs. This also suggests an overreaction pattern. The Granger causality test results $( \chi ^ { 2 } = 1 0 7 . 5 5 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ) also show that only negative sentiment has a significant influence on return at the hourly frequency. This provides evidence for the instantaneous predictive power of the sentiment on StockTwits in predicting DJIA return.

Figure 6 (left) provides additional evidence of the instantaneous predictive power of negative sentiment on return. It shows that one (positive) shock to negative sentiment decreases the index return by about four basis points in the next one hour. This effect is comparable to the effect of news sentiment at the daily frequency reported by Tetlock (2007), and is both statistically and economically significant. Specifically, the average hourly return of DJIA is about one basis point, which means that there would be a negative offset to return by a shock to negative sentiment. However, this effect quickly wears out as the response does not continue to increase after the first hour of shock. After the fifth hour, the effect is partially reversed, which is consistent with Tetlock’s finding on the overreaction behavior of noise traders. Figure 6 also shows that a shock to positive sentiment slightly increases return (right). However, this effect is much weaker than that of negative sentiment.

## Robustness Checks

When capturing sentiment in news articles, we used the positive and negative word lists from GI. Loughran and McDonald (2011) suggested that the entries in GI may not accurately reflect sentiment in the finance domain. In their study, a sentiment lexicon specifically for the finance domain was created. To show how sensitive our results are to the choice of sentiment lexicon, we tested all of the four models again using the lexicon proposed in Loughran and McDonald. The results (summarized in Appendix C) are very similar to our main results.

To show that the effects of return on sentiment are robust to market risk factors discussed in Fama and French (1993), we calculated daily abnormal return for each stock following Luo et al. (2013), using a 250-trading day rolling window. Since the risk factors are not available at the hourly frequency, we only ran Model 3 again using daily abnormal return. The results show that a 1% change in abnormal return predicts 11.61% change in positive sentiment and -27.28% in negative sentiment, both effects are significant at the 1% level. This is consistent with our previously discussed results.

To account for bid-ask bounce, we implemented the “tick test” proposed by Lee and Ready (1991) to identify the previous trade triggered by a market sell (buy) order if the last trade is triggered by a market buy (sell) order. We then used the mid-point of these two trades as the new price to calculate hour-level return. Since our data are already high-frequency trade data, we could trace early prices from the last price at each hour backward. If the first different trade price is higher (lower), we can conclude that the last price at the hour reflects the transaction of a market sell (buy) order, bouncing back (up) from a previous market buy (sell) order. In both the buy and sell order cases, all we need to do empirically is use the average of these two prices instead of the last price to calculate return for our hour-level models. The results (reported in Appendix D) obtained from this procedure are largely consistent with our main results. This is reasonable as the average bid-ask spread for our 44-stock sample is only 3 cents, or in a relative sense, 0.04% of the stock price. We can see that these stocks have very liquid trading, making them barely affected by bid-ask bounce.

## Discussion of Findings and Implications

The relationship between microblog sentiment and stock returns has drawn a lot of attention in the last few years. Recent research efforts in the area have explored this relationship using predictive models as well as explanatory models. The predictive models demonstrated the usefulness of microblog sentiment for predicting future stock returns (e.g., Bollen et al. 2011; Nann et al. 2013; Oh and Sheng 2011; Oliveira et al. 2013). However, those models lack explanatory power (Shmueli 2010); they cannot be employed to test the presence of a significant relationship between microblog sentiment and return.

Explanatory models, on the other hand, can be used to test the statistical significance of relationships between theoretical constructs (Shmueli 2010). However, prior studies in the area did not find that positive or negative sentiment on microblog platforms has a significant influence on subsequent stock returns (e.g., Bollen et al. 2011; Sprenger et al. 2014; Yu et al. 2013). Using explanatory models, our study is the first to reveal and explain the significant influence of negative sentiment expressed in a microblog on stock returns. It contributes not only to the finance literature by empirically showing how a new factor explains stock returns, but it also contributes to the IS literature by demonstrating an important economic impact of a universally adopted IT artifact: a microblog. It is this artifact that provides a high-velocity platform for big data—a topic of great interest to IS researchers— thereby helping us uncover a major phenomenon in the domain.

Using a series of rigorously designed analyses on a very large data set, we did not find the influence of microblog sentiment on stock returns (neither for index nor for individual stocks) to be significant at the day level. This finding is consistent with the Granger causality test results of Bollen et al. (2011) and the time-sequencing regression results in Sprenger et al. (2014). However, we did find the influence at the hour level for both DJIA and individual stock returns. This effect is both statistically and economically significant. At the market level, this effect was partially reversed, providing evidence for the overreaction bias of noise traders. Our study is also the first to provide empirical evidence for the influence of microblog sentiment on stock returns during a relatively short time window: an hour.

Our analysis of microblog data revealed a major phenomenon in the domain: the influence of negative sentiment on stock returns at the hour level but not at the day level. We believe that the reason behind this is that the impact of high-velocity big data lasts only for a relatively short duration. Large volumes of messages move through StockTwits at a high velocity, making it possible for intraday noise traders to react to signals at near real-time speeds. The microblog platform, therefore, provides the perfect environment for those intraday traders to take their cues from the negative sentiments expressed in the latest messages, rather than be influenced by those expressed in messages that are several hours old. In other words, the latency of the influence of negative sentiment on stock returns is much shorter than a day in the context of a high-volume, high-velocity microblog platform. Compared to related prior studies (e.g., Bollen et al. 2011; Sprenger et al. 2014), we were able to detect this influence by diving deeper into shorter time horizons in which intraday traders operate.

Microblogs are essentially different from traditional media, such as news. Usually, a news agency does not publish news articles about the same company multiple times a day. While the finance literature found that news is useful in predicting daily stock returns, such low-frequency update of information is not that useful to intraday traders. In contrast, microblogs are updated at a much higher frequency. The discussion of a publicly traded company on a microblog platform can be very active throughout a day. This information source is potentially useful to intraday traders. In fact, in our sample, about 60% of the users identified themselves as either day traders or swing traders among those who reported holding period and posted 65% of the messages. These investors change positions frequently and rely on short term information about the stock market.

Although the finance literature has used investor sentiment to explain abnormal returns (e.g., Baker and Wurgler 2006; Tetlock 2007), the findings in our study are new and important. Compared to prior studies that investigated the effect of news sentiment on stock returns (e.g., Fang and Peress 2009; Tetlock 2007), our focus in this study is on investigating the influence of sentiment expressed in messages posted on a microblog platform, a new and totally different context.

Contexts that have direct impacts on IT use are of great importance in IS research (Gopal and Prasad 2000; Hevner et al. 2004; Hong et al. 2013). In terms of context, microblogs differ significantly from traditional news sources. First, news articles are usually written by professional journalists while microblog messages are published by a wide variety of users. Second, news articles are usually updated daily, while microblog messages are updated in real-time. Third, the volume of microblog messages is several magnitudes larger than that of news articles. Fourth, news sentiment can be considered as an indirect proxy for investor sentiment, while microblog sentiment directly reflects investor sentiment. Given these characteristics, understanding the implications of microblogs on organizations and users is important for IS researchers (Kane et al. 2014).

In addition to the context, our findings differ significantly from those in Baker and Wurgler (2006) in several ways:

(1) We studied stock returns in a much shorter horizon (i.e., daily and hourly) than did Baker and Wurgler (i.e., yearly). It is common in the finance literature that findings in a longer horizon do not apply to a shorter horizon, which turns out to be the case here.

(2) We found that negative sentiment predicts lower return in the short term, while Baker and Wurgler found that lower sentiment predicts higher subsequent return in the long term.

(3) Baker and Wurgler used indirect proxies to construct a market-wide sentiment index while we analyzed microblog postings to assess investor sentiment toward individual stocks.

Among our findings, the pattern of how negative sentiment influences the stock market at the hourly frequency confirms that microblog sentiment significantly influences the investment decision of noise traders, who are risk-averse and constrained in capital. In other words, investors are more compelled to take action when they perceive negative information, rather than positive information. Although Tetlock (2007) found that negative sentiment in news is more useful in explaining subsequent stock returns, the role of the negative sentiment of average microblog users has not been examined before. Our results show that negative sentiment on StockTwits explains subsequent returns even after controlling for news sentiment and volume, suggesting the importance of user opinion. Tetlock also suggested that the agreement among investors may be effective in capturing investor opinion bias in the stock market. Our results show that negative sentiment is more effective even after controlling for the agreement index. We have extended our understanding of this important investor behavioral bias to the context of the microblog, which provides actionable implications (Weber 2003). In practice, data scientists can assign more importance to negative sentiment when developing a predictive model.

We also found that, in the other direction, negative sentiment is significantly influenced by stock returns in all of the four models we tested. This finding provides evidence for the bidirectional causality between microblog sentiment and stock returns. It also demonstrates the endogeneity of microblog sentiment and supports the appropriateness of using VAR in our data analysis. Our finding on the asymmetrical response of positive sentiment and negative sentiment to stock returns is consistent with our previous discussion that popular stocks superior historical returns shape investors’ prior beliefs, and subsequent negative returns contradict these beliefs and cause stronger emotional reactions (than positive returns do). This finding contributes to our knowledge on the social behavior of microblog users. It also provides new evidence of investor bias characterized in behavioral finance, such as feedback trading and extrapolative expectation.

Based on findings on the bidirectional causality between microblog sentiment and stock returns, data scientists are informed of the potential feedback loop that exists between microblog sentiment and related economic activities when developing predictive models. It would help them avoid the potential pitfall of “forecasting the past.” This finding also calls for more attention to the monitoring value of social media in addition to its predictive value in various domains. For instance, economic policy makers can measure public confidence and/or pessimism directly from social media in supplement to (or instead of) surveys. Another potential area of inquiry is a marketing campaign, where the consumer response to a promotion effort can be gauged by the change in social media sentiment toward the product or service after the campaign.

Moreover, we found that the effect of stock returns on microblog sentiment is much stronger and exists longer at the market level than at the stock level. This suggests that market participants consider the fluctuation of the stock index as the market trend, which reflects systematic risk and needs to be taken seriously. However, market participants are less sensitive to the fluctuation in individual stock prices since they usually hold portfolios of stocks and idiosyncratic risk has already been diversified to a certain degree. Through a comprehensive empirical analysis, we were able to reveal the different patterns of how microblog sentiment interacts with stock returns at different levels. Nonetheless, these patterns need to be validated using additional evidence in future research.

As discussed earlier, many hedge funds, which are often known to be trading at high frequency, have already been executing trading strategies based on social media information. The trading volume of such institutional traders is likely to cause fluctuations in the market. This also provides an additional explanation on why the predictive power of microblog sentiment exists at the hour level. This finding in our study could also benefit such institutional traders and their analytics solution providers. For data scientists who have been developing predictive models at the daily frequency, our finding suggests that they need to respond to the high-velocity microblogs more promptly and forecast more frequently to be able to exploit the transient opportunities.

Our empirical study was designed to address a set of research questions relating to sentiment and stock returns in the context of microblogs. Specifically, we examined how the relationship between microblog sentiment and stock returns is influenced differentially by sentiment polarity, observation time horizon, and breadth of selected stocks. We also provided explanations on why those relationships exist. For instance, we found that negative sentiment significantly influences stock returns over a shorter time horizon (hour), but not over a longer horizon (day). We provided a causal explanation of this relationship by identifying a distinctive feature of the microblog environment—that it is high velocity—compared to traditional online platforms, such as blogs and news sites. The finding that negative sentiment expressed in high-velocity messages influences stock returns is a new and important contribution to theory in the field. It can be tested using other high-velocity platforms operating in different domains, such as music sales, movie sales, and Internet downloads. Replication of our study in new settings— using different platforms across multiple domains—will help extend the generalizability of the theoretical findings of this study.

## Conclusions and Future Directions

In this study, we comprehensively investigated the relationship between microblog sentiment and stock returns at the market and individual stock levels, at the daily and hourly frequencies. The findings have valuable implications for research and practice. Although there have been efforts at predicting stock returns by analyzing microblogs, ours is the first study to reveal the significance of the effect at the hour level. We have also shown the bidirectional causality between microblog sentiment and stock returns, the difference between the market and individual stock levels, and the asymmetry of positive sentiment and negative sentiment of microblog users. This study has extended our knowledge of microblog users and their behavioral bias in trading stocks.

This study has a few limitations, which may be addressed in future research. In our panel models, only large companies with frequent microblog mentions were studied. Thus, our findings may only pertain to such companies. Furthermore, we only included the 44 most frequently mentioned stocks in our panel. Although the panel size is comparable to that in Antweiler and Frank (2004), it may also suffer from small sample bias. However, a larger panel is not yet feasible due to the limited mention of most stocks on microblogs and needs to be investigated in future research.

Although we used a seemingly competitive sentiment analysis tool available for our data, the sentiment classification accuracy is not perfect. A more accurate assessment of the relationship strength between microblog sentiment and stock returns could be achieved when a better tool for sentiment analysis of microblog messages becomes available in the future. Moreover, future research can examine microblog sentiment in more dimensions to reveal other interesting relationships.

The predictive power of microblog sentiment on stock returns is subject to malicious manipulations, which we did not examine in this study. Recently, an individual trader was charged for market manipulation by posting false statements about two companies on Twitter (U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission 2015). Future research could explore the possibility of detecting market manipulation on social media.

Prior literature explaining stock returns mainly focused on quantitative information. Not until very recently did research efforts start investigating the qualitative information reflected in documents, such as news. This study extends this literature by providing important empirical findings through microblog data. However, the sentiment analysis techniques by nature do not capture the quantitative information that could indicate a public company’s prospects. Future research could integrate both sentiment information and quantitative information in one predictive model.

In our analysis, we did not distinguish the news posted in microblogs from user opinion. Thus, the microblog sentiment measured in our study partially incorporates the effect of news sentiment. Future analytics research could develop classification models to distinguish between news and opinion on social media. Once the news component and user opinion component on microblogs can be effectively separated, future research could also assess their relative importance in predicting stock returns.

This study weighted each microblog message equally. But certain microblog users (e.g., public company executives, government officials, and professional analysts) may have more influence on ordinary microblog users. Future research could weight microblog opinions based on user influence in order to yield more precise estimates of the effect of microblog sentiment on stock returns.
