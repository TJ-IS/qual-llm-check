# THE VALUE OF RECIPROCITY IN ONLINE BARTER MARKETS: AN EMPIRICAL INVESTIGATION<sup>1</sup>

Shun Ye School of Business, George Mason University, Fairfax, VA 22030 U.S.A. {sye2@gmu.edu}

Siva Viswanathan and Il-Horn Hann R. H. Smith School of Business, University of Maryland, College Park, MD 20742 U.S.A. {sivaswana@rhsmith.umd.edu} {ihann@rhsmith.umd.edu}

This study empirically examines online peer-to-peer (P2P) barter markets, in which individuals trade goods or services without the use of money. Using detailed transaction data and weblog data from a leading online P2P barter market for books, we examine the role of reciprocal relationships in the initiation and fulfillment of exchanges. We find that avid users with a higher demand for books are more likely to establish and utilize reciprocal relationships compared to non-avid users. In examining the value of reciprocal relationships, we find that the use of reciprocal relationships provides significant value throughout the entire market exchange process. First, reciprocal relationships enable an avid user to find new books and new exchange partners through exploratory browsing, which lowers her search costs. Second, browsing reciprocal partners enable an avid user to discover books in new topics, despite that reciprocal partners share similar book tastes with the user. Finally, reciprocal relationships help improve the exchange outcome through lower rejection rates and faster delivery speeds. Our findings provide important insights into the role of reciprocal relationships in facilitating exchanges in online markets and provide useful implications for the design of decentralized online P2P markets.

Keywords: Online barter markets, online sharing platforms, reciprocity, information asymmetry, empirical analyses

## Introduction

Developments in Internet and Web 2.0 technologies have brought about a significant transformation in commerce, with online platforms such as eBay and Amazon.com serving as classic exemplars of the Web 2.0 economy. However, more recently, we have witnessed the emergence and growth of another category of online transaction platforms: online peerto-peer (P2P) barter markets, wherein individuals exchange underutilized goods and services without money changing hands (Botsman and Rogers 2010). Even though the exchange of goods or services through barter has been around since prehistoric times, barter markets have, for the most part, been replaced by monetary or currency-based markets. However, with recent technological developments, the last few years have seen a rapid growth of online P2P barter markets, such as SwapStyle.com for clothes, PaperBackSwap. com for books, SwapaCD.com and SwapaDVD.com for music and movies, HomeExchange.com for housing, among others, making these curiosities a very distinct reality.

Online P2P barter markets differ from canonical online markets such as Amazon.com and eBay in three major ways. First, whereas traditional online marketplaces like eBay are often characterized by one-shot interactions (Resnick and Zeckhauser 2002), P2P barter markets encourage participants to develop lasting relationships and repeated interactions over time (Codagnone and Martens 2016). This is particularly salient in online P2P barter markets focusing on goods such as music and books, which bring like-minded individuals together to engage in long-term exchanges (Finck and Ranchordas 2017). Second, in online markets like Amazon. com, each participant often takes on the role of either a buyer or a seller. In addition, numerous individual buyers are often served by a few sellers on the other side of the market. Due to the larger transaction volume for each seller on average in these markets, reputation systems that signal the quality of the seller have proven effective in facilitating transactions (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006). However, in online P2P barter markets, each participant often takes on the role of a buyer in some transactions, and the role of a seller in others. Consequently, these markets typically have an equal number of buyers and sellers, with participants having much smaller transaction volumes on average compared to sellers in markets such as eBay and Amazon.com. Due to the smaller number of transactions for each individual as well as the temporal nature of the role played by a participant, reputation mechanisms that work very well in other markets with sufficient transactional history may be less effective in online P2P barter markets. Instead, the two parties involved in a market exchange might rely on other mechanisms to make decisions. Third, traditional online markets often involve formal contracts and escrow services to govern transactions that are valued according to the universally accepted medium of exchange: fiat money. In contrast, online barter markets are nonmonetary marketplaces. Given that that no real money is involved, market participants may rely on implicit contracts such as reciprocity and trust to facilitate exchanges (Albinsson and Perera 2012).

The above distinct features of online P2P barter markets imply that reciprocal relationships among participants that are built over repeated interactions might play an important role. While a healthy body of research on online markets has examined various trust building mechanisms such as reputation systems that facilitate buyer–seller monetary transactions (e.g., Ba and Pavlou 2002; Dellarocas 2003; Maslet and Penard 2012), very little is known about how prior relationships among participants might also affect transactions. Our study tries to fill this gap by examining to what extent and through what mechanisms reciprocal relationships—the state of two participants having received at least one good from and given at least one good to each other—influence exchanges in P2P barter markets.

Figure 1 illustrates the research framework of this study. The main objective of the study is to examine how the use and outcomes of reciprocal relationships differ from those of nonreciprocal relationships in online P2P barter markets. For an exchange to happen, the user initiating the exchange needs to first search for the good she is interested in through different mechanisms provided by the market. The user then chooses one good from the search results and sends a request to the owner. Once the exchange is initiated, the good holder makes decisions regarding whether and/or how to fulfill the request. In order to uncover the mechanisms through which reciprocal relationships influence outcomes in P2P barter markets, our three primary research questions (RQ1, RQ2, and RQ3) examine the effects of reciprocal relationships at each stage of the exchange process. RQ1 asks, what are the impacts of reciprocal relationships as compared to nonreciprocal relationships on the search behaviors of users? RQ2 asks, what are the impacts of reciprocal relationships as compared to nonreciprocal relationships on the search outcomes? Finally RQ3 asks, what are the impacts of reciprocal relationships as compared to nonreciprocal relationships on the quality of exchange outcomes? In answering these questions, we also examine if the effects of reciprocal relationships differ depending on the type of users.

Using a unique dataset of detailed transactions and weblog data from one of the largest online P2P barter markets for books, we first show that the emphasis on developing reciprocal relationships is salient for avid users who have higher volumes of transactions but insignificant for non-avid users with lower volumes of transactions. We further find that the impacts of reciprocal relationships manifest throughout the entire exchange process. First, avid users actively browse within their reciprocal networks to find new books, albeit having the option to get the same book from other book owners in the marketplace. This deliberate use of reciprocal relationships to initiate exchanges, however, is not salient for non-avid users. Second, the use of reciprocal partners to initiate new requests helps lower search costs for avid users compared to non-avid users. Although reciprocal relationships are established among participants with similar book tastes, counterintuitively, browsing their reciprocal networks enables avid users to discover books in new topics given that browsing often represents an exploratory attempt without a target topic in mind. Finally, requests from reciprocal partners are treated more favorably by avid users but not non-avid users. Avid users are less likely to reject requests from their reciprocal partners and more likely to deliver the requested book faster.

Our study is among the first to systematically examine transaction dynamics in emerging P2P barter markets. Our study also makes important contributions to the literature on online markets by examining the value added by reciprocal relationships. Our study shows that reciprocity plays a pivotal role in facilitating cooperation and exchanges among strangers when repeated interactions among participants are enabled. Our study also has implications for the design of such online barter markets. We show that encouraging reciprocal relationships between market participants and displaying such information may help increase market efficiency in the absence of enforceable contracts to govern nonmonetary transactions in P2P barter markets.

## Literature Review and Theoretical Background

## Barter

Barter markets have been around since prehistoric times. There are two major forms of barter: direct barter and indirect barter (Oh 1989; Rice 2003). Direct barter is the direct exchange of one good for another, while indirect barter involves the use of an intermediary good such as a token. In indirect barter, one good is given in exchange for the intermediary good, and the intermediary good can then be used to obtain a different good from a third party. This overcomes the “double coincidence of wants” problem in direct barter and increases the efficiency of barter exchanges (Heller and Starr 1976; Jevons 1985). Naturally, indirect barter markets have been relatively more popular than direct barter markets.

Previous research on barter falls into two major streams. The first stream of research focuses on the macroeconomics of barter markets and finds that barter markets are in general less efficient compared to monetary markets (e.g., Banerjee and Maskin 1996; Brunner and Meltzer 1971; Freeman 1989).

Nevertheless, barter markets are still used widely. According to the U.S. Department of Commerce, barter accounts for about 30 percent to 40 percent of the world’s total business, with over 250,000 U.S. businesses actively participating in barter (Rice 2003). The second stream of research focuses on barter practice between firms and provides theoretical justifications for the use of barter by firms. Magenheim and Murrell (1988) show that barter can serve as a hidden price discrimination device by helping conceal the firm’s type to future customers. Prendergast and Stole (2001) show that barter helps firms to generate liquidity and segment the market into high-demand and low-demand customers when liquidity constraints do not allow firms to discriminate through money. Guriev (2004) shows that barter can emerge as a means of screening high quality buyers from low quality buyers even when there are no financial constraints. Marin and Schnitzer (2005) show that barter can be used by a firm to collateralize a trade credit to maintain production when the firm faces a severe creditworthiness problem.

Despite the popularity of barter among firms, little research has been done on barter exchanges among individuals. The few exceptions have examined what motivates individuals to participate in P2P barter (e.g., Albinsson and Perera 2012; Hamari et al. 2016). In another study, De Sousa and Munro (2012) find that experienced individuals in online role-playing games are more likely to engage in barter. To the best of our knowledge, our study is the first to examine the dynamics of barter exchanges among individuals using large scale transactional data.

## Facilitating Transactions in Online Markets

One major barrier to facilitating transactions in online markets is information asymmetry between the buyer and the seller (Dellarocas 2005). Prior studies have examined various mechanisms that help reduce information asymmetry and promote trust between strangers. For example, Pavlou and Gefen (2004) find that the availability of institutional mechanisms such as escrow services and credit card guarantees increases buyers’ intentions to engage in online exchanges. Ba and Pavlou (2002) show that electronic reputation systems benefit online markets by increasing calculus-based credibility trust between buyers and sellers without repeated transactions. The findings are echoed by many other studies of reputation mechanism design (e.g., Bolton et al. 2004; Fan et al. 2005; Maslet and Penard 2012).

More recent studies have focused on examining how the availability of digital information helps facilitate economic transactions in online markets. For example, Lin et al. (2013) find that an individual’s online friendships serve as signals of credit quality and affect her funding activities in the P2P lending context. Through field experiments, Bapna and Umyarov (2015) show that economic transactions can be facilitated by persuading market participants’ online social ties. Dimoka et al. (2012) suggest that provision of diagnostic product descriptions and third-party product assurances helps reduce the buyer’s uncertainty about the quality of the product and thus helps promote transactions in online markets.

While existing research on facilitating transactions in online markets mainly focuses on markets characterized by one-shot interactions between market participants, there is a lack of understanding of how an individual’s relationship developed through repeated interactions with another participant, rather than her aggregate reputation through interactions with all other participants, plays a role in online markets—a void our study tries to fill.

## Buyer–Seller Exchange Relationships

An exchange between a buyer and a seller can be viewed as discrete (i.e., transactional) or continuous (i.e., relational) (Macneil 1980). The discrete transactional view treats exchanges as characterized by very little communication between the buyer and the seller, one-time interactions, and sharp ending of the buyer–seller relationship. In contrast, the relational view treats the exchange between the buyer and the seller as an ongoing relationship that transpires over time. According to this view, the buyer and the seller may develop obligations and norms to facilitate future collaborations. Researchers like Dwyer et al. (1987) argue that some elements of a “relationship” underlie all transactions and the exchange between the buyer and the seller should be treated as a continuum, ranging from discrete to relational.

The transactional versus relational view of buyer–seller exchanges has been applied in many contexts. For example, Coviello et al. (2002) find that different firms view transactions with customers in different ways; whereas some firms are predominantly transactional-oriented, other firms are predominantly relational-oriented. Pels (1999) argues that the transactional- or relational-oriented view not only applies to firms, but also to its customers as different customers may take different stances on how to view the exchange relationship. Cannon et al. (2010) examine the exchange relationship between a buyer firm and its suppliers and find that firms in different cultural regions have different exchange orientations. In the IT outsourcing context, researchers have suggested that whether an outsourcing contract should be governed by transactional mechanisms or relational mechanisms depends on the nature of the client–vendor relationship (Cannon et al. 2000; Zaheer and Venkatraman 1995).

To what extent market participants in online P2P barter markets treat their relationships with others as relational and how such relational exchanges affect market outcomes remains an open empirical question. On the one hand, the lack of face-to-face communication and the availability of large alternative choices might induce individuals to be more transactional-oriented without commitment to long-term relationships. On the other hand, the ease of keeping track of transaction partners and the interactive features such as social networking and online communication might encourage market participants to be relational-oriented and engage in repeated and reciprocal exchanges with certain partners. Prior studies have suggested that in economic interactions wherein no enforceable contracts are available, individuals care about who their trading partners are (Bapna et al. 2017; Burtch et al. 2014; Leider et al. 2009). Our study contributes to these streams of research by empirically examining reciprocal and nonreciprocal relationships in online barter markets and their implications for different types of users.

## Reciprocity

The study of reciprocity traces back to research in evolutionary biology on cooperation in humans and other species. The theory of reciprocal altruism posits that species engage in bilateral cooperation in pursuit of net benefits (Trivers 1971). This theory to explain human behavior by evolutionary biologists is echoed by behavioral experiments in economics. Berg et al. (1995) design an investment game to study reciprocity among individuals in two different rooms and find that over 90% of the subjects in room A sent some money to the other party in room B in the expectation of a positive return. This finding is further supported by other experimental researchers (e.g., Charness 2004; Kirchler et al. 1996). The conflict between the observed preference for direct reciprocation and the hypotheses of self-interest and rationality can be explained by evolutionary game theory. According to evolutionary game theory, more successful strategies and behavior survive, while less successful ones are eliminated (Sethi and Somanathan 2003). Although selfinterested individuals can gain more by defecting (i.e., not reciprocating), humans have evolved mental algorithms for identifying and punishing cheaters (Hoffman et al. 1998). Guth et al. (1982) and Ochs and Roth (1989) find that individuals are willing to punish opportunists even when it is costly. Self-regarding cheating is thus unstable in the presence of preference toward reciprocal cooperation in the long run and reciprocity dominates as a norm during the evolutionary process (Sethi and Somanathan 2001).

The importance of reciprocity is also echoed by proponents of network exchange theory and the principle of homophily. According to network exchange theory, individuals deliberately choose partners by carefully evaluating their resources and the possibility of reciprocation before engaging in an exchange relationship (Willer 1999). According to the principle of homophily, users with similar characteristics tend to reciprocate to each other as they share similar resources, are sympathetic to each other’s needs to preserve resources, and are prepared to provide support for each other (McPherson et al. 2001). Therefore, reciprocity is often seen to occur between similar individuals who possess similar resources (Lin 2002). For example, Olk and Gibbons (2010) find that adults with the same gender, ethnicity, or age cohort tend to develop reciprocal friendships with each other. Hopcroft et al. (2011) find that Twitter users who share similar social status are more likely to develop a reciprocal relationship through mutually following each other. In addition, when individuals engaging in network exchange are dependent on the actions of multiple others from whom they cannot directly benefit, the risk of nonreciprocity increases (Molm et al. 2007). Therefore, individuals prefer bilateral reciprocation with others to obtain a higher amount and quality of exchange outcomes (Buchan et al. 2002).

A complementary view is echoed by psychological contract theories. A psychological contract is an individual’s belief in mutual obligations between herself and another party and is formed under the norms of reciprocity (Coyle-Shapiro and Kessler 2002; Dabos and Rousseau 2004). An individual is committed to contribute more to the partner when the psychological contract is fulfilled (Henderson et al. 2008). Psychological contracts exist between buyers and sellers in online markets and affect their transaction decisions (Pavlou and Gefen 2005). A recent study finds that reciprocal relationships between project initiators and project backers have a positive impact on the success rate of projects in online crowdfunding (Zvilichovsky et al. 2013). Specific to our context, barter can be seen as a subtype of gift exchange (Bell 1991), which gives rise to an implicit psychological contract between the giver and the receiver (Davis 2009; Schein 1965), which in turn may have a significant impact on subsequent exchanges between the two individuals. Therefore, from the perspective of psychological contract theory, it is expected that participants in P2P barter markets value reciprocity and utilize it to make exchange decisions.

Whereas previous studies mostly use lab experiments to show that reciprocity helps facilitate cooperation, it is unclear to what extent and how reciprocity plays a role in online barter markets. Our study adds to this literature by empirically examining the role of reciprocal relationships in the initiation and consummation of transactions in online P2P barter markets.

## Summary of Theoretical Background

Overall, the aforementioned literature suggests that reciprocal relationships may exert a positive influence on market exchanges in online barter markets for four major reasons. First, prior studies have shown that individuals care about the identities of their trading partners (Bapna et al. 2015; Leider et al. 2009). In the presence of information asymmetry between two trading partners, reciprocal relationships may serve as an effective implicit or informal contract that helps govern transactions (Lambe et al. 2000). Second, network exchange theory suggests that a reciprocal relationship benefits exchanges by enhancing exchange outcomes and the satisfaction of exchange partners as it induces trust (Buchan et al. 2002). Third, when psychological contracts exists between participants in online markets, its fulfillment is symbolized by the establishment of a reciprocal relationship. Therefore, a reciprocal relationship should contribute positively to the outcomes of an exchange as the two parties are motivated to better service each other due to the fulfillment of the psychological contract between them. Fourth, since reciprocal relationships are often developed between two similar individuals who have high sympathy for each other, they would help each other through improving the outcome quality of exchanges.

However, the expected positive impacts of reciprocal relationships on market exchanges may differ for different market participants. Previous studies have suggested that individuals may view the exchange relationship with others differently and take different stances on the importance of long-term and reciprocal relationships; whereas some are more relationaloriented and prefer to build long-term and ongoing relationships through repeated and reciprocal exchanges, others may be more transactional-oriented and focus on the current exchange without anticipating future interactions and benefits (Pels 1999). Compared to users who conduct sporadic exchanges in P2P barter markets, users who engage in frequent exchanges are more likely to care about their long-term success of exchanges in the market. As a result, users with higher volumes of exchanges—avid users—are more likely to be motivated to establish reciprocal relationships with others and utilize them as compared to non-avid users. In addition, in online barter markets where there is an information asymmetry problem, it is not costless to find a trustworthy exchange partner. Rather than having to carefully evaluate each potential partner’s reputation and reliability, a user can quickly explore a reciprocal partner’s inventory to initiate exchanges as the reciprocal relationship built from past mutually successful exchanges serves as a positive signal for the credibility of the partner. Therefore, we posit that an avid user is more likely to browser her reciprocal partners to search for goods and initiate an exchange compared to a non-avid user.

Given that reciprocal relationships are likely to develop among users with similar preferences, we expect an avid user to be more likely to find the goods she needs by browsing her reciprocal partners in a P2P barter market. Further, given that leveraging reciprocal partners reduces the need of avid users to spend time and effort in searching, evaluating, and comparing trading partners, we posit that an avid user who leverages her reciprocal relationships has a lower search cost of initiating an exchange compared to a non-avid user. Finally, given that avid users are more likely to value and utilize reciprocal relationships, we posit that an avid user is less likely to reject a request from a reciprocal partner, and more likely to service a request from a reciprocal partner better, than a non-avid user.

In summary, reciprocal relationships are expected to have a significant impact on market exchanges in P2P barter markets but its effect is likely to be different across different types of users. Our study empirically examines if this is true by uncovering how reciprocal relationships influence exchanges in P2P barter markets for avid and non-avid users.

## Research Context and Data

We collect data from one of the leading P2P barter marketplaces for books, in which more than 1,000 books are exchanged every day. Even though the site operates internationally, most of the participants are from the U.S. Each market participant creates an inventory list of books she is willing to give away and a wish list of books she wants from others. The inventory list and the wish list of any individual, which reflect her book taste, are visible to all participants in the market. A typical exchange in the market happens in three steps. First, the book seeker finds the book she wants and sends the book owner a request. This can be achieved through three different ways:

(1) The book seeker conducts a keyword search within the site to locate the book she needs. In this case, the site lists all the users who have the book available for exchange. The book seeker can view the profiles of all choices as all the information is public and accessible, and then chooses a book owner and sends her a request for the book.

(2) The book seeker browses an individual’s inventory to find the book she is interested in and then directly requests the book from the owner.

(3) The book seeker checks her wish list and sends a request to the individual who is displayed as an owner of any one of the books in the requester’s wish list.

The book owner, upon receiving the request, decides whether to accept or reject it. Third, upon accepting the request, the book owner ships the book to the book seeker. In contrast to traditional online markets such as eBay in which the buyer pays for the shipping, the book owner actually pays for the postage for mailing the book to the book requester. Regardless of the monetary value of the book, each book is valued as 1 virtual point in this market. In general for any book exchange, 1 point is transferred from the book seeker to the book owner. To encourage overseas exchanges, the book requester has to pay 2 extra points to the book owner for international requests.

To reduce information asymmetry among participants, the market tracks each book exchange and displays an individual’s past performance in her public profile through a variety of measures, including the number of books requested from others, the number of books given to others, the ratio of number of books received to number of books given, the number of book requests she has rejected, the number of books reported as lost in the mail by the exchange partner, etc. In addition, the market provides a feedback mechanism which allows exchange partners to rate each other’s performance. Each individual’s feedback score is also publicly available on her profile page. These performance metrics serve to signal each participant’s reputation reflected in previous activities.

We first take a snapshot of all individuals on November 1, 2010. This snapshot allows us to characterize each individual based on their historical information. Then starting from November 1, 2010, to April 30, 2011, we collect 6 months detailed and dynamic transaction (book exchange) data in the marketplace on a daily basis. A total of 245,191 books involving 19,261 users are traded during this period. In addition, we collect 3 months’ of detailed weblog data for all users from November 1, 2010, to January 31, 2011, which allows us to examine granular search behaviors of the market participants. To allow for a more accurate measurement of user characteristics and behavior based on their exchange histories, we restrict our sample to 11,721 users who had used the market for at least 3 months by November 1, 2010, and had conducted at least one exchange in the subsequent six-month observation period of the study. We empirically define avid users as users who on average receive (i.e., successfully request) at least 2 books per month based on their historic exchanges before November 1st 2010 for two reasons.<sup>2</sup> First, the average number of books received per month for the entire sample is 1.99. Second, the market requires that each participant can request a maximum of two books for each book given out to others. Our definition of avid users ensures that they represent the pool of participants who are more active than an average user in terms of requesting books and engaging in exchanges. In contrast, non-avid users are defined as users who on average receive less than 2 books per month. We further use the propensity score matching method to correct for potential sample selection bias due to any observable differences between an avid user and a non-avid user (Dehejia and Wahba 2002). Details of the propensity score matching are provided in Appendix A. It results in a sample of 11,308 users (5,654 avid users and 5,654 non-avid users) that are used for subsequent major analyses. For a given individual, we define her exchange partner a reciprocal partner if the individual and the partner have given books to, and received books from, each other. Otherwise, we call the exchange partner a nonreciprocal partner of the focal individual. We find that users with reciprocal partners have engaged in significantly more exchanges than users without reciprocal partners (see Table B1 in Appendix B), suggesting that avid users are more likely to take advantage of reciprocal relationships compared to nonavid users. Therefore, we will compare and analyze how avid users and non-avid users leverage reciprocal partners to search for books and make exchange decisions during the subsequent 6-month observation period.<sup>3</sup>

It is pertinent to note that the P2P barter market has a wellestablished reputation system to signal the reputation of each participant. Therefore, it is important to not ignore the effect of the reputation system on market exchanges. Given our focus on the value of reciprocal relationships in market exchanges as well as how it might differ for avid users and non-avid users, we also control for the reputation system’s effect by including different reputation measures of the exchange partner as control variables in all the major econometric analyses.

## Analyses of an Individual’s Choice of Exchange Partners

While the above analysis shows that an avid user browses reciprocal partners and requests books from them, this could be due to the fact that the reciprocal partner is the only owner of the book the avid user wants. In this case, the selection of the reciprocal partner by an avid user is driven purely by the scarcity of supply rather than by deliberate choice. To account for this possibility, we empirically examine how individuals initiate an exchange when there are multiple alternative exchange partners to choose from. For each book request, the choice set is created by including each book owner who has the requested book available for giving away at the time of the request. We use a conditional logit model, which has been commonly employed in research on consumer choice, to estimate the marginal effect of each explanatory variable given an individual's specific choice set (Greene 2003).

While our main explanatory variable of interest is ifReciprocal, which indicates whether there is a reciprocal relationship between the possible book giver and the focal individual, we also need to control for other possible factors that might influence an individual’s choice of exchange partners in the presence of multiple choices. Even though the book requester may have not established a reciprocal relationship with a possible exchange partner (i.e., a book owner) yet, she might expect reciprocation in the future when deciding which book owner to choose. As a result, the focal user (i.e., the book seeker) would be more likely to choose someone who shares similar book taste—one who is more likely to become a reciprocal partner in the future. To account for this, we include two book taste similarity measures between the focal user and the exchange partner—tasteSimilarity and sharedGenre—in the utility model specification. The variable tasteSimilarity measures how similar two individuals are in their book tastes using the cosine similarity measure based on the two users’ inventory lists and wish lists. On average, each user has 54.17 books in her inventory list and 183.66 books in her wish list. We first calculate each individual’s percentages of books in each of the 36 book genres used by the context of study (e.g., Action & Adventure, Arts & Photography, Business & Investing, etc.) and store them as a vector to represent the individual’s book taste. The cosine similarity of book tastes between individual A and individual B is then calculated using the following formula (Salton et al. 1975):

$$
\text { similarity } (A, B) = \cos (\theta) = \frac {A \cdot B}{\| A \| \| B \|} = \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} A _ {i} * B _ {i}}{\sqrt {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} A _ {i} ^ {2}} * \sqrt {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} B _ {i} ^ {2}}}
$$

where $A _ { i }$ and $B _ { i }$ denotes their percentages of books in book genre i, and n denotes the total number of book genres. The resulting similarity ranges from 0 to 1, with 0 indicating very different book tastes, 1 indicating exactly the same taste, and values between 0 and 1 indicating some intermediate degree of similarity. The variable sharedGenre measures the count of common genres of books in which the two individuals are both interested.

In addition, we control for the depth of the potential exchange partner’s inventory from the focal user’s perspective at the time of the request using two measures: p\_focalDepthLog, which measures how many books in the genre of the requested book the exchange partner has, and $p _ { \mathrm { ~ - ~ } }$ \_sharedDepthLog, which measures how many books in all shared genres the exchange partner has. We also control for each potential exchange partner’s basic profiles and for the characteristics of past and pending exchanges between the two individuals.

For the book requester of request i, her utility function of choosing partner j is

$$
\begin{array}{l} U _ {i j} = c _ {i} + \gamma_ {1} \text {UserCharacteristics} _ {i} + \gamma_ {2} \text {ifReciprocal} _ {i j} \\ \quad + \gamma_ {3} \text {PartnerCharacteristics} _ {i j} + \gamma_ {4} \text {PartnerReputation} _ {i j} \\ \quad + \gamma_ {5} \text {Similarity} _ {i j} + \gamma_ {6} \text {PartnerInventoryDepth} _ {i j} \\ \quad + \gamma_ {7} \text {CurrentTransCharacteristics} _ {i j} + \gamma_ {8} \text {PastTransCharacteristics} _ {i j} \\ \quad + \gamma_ {9} \text {OtherControls} _ {i j} + \varepsilon_ {i j} \end{array} \tag {1}
$$

where $j { = } I , 2 , 3 , . . . , N _ { i } .$ N denotes the number of choices for request $\begin{array} { r l } { i . } & { { } \varepsilon _ { i j } } \end{array}$ is the disturbance term that is independently distributed with the Type I extreme value distribution (McFadden 1974). The probability of partner j is selected out of $\mathrm { \Delta } N _ { i }$ choices for request i ( ifChosen = 1) is

$$
\begin{array}{c} \text {Prob} \bigl (\text {ifChosen} _ {i j} = 1 \bigr) = \frac {\exp \bigl (U _ {i j} \bigr)}{\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {N _ {i}} \exp \bigl (U _ {i j} \bigr)} \\ j \in \{1, 2, 3, \dots , N _ {i} \} \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

All coefficient vectors are then, estimated through maximum likelihood estimation:

$$
\begin{array}{c} \ln L = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {T} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {N _ {i}} i f C h o s e n _ {i j} \ln P r o b (i f C h o s e n _ {i j} = 1) \\ T: t o t a l n u m b e r o f r e q u e s t s \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

Substituting (1) into (2) and (3), we will see that $\mathrm { c } _ { i }$ and any user fixed characteristics associated with request i will be cancelled out.

The descriptive statistics and the correlation matrix of all variables used in the estimation are provided in Table B2 and Table B3 in the Appendix B respectively. Certain partner characteristics such as $p _ { _ - }$ \_give and ${ \bf \nabla } \cdot { \boldsymbol { p } } _ { - }$ receive are not included in the estimation due to their high correlations with partner characteristic p\_fbScoreLog. There is no multicollinearity among the independent variables as the maximum VIF is 8.24, below the threshold of 10.

We first estimate the conditional logit model using the full sample. We then split the sample into avid users and nonavid users. For each group of users, we further split the sample into two subsamples: a subsample where there is a reciprocal partner in the choice set of each request and a subsample where there is no reciprocal partner to choose for each request.

The estimation results of the full sample and all subsamples are shown in Table 4. In column (1) for the full sample estimation, the coefficient of ifReciprocal is not significant. However, the coefficients of the interaction term ifAvid \* ifReciprocal is significant and positive. In column (2) for the estimation on avid users with at least one reciprocal partner in the choice set, the coefficient of ifReciprocal is significant and positive. In contrast, in column (4) for the estimation on non-avid users with at least one reciprocal partner in the choice set, the coefficient of ifReciprocal is insignificant. These results consistently suggest that avid users rather than non-avid users are more likely to choose a reciprocal partner to request a book despite the presence of other owners of the same book. In addition, the significant and positive coeffi cient of $i f A \nu i d ^ { * }$ tasteSimilarity in the full sample estimation and the significant and positive coefficient of tasteSimilarity in the subsample estimations for avid users suggest that an avid user intentionally chooses the book owner who shares the highest similarity despite the presence of other alternatives. This deliberate choice of avid users indicates that they are forward-looking and choose partners to build potential reciprocal relationships since higher taste similarity leads to a higher chance of forming a reciprocal relationship as reported in Appendix C. The significant and positive coefficient of ifAvid \* p\_sharedDepthLog in the full sample estimation and the significant and positive coefficient of p\_sharedDepthLog in the subsample estimations for avid users suggest that avid users also care about the depth of the partner’s inventory in shared genres when choosing an exchange partner from all possible choices. The coefficient of ifPartnerOfReci is significant and positive when there is no reciprocal partner to choose from in the choice set for avid users as shown in column (3). However, it is not significant for non-avid users as shown in column (5). This result suggests that an avid user also takes advantage of partners of (reciprocal) partners while non-avid users do not.

Our results also show that book requesters are inclined to choose book owners from the same country, as indicated by the significant and positive coefficient of ifSameCountry across all estimations. This is consistent with our expectation that book requesters are concerned about international requests being more likely to be rejected due to higher shipping costs for the book owner. The results also consistently indicate that book requesters choose book owners based on their overall past performance: book owners who reject others’ requests more, book owners who have more books reported as missing by book requesters, and book owners who receive more books than they give out are less likely to be chosen. In contrast, book owners who have a higher feedback score are more likely to be chosen, indicating that a good reputation helps a user clear out her inventory and get virtual points.

## Analyses of the Growth of Individuals Exchange Networks

While the above analyses illustrate the intentional use of reciprocal partners by avid users based on exchanges within a six-month observation period, we also examine if this search strategy is consistently employed by avid users over the longer period of time. From a social network perspective, an individual’s utilization of reciprocal partners to search for books and create new exchanges should also be observed in the dynamic evolution of her exchange networks. If avid users are more likely to browse the lists of reciprocal partners (i.e., neighbors that are reciprocal) and partners of reciprocal partners (i.e., second degree neighbors), we should expect an avid user to have more ties to members in her network over time. As a result, we should observe the avid user gradually increasing her reach within her own community, which is defined as the distance between the focal individual and the furthest node she could reach within the community (e.g., 5 means the focal individual could reach the fifth degree neighbor), while being connected to fewer other communities over time. On the contrary, if a non-avid user relies primarily on keyword searches rather than on browsing her reciprocal networks, the exchange partner she finds is likely to belong to a new community different from the one that to which she is already connected. This new exchange partner then serves as a bridge between the non-avid user and the new community. Therefore, we expect a non-avid user to be connected to more new communities and her reach within her own community to not change significantly over time.

Using Gephi 0.8.2, a social network analysis software, we extract the community structure of the entire exchange network at the beginning of each quarter from October 1, 2008, to October 1, 2010, based on the Louvain method for calculating modularity and detecting communities in a large social network (Blondel et al. 2008). Then based on the dynamically changing communities (i.e., clusters), we compute the number of connected clusters as well as reach within the cluster for each user over time.<sup>5</sup> The time-series trends of connected clusters for an average avid user and an average non-avid user are shown in Figure 2. Whereas a non-avid user connects to more and more clusters, the number of connected clusters for an avid user decreases over time. This trend is consistent with our expectation. In terms of withincluster reach, the trend in Figure 3 shows that an avid user significantly expands her reach whereas a non-avid user’s reach almost remain the same over time, confirming our conjecture.

We also randomly draw two avid users and two non-avid users to illustrate their network growth patterns given that it is infeasible to show each node clearly for a very large network. The results are shown in Figure 4 and 5 respectively.<sup>6</sup>

Consistent with our earlier findings, Figure 4 shows that avid users find exchange partners and grow their exchange networks through exploring reciprocal partners and partners of reciprocal partners. Figure 5 shows that we do not observe similar patterns for non-avid users.

Overall, the analyses of individuals’ search patterns, together with the analyses of how individuals choose exchange partners out of all possible choices, and the analyses of evolution of exchange networks, show that avid users are deliberately, consistently, and significantly more likely to choose and browse reciprocal partners during the first stage to initiate an exchange.

## Stage 2: Examining the Effect of Reciprocal Relationships on Search Outcomes

While the above analyses show that avid users utilize their reciprocal networks to search for books and initiate exchanges, it is also worthwhile to examine how this search preference affects their search outcomes. Specifically, we examine two search-related outcomes: the overall search cost to initiate an exchange, and the types of books that are discovered through the search and requested by users in the initiated exchange. We first examine the search costs associated with transacted books. The search cost to initiate an exchange is measured by the number of clicks that are involved from performing the search to sending the exchange request. The results are shown in Table 6. We find that overall the search cost for an avid user is almost two clicks less than the search cost for a non-avid user. Within each search type, however, there is no difference between avid users and non-avid users. This helps rule out the possible alternative explanation that an avid user’s search cost is lower just because she is more experienced and tech-savvy compared to a non-avid user. Our results show that keyword searches and wish list checks tend to have higher search costs compared to browsing a reciprocal partner. A further analysis of the weblog data indicates that this is due to higher cost associated with going through multiple choices in the search results to choose the book owner. Overall, the results indicate that browsing reciprocal networks helps avid users to reduce the cost of finding a book.

We then compare the types of books that are found by avid users using different search channels given that the effects of reciprocal relationships are only salient for avid users. We focus on two measures of the discovered book: ifSameGenre and ifNewTopic. As explained earlier, each individual’s book taste is measured by the distribution of books in different book genres based on her inventory list and wish list. ifSameGenre measures if the requested book is within the genre list of the focal individual. In addition to the genre information for each book, we also have the detailed topic terms that are related to each book and provided by the site. For example, the book 21<sup>st</sup> Century Advertising for New Home Builders is classified as belonging to the genre “Home & Garden” with covered topic terms such as “Financial,” “Multilevel,” “Standards,” etc. ifNewTopic measures if the requested book has a new topic term that is beyond the list of all topic terms covered in the focal individual’s inventory list and wish list.

We examine two exchange outcomes: the probability of rejecting a request and the delivery speed for an accepted request. A book requester who is eager to get a book might send a private message to the book owner, resulting in the book owner accepting the request and meanwhile sending out the book quickly. This suggests that there might be a sample selection bias if we analyze the book owner’s decision on delivery speed of accepted requests and decision on rejection of all requests separately due to unobserved factors that might affect the focal user’s decisions. To overcome this problem, we estimate a Heckman two-stage model when examining the decision on delivery speed. In the first stage, we use ifSameCountry as an instrument variable to predict how likely a request is to be rejected by the focal book owner. Whereas ifSameCountry might have an influence on rejection decision as a domestic request is less likely to be rejected due to lower shipping cost compared to an overseas request, it should not affect how soon the focal book owner mails the book once the request is accepted. Therefore, ifSameCountry serves as a good instrument variable in the first stage. In the second stage, the inverse mill’s ratio Lamda calculated from the first stage probit estimation is added in the model specification for delivery speed.

The descriptive statistics of all variables used in the analyses and the correlation matrix of the variables are provided in Table B4 and Table B5 in Appendix B. The maximum VIFs in estimations for the two dependent variables are 9.05 and 8.94 respectively, below the threshold of 10, indicating that multicollinearity among the independent variables is not a concern.

In terms of other control variables, the coefficient of Rejected is significant and positive. In addition, the more books the focal user is to send to the partner, the less likely that the focal user will reject the current request from the same partner, as suggested by the negative and significant coefficient of pendingToPartner. The significant and negative coefficient of pendingToPartner suggests that an individual will delay shipping the book if she has fewer other books to send to the same partner. This is probably due to the individual’s need to collate multiple requests from the partner to save on shipping costs. In addition, the coefficient of numPending is significant and negative. This provides evidence that individuals are inclined to make shipments only after they have accumulated a sufficient number of books to send out to economize on transaction costs.

Also, very interestingly, the decisions relating to accepting a request and sending out the book are not dependent on the reputation scores of the partner. This suggests that the role of traditional reputation signals is limited: the value of good reputation is only manifested in helping individuals clear out inventories rather than helping them fulfill wish lists. Overall our results consistently suggest that the value of reciprocal relationships also manifests in improved exchange outcomes in addition to facilitation of new exchanges. ## Additional Robustness Checks

In addition to the empirical analyses reported above, we also conduct several robustness checks that corroborate our major findings.

## Analyses Using Coarsened Exact Matching

To validate the robustness of our findings, we also replicate the major analyses using the sample of avid users and nonavid users based on coarsened exact matching (CEM). CEM is a nonparametric approach that does not require an estimation model of propensity scores. It has been used extensively in recent work to mitigate endogeneity concerns (Furman et al. 2012; Ho et al. 2007). As shown in Table D1 in Appendix D, we perform CEM based on the same covariates used in the propensity score matching. We employ the k-to-k matching so that there are equal numbers of treated and control units within each strata. This help removes the need to weight each observation according to the size of its strata for econometric estimations (Iacus et al. 2012). The CEM matching yields 4,790 non-avid users and 4,790 matched avid users. We then replicate our major analyses using this new sample.

We obtain qualitatively consistent results. For example, the estimation results in Tables D2 and D3 in Appendix D show that in comparison to non-avid users who do not utilize reciprocal relationships, avid users are more likely to choose a reciprocal partner as the book giver despite the presence of other choices. Also, avid users deliver higher service quality (i.e., lower rejection rate and faster delivery speed) for the request from a reciprocal partner, while non-avid users do not.

## Ruling out Confounding Explanations

One potential concern that may hurt the validity of our results is that interacting with reciprocal partners may lead a user to become more avid. To mitigate this concern, we examine whether a user changes her volume of exchange requests after gaining at least one new reciprocal partner using two event windows. We first define January and February 2011 as the event window during which users may gain new reciprocal partners. We compare 1,023 users (936 avid users and 87 non-avid users) who gained new reciprocal partners during this event window with 10,285 users (4,718 avid users and 5,567 non-avid users) who did not gain any additional reciprocal partner within the same period on how they change the number of requests from a two-month period prior to the event (i.e., November and December 2010) to a two-month period after the event (i.e., March and April 2011). The average number of new reciprocal partners gained by the 1,023 users during January and February 2011 is 3.77, with a maximum of 46 and a standard deviation of 8.16. The second shorter event window is defined as December 2010. We compare 770 users (724 avid users and 46 non-avid users) with new reciprocal partners in December 2010 with 10506 users (4930 avid users and 5608 non-avid users) who did not gain new reciprocal partners in December 2010 on their changes in the number of book requests from a one-month period prior to the event (i.e., November 2010) to a onemonth period after the event (i.e., January 2011). The average number of new reciprocal partners gained by the 770 users in December 2010 is 2.69, with a maximum of 24 and a standard deviation of 4.10.

The results are reported in Table 11. Regardless of which event window is used, we find that neither users with new reciprocal partners nor users without new reciprocal partners significantly change the number of exchange requests. We also replicate the analysis separately for avid users and for non-avid users. We obtain a consistent result that there is no significant change in the number of book requests for either avid or non-avid users upon gaining one or more reciprocal partners. This helps rule out the confounding mechanism that having reciprocal partners could cause a user to become avid.

In the survey, we also measure an individual’s two other psychometric views that might also affect how she values reciprocal relationships: one is her altruistic orientation, as an individual who is more altruistic toward others might not care about reciprocal relationships; the other is her disposition to trust, as an individual who is inclined to trust other partners might not have incentives to build reciprocal relationships to reduce her search cost. ## Theoretical Contributions

This study makes important contributions to the literature on online markets. Whereas prior studies have examined different mechanisms such as reputation systems and assurance services to facilitate transactions among market participants in the presence of information asymmetry (e.g., Dellarocas 2005; Dimoka et al. 2012; Pavlou and Gefen 2004), they focus on contexts wherein the transactions between market participants are monetary and mostly one-shot. We advance this stream of research by examining online P2P barter markets wherein transactions are nonmonetary and repeated interactions are frequent. Like traditional online marketplaces, online P2P barter markets also suffer from the issue of adverse selection: an individual might shirk from her responsibility to help others after she has received a good from others. We show that in the absence of enforceable contracts to govern nonmonetary transactions, reciprocal relationships developed through repeated interactions in the past help mitigate the “lemons” problem (Akerlof 1970). In contrast to prior studies that advocate the effectiveness of reputation systems in traditional online markets, our finding that the transaction partner’s reputation has a negligible impact on exchange outcomes suggests that the value of reputation mechanisms may be limited in P2P barter markets. Instead, mechanisms such as reciprocity that are based on the repeated interactions between market participants might play a major role in enabling transactions. Our results also indicate that it is worthwhile to examine other possible facilitating mechanisms in online markets that could foster reciprocity and encourage repeated interactions among market participants such as social networks.

Our study also contributes to the literature on reciprocity. Whereas several prior studies have shown that reciprocal relationships help facilitate cooperation among strangers in lab experiments (e.g., Berg et al. 1995; Buchan et al. 2002), there is limited understanding about how reciprocity might affect transactional decisions in real markets. Using real transactional data, our study provides empirical evidence that reciprocity plays an important role in facilitating transactions. We also uncover the mechanisms through which reciprocity enables transactions and improves market efficiencies. We show that reciprocity enables new transactions by helping reduce the search costs for a market participant, while still enabling her to diversify and find books in new topics. We also show that reciprocity helps improve the fulfillment outcomes in P2P barter markets once a transaction has been initiated.

This study also contributes to the nascent literature on the sharing economy. A few studies have examined the macrolevel social impacts of P2P sharing services. For example, Zervas et al. (2015) examine the impact of Airbnb on the hotel industry and find that a 1% increase in Airbnb listings could lead to a 0.05% decrease in quarterly hotel revenues. Lin et al. (2013) find that the actions of a borrower’s social network can serve as a credible signal of creditworthiness in an online crowdfunding market. While these studies focus on P2P sharing platforms wherein participants typically take a relatively fixed role of the two-sided platform (e.g., host versus guest, borrower versus lender) to engage in monetarybased transactions, we focus on a P2P sharing platform wherein participants often take both roles and switch between them frequently to engage in nonmonetary exchanges. A few other studies have examined what motivates individuals to participate in P2P sharing services. For example, Hamari et al. (2016) find that enjoyment of sharing and economic gains incentivize individuals to engage in P2P sharing. Our study makes contributions to this emerging stream of research by studying the dynamics of micro-level transactions in P2P sharing services. Our study provides important insights on how the transactions are initiated and consummated on an online sharing platform.

## Implications for Online Market Design

Our study provides several implications for the design of P2P barter markets. The sustainability of a P2P barter market depends on its ability to enable participants to find the right transaction partners efficiently. Our findings suggest practical guidelines for a P2P barter market to facilitate effective search and help consummate transactions.

The first is the design of appropriate and effective search mechanisms. Our study finds that despite the existence of a keyword-based search engine for participants to find books in which they are interested, avid users prefer browsing their reciprocal networks to find books. This indicates that it might be worthwhile for the market designer to implement an additional participant-based search mechanism that allows participants to search over the inventory of a specific individual or a defined group of individuals. Our finding suggests that avid users utilize both reciprocal partners and partners of reciprocal partners to find new books and initiate new exchanges. However, currently a participant needs to manually navigate through the transaction history pages of a reciprocal partner to discover the second degree partners. To make such search more cost effective, it will be useful to illustrate an individual’s transaction network through network visualization techniques and display such information to help each user with the participant-based search.

Our findings also highlight the need for such markets to display information that is useful and informative for users in making decisions. Our study indicates that one such piece of information is the reciprocal relationships among participants. We show that signals related to reciprocity are used by participants to choose an exchange partner from available choices. Therefore, it will benefit the market participants if they are able to track and recognize their reciprocal partners on various pages of the P2P barter market during the search process, including the member profile page, and the search results page, among others. Our results also show that participants take reciprocal relationships into consideration when making decisions about incoming exchange requests. Therefore, the market designer should consider computing measures related to reciprocity (e.g. whether the requester is a reciprocal partner, the taste similarity between the two parties, etc.) for each exchange request and display them to the focal user. Such a strategy on providing reciprocity-based signals in addition to the traditional reputation-based signals would greatly help improve the market efficiency of a P2P barter market. In addition, both avid users and non-avid users use reputation systems to assess the credibility of trading partners in P2P barter markets. Therefore, it might be useful to allow market participants to customize the information relating to transaction partners that are made visible in these markets.

Our findings also highlight the need to design good recommendation systems that help reduce market participants search costs. Currently, most online barter markets have traditional recommendation systems that recommend books in which a user might be interested. However, we find that an avid user extensively explores her reciprocal partners to find new books. This suggests that a recommendation system which intelligently recommends potential transaction partners will benefit market participants. As reported in Appendix C, we observe high book taste similarity between reciprocal partners. This result is consistent with the finding from prior studies on homophily that individuals with similar characteristics are more likely to form ties with each other (Gu et al. 2014; McPherson et al. 2001). Given the observed homophily effect in online social networks, researchers have called for the design of homophily-based recommendation systems for friendship suggestions (Carullo et al. 2015). Our result echoes this call by suggesting that a homophily-based recommendation system that takes into account the taste similarity between two market participants would be beneficial for online barter markets. In addition, our results indicate that the algorithm of the recommendation system should also evaluate the depth of inventory in all shared genres between two individuals.

Our findings also highlight the need to leverage policies and other features of the site to facilitate reciprocity. For example, policies that discourage anonymity and encourage face-to-face communications within granular geographic areas could be beneficial to online P2P barter markets. It would be beneficial for the market designer to display more personal and verified information about each user to help foster trust among market participants. For example, a P2P barter market could integrate with third-party social networking services such as Facebook so that each user’s Facebook information might serve as an additional proxy for her credibility, which will complement with the transaction-based signals in the market.

Finally, the success of a P2P barter market is also dependent on its ability to ensure market liquidity and the free flow of goods among all the market participants. In this study, we find that an avid user gradually relies increasingly on her reciprocal network for transactions. Whereas this strategy improves the exchange outcomes without damaging the diversity of her search, it might hinder the flow of books to outside communities. The market designer might need to carefully evaluate the long-term impacts of reciprocity on market efficiency and liquidity and decide whether it is beneficial to selectively emphasize other available sources outside a user’s reciprocal network.

In addition to the above implications specifically for the design of P2P barter markets, which are often nonmonetary, our study also offers insights for the design of other monetary-based decentralized markets. Consistent with an earlier study (Zvilichovsky et al. 2013), which shows that the success rate of a crowdfunding project is positively influenced by the reciprocal relationships between the project initiator and project backers, our findings suggest that the relationship between two participants built through past interactions helps facilitate transactions. Computing and highlighting such information, therefore, should be helpful for market participants when they are making decisions about monetary transactions in online markets.

## Limitations and Conclusion

This study is one of the very first attempts to examine P2P barter markets, an emergent trading model with growing popularity in practice. Our findings shed light on the beneficial role of reciprocity in online markets and provide useful guidelines for the design of P2P barter markets and other decentralized markets wherein frequent interactions between market participants are not rare. Even though this study focuses on one specific, indirect barter market for book exchanges, the major findings should be generalizable to other indirect barter markets focusing on other goods or services. However, since the context of the study is an indirect barter market wherein the establishment of a reciprocal relationship requires at least two transactions, the findings may not apply to direct barter markets wherein the same definition of reciprocity is embedded in a single transaction. The study can also be extended in several interesting ways. First, due to data constraints, we are not able to examine in-depth how a reciprocal relationship between two individuals is developed from scratch (e.g., who reaches out to who first) and how it evolves over time. Future research on the evolutionary patterns of reciprocal relationships could help better understand the formation and maintenance of reciprocal relationships in the markets. Second, the P2P barter market for books in our research context provides an alternative channel for individuals to get books. Relevant analyses could also be conducted to examine how an individual chooses between P2P barter markets and traditional online markets such as Amazon.com and how the decision might be related to the strength of her reciprocal network. Future studies could also analyze the economic impact of P2P barter markets on traditional moneybased markets.

## Propensity Score Matching

For each user, the propensity score of being an avid user (i.e., the treatment condition) is predicted using a logit regression on several key covariates, including whether the user provides a bio, whether the user provides a photo, how long the user has been in this market, the user’s last login time, the user’s country dummies, and the user’s percentages of each book genre in her inventory list and wish list. Because the bias in the estimated treatment effect tends to increase when increasing the number of untreated subjects matched to each treated subject in propensity score matching (Austin 2010), we choose one-to-one matching and identify a matching non-avid user in the control group for each avid user in the treatment group using nearest neighbor matching on the propensity score. The distribution of propensity scores in both the treated group and the untreated group is shown in Figure A1. We observe a significant overlap of propensity scores between the treated group and the untreated group, validating the common support assumption (Ho et al. 2007). Therefore, treatment observations with propensity scores higher than the maximum or less than the minimum score of the controls are dropped by enforcing common support condition.

All the Cronbach’s alpha values are above the recommended threshold of 0.70, suggesting good reliability for all construct scales (Fornell and Larker 1987). One way to evaluate the convergent and discriminant validity of each construct is to examine the factor loadings of each indicator. Each indicator should have higher loadings on the construct of interest than on any other construct (Chin 1998). Table E2 shows the factors loadings and cross-loadings for all the constructs. An inspection of this table suggests that the measurement model of all constructs provides adequate discriminant and convergent validity.
