# INFORMATION SPILLOVER AND SEMI-COLLABORATIVE NETWORKS IN INSURER FRAUD DETECTION<sup>1</sup>

Nirup M. Menon School of Business, George Mason University, Fairfax, VA 22030 U.S.A. {nmenon@gmu.edu}

Information spillovers are benefits that a party obtains from the IT efforts of another party. Because these benefits arise from data and information sharing, they are best studied at a process level. Medical claims fraud detection is a prototypical data- and information-intensive process in insurance companies. Fraud detection efforts of one insurer can create spillover benefits through data and information sharing that occur from socialization between analysts and labor mobility between insurers. This paper theorizes three semi collaborative networks formed between state-level subsidiaries of insurers (regulation-bound network), between subsidiaries of an insurer parent company (sibling network), and between insurers and hospitals (risk-sharing), and hypothesizes that these networks convey information spillovers. Because benefits realized by another party can lead to the reduction of IT investments by that party, the paper also examines the impact of semicollaborative networks on future IT-related investments. The empirical analysis was conducted using 2011– 2013 data. A generalized linear model with a Tweedie distribution is used to correct for the finite mass of zeros for the dependent variables. The results reveal that the sibling network conveyed most of the spillover benefit, and the risk-sharing network did not contribute to fraud detection. The sibling network is also found to depres future spending on fraud detection

Keywords: Information spillover, medical claims fraud, fraud detection, semi-collaborative network, social embeddedness theory, hospital IT, generalized linear model, Tweedie distribution

## Introduction

The health care industry in the United States and several countries follows a third-party payer system for covering individuals’ health care expenses. Under this system, an individual contracts with an insurance company, the third party, to cover a portion of expenses in exchange for paying insurance premiums (Weisbrod 1991). Health care providers (or patients) submit bills to insurers for services provided (or received). Insurers in turn coordinate with health care providers and other insurers to reimburse bills—medical claims, in industry parlance—in accordance with contractual obligations between insurers and patients and between insurers and health care providers. Insurers are not able to examine patients or observe patient care, which leads to the possibility that health care providers or patients could submit fraudulent claims to insurers for reimbursement (Blomqvist 1991). Fraudulent claims can include billing for services or drugs not provided to a patient, billing individually for services that should have been provided as part of a bundle warranting a single and lower payment, upcoding marginal patients to having had a related but more complex illness or treatment to obtain higher reimbursement, duplicate testing, and unnecessary testing (Becker et al. 2005; Silverman and Skinner 2004; Sparrow 2000).

Fraud in medical claims is socially wasteful and is estimated to result in avoidable costs of more than \$250 billion across the U.S. health system (Berwick and Hackbarth 2012; Hyman 2001). Insurers invest heavily in data analytics automated by software and hardware and in fraud analysts to detect fraud, but little is known about the dynamics of these investments.

First, the payoff from private investments in fraud analytics has not been studied in academic literature. Second, although fraud detection consists of manual and automated data mining and analytics, the number, type, and amount of fraudulent claims observed by any one insurer is small. The imbalance in the number and type of non-fraudulent and fraudulent claims makes finding fraud through data analytics and data mining challenging (He and Garcia 2009). Thus, insurers must combat diminishing returns from their own efforts through industry networks that convey examples of fraudulent claims, and share data and knowledge about fraud patterns across organizational boundaries. Understanding the dynamics between private investments of insurers, information spillovers via industry networks, and fraud outcomes is valuable to regulators, industry members, and researchers.

Information spillovers, which the research and development (R&D) literature defines as knowledge leakage between firms, result from sharing data and knowledge results across organizational boundaries (Spence 1984). Fraud analysts in the insurance industry meet with each other to share data and information about claims and fraud and to share their findings on fraud detection. Their meetings may be electronic, face-toface, one-to-one, or many-to-many such as workshops and conferences. Information spillovers arise from informal ties that bind firms, which are ties that analysts and other employees working for these firms create that result in data and knowledge networks within and across organizations (Burt 1992; Owen-Smith and Powell 2004). Informal ties are the social relationships that employees across insurers develop from attending common workshops and conferences.

Information spillovers also arise from labor mobility, such as when an analyst moves from one insurer to another (Balsvik 2011; Tambe and Hitt 2014a). The transmission of knowledge across firm boundaries when one employee switches jobs is an example of information spillover (Owen-Smith and Powell 2004). The use of standard representations of medical and claims data, which reduces the cost of sharing data electronically, further strengthens industry networks. For example, the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) standardizes the coding of patient visits and diagnoses across providers and insurers, thus facilitating seamless and inexpensive data transfers in the health care industry.

To conceptualize knowledge sharing and leakage through social ties among organizations, this paper advances the concept of the semi-collaborative network.<sup>2</sup> A semi-collaborative network of firms results from the social ties between employees working in firms that engage in competition in some areas, cooperation in other areas, and supply chain partnerships with each other. In essence, the nodes of the network consist of firms, and the ties between employees working at these firms create the links between the firms. According to the social embeddedness theory, the social networks in which a firm is embedded affect the conduct and outcome of economic transactions in which the firm engages (Granovetter 1985; Uzzi 1999).

When applied to fraud detection in the insurance industry, the social embeddedness theory predicts that analyst-level mechanisms such as labor mobility and socialization cause insurerlevel information spillover. However, not all social networks in which a firm is embedded are alike, nor are their effects on the conduct and outcome of economic transactions similar. The difference in social networks stems from the differences in competition, cooperation, and supply chain partnerships between insurers and health care providers. Accordingly, three distinct semi-collaborative networks—regulation-bound network, sibling network, and risk-sharing network—are proposed here as examples corresponding to firms engaged in competition, firms cooperating, and the supply-chain network.

Of the three semi-collaborative networks, the regulationbound network is an example of a set of firms competing with each other in a market. Insurers operate independent subsidiaries across the various states in the United States. For example, Blue Cross Blue Shield is a corporation comprised of multiple independent companies. Subsidiaries of different parent companies in a state compete with each other for customers, but have an incentive to cooperate on fraud detection. The sibling network is an example of a set of firms cooperating with each other. Subsidiaries of a parent insurance company do not compete with each other, because they serve different markets, but they have an incentive to cooperate with each other on fraud detection. The risk-sharing network is an example of the supply-chain network of an insurer in the context of healthcare. Each insurer subsidiary transacts with hospitals in a state to process claims so that the network in which the subsidiary is embedded includes hospitals in the state. Because hospitals can be involved in over-billing and duplicate testing, insurers view all claims with a degree of mistrust. Cooperation between a subsidiary and its hospital network on fraud detection will differ from the other two networks.

Three of the four hypotheses proposed in this paper encapsulate the logic for the impact of each of three semicollaborative networks on fraud recovery outcomes. A fourth hypothesis tests whether firms reduce their own effort and free-ride on these network resources. A firm that obtains information spillover benefits from interconnected firms may not need to invest as much in fraud detection analytics, as it would absent spillover benefits. Thus, there is a substitution effect, also called an incentive effect in the R&D context, of information spillover benefits on fraud recovery investments. In the R&D context, the incentive effect reflects lower R&D effort due to firms’ inability to extract rents from R&D when competitors imitate their innovations (Spence 1984).

In the context of fraud analytics, there are no competitive rents to extract from fraud detection because fraud recovery is not a competitive activity. Nonetheless, free-riding is possible, so that a firm receiving information spillover will lower its effort on fraud detection. An alternate possibility is that insurers do not free-ride, but that each subsidiary invests in complementary data analytics capabilities to exploit the spillovers from the three networks. In such a case, a firm receiving information spillover will increase its effort on fraud detection. Prior information technology (IT) spillover literature does not include the presence or absence of freeriding as a topic examined.

Empirical results from fine-grained financial data on insurance subsidiaries and hospitals from 2011 to 2013 revealed that the regulation-bound and the sibling semi-collaborative networks improve fraud detection outcomes for an insurance subsidiary. There was no benefit from the risk-sharing semicollaborative network. The payoff for fraud outcomes from the sibling network proved to be higher in magnitude than that from the regulation-bound network and from private investments. Results also revealed that there is a free-riding effect from the sibling semi-collaborative network, in that an insurance subsidiary appears to reduce spending on fraud detection in response to the spending by other insurance subsidiaries of the parent company.

## Theoretical Background and Prior Research

Although IT has been shown to contribute to both firm-level and process-level outcomes, it may not contribute to competitive advantage when competing firms adopt the same IT, as the advantages of IT are competed away (Clemons and Row 1993; Hitt and Brynjolfsson 1996). Competing firms undertake the quality improvements of their products and services using IT, but are unable to extract rents by raising prices from customers due to competition. Thus, firms investing in IT are unable to extract benefits, whereas their customers benefit through low prices or high quality. This phenomenon is a type of spillover called rent spillover.

An examination of industry-level data and input–output tables to identify supplier–customer relationships among industries indicated that significant rent spillover exists from IT investments of supplier industries to downstream industries (Cheng and Nault 2007). Industries also benefited from the IT investments of downstream industries (Cheng and Nault 2012). Not all downstream industries enjoy spillover benefits uniformly; downstream industries that are IT intensive and competitive extract the most out of the spillover benefits that upstream industries provide (Han et al. 2011).

Knowledge spillover is a term used for the transmission mechanism for spillover effects from the IT investments of one national economy to another (Park et al. 2007). A study on the knowledge spillover that occurs when IT products are traded across national boundaries revealed that IT products imported from IT-intensive economies tended to boost the productivity of the importing nation (Park et al. 2007). Recipient characteristics such as the development status of a nation and Internet penetration further determined whether spillover benefits surface (Gholami et al. 2009). Just as researchers have studied IT productivity at various levels, such as national, sectoral, firm-level, and process-level, IT spillover research encompasses firm-level studies in addition to the above-mentioned national and sectoral studies. Firmlevel evidence of IT spillovers exists, and Chang and Gurbaxani (2012) confirmed the industry-level finding with a firm-level analysis that indicated high-IT-intensive firms benefited more from spillover from firms in the IT-services industry than did low-IT-intensive firms.

The epistemological root of IT spillover is the research on information spillover in the economics of R&D (Bloom et al. 2013; Griliches 1991). In addition to understanding and measuring spillover effects from university R&D to private firms, the economics of R&D research is also interested in the impact of the R&D of one private firm on other private firms. The focus of this body of work has been on mechanisms and measurements for spillover based on patent citations, proximity to labor availability, and labor mobility to theorize and measure knowledge transfer from one firm’s R&D to another in the high-technology industry. Recent research indicated that informal mechanisms contribute to R&D spillovers, such as obtaining information directly from managers in other firms, indirectly from consultant firms, at conferences and workshops, and from published or online materials, vendors, and customers (Knott et al. 2009). Mechanisms such as common data formats that are relevant in the data analytics and fraud detection context were not addressed in this literature.

Information spillover is a term used for informational benefits that a firm obtains from the IT efforts of another firm.<sup>3</sup> Such benefits are possible when one company invests in IT, and its data-processing and information-sharing capabilities benefit other companies. For example, in the supply chain context, a customer’s IT could benefit its suppliers by lowering demand uncertainty when the customer shares its data and information with suppliers (Han et al. 2011; Subramani 2004). Researchers are seeking to understand the transmission paths of information spillover, such as data and information sharing, and to measure these spillovers to have a better understanding of the nuances of how IT contributes to business value and what factors influence IT investments (Chang and Gurbaxani 2012; Cheng and Nault 2007, 2012; Tambe and Hitt 2014b).

The above literature review revealed a few gaps in the understanding of the phenomenon, summarized in Table 1. First, when looking for evidence of data and information spillovers, it is appropriate to look at process-level or nonmonetary firmlevel outcomes, so that the results are not confounded with rent spillover. Firm- and industry-level productivity and profitability measures confound rent spillover with information spillover. The context in this paper is medical claims fraud detection conducted by insurers, which is data intensive, and spillover in this context is data and information related. Second, prior work has not conceptualized the different networks in which a firm might be embedded when deriving spillover benefits. Three types of semi-collaborative networks with differential information spillovers are identified in this paper.

Third, prior IS research has not looked at the impact of information spillover on future IT investments. Although abovenormal benefits from IT may be due to spillover effects not internalized by firms (Tambe and Hitt 2014a), rational managers would account for externalities when making resource allocation decisions. Free-riding behavior and benefits have been observed in open innovation alliances, where firms that do not join a consortium of innovating firms benefit from knowledge leakage nonetheless (Han et al. 2012). In contrast, firms might need to make complementary IT investments to exploit the data and information transmitted from other firms. In light of these contrasting views of the phenomenon, a model of the impact of potential information spillover on private investments on fraud detection is worth testing, as is done in this paper.

## Networks and Knowledge/ Information Spillovers

The credit for originating the concept of knowledge spillovers goes to Marshall (1920; see also Adams and Jaffe 1996). Management and strategy researchers interested in knowledge spillovers beyond the R&D context conceptualize knowledge spillovers as information flows occurring in a network of firms (Uzzi and Gillespie 2002). A central proposition of this economic sociology of networks is that formal and informal ties that generate spillover benefits result in a network of firm actors (Burt 1992, Owen-Smith and Powell 2004). Formal ties are strategic alliances and joint ventures, whereas informal ties include geographical proximity that leads to social relationships among individuals within these corporate actors and to labor mobility among them. For example, firms that participated in technology partnerships saw an increase in firm-level innovation due to informal and formal ties between a firm and its partners (Hagedoorn and Schakenraad 1994). This notion of networks crystallized from social embeddedness theory, which postulates that a firm’s actions depend on the interfirm relations (i.e., networks) in which it is embedded (Granovetter 1985; Uzzi 1999). Such networks also reflect the existence of informal communities of practice, which denotes the coming together of individuals working on similar problems or similar technologies in less formal circumstances (Brown and Duguid 1991).

This literature is important because informal ties are necessary for fraud analytics, such as to share examples of the variety of fraudulent claims observed and the patterns of fraud recovered. This study extends the economic sociology of networks literature by applying it to the context of fraud detection. Spillover benefits are obtained when insurance subsidiaries collaborate with each other informally and formal collaborations have not materialized. The study also extends the literature by proposing that each subsidiary is embedded in three types of semi-collaborative networks—regulationbound, sibling, and risk-sharing based—leading to different outcomes from its fraud detection efforts.

## Hypotheses

Information spillovers within the insurance industry occur when fraud investigators of one insurance subsidiary communicate with investigators of other insurers on medical claims flagged for review. Formal ties (e.g., contractual agreements between insurers to share fraud data) are not common in data analytics, but are common in strategic actions such as strategic alliances and joint ventures (Gulati et al. 2000). Informal communities of practice develop among fraud analysts to help each other address the challenges with data analytics of fraud detection. Because of these mechanisms, an insurer subsidiary is socially embedded in semicollaborative networks consisting of other corporate actors.

Insurers have long recognized the value of sharing information (Konsynski and McFarlan 1989). Pooled insurance claim data are used to assess risk for underwriting and actuarial purposes, such as the CLUE database maintained by LexisNexis. The dearth of examples of fraudulent claims available from any one subsidiary is another reason for sharing data on claims. Further, some types of fraud, such as upcoding and unnecessary tests, are difficult to model and detect, because of the need to coordinate data from different providers and insurers, and because such claims fall in a gray area. Should a health care provider eventually face charges of fraud, all parties who were victims of this provider would benefit, so insurers have an incentive to share information. Thus, fraud investigators share, either publicly in workshops or privately in conversations with other insurers, patterns in billing that indicate the possibility of different types of fraud.

Standardization of data and information across insurers and health care providers makes it less costly to share and pool data and fraud recovery results. The increasing use of data mining for patterns helps to detect anomalies and fraud, which has created a demand for more data on patients, providers, treatments, and claims. Although there are reasons that insurers must share fraud data at a national level, as seen from efforts such as those of the National Insurance Crime Bureau and National Healthcare Anti-Fraud Association to improve information sharing, insurers participate in other semicollaborative networks to improve fraud detection relevant to them. As opposed to prior work on IT spillovers that operationalize spillover over the broad national economy, such as the sum of the IT expenses of all other firms in the same industry in the sample (Tambe and Hitt 2014b), and based on input–output tables between industries (Cheng and Nault 2012), the focus here is on three smaller networks: regulation-bound, sibling, and risk-sharing.

## Regulation-Bound Semi-Collaborative Network

The McCarran-Ferguson Act of 1945 protects insurers in the United States from interstate competition by granting states the right to regulate health plans within their borders (Weller 1978). Insurers that operate in multiple states do so by setting up an independent company (i.e., subsidiary) in each state in which they operate. The health care industry is circumscribed by state boundaries due to regulatory differences across state lines. The licensure for physicians and pharmacists, who are the likely perpetrators of fraud, follows state-level jurisdiction (Federation of State Medical Boards 2015). Owing to the state-level coverage of insurance companies, referrals of patient care are made within state boundaries. A health care provider or a patient is more likely to present false claims to multiple insurers in one state and is less likely to travel between states to perpetrate fraud in a given time frame (Grace and Phillips 2007). Patients typically see medical providers in a limited geographical area, in close proximity to their location (McGuirk and Porrell 1984).

Subsidiaries of various insurers in a state are thus regulation bound, and the network that a subsidiary belongs to is a regulation-bound network. Insurance subsidiaries in a state compete with each other for premium revenues, but cooperate with each other on fraud detection. In a semi-collaborative network, employees share information through trade conferences, news articles, employee movement, and so forth (Tambe and Hitt 2014a). The role of geography and colocation is significant in R&D spillover (Black and Henderson 1999; Ellison and Glaeser 1997) due to physical proximity for socialization (Owen-Smith and Powell 2004).

When investigating a possible fraud scenario, a subsidiary requests information from other companies that may have interacted with the same health care provider, patient, and so forth. Sharing knowledge between insurers within a state is also facilitated by the state insurance fraud bureau, which collects information on fraud activities from and disseminates the information to other insurers in the state. The state insurance fraud bureau thus plays the role of a hub in the network of subsidiaries. Because the size of the regulation-bound semi-collaborative network and available resources from this network will determine the fraud detection benefits that a subsidiary will enjoy, empirically testing the following hypothesis is proposed:

H1: The regulation-bound network relative to an insurance subsidiary is positively associated with fraud recovered by the subsidiary.

## Sibling Semi-Collaborative Network

Data and information spillovers occur between subsidiaries of an insurer, even though they may operate in different states. A subsidiary benefits other subsidiaries of a firm by sharing, both formally and informally, data and information related to claims and fraud. This is the sibling network, and the size of the sibling network and the available resources from this network benefit a subsidiary of a firm. The formation of a sibling network can enhance know-how complementarities and reduce technological distance between siblings due to the social relationships and communications between employees. Know-how complementarity, which refers to the development of knowledge and skills in one business division to exploit the knowledge and skills in another division, was found to improve the effectiveness of R&D spillover (Helfat 1997). Technological distance refers to the differences in technologies across two firms, and low technological distance has been found to be useful in exploiting information spillovers (Adams and Jaffe 1996).

The impact of the sibling network on fraud recovery will be different from that of the regulation-bound network, because social relationships between employees of subsidiaries of a firm are different from social relationships between employees of different firms. Subsidiaries of a firm do not compete with each other, as they operate in different markets or states. There is no fear that data sharing will lead to a loss of market share, and there are likely no organizational policies creating friction for data sharing within a firm.

A subsidiary of an insurer in one state can use the knowledge that other subsidiaries of the insurer in other states have gained on billing patterns and fraud detection. This knowledge includes patterns of abuse discovered by another subsidiary through data analytics. Such knowledge is typically generated from data and IT and is transmitted within the organization via IT, e-mails, memos, training, and conversations between employees. In the few fraud cases that span multiple states, such as an unscrupulous medical practitioner relocating to another state and subsidiaries in the two states having fraudulent claims from this practitioner, pooling data can help. Because the size and resources of the sibling network will benefit a subsidiary, empirically testing the following hypothesis is proposed:

H2: The sibling network relative to an insurance subsidiary is positively associated with fraud recovered by the subsidiary.

## Risk-Sharing Semi-Collaborative Network

Insurance subsidiaries are embedded in a risk-sharing supply chain network with health care providers, pharmacies, and patients. They pool financial liabilities across patients and negotiate service prices with health care providers and pharmacies. The participation of insurers in this risk-sharing network and the resources generated by the network lead to spillovers for fraud detection. The semi-collaborative network formed by risk sharing differs from the regulation-bound and the sibling networks, because insurers understand that unscrupulous actors of fraud come from the population of health care providers and pharmacies. Health care providers and pharmacies are not interested in fraud detection, as it does not benefit them. Hence, relationships between these companies may be more formal and less social than between subsidiaries of insurers.

When health care providers and pharmacies present claims electronically to insurers, insurers are able to check the incoming data for fraud in a shorter amount of time than the time it used to take with paper-based processes. The level of automation of upstream partners benefits downstream companies (Han et al. 2011; Subramani 2004). For example, order data sent electronically by suppliers are more structured than paper records and are likely to have fewer errors. Knowledge about innovations in processes and practices at one organization transfer to supply chain partners through repeated interactions (Han et al. 2011). Because the size and resources of the risk-sharing network will benefit an insurance subsidiary, the following hypothesis is empirically tested:

H3: The risk-sharing network relative to an insurance subsidiary is positively associated with fraud recovered by the subsidiary.

## Semi-Collaborative Networks and Free-Riding

In a semi-collaborative network, managers become aware of how much IT others in the insurance industry and health care industry are adopting through trade conferences, news articles, employee movement, and so forth. From a rational economic perspective, when managers anticipate receiving spillover benefits, their incentive to invest in their own infrastructure at the margin is reduced (Katz and Shapiro 1994). A firm enjoying spillover benefits does not need to invest in as much IT as firms not enjoying such spillovers. Managers could delay, or even reduce, fraud detection investments in areas covered by another firm’s application software or data resources. Otherwise, they would be overinvesting in IT for a given level of total expected benefits from fraud detection efforts. As a result, social returns from IT could lead to lower private IT investments.

All three networks are available to any firm in the insurance industry to exploit. The use of a network by one firm does not reduce the availability for other firms, so it is nonrivalrous. The availability and non-rivalrous nature of these pools make them akin to public goods financed by the insurers, hospitals, and ambulatory care providers that make up the network of interacting firms. Research on public goods bears evidence that individual parties lack incentives to increase private investments when they are enjoying social benefits of a public good (Bergstrom et al. 1986).

These arguments suggest that information spillover generates a substitution effect on a subsidiary’s fraud detection efforts. A counterargument against the substitution effect is that a firm that does not make complementary IT investments to exploit the data and information that flows from other firms is not able to exploit the data and information to the fullest extent. A firm must make a deliberate effort to generate know-how complementarity (Helfat 1997). Indeed, IT spillover benefits increase over time because of the complementary investments made over time (Han et al. 2011). In this study context, although fraud and claims data are shared with an insurer, unless it invests in data analytics technologies, processes, and people, the insurer may not benefit from shared data. Thus, a firm seeks to enhance its fraud detection investments in response to the fraud detection investments made by other insurers. Empirically testing the following substitution effect hypothesis is proposed:

H4: The availability of semi-collaborative networks relative to an insurance subsidiary is negatively associated with fraud detection expenses by the subsidiary.

## Data

Two data sets for 2011–2013 were obtained. The first, the Medical Loss Ratio data set, was downloaded from the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) website. The Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act requires insurance companies to report to the CMS on an annual basis the amounts they spend on patient care, salaries, health IT, administration, and marketing (Harrington 2010). The Center for Consumer Information and Insurance Oversight of the CMS uses these detailed financial data to determine whether insurers are adhering to the 80/20 provision of the law to spend at least 80% of their premium revenue on patient care. Insurers that violate this rule are penalized by the Center for Consumer Information and Insurance Oversight. This data set contains fraud detection expenses, and the fraud recovered for each state-level subsidiary of insurance companies in the United States (see Table 2).

The 2011 to 2013 hospital-level IT data are from the HIMSS (formerly Dorenfest) Analytics. HIMSS collects data on applications installed at each hospital through mail-in, faceto-face, and telephonic surveys of IT managers of hospitals. The data collected at the application level indicate whether an application is live and operational at the hospital versus being implemented, planned for, not planned for, and so forth. The data have been widely used in health IT studies (e.g., Lee et al. 2011).

Live and operational applications are coded as 1 and the remaining status of applications as 0. As there were 120 applications, to reduce dimensionality, their status was summed on all applications for all hospitals in a state. Table 3 shows the average number of applications live in a hospital over the 3 years. The decrease in the average number of applications in 2012 compared to 2011 was due to the increase in the number of hospitals responding to the HIMSS data collection. Only hospitals with 3 years of data on all data sets were used, which provided 5,388 observations for analysis.

The measures for information spillovers were constructed following the definitions of the different types of semicollaborative networks. A proxy for the spillover that occurs within a regulation-bound network, denoted as RFT, is the sum of annual FT values of all subsidiaries in the state, not including the focal firm. A proxy for the spillover from the sibling network, denoted as SFT, is the sum of the FT values of all subsidiaries in an insurer, not including the focal firm. Finally, the spillover from the risk-sharing network, denoted by RSN, was constructed by summing the HIT values of all hospitals in a state to which the focal firm belongs. Proxies for information spillovers have been based on the investments made by firms other than the focal firm in a network (Chang and Gurbaxani 2012; Cheng and Nault 2007, 2012; Tambe and Hitt 2014b). That is, the proxy for a network operationalizes the socialized collaboration in that network.

## Model Development

Analysts conduct fraud detection and recovery in the organizational context so that the amount of fraud recovered will be affected by the availability of resources to investigate fraud, resources that are also spent in the pursuit of premium revenues and claims processing. Therefore, a multi-input, multi-output model is used in which revenues from premiums, claims processed, and fraud recovered by a state-level subsidiary are the three outputs of the subsidiary. Processing medical claims submitted by individuals and providers and reimbursing them is an important aspect of the business of the subsidiary. A key step in processing claims is detecting fraudulent claims and denying or appropriately reducing payments on fraudulent claims. An outcome of this process is the amount of fraud recovered. Because reimbursing and investigating claims is embedded in the business processes of the subsidiary, fraud recovered is an outcome of the subsidiary, in addition to premium revenues and claims processed.

## Fraud Recovered Model

The model for fraud recovered as the dependent variable is log-linear, which means that the model is linear after dependent and explanatory variables are transformed by the logarithm function. This model tests hypotheses H1 through H3 by incorporating the variables for the three semicollaborative networks. The derivation of equation A6 in Appendix A shows how the log-linear form is based on production economics principles. The log-linear formulation is appropriate for the insurance industry (see Fecher et al. 1993). Prior works on IT and R&D spillover effects have used a log-linear relationship as well (Chang and Gurbaxani 2012; Cheng and Nault 2007, 2012; Dranove et al. 2014; Tambe and Hitt 2014b).

In the study’s context, fraud recovered, fraud detection expenses, and the sibling network take on zero values for many observations. Unlike traditional production economics where outputs are never zero, fraud recovered could be zero, and taking log of zero leads to a missing value. To ensure observations are not lost where fraud recovered is zero, when sibling network is zero, and other variables are not zero, the log of zero was set to zero for all variables.<sup>4</sup> Equation 1 (see

Figure 1) corresponds to equation A6 in the appendix in terms of the main spillover proxy variables, fraud detection expenses, general and administrative expenses, salaries, revenue, and claims. The remaining variables in this equation are control variables added to improve the robustness of the model.

All independent variables, except the size of networks which are relatively stable, are lagged by one period. This is because the data collected are annual, and there is a threat to the validity of the estimation due to the simultaneity of managerial decisions on inputs and outputs over a year. Further, it takes time for IT investments to pay off (Devaraj and Kohli 2003). It also takes time for IT spillovers to increase due to learning effects and complementary investments (Han et al. 2011). One of the explanatory variables is $H Q _ { i , t - 1 } ,$ the health quality expenses borne by a subsidiary. According to the 80/20 rule of the Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act, insurers must spend a certain percentage of expenses on patient wellness activities (Baker 2011; Harrington 2010). These activities are complementary in a semi-collaborative network because they improve collaboration and cooperation between health care providers and insurers. Another explanatory variable is $F D P _ { i , t - 1 } ,$ the lagged dependent variable, which takes into account that past fraud recovered can be helpful for uncovering future fraud.

Some of the important explanatory variables, such as fraud expenses and sibling network, have many zeros, and dropping observations with zero values could lead to a sample selection bias, so the log of zero was replaced by zero. But the production function should reflect that these values differ from true positive values, so dummy variables $( D _ { 1 } , D _ { 2 } , D _ { 3 } ,$ and D ) were introduced in the production function, which are assigned 1 when the four explanatory variables $( F T _ { i , t - 1 } , S F T _ { i , t - 1 } , F D P _ { i , t - 1 } ,$ and $H Q _ { i , t - 1 } )$ are respectively zero. Such an adaptation of the production function leads to a consistent and unbiased estimate of the production function when some inputs are not used in some firms in the sample (Battese 1997; see Appendix B for more details).

Hence, in place of an ordinary least squares (OLS) estimation that assumes that the error terms are distributed following a standard normal distribution, generalized linear model (GLM) setups are used for equations 1 and 2, so that a nonnormal distribution may be specified for the error term (Nelder and Baker 2006). The nonnormal distribution used is a zeroadjusted compound Poisson-gamma distribution in which the error term follows a mixture of a discrete binary distribution for zero versus nonzero values and a continuous gamma distribution for the nonzero values of the dependent variable (Jørgenson and de Souza 1994). This distribution is also known as the Tweedie, compound Poisson, and compound gamma models.

The term GLM-Tweedie is used to refer to this analysis. The distribution is widely used in actuarial sciences, fisheries, climatology, ecology, survival analysis, and biostatistics where many phenomena exhibit a high probability of zero values for dependent variables (see Dunn and Smyth 2005). This approach is particularly useful in modeling insurance claims when the number of the claims is not observed and the amount or cost of claims is observed in the aggregate (Smyth and Jørgenson 2002). The data in this study allowed one only to observe aggregate fraud detected amounts and not to observe individual fraud cases or the number of fraud cases. Under an assumption that fraud recovery occurs at a Poisson rate, and that the sizes of claims are independently distributed in a gamma fashion, the aggregate fraud recovered for each firm follows the GLM-Tweedie model (Frees 2010).

It is instructive to conduct standard linear regression analysis on equations 1 and 2. The first type of analysis is the OLS estimation of the two equations separately, and the second is a seemingly unrelated regression (SUR) of the two equations as a system. SUR is superior to OLS because it internalizes the information that the error terms of the two equations could be correlated, thus leading to unbiased estimates (Greene 2012). Further, the graph in Figure 3 of $1 { \mathrm { n } F } D P _ { i t }$ versus ln $R F T _ { i t }$ shows that there is an upward trend among nonzero values for the dependent variable. The graph reveals that zero values would pull this trend downward, falsely leading to the conclusion that $\ln { F D P _ { i t } }$ is not related to $\ln { R F T _ { i t } } .$ Thus, the GLM-Tweedie is the better alternative in this study compared to the least squares models.

## Discussion

The results show distinct differences in the estimates of the spillover effects from the three semi-collaborative networks, which supports the premise that these networks are present and that their impacts vary. A comparison of the impacts of the regulation-bound network and the sibling network reveals that the latter exhibits a higher impact on fraud recovery. The support for the positive effects of the semi-collaborative networks suggests that more data and information sharing would lead to better outcomes from fraud analytics. When subsidiaries in a state invest in data standardization and fraud detection, benefits are available to all subsidiaries through the regulation-bound network. As hypothesized, facilitating employee mobility across firms, and conferences, workshops, and social gatherings among fraud analysts, can improve data analytics outcomes.

The size of the regulation-bound network has no effect on fraud recovery outcomes. A positive effect of size would have been observed if analysts from more firms that collaborated and socialized enriched the knowledge leakage in the regulation-based network. In contrast, a negative effect of size would have been observed if the collaboration and socialization among employees of more firms led to information overload for these employees; results reveal neither effect from the size of the regulation-bound network. However, there is a distinct negative effect of the size of the sibling network on fraud recovery. Conjecturally, because larger sibling networks are more geographically dispersed, the impact of collaboration and socialization is less pronounced. Geographical propinquity makes it easy for informal socialization and labor mobility that facilitate information transfer (Owen-Smith and Powell 2004).

In the risk-sharing network, the effect of aggregate hospital IT on a subsidiary’s fraud detection is insignificant, so H3 is not validated. The regulation-bound network is characterized by competition between subsidiaries for premium revenues and by collaboration among them for fraud detection and recovery. The sibling network is a network of subsidiaries that collaborate on fraud detection and recovery. However, the risk-sharing network is characterized by competition among nodes to hide fraud. Because providers of health care and pharmacists are the potential perpetrators of fraud, there is friction in sharing fraud-related data and information on this network. These providers will try to make it difficult for fraud analysts to detect overcharges, upcoding, and similar practices (Silverman and Skinner 2004). That the size of the risk-sharing network does not affect fraud recovery underscores the lack of socialization in fraud detection in the risksharing network.

With regard to the effect of semi-collaborative networks and free-riding, the results are mixed in that free-riding was not observed in the regulation-bound network, but was observed in the sibling network. Each subsidiary is fairly independent in allocating resources for fraud recovery. The lack of a positive impact of the regulation-bound network, the sibling network, or the risk-sharing network on fraud detection spending indicates that subsidiaries are not yet making complementary investments to exploit the information spilled over from these networks.

By postulating the social embeddedness of insurers and health care providers via employee-level mechanisms of socialization, this paper extended the current level of theorization of IT spillovers in the literature. One of the limitations of this work however, is that the information that spilled over from the semi-collaborative networks was measured using the sum of fraud detection expenses of the participants in the network. Although this approach is consistent with prior literature such as Tambe and Hitt (2014b), researchers must find ways to measure information spillover in a network more effectively through observations of levels of socialization and labor mobility. Unlike prior research that was not able to separate rent-based spillover from data and information spillovers, focusing on fraud detection networks allowed measuring and testing data and information spillovers.

This research is the first to estimate the business value of fraud analytics. The results show that subsidiaries of insurers reap benefits in fraud recovery through private investments in fraud detection technologies and processes. Interestingly, the magnitude of this benefit is smaller than the benefit obtained from the sibling network. This result implies for managers that a federal governance approach rather than a centralized approach may be warranted for fraud analytics, wherein the effort put in by each subsidiary is supported by informal collaboration between subsidiaries. Results also show that data standards in the health industry, such as ICD-10, augment insurers’ abilities to detect and recover fraudulent claims.

This research has policy implications as well. When regulators formulate policy on IT spending in one industry, they are advised to take the impact of information spillovers and the networks in which firms in the industry are embedded into account to ensure that subsidies are not duplicated. For example, providing subsidies to both hospitals and insurers on fraud detection IT spending, without considering the level of information spillovers, may lead to wasting tax dollars. While policy makers should not duplicate subsidies, they are advised to use IT and related subsidies to strengthen semicollaborative networks in the industry. Finally, policy makers are also advised to consider the detrimental consequences of information spillovers on private investments, and institute policies that incentivize firms to increase private investments that will better exploit information spillovers.

## Conclusions

According to a 2014 report by the U.S. Congressional Budget Office titled “How Initiatives to Reduce Fraud in Federal Health Care Programs Affect the Budget,” true values of medical claims fraud are unknown. However, 1,700 defendants were charged with falsely billing the Medicare program for more than \$5.5 billion between 2009 and 2014. Furthermore, there were 428 criminal and 383 civil actions in one year, and 1,662 individuals were blacklisted by Medicare during the first half of 2016 (Coalition Against Insurance Fraud n.d.). These statistics indicate the importance of improving fraud detection. Because the examples of fraud are small compared to nonfradulent claims (e.g., CMS and its contractors process 4.5 million claims each day, out of which the number of fraud cases is in the hundreds; Coalition Against Insurance Fraud n.d.), sharing data and information is one way for analysts to alleviate the diminishing returns from fraud detection efforts.

A goal of this research was to conceptualize three types of semi-collaborative networks in the fraud detection activities of insurance subsidiaries and to measure the impact of information spillover via these networks on fraud recovery by these subsidiaries. With a focus on fraud detection, it was possible to isolate and study information spillover without confounding the spillover with rent-based spillover, as has been done in prior work on IT spillovers. By applying the theory of social embeddedness to conceptualize semicollaborative networks, this study unhooked the conceptual underpinning of information spillover from that in the traditional R&D spillover literature, which has been the staple of IT researchers thus far. The approach of considering types of semi-collaborative networks is likely to pave a way for additional research on the impact and usefulness of social embeddedness in data analytics activities.

The study faced empirical challenges, including one of finite masses at zeros for some of the key variables, which was resolved using a generalized linear model and a compound Poisson-gamma distribution. This approach has been used to analyze aggregate claims data in the insurance literature and has promise for future research looking at data analytics outcomes at the aggregate firm level rather than transactionlevel outcomes. The results show that there is information spillover between insurers’ subsidiaries in a state, which indicates the usefulness of a regulation-bound semicollaborative network. There is also evidence of the usefulness of a sibling semi-collaborative network. Subsidiaries of a parent firm benefit other subsidiaries of the firm in terms of information spillover leading to fraud recovery owing to socialization and collaboration. The study also finds that fraud detection technology and data standardization contribute to fraud recovery.

The second goal of this research was to validate the incentive effect of information spillover (i.e., whether benefits from semi-collaborative networks affected fraud detection investments in the insurance industry). There is evidence that spillover benefits from the sibling network can depress a subsidiary’s fraud detection investments. No relationship between the regulation-bound network and the risk-sharing network on a subsidiary’s fraud detection investments was found, but conjecture that complementary investments in exploiting information spillover from these networks could improve fraud recovery in the future. Future research should focus on providing prescriptions to practice for improving data and information sharing for data analytics.

## Appendix A

The production function of an insurer subsidiary is modeled as a log-log function of revenues from sales of premium as the dependent variable, and salaries and general and administrative expenses as the independent variables:

$$
\ln R e v _ {i j k t} = \ln A + a _ {1} \ln S a l _ {i j k t} + a _ {2} \ln G A E _ {i j k t}\tag{A1}
$$

where $i \in I$ is a subsidiary of firm $j \in J$ in state in year t. A is a constant. The coefficients $a _ { 1 }$ and $a _ { 2 }$ capture the unit contribution of each type of cost in the production of a unit of revenue. The subsidiary employs a sales force that costs an amount $S a l _ { i j k t } .$ It incurs administration costs $G A E _ { i j k t } ,$ which include salaries for administration, capital depreciation, and overhead.

Let $F D P _ { i j k t }$ denote the fraud that a subsidiary of an insurer is able to detect, which is an output of the activities of the subsidiary, and $C L M _ { i j k t }$ denote the number of claims that the company processes. Let $F T _ { i j k t }$ denote the fraud detection expenses for technology, people, and processes that it employs to detect fraudulent claims. As there are two outputs, revenues and fraud detected, and one additional input, fraud detection expenses, we rewrite equation A1 so that fraud detected is entered in the left-hand side of the equation and fraud detection expenses is entered in the right-hand side of the equation:

$$
x _ {1} \ln R e v _ {i j k t} + x _ {2} \ln C L M _ {i j k t} + x _ {2} \ln F D P _ {i j k t} = \ln A + a _ {1} \ln S a l _ {i j k t} + a _ {2} \ln G A E _ {i j k t} + a _ {3} \ln F T _ {i j k t}\tag{A2}
$$

The coefficients $x _ { 1 }$ and $x _ { 2 }$ model the relationship between simultaneously producing the two outputs and simultaneously using the two inputs (Panzar and Willing 1977).

Following information systems literature on entering available IT spillover as an independent variable (e.g., Tambe and Hitt 2014b), we include $R F T _ { m p k t }$ , a proxy for the spillover available from the regulation-bound network, in Equation A2 to arrive at the revised model A3:

$$
\begin{array}{c} x _ {1} \ln R e v _ {i j k t} + x _ {2} \ln C L M _ {i j k t} + x _ {3} F D P _ {i j k t} \\ = \ln A + a _ {1} \ln S a l _ {i j k t} + a _ {2} \ln G A E _ {i j k t} + a _ {3} \ln F T _ {i j k t} + a _ {4} \ln R F T _ {m p k t}, \\ m \in l; m \neq i; n \in K; n \neq k; \forall p \neq j \end{array}\tag{A3}
$$

To account for cross-subsidiary spillover of an insurer, we include the spillover available from the sibling network, $S F T _ { m j n t } , m \in l ; m \neq i ; n \in$ $K ; n \neq k ,$ of company j in other states.

$$
\begin{array}{l} x _ {1} \ln R e v _ {i j k t} + x _ {2} \ln C L M _ {i j k t} + x _ {3} F D P _ {i j k t} \\ = \ln A + a _ {1} \ln S a l _ {i j k t} + a _ {2} \ln G A E _ {i j k t} + a _ {3} \ln F T _ {i j k t} + a _ {4} \ln R F T _ {m p k t} + a _ {5} \ln S F T _ {m j n t}, \\ m \in l; m \neq i; n \in K; n \neq k; \forall p \neq j \end{array}\tag{A4}
$$

Similarly, the information spillover that health care providers endow, $R S N _ { k t } ,$ in the state k will improve the fraud detection capabilities of a subsidiary. The model thus is

$$
\begin{array}{r l} x _ {1} \ln R e v _ {i j k t} + x _ {2} \ln C L M _ {i j k t} + x _ {3} F D P _ {i j k t} \\ & = \ln A + a _ {1} \ln S a l _ {i j k t} + a _ {2} \ln G A E _ {i j k t} + a _ {3} \ln F T _ {i j k t} + a _ {4} \ln R F T _ {m p k t} + a _ {5} \ln S F T _ {m j n t} + a _ {6} \ln R S N _ {k t} \end{array}\tag{A5}
$$

Rearranging the above model and dividing throughout by $x _ { 2 } \mathrm { ~ . ~ }$ yields the following model that is in a form that can be estimated empirically:

$$
\begin{array}{c} \ln F D P _ {i j k t} = \frac {1}{x _ {3}} \ln A + \frac {a _ {1}}{x _ {3}} \ln R F T _ {m p k t} + \frac {a _ {2}}{x _ {3}} \ln S F T _ {m j n t} + \frac {a _ {3}}{x _ {3}} \ln R S N _ {k t} + \frac {a _ {4}}{x _ {3}} \ln F T _ {i j k t} \\ + \frac {a _ {5}}{x _ {3}} \ln G A E _ {i j k t} + \frac {a _ {6}}{x _ {3}} \ln S a l _ {i j k t} - \frac {x _ {1}}{x _ {3}} \ln R e v _ {i j k t} - \frac {x _ {2}}{x _ {3}} \ln C L M _ {i j k t} \end{array}\tag{A6}
$$

Equation 1 in the main text of the paper is based on equation A6 above in terms of the main variables, the spillover proxy variables, fraud detection expenses, general and administrative expenses, salaries, revenues, and claims. Other variables in equation 1 are control variables that add robustness to the model estimation.

## Appendix B

One challenge faced with the fraud recovery model is that it is possible that fraud detection expenses are zero for several companies. Similarly, for companies with only one subsidiary, the sibling network has zero membership. To ensure cases where production input is zero do not drop off from the estimation due to missing values, the assumption is that such cases follow a production function with a different intercept and a limited set of production inputs. Suppose a firm uses two input factors $( X _ { 1 } , X _ { 2 } )$ to produce one output, whereas another firm uses only one of the input factors $( X _ { 1 } )$ to produce the same output. The amount of $X _ { 2 }$ used in the latter case is zero. The production function for the former firm is

$$
\ln Y = a _ {1} + b _ {1} \times \ln X _ {1} + b _ {2} \times \ln X _ {2}
$$

The production function for the latter firm is

$$
\ln Y = a _ {2} + b _ {2} \times \ln X _ {1}
$$

The different intercepts, $a _ { 1 }$ and ${ { a } _ { 2 } } ,$ indicate that the two firms operate on different production frontiers. We combine the two equations into one production function as

$$
\ln Y = a _ {1} + \left(a _ {2} - a _ {1}\right) D _ {1} + b _ {1} \times \ln X _ {1} + b _ {2} \times \ln X _ {2}
$$

where $D _ { 1 } = 1$ and $\mathrm { l n } X _ { 2 } { = } 0 ,$ , when $X _ { 2 } = 0 . ~ D _ { 1 } = 0 \mathrm { f o r } X _ { 2 } > 0 .$ . Battese (1997) shows that the estimates of the coefficients of the production function are unbiased and consistent.
