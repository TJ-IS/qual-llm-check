# HOW DOES STRATEGIC ALIGNMENT AFFECT FIRM PERFORMANCE? THE ROLES OF INFORMATION TECHNOLOGY INVESTMENT AND ENVIRONMENTAL UNCERTAINTY<sup>1</sup>

Rajiv Sabherwal Walton College of Business, University of Arkansas, Fayetteville, AR 72701 U.S.A. {rsabherwal@walton.uark.edu}

Sanjiv Sabherwal College of Business, University of Texas–Arlington, Arlington, TX 76019 U.S.A. {sabherwal@uta.edu}

Taha Havakhor Fox School of Business, Temple University, 210 Speakman Hall, Philadelphia, PA 19122 U.S.A. {taha.havakhor@tahahavakhor.com}

Zach Steelman Walton College of Business, University of Arkansas, Fayetteville, AR 72701 U.S.A. {zsteelman@walton.uark.edu}

The alignment between a firm’s business and information technology (IT) strategies continues to be important for research and practice. Prior research investigating the performance consequences of strategic IT alignment (SITA) has produced inconsistent results. This paper distinguishes between two roles of SITA: (1) as a state of congruence between business and IT, which is the primary focus of empirical studies, and (2) as reflecting a capability that may enable or inhibit the leveraging of IT investments, as has been discussed theoretically but not examined empirically. Based on the resource-based view (RBV), IT investment (ITI) is explicitly included as the resource that SITA as a capability can inherently help leverage. Also based on RBV, we argue that environmental uncertainty, which is examined in terms of dynamism, complexity, and munifi cence, moderates the effect of SITA on the relationship between ITI and firm performance. The research model is tested through panel-data analyses of data from 1999–2008, including 758 firm-year observations from 242 firms. This study is the first to find that SITA as a state directly improves firm performance even when considering ITI and its interaction with SITA. Moreover, the effect of the interaction between SITA and ITI on firm performance increases with an increase in environmental dynamism or complexity and with a decrease in environmental munificence. We also find that the effect of the interaction between SITA and ITI can be negative under some environments. Specifically, the results suggest that (1) in dynamic, complex, and hostile environments, SITA does reflect a capability that enhances the positive effect of ITI on firm performance, but (2) in stable, simple, and munificent environments, SITA reflects a rigidity that reduces the positive effect of ITI on firm performance. The results are robust under a variety of statistical specifications and estimations.

Keywords: Strategic alignment, information technology investment, firm performance, environmental uncertainty, strategic planning, information technology strategy, panel data

## Introduction

“I see my main role as linking IT and business strategy,” Marcy Klevorn, Ford CIO (Qualtrough 2016).

Klevorn is not alone in recognizing the importance of strategic alignment between business and information technology (IT). Annual surveys of chief information officers (CIOs) and other senior executives consistently find strategic IT alignment (SITA) to be among the top three most important IT management issues (Kappelman et al. 2014). Extensive literature over the past four decades (e.g., King 1978; Wu et al. 2015), including a recent meta-analysis (Gerow et al. 2014) and a bibliometric study (Renaud et al. 2016), also highlights its importance.

Although most studies find positive returns from increasing SITA (e.g., Bergeron et al. 2004; Chan et al. 1997; Gerow et al. 2014; Sabherwal and Kirs 1994), some authors suggest that SITA may lead to stagnation, inflexibility, and competitive disadvantage (e.g., Chen et al. 2010; Sabherwal et al. 2001; Tallon and Pinsonneault 2011), and a decline in performance (e.g., Palmer and Markus 2000; Tallon 2008; Tallon and Kraemer 2003). Thus, despite the continuing importance of SITA, there is a lack of a clear understanding regarding whether SITA is always value enhancing, and whether firms should pursue SITA under different contexts.

This paper develops the argument that the variation in the performance consequences of SITA may be related to two distinct, albeit interrelated, ways in which SITA can create value. First, as in an overwhelming body of literature, SITA may be viewed as a state of congruence (e.g., the degree to which a firm’s IS strategies conform to business strategies) that directly affects firm performance (e.g., Bergeron et al. 2004; Chan et al. 1997; Sabherwal and Kirs 1994). Second, SITA may reflect a capability (e.g., Byrd et al. 2006; Preston and Karahanna 2009) to leverage the firm’s IT investments (ITI). The following quote illustrates the second view: “IS strategic alignment is necessary to allow organizations to capitalize on their IS investments” (Preston and Karahanna 2009, p. 159, emphasis added).

Despite occasional hints that SITA reflects a capability to leverage ITI (e.g., Croteau and Raymond 2004; Sabherwal and Chan 2001), this role is rarely empirically examined. The only empirical study that we could find on this role of SITA (Byrd et al. 2006)<sup>2</sup> supports the leveraging (i.e., moderating)

effect of SITA on the relationship between ITI and firm performance, but not the direct effect of SITA. Considering SITA as reflecting a capability to leverage ITI, along with considering SITA as a state of congruence, can shed more light on the inconsistent findings of previous studies regarding the value of SITA for firms. So, this paper addresses the following first research question: Does SITA<sup>3</sup> directly affect firm performance, or play a role in leveraging ITI to improve firm performance, or both? We argue that both direct and leveraging (i.e., moderating) effects of SITA are important, and test them simultaneously.

In order to develop a richer understanding of the relationship between SITA and ITI in affecting firm performance, we focus on one contextual factor—environmental uncertainty— that has received considerable attention in the literature on SITA. However, empirical results have been inconsistent both when uncertainty is viewed as an antecedent of SITA (e.g., Chan et al. 1997) and when it is viewed as a moderator of the effect of SITA on firm performance (e.g., Yayla and Hu 2012). The exclusion of ITI from such research has precluded empirical investigation of whether the influence of environmental uncertainty is because it affects the extent to which SITA helps generate value for the firm. SITA as a capability reflects an on-going process (Chen et al. 2010) that requires developing procedures, routines, and communication channels (e.g., Preston and Karahanna 2009) but changes in the environment may influence how SITA and these underlying aspects are leveraged over time (Sabherwal et al. 2001). So, and consistent with prior recommendations for simultaneously considering capabilities, ITI, and uncertainty (El Sawy et al. 2010), we argue that viewing SITA as reflecting a capability, rather than only as a state of congruence, may help understand the effects of SITA in different environments.

In order to better understand the effect of environmental uncertainty on the role of SITA in leveraging ITI, we recognize that uncertainty poses both obstacles and opportunities to firms. Moreover, it is not clear whether SITA plays a greater role in generating value through ITI in uncertain environments, where achieving it may be difficult (e.g., Chen et al. 2010) but more beneficial (e.g., Tallon and Pinsonneault 2011), or in more certain environments, where achieving it may be easier but less beneficial. To investigate these alternative possibilities, we build on the resource-based view (RBV) of IT, which suggests that environmental conditions influence the value of capabilities (e.g., Nevo and Wade 2010). Thus, this paper addresses the following second research question: Does environmental uncertainty influence the role of SITA in leveraging ITI to improve firm performance? Addressing this research question also enables us to examine whether SITA, instead of reflecting a capability as argued above, may sometimes reflect a rigidity, as implicit in prior arguments associating it with “tunnel vision” (Tallon and Pinsonneault 2011), “complacency and inertia” (Sabherwal et al. 2001), and a “rigidity trap” (Chen et al. 2010; Tallon 2008). Moreover, by considering the joint role of ITI and environmental uncertainty in examining the leveraging effect of SITA, this paper extends prior studies that include SITA and ITI but not uncertainty (Byrd et al. 2006; Lee and Mithas 2014).

We address the above research questions using longitudinal data from InformationWeek, COMPUSTAT, and CRSP over a 10-year period, allowing SITA, ITI, and firm performance to change over time. The sample consists of 758 firm-year observations from 242 firms. We use panel-data analyses and extensive data from public announcements to validate the results. We believe that this is the first paper to use objective measures and panel data to examine the effects of SITA on firm performance and address the previously acknowledged limitations of cross-sectional surveys (Byrd et al. 2006; Lee and Mithas 2014; Tallon and Pinsonneault 2011).

The results support our expectation that, in terms of its direct effect, SITA as a state of congruence is value enhancing in both uncertain and certain environments even when considering the direct effect of ITI. Although prior research has generally found SITA to be value enhancing, the effect has not been found when also considering ITI (Byrd et al. 2006). Second, the paper finds that the leveraging role of SITA differs across environments; whereas SITA is a capability in uncertain environments, increasing the effect of ITI on firm performance, it is a rigidity in more certain environments, reducing the effect of ITI on firm performance.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. The next section develops the theoretical model for the paper. The subsequent section describes the data, including the sample and the measures. A description of the analyses and results follows. The paper concludes with a discussion of the emergent findings and their implications for future research and practice.

## Theoretical Development

## The Dual Role of Strategic IT Alignment

The predominant view of SITA is as a state of congruence between IT strategy and business strategy (e.g., Sabherwal and Chan 2001; Tallon and Pinsonneault 2011; Wu et al. 2015). By contrast, some authors view SITA as reflecting a capability to leverage IT (e.g., Baker et al. 2011; Schwarz et al. 2010). When SITA is viewed as a state of congruence between business and IT, the focus is on its direct effect on firm performance; but when SITA reflects a capability, its role is in leveraging ITI to influence firm performance.

Moreover, the view of SITA as reflecting a capability complements, rather than contradicts, the view of SITA as a state of congruence. Summarizing prior studies on SITA, Table 1 illustrates how studies that conceptualize and measure SITA as a state of congruence also often describe SITA as reflecting a capability. The view of SITA as also reflecting a capability is also consistent with the prior strategy literature, wherein alignment is conceptualized as reflecting a capability that can generate business value (e.g., Newbert 2008; Powell 1992).

Thus, based on prior literature, we consider SITA to have a dual nature, as both (1) a state of congruence between business and IT strategies, which has a direct effect on firm performance; and (2) reflecting a capability that helps integrate IT more effectively with organizational resources, and thereby helps leverage ITI to improve firm performance. We draw upon arguments from the resource-based view (RBV) of the firm (Barney 1991) that a firm creates value by using its capabilities to leverage its resources (Amit and Shoemaker 1993), and the firm’s environmental conditions affect its ability to do so (Wade and Hulland 2004).

Prior literature (Aral and Weill 2007; Mithas and Rust 2016) generally agrees that IT as a resource improves firm performance. Considering this, as well as the RBV arguments that capabilities enhance the value obtained from resources (Amit and Shoemaker 1993), we propose that the capability reflected by SITA enables the firm to leverage the resource represented by ITI. It is therefore important to consider SITA’s joint effect with ITI to explain performance gains.

Consistent with RBV’s argument regarding the importance of the environment, and specifically uncertainty (Wade and Hulland 2004), we examine how uncertainty affects the nature of the capability that SITA reflects and thereby influences the role SITA plays in leveraging ITI. Two prior studies on SITA (Tallon and Pinsonneault 2011; Yayla and Hu 2012) view the environment as a moderator but find inconsistent results, as shown in Table 2. These inconsistent results may be because the environment affects how SITA helps leverage ITI, which has not been tested due to ITI being excluded in these studies. We build upon the prior literature to examine how uncertainty moderates the effect of the interaction between SITA and ITI on firm performance. Specifically, we focus on three dimensions of uncertainty: dynamism (or the speed of change), complexity (or heterogeneity), and munificence (or the lack of hostility) in the firm’s industry environment (Newkirk and Lederer 2006; Yayla and Hu 2012).

## Strategic IT Alignment as a State of Congruence

Strategic alignment between IT and business is generally viewed as a desirable state of congruence that affects firm performance. Empirical studies adopt this view of SITA as a desired state and focus on its effects on the performance of the IT function and the organization (e.g., Chan et al. 1997; Parthasarathy and Sethi 1993; Sabherwal and Kirs 1994). Moreover, alignment, as a state of congruence, may be viewed in terms of either intended strategies, usually examined through surveys, or realized strategies, usually examined through objective data (Mintzberg and Waters 1985; Wu et al. 2015). Prior literature generally examines SITA in terms of intended business and IT strategies. The few prior studies that view SITA in terms of realized strategies (Chan et al. 2006; Sabherwal and Chan 2001; Wu et al. 2015) do so using surveys to measure business and IT strategies; for example, measuring IT strategy in terms of how the firm’s IT helps in distinguishing its products from those of its competitors (Wu et al. 2015).

This paper recognizes the importance of viewing actual resource commitments rather than intentions (Mintzberg and Waters 1985) and examines SITA as the fit between realized business and IT strategies. This is consistent with Wu et al. (2015, p. 503), who define SITA as “how well the content of the realized business strategy matches the content of the realized IT strategy.” Based on such prior conceptualizations (Lee and Mithas 2014; Oh and Pinsonneault 2007; Weill and Aral 2006), we define SITA as the extent to which a firm’s relative investments in different areas of IT are consistent with the firm’s business strategy.

Several prior studies find SITA to improve perceived organizational performance (Wu et al. 2015), perceived IT effectiveness (Chan et al. 1997), use of IT for competitive advantage (Kearns and Sabherwal 2007), and sales, profits, and costs (Oh and Pinsonneault 2007). Some studies find mixed results with SITA affecting only some aspects of performance (Croteau and Bergeron 2001; Parthasarathy and Sethi 1993), affecting performance only in some contexts (Sabherwal and Chan 2001; Tallon 2008), or affecting performance indirectly (Tallon and Pinsonneault 2011). A study in the retail industry finds SITA to not affect firm performance (Palmer and Markus 2000). More recently, a meta-analysis of the literature on SITA (Gerow et al. 2014) finds SITA to positively affect firm performance. In light of the considerable prior support (Table 2), we expect a direct positive effect of SITA on firm performance.

## Strategic IT Alignment as Also Reflecting a Capability

RBV has been used frequently in the IS literature, which considers interdependencies among IT resources (Wade and Hulland 2004) and the complementarity between IT and other resources (Bardhan et al. 2013; Ravichandran and Lertwongsatien 2005). RBV argues that firms compete based on capabilities and resources and distinguishes between capabilities and resources (Barney 1991; Nevo and Wade 2010). For example, Amit and Schoemaker (1993, p. 35) remark “Capabilities, in contrast, refer to a firm’s capacity to deploy

Resources, usually in combination, using organizational processes, to effect a desired end.” Thus, capabilities enhance the value resources provide to the firm (Bharadwaj 2000; Ravichandran and Lertwongsatien 2005).

As shown in Table 1, prior empirical literature views SITA as a desirable state, but SITA is often conceptually viewed as also reflecting a capability to leverage an underlying resource (i.e., ITI). However, that underlying resource is not explicitly considered in most prior research on SITA. By contrast, to remain consistent with RBV, we explicitly incorporate ITI. RBV also offers a theoretical lens for using capabilities to explain how firms obtain different performance benefits from a similar increase in ITI (Melville et al. 2004; Nevo and Wade 2010). We build on this literature and propose that a firm’s SITA reflects a capability, specifically the ability to leverage ITs within the business (Mitchell 2006). Some authors view SITA directly in terms of such capabilities (e.g., Baker et al. 2011; Schwartz et al. 2010).

The view of SITA as a capability is consistent with considerable prior literature on SITA suggesting that organizations do not achieve high SITA through luck (e.g., Baker et al. 2011). Instead, for organizations to achieve SITA, social and structural integration between business and IT are considered essential. The social aspects that have been found to enable SITA include shared understanding (Chan et al. 2006; Preston and Karahanna 2009) and mutual trust (e.g., Reich and Benbasat 2000) between IT and business; and common interests between senior IS and business executives (e.g., Nelson and Cooprider 1996). The structural aspects that have been found to enable SITA include open channels of communication between business and IT (e.g., Ravichandran and Liu 2011); business managers’ participation in IT planning (Kearns and Sabherwal 2007); the inclusion of senior IS executives in the board of directors (Kuruzovich et al. 2012); and IT managers participation in business planning (Kearns and Sabherwal 2007).

The social and structural integration underlying SITA provides the firm with a capability, consistent with the notion that organizational capabilities are created through processes and routines (Teece et al. 1997). Thus, SITA is not merely a state of congruence between a firm’s business strategy and ITI, but it may also reflect a capability in terms of social and structural integration between business and IT. ## SITA with low social/structural integration

According to RBV, organizational capabilities help leverage resources to improve firm performance (Eisenhardt and Martin 2000; Nevo and Wade 2010; Ray et al. 2004). Similarly, SITA, as reflecting a capability (Baker et al. 2011), has been argued to help leverage ITI to improve firm performance (Byrd et al. 2006; Preston and Karahanna 2009). Indeed, as discussed earlier, the conceptual arguments about how SITA affects firm performance largely focus on how it reflects a capability to leverage ITI (Gupta et al. 1997; Peppard and Ward 2004). Prior studies provide several reasons for expecting SITA to help leverage ITI to improve firm performance. Greater SITA reflects a closer working relationship between IT and business executives, which should lead to the development of more effective and high-potential systems (Byrd et al. 2006; Chan 2002). By contrast, in organizations with a low level of SITA, IT executives may find it difficult to target investments to the most important aspects in the firm and may make decisions that lead to redundant and disjointed systems (Chan 2002).

The primary task of senior IT executives is to leverage ITI to improve firm performance. A high level of ITI increases both the stakes and visibility associated with IT, enhancing the expectation that ITI would be leveraged to improve firm performance, whereas a high level of SITA improves the ability of the firm’s executives to do so. If ITI is high, a high level of SITA would improve performance to a greater extent than if ITI is low. So, we expect SITA to have a positive moderating effect on the relationship between ITI and firm performance. Thus, we argue that SITA has both a main effect (H1a) as a state of congruence, and a leveraging effect (H1b) as reflecting a capability, on firm performance. Each effect is supported in different studies, but not together (Byrd et al. 2006). We propose that both these effects occur together.

H1a: SITA in year t-1 has a positive direct effect on firm performance in year t.

H1b: SITA in year t-1 has a positive moderating effect on the relationship between IT investment in year t-1 and firm performance in year t.

## The Effect of Environmental Uncertainty

Environmental uncertainty has been emphasized in the RBVbased IS literature (El Sawy 1985; Havakhor et al. 2019; Pavlou and El Sawy 2010). Consistent with the prior literature (Miller and Friesen 1980; Yayla and Hu 2012), we view uncertainty in terms of dynamism (or turbulence), complexity (or heterogeneity), and munificence (considered the opposite of hostility<sup>5</sup>). Prior studies on the effects of environmental uncertainty on both SITA and the relationship between SITA and firm performance offer inconsistent results. A surveybased study (Yayla and Hu 2012) finds uncertainty to moderate the effect of SITA on firm performance, such that this effect increases with an increase in uncertainty. Another survey (Tallon and Pinsonneault 2011) and case studies (Chang et al. 2008) do not find uncertainty to moderate the effect of SITA. Also, some studies (Choe 2003; Kearns and Lederer 2001) find uncertainty to increase SITA, but others (Sabherwal and Kirs 1994; Newkirk and Lederer 2006) do not. In the light of these inconsistent effects of uncertainty on the direct effect of SITA on firm performance, we argue that uncertainty moderates the leveraging effect of SITA on the relationship between ITI and firm performance. Specifically, we expect this leveraging role of SITA to increase with an increase in uncertainty (i.e., with an increase in dynamism and complexity and a decrease in munificence). Three broad reasons lead us to these expectations, which we later state as Hypotheses 2a–2c.

First, an increase in environmental uncertainty makes it difficult to make decisions regarding where to target IT investments and how to appropriately leverage them (Havakhor et al. 2019; Qualtrough 2016). To effectively deal with these difficulties, a greater level of the capability reflected by SITA is needed. In the presence of social and structural aspects underlying SITA, IT executives can identify specific areas of the business where ITI can contribute. Due to business executives’ greater proximity to the business environment, IT executives with more frequent interactions with business executives will have more knowledge of the opportunities and challenges in the environment (Ravichandran and Liu 2011). They will therefore be better positioned to leverage ITI to address organizational problems.

Second, environmental uncertainty inhibits the development of shared understanding between business and IT, and without the structures needed to facilitate such an understanding, valuable time and resources would be directed at building them. Executives at a firm that has been able to achieve a high level of SITA in an uncertain environment would be in a better position to address the challenges posed by the uncertainty. They would therefore be more likely to direct the firm’s scarce resources to improve how ITI is used to address organizational problems. By contrast, firms with low SITA, and in an environment where establishing shared understanding is hard, would need to first find scarce resources to establish a shared understanding, thereby finding it more difficult to effectively leverage ITI.

Third, in more uncertain environments, business strategies are “better” due to necessity, because poor business strategies are likely to be severely punished. Consequently, IT strategies that are aligned to better business strategies are better able to obtain value from ITI. In less uncertain environments, business or IT strategy can be “inappropriate” with minor consequences, and therefore less SITA is needed to leverage ITI to improve firm performance.<sup>6</sup>

The above arguments about environmental uncertainty in general apply to each of its three dimensions. We first consider environmental dynamism, which is viewed as “the rate and unpredictability of environmental change” (Newkirk and Lederer 2006, p, 394). It represents the environment’s instability, which challenges managers to quickly and frequently adopt new strategies and tactics (Yayla and Hu 2012). In a stable environment, any advantage attained by a firm is likely to be sustained over an extended period (Miller and Shamsie 1996); whereas in a dynamic environment, competitive advan tage may be short term (Foss 1998).

As dynamism in a firm’s environment increases, the firm may need to make frequent changes to its activity systems (Sull et al. 2015) and focus on exploration of new opportunities for ITI<sup>7</sup> (De Haes et al. 2011). Rapid changes in the environment reduce the time available to absorb new knowledge and refocus IT resources to leverage transient opportunities (Van den Bosch et al. 1999). The capability reflected by SITA includes aspects such as close ties and frequent interactions between business and IT executives, which enhance IT execu tives’ knowledge of the upcoming business opportunities. This would enable quick innovations with additional ITI, as needed with increasing dynamism (e.g., Lawrence and Lorsch 1967; Sull et al. 2015). Thus, consistent with the above arguments regarding environmental uncertainty, we posit:

H2a: Greater environmental dynamism at time t-1 increases the moderating effect of SITA on the relationship between a firm’s ITI at time t-1 and its performance at time t.

Environmental complexity refers to “the heterogeneity and range of an industry and/or an organization’s activities” (Wade and Hulland 2004, p. 127). Environmental complexity makes it difficult for managers to comprehend the drivers of performance (Newkirk and Lederer 2006). Also, in complex environments, firms need to simultaneously adjust several aspects of their business and IT strategies in order to maintain SITA (Sabherwal et al. 2001).

When complexity is high, the firm needs to absorb the heterogeneous information from diverse sources in order to synchronize its actions (Wade and Hulland 2004). A firm with a high level of SITA would be able to more effectively respond to such complexity than a firm with a lower level of SITA, due to their IT executives’ better knowledge of the intricacies of the business environment, resulting from their regular interactions with business managers. Thus, and in light of the above arguments regarding environmental uncertainty, we posit:

H2b: Greater environmental complexity at time t-1 increases the moderating effect of SITA on the relationship between a firm’s ITI at time t-1 and its performance at time t.

Environmental munificence refers to the extent to which a firm’s environment supports sustained growth (Dess and Beard 1984; Wade and Hulland 2004). Mature or shrinking environments generally have a low level of munificence, whereas rapidly growing markets are usually associated with a high level of munificence.

In hostile, or less munificent, environments, stiff competition is usually present and adversely affects the accomplishment of organizational goals (Miller and Friesen 1980). In such an environment, a firm with close ties between business and IT executives, associated with a high level of SITA, would be able to better leverage ITI than a firm with internal inefficiencies of coordination and integration of business and IS, as in the case of a firm with a low level of SITA. By contrast, munificent environments are more forgiving and support firm performance despite inappropriate business and IT strategies (Wade and Hulland 2004). As a result, in a munificent environment, a firm with a low level of SITA might not be much worse off than a firm with a high level of SITA in terms of its ability to leverage ITI. Therefore, and based on the three arguments advanced earlier about environmental uncertainty in general, we posit H2c, and depict it along with the other hypotheses in the detailed research model in Figure 2:

H2c: Greater environmental munificence at time t-1 reduces the moderating effect of SITA on the relationship between a firm’s ITI at time t-1 and its performance at time t.

## Data

## Sample

This study is based on secondary data from InformationWeek, COMPUSTAT, and CRSP. Each year, InformationWeek provides a ranking of the 500 most innovative corporate users of IT. We were able to obtain from InformationWeek the complete data from the 1999–2007 surveys. We supplement it with COMPUSTAT and CRSP data, including firm performance for 2008.

We exclude financial firms (firms with SIC codes between 6000–6999) from the firms in the InformationWeek surveys because the nature of some explanatory variables in the regressions differs between financial and nonfinancial firms (Fama and French 2001; Von Eije and Megginson 2008). We also exclude firms for which business segment data is not available to compute market share. Moreover, we exclude private firms, foreign firms,<sup>8</sup> and subsidiaries and divisions, because their required stock market information is not available. This results in a sample of 657 firms spread over one or more years, for a total of 2,224 firm-year observations.<sup>9</sup>

As discussed below, we perform panel data analyses with lagged variables to test the hypotheses. So, for a firm to be included in the analysis, data on two or more consecutive years is needed. Moreover, we exclude observations for which any variable had missing values. This reduces the sample for panel data analyses to 758 firm-year observations, based on 242 firms.<sup>10</sup>

## Measures

We measure firm performance using Tobin’s q ratio, which has been used to measure firm performance in studies on IT impacts (Bharadwaj et al. 1999; Chari et al. 2008). Tobin’s q incorporates a market-based measure of firm value, which is forward looking, risk adjusted, and less vulnerable to changes in accounting practices, and is considered suitable for measuring the performance impact of ITI (Chari et al. 2008). A Tobin’s q value above one indicates that the long-run equilibrium market value of the firm is greater than the replacement value of its assets signifying an unmeasured source of value (Bharadwaj et al. 1999). We use the wellknown Chung and Pruitt (1994) measure of the Tobin’s q ratio.<sup>11</sup>

IT investment (ITI) is measured as the firm’s annual IT budget as a proportion of the firm’s annual sales revenue. We do this using the InformationWeek survey response to a question such as the following: “What percentage of your company’s worldwide projected 2007 annual sales revenue does your total 2007 worldwide IT budget represent? (The budget should include the same elements as above. May be rounded to nearest ½%).” For example, if Firm A spent 1 percent of its annual sales revenue on IT, ITI would be 1 percent (i.e., 0.01). We measure each variable in the study, including ITI, for each year for each firm in the sample.

As indicated earlier, strategic IT alignment (SITA) is defined as the extent to which a firm’s relative investments in different IT areas are consistent with the firm’s business strategy. Accordingly, we measure SITA in terms of the alignment between the firm’s business strategy and the relative emphasis of its IT investments. Also, since capabilities evolve over time, SITA, both as a state of congruence and as reflecting a capability, is dynamic, making it appropriate to measure it each year for each firm. We compute SITA based on the widely used profile-deviation approach (e.g., Sabherwal and Chan 2001; Sabherwal and Sabherwal 2007; Tallon 2008; Tallon and Pinsonneault 2011). Appendix B provides the details of its computation.

We compute and analyze ITI and SITA as independent constructs. Specifically, we compute ITI as the ratio of the firm’s annual IT budget to annual sales revenue, and SITA using the ITI profile (i.e., how ITI is distributed across R&D, insourcing, outsourcing, hardware, application, and maintenance) as the starting point. For example, if Firms A and B spent 1 and 10 percent, respectively, of their annual sales revenue on IT, their ITI would be 0.01 (i.e., 1%) and 0.10 (i.e., 10%), respectively. If both distribute IT budgets equally across R&D, insourcing, outsourcing, hardware, application, and maintenance, the ITI profiles for both firms would be identical with proportions of 0.166 for each area for both firms. Thus, the starting point for computing SITA (i.e., proportion of ITI spent on each area) is independent of ITI. Accordingly, the correlations of ITI with R&D (0.04), insourcing (0.01), outsourcing (0.03), hardware (0.03), application (0.01), and maintenance (0.03) are all nonsignificant (p-value > 0.05).

We measure the three dimensions of environmental uncertainty using prior measures and COMPUSTAT data. Following prior literature (Keats and Hitt 1988; Xue et al. 2011), we measure environmental dynamism by quantifying the volatility of industry sales.<sup>12</sup> For each firm, we regress the natural log of total sales of the four-digit SIC industry code to which a firm belongs against an index variable of years, for a period of five years (t, t-4). We then use the antilog of the standard error of the regression coefficient to measure sales volatility as a proxy for a firm’s environmental dynamism. We measure environmental complexity as the reciprocal of industry concentration. This is consistent with the logic that in an industry with fewer competitors (i.e., more concentration), the competitors and their likely actions are well known.

## Analyses and Results

## Estimation Procedures

We reduce the potential threat of artificial multicollinearity by first standardizing all the variables in the model and then creating the interaction terms (Aiken and West 1991; Cohen et al. 2003). Due to the cross-sectional time-series data, we test for heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation. We anticipate some panel-specific heteroskedasticity, as the variance of the error terms for each industry is likely to fluctuate over time and across industries. Our likelihood ratio test (p < 0.001) indicates the presence of panel-specific heteroskedasticity. The Wooldridge test (2010) to evaluate autocorrelation shows the presence of AR(1) autocorrelation in our dataset (p < 0.001). The autocorrelation may also differ in magnitude across firms presenting panel specific AR(1) (PSAR1). Our likelihood ratio test for PSAR1 (p < 0.001) indicates the existence of PSAR1. Based on these results of diagnostics tests, we estimate the models using feasible GLS with corrections for PSAR1 autocorrelation and panel-specific heteroskedasticity.

IS economics literature (e.g., Bardhan et al. 2013; Mithas et al. 2012) cautions about a potential reverse causality between the firm’s financial performance and ITI. This reverse causal relationship can violate the exogeneity assumption regarding residuals of GLS estimation, and make the coefficients biased (Greene 2011). This potential endogeneity can be addressed in two ways. First, we can find instrumental variables to run two-stage or three-stage least square estimates. However, consistent with the previous research (Bardhan et al. 2013) using data similar to ours, we are unable to find strong exogenous instruments. The second alternative is to utilize the Arellano-Bover/Blundell-Bond system of generalized method of moments (AB-GMM) estimator (Arellano and Bover 1995; Blundell and Bond 1998), which utilizes the lagged values of endogenous variables’ first-difference as instruments.

We follow suggestions by Aral et al. (2012) in running the AB-GMM model. We treat both ITI and SITA as endogenous, consistent with prior studies (e.g., Bardhan et al. 2013). Our two-step system GMM estimates are robust to heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation. We estimate robust standard errors to correct for potential bias in standard errors due to heteroskedasticity. We use the second lag of first-differences as instruments. Post-estimation results of the GMM model (Arellano-Bond test) fail to reject the null hypothesis that there is no autocorrelation of order 2 (z = –0.97, p = 0.33). Hansen’s test of over-identification also fails to reject the null hypothesis that the instruments are exogenous (χ<sup>2</sup> (5) = 3.12, p = 0.68). Finally, the results of the Difference-in-Hansen test of exogeneity fail to reject the null hypothesis that the subset of instruments used in the levels equations is exogenous (χ<sup>2</sup> (5) = 2.88, p = 0.72). Based on these results, we estimate the models using feasible GLS with corrections for PSAR1 auto-correlation and panel-specific heteroskedasticity, with AB-GMM as an additional robustness check.

## Hypothesis Tests

IT involves investment in the short term but provides benefits later (Weill and Broadbent 1998), which suggests a time lag in obtaining benefits from ITI (e.g., Brynjolfsson and Hitt 1998; Mithas et al. 2012). Consistent with Aral and Weill (2007), who include one-year lags in examining the effects of ITI, we incorporate one-year lagged effects of both ITI and SITA.

Table 4 presents the results of the main tests of the research hypotheses. We run multiple regressions with firm performance (Tobin’s q) as the dependent variable and use either GLS (Models 1–3) or AB-GMM (Model 3R) while accounting for the fixed effects of time and industry through year and industry dummies. We first enter the controls and the main effects of the three uncertainty dimensions (dynamism, complexity, and munificence), ITI, and SITA (Model 1). Next, we add the non-hypothesized two-way interactions of each uncertainty dimension with both ITI and SITA (Aiken and West 1991) (Model 2). We then add the three 3-way interactions involving ITI, SITA, and each uncertainty dimension (Model 3). We use AB-GMM to test the robustness of the GLS results in Model 3R.

SITA (supporting H1a) and ITI (as expected but not hypothesized) have a positive and significant effect on firm performance in all four models. The two-way interaction between ITI and SITA is positive and significant in all three models (2, 3, and 3R) in which it is included, thereby supporting H1b. Both Models 3 and 3R also support the hypotheses concerning the positive effect of the three-way interaction between SITA, ITI, and dynamism (H2a); the positive effect of the three-way interaction between SITA, ITI, and complexity (H2b); and the negative effect of the three-way interaction between SITA, ITI, and munificence (H2c). For example, the positive coefficients of the three-way interaction between SITA, ITI, and dynamism in Models 3 and 3R indicate that at higher levels of environmental dynamism (at time t-1) the moderating effect of SITA on the relationship between a firm’s ITI (at time t-1) and its performance (at time t) would be higher, thus supporting H2a. Thus, all five hypotheses are supported.

## Interaction Plots and Split-Sample Analyses

## Discussion

SITA has generally been regarded as an organizational state of congruence that leads to performance gains. This paper seeks to provide richer insights into the performance effects of SITA by departing from the prior literature in three main ways. First, we highlight the dual nature of SITA that may reflect a capability to leverage ITI in addition to its traditional view as a state of congruence between business and IT strategies. We argue that SITA as a state of congruence directly affects firm performance, while SITA as reflecting a capability moderates the impact of ITI. Second, by being the first to simultaneously examine the effects of environmental uncertainty, SITA, and ITI in influencing firm performance, this paper is able to examine whether uncertainty plays a different role than previously examined. Specifically, this study considers a three-way link between SITA, ITI, and environmental uncertainty, which have previously been considered separately or in two-way combinations, as a way to further the existing insights about how SITA generates value for firms. Third, by using panel data from a variety of sources to examine SITA over time, in contrast to the use of either cross-sectional surveys or longitudinal cases in the prior literature, we are able to identify each firm’s business strategy, ITI, SITA, and performance each year and examine lagged effects.

The results of the study support the presence of a three-way link between SITA, ITI, and environmental uncertainty in explaining firm value, indicating the importance of simultaneously considering ITI and environmental uncertainty along with SITA. Specifically, the results suggest that environmental uncertainty enhances the positive impact SITA has on firm performance through enhancing the value of ITI. Moreover, further analysis of interactions between SITA and ITI in environments with high and low uncertainty (as evidenced in our split-sample analysis) hints at the marked difference between what SITA reflects, a capability or a rigidity to utilize ITI. The results show that SITA and ITI interact positively to affect firm value in more uncertain environments, consistent with characterizing SITA as reflective of a capability to leverage IT. However, in less uncertain environments, the interaction between SITA and ITI turns negative, indicating that SITA in less uncertain environments does not reflect a capability, but instead reflects a rigidity. Figure 5 depicts the emergent model.

We offer three somewhat interrelated explanations for the negative effect of SITA in stable, simple, and munificent environments. First, although the social and structural aspects that enable SITA (e.g., shared understanding and interaction between business and IT) are generally viewed favorably (Wu et al. 2015), they may also have dysfunctional consequences.

When business and IT executives have strong ties, they may not question each other due to the fear of hurting their relationship (Miller 1991). Similarly, shared knowledge could lead to the exclusion of differing perspectives (Starbuck and Milliken 1988) and lead to “groupthink” (Janis 1982). In stable, simple, and munificent environments, where decisions do not need to be made quickly or may be easier, the social and structural aspects underlying SITA may increase the likelihood that IS executives focus additional ITI on the same areas as past investments, and thereby become a rigidity that limits the benefits from ITI. Thus, SITA may reflect a rigidity in less uncertain environments, which finds some support in the prior literature suggesting that a high level of SITA might imply that the organization has fallen into a rigidity trap (Coltman et al. 2015, p. 91) or has a constricted outlook (Baker et al. 2011).

Second, firms in less uncertain environments do not need to make frequent changes to their organizational systems and may instead seek to reap the benefits of the established systems (Sull et al. 2015). They may focus on the administration and exploitation of prior IT investments (De Haes et al. 2011). Thus, SITA might inhibit decisions on how to use additional ITI to enhance the value from prior systems or create innovative opportunities, and thereby represent a rigidity.

Both the above explanations for the negative moderating effect of SITA on the relationship between ITI and firm performance in less uncertain environments are based on the possibility of SITA being a rigidity in such environments, which finds support in the empirical results in Appendix E. The third possible explanation, which we are unable to test, is that although a high level of SITA does not usually result from luck alone, in less uncertain environments, it could simply result from “complacency and inertia”<sup>22</sup> (Sabherwal et al. 2001, p. 182). The business strategy could be inappropriate in such an environment with minor consequences; and the aligned IS strategy would be inappropriate as well. But the inappropriate IT strategy would imply that additional ITI has adverse performance effects, explaining the negative leveraging effect of SITA in such environments.

## Limitations

Before discussing the implications of the study’s results, we note its limitations. First, the sample for the study is based on firms from InformationWeek 500 surveys. Since the firms in the InformationWeek 500 list may be viewed as being better at using IT, further research using other samples of firms is needed to examine the generalizability of the results.

Second, our conclusions are based on viewing SITA as both a state and as reflecting either a capability or a rigidity. However, while SITA is measured as a state, the capability (or rigidity) reflected by it is not directly measured due to the longitudinal and retrospective nature of the data. But we are encouraged by the fact that the results of the supplementary analyses are consistent with the notion of SITA reflecting aspects that may comprise either a capability<sup>23</sup> or a rigidity.<sup>24</sup> Future research might provide further insights by measuring both SITA as a state and the capability or rigidity it reflects, but it would be difficult to measure them over time and in conjunction with firm performance, ITI, and environmental turbulence. Such future research might benefit from longitudinal studies based on cases or periodic surveys.

Finally, we are not able to account for time-variant heterogeneity at the firm level, although we do control for unobserved heterogeneity at the level of industry and year and account for the unobserved heterogeneity at the firm level (i.e., the time-invariant heterogeneity) using fixed- and random-effect models as suggested by Greene (2011) and reported in Appendix C.

## Implications for Research

Despite the above limitations, the paper has several potential implications for research. First, it has sought to address the previous inconsistencies regarding the direct effect of SITA on firm performance. Although most prior empirical studies find SITA to positively affect firm performance, no study has hitherto found this positive effect when also explicitly including ITI. Our results indicate that SITA, as a state, consistently has a positive main effect on firm performance, when also including a variety of other factors, including ITI, environmental dynamism, complexity, and munificence, organizational slack, and other control variables.

Second, while the existing literature on SITA regards it as a state with expected positive effects on firm performance, this paper extends this view and suggests that SITA also reflects a capability. Specifically, this paper indicates that SITA operates to enhance or erode the value of ITI depending on environmental uncertainty. This view complements the existing theories of SITA and suggests that a joint consideration of the extent of SITA, ITI, and environmental uncertainty dimensions (i.e., dynamism, complexity, and munificence), instead of considering the extent of SITA in isolation, provides a better explanation of how SITA contributes to the performance of a firm. Specifically, considering environmental uncertainty while examining the role of SITA’s reflected capability in leveraging ITI provides theoretical clarity about the conditions in which SITA may erode value from ITI. Moreover, this paper’s results offer a more nuanced view of the effects of environmental uncertainty, where it affects the way in which SITA, as reflecting either a capability or a rigidity, helps or hurts the leveraging of ITI to improve firm performance. Thus, the findings from the emergent model indicate a significant shift in understanding of the role of the environment in conjunction with SITA and ITI.

Third, this paper extends the current empirical evidence on the performance effects of SITA by (1) considering a marketoriented and forward-looking measure of firm performance (i.e., Tobin’s q), and (2) examining the lagged effects of SITA. Prior literature on SITA has mainly focused on perceptual or accounting measures of firm performance. This study’s use of Tobin’s q to measure firm performance provides empirical evidence that the performance value of SITA extends beyond perceptual and backward-looking measures. Also, the longitudinal nature of our data offers evidence regarding the effects of SITA on firm performance in the following year, indicating the temporal precedence of SITA over firm performance.

## Implications for Practice

The paper also has some potentially important implications for practice, which are summarized in Figure 6. The results indicate that even when the effects of ITI and environmental uncertainty are explicitly included, SITA as a state does have a direct positive effect on firm performance. This direct effect of SITA is found in all types of environments.

The results indicate that senior executives of firms in dynamic, complex, and hostile environments should unequivocally try to align ITI with their business strategy. They should actively seek IT knowledge, participate in IT planning, and involve senior IT executives in strategic planning. If they do so, the firm’s performance would improve not only due to the direct effect of SITA, but also because SITA would help leverage ITI to improve firm performance.

By contrast, senior executives of firms in stable, simple, and munificent environments should pursue SITA with caution. In such environments, SITA as a state directly helps improve firm performance but it seems to reflect a potential rigidity that reduces the positive effect of ITI on performance. Thus, senior executives of firms in such environments should pursue SITA but be careful in establishing ties, such as IT participation in strategic business planning or senior business executives’ participation in strategic IT planning, that could lead to rigidity or myopia.

## Conclusion

This paper finds strong empirical support for both the direct effect of SITA on firm performance and its moderating effect on the relationship between ITI and firm performance. It provides new insights into the effects of SITA on firm performance by recognizing its dual nature as a state and as reflecting a capability or rigidity, depending on the environment. Based on panel data analysis of secondary data while using several control variables, it finds that (1) as a state, SITA enhances firm performance under all environments; and (2) in a dynamic, complex, or hostile environment, SITA reflects a capability that enhances the positive effect of ITI on firm performance; but (3) in a stable, simple, or munificent environment, SITA, surprisingly, reflects a rigidity that reduces the positive effect of ITI on firm performance.

## Appendix A

## States of Low SITA–High Social/Structural Integration and High SITA–Low Social/Structural Integration

## High Environmental Uncertainty

In an uncertain environment, unpredictability increases organizational entropy (or disorder) and the likelihood of divergence between IT and business strategies. Social/structural mechanisms that enable internal order in the face of uncertainty are needed to achieve a high level of SITA. Therefore, a state of low SITA–high social/structural integration would quickly lead to either (1) the high social/structural integration enabling an increase in SITA (such as when a major environmental shift requires the firm to change its business and IT strategies, and the social/structural integration enables this while achieving SITA); or (2) the low SITA causing a decrease in the social/structural integration (because the business and IT executives may communicate less and have lower shared understanding) (Hirschheim and Sabherwal 2001; Sabherwal et al. 2001).

Also, a firm in an uncertain environment would not be able to sustain a state of high SITA–low social/structural integration. Instead, it would need to either develop social/structural integration or encounter a decrease in SITA. This parallels the arguments that organizations overcome entropy arising from the environment by using “proper organization culture, structures, processes, and resources” (Tan and Tan 2005, pp. 144- 145) and that SITA “result[s] from skill rather than luck” (Baker et al. 2011, p. 303). Thus, a firm in an uncertain environment can have a state of either low SITA–high social/structural integration or high SITA–low social/structural integration only in a transition period. From either of these states, it would quickly move to a state consistent with the notion of SITA reflecting a capability (i.e., either high SITA–high social/structural integration, or low SITA–low social/structural integration).

## Low Environmental Uncertainty

Low environmental uncertainty reduces the likelihood of both aspects that can cause divergence between IT and business strategies, that is, organizational entropy (or disorder) and changes to organizational and IT strategies. If the level of social/structural integration was high, it would enable the firm to enhance SITA, causing a state of low SITA–high social/structural integration to be unlikely. However, a state of high SITA–low social/structural integration could exist in a less uncertain environment. In such an environment, the firm does not change its business and IT strategies, and managers do not need to be socially/structural integrated to maintain SITA. Instead, SITA would be maintained in such an environment due to organizational inertia as no significant new initiatives are pursued and neither business nor IT strategy is changing (Sabherwal et al. 2001).

## Appendix B

## Measurement of Strategic IT Alignment

Prior studies compute strategic IT alignment (SITA) (Oh and Pinsonneault 2007; Sabherwal and Chan 2001) using three broad steps: (1) identi fication of the firm’s business strategy; (2) identification of the relevant aspects of the firm’s IS strategy; and (3) calculation of alignment. We next examine how each of these steps is done in this study.

## Identification of Business Strategy

We first classify each firm’s business strategy in each year into the popular typology of Defender, Analyzer, or Prospector (Doty et al. 1993; McLaren et al. 2011; Miles and Snow 1978; Steelman et al. 2019). This typology is based on the degree of business aggressiveness, viewed as the firm’s tendency to innovate, lead, and take risks (Ferrier 2001). Prospectors, who are at one end of the spectrum, strive to innovate and benefit from the “first-mover” advantage while taking major risks (Miles et al. 1978). Defenders, at the other extreme, value gaining efficiency and finding stable and secure advantages. Analyzers, who are in the middle of the aggressiveness spectrum, are not as risk-taking as Prospectors, but are also less committed to stability (Doty et al. 1993). We classify each firm in each year into one of these three types based on six business strategy attributes (scope, liquidity, asset efficiency, fixed-asset intensity, long-range financial liability, and research and development (R&D) intensity). The measure of each attribute and its ideal value for each strategy are same as those Sabherwal and Sabherwal (2007) develop using prior literature. Consistent with the profile-deviation approach, and using prior literature, we set the ideal values for each attribute at low, medium, or high for these three strategies.

We normalize each strategic attribute using the sample mean and standard deviation for that year. The sample firms are classified into Defenders, Analyzers, and Prospectors based on the proximity of each firm’s business strategy to the ideal profiles for these strategies. We operationalize high, medium, and low values for ideal business strategy attributes as 0.5, 0.0, and –0.5, respectively (Sabherwal and Chan 2001). We use the root mean square (RMS) distances between each firm’s business strategy and the three ideal business strategies to classify each firm’s strategy to the closest ideal profile. We compute RMS as $\sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { n } d _ { i } ^ { 2 }$ where $d _ { \mathrm { i } }$ is the distance between the actual and ideal profiles in terms of variable i and n is the number of variables used in the analysis $( n = 6$ in this study). This procedure led to 2,224 firm-year observations in the sample classified into 804 Defenders, 931 Analyzers, and 489 Prospectors.

To validate the business strategy classification, we use the Scheffé (1959) test to compare the business strategies in terms of several strategic attributes other than those used to identify business strategies. The results indicate that firms pursuing the three business strategies differ as expected in other aspects as well–specifically, market share, return on assets, return on sales, related diversification (measured as related entropy), cost efficiency (measured as the ratio of cost of goods sold to net sales), and firm size (in terms of the natural log of number of employees)–validating the strategy classification.<sup>1</sup>

We identify the ideal ITI profile for a firm based on its realized business strategy described above. ## Computing Strategic Alignment

Consistent with the prior literature (Delery and Doty 1996; Govindarajan 1988; Sabherwal and Chan 2001), we use the difference between the normalized score (based on the sample mean and standard deviation for the year) of each area of IT investment and the ideal value of that investment for Defenders, Prospectors, and Analyzers to compute the distance between each firm’s actual ITI profile and the ideal ITI profile based on its business strategy. As mentioned for business strategy attributes, we operationalize the ideal values as 0.5, 0, and -0.5, respectively. Next, we compute SITA by subtracting the above distance from 1, because smaller distances imply that the actual ITI profile is closer to the ideal profile.

## Robustness Analyses

In addition to the main analyses, we test several alternative robustness models to explore the generalizability and consistency of our findings, the results for which are reported in Table C1. These models include a different dependent variable (Model R1); different measures of the dimensions of environmental uncertainty (Models R2–R4); and different measures of SITA (Models R5–R9). Each model starts with the primary research model (Model 3, Table 4), and makes one change. While Tobin’s q is a forward-looking measure of performance, the existing literature (e.g., Oh and Pinsonneault 2007) has also examined the effects of SITA and ITI on accounting measures of performance such as return on assets (ROA). To further examine the generalizability of our findings, Model R1 uses ROA as an alternative and more reactive measure of firm performance (Oh and Pinsonneault 2007). Further, the existing literature (e.g., Xue et al. 2011) has used alternative measures of environmental uncertainty that we did not utilize in our main analysis. Models R2–4 utilize these alternative measures of environmental uncertainty to examine the robustness of our findings when other well-established measures are utilized. Model R2 uses the industry’s operational income volatility as the measure of environmental dynamism. Model R3 uses the log value of the reciprocal of the Herfindahl index of the market shares of all firms in the industry as the measure of complexity. Model R4 uses industry’s operational income growth as the measure of environmental munificence. The results, presented in Table C1, are consistent with the primary results.

One could argue that the specific investment ratios used in the original measurement of the IS strategy may not clearly differentiate firm strategies. Specifically, a big portion of a firm’s investment in hardware, software applications, outsourcing, or maintenance, may be due to legacy costs that move from one year to another without being affected by IT strategy. Therefore, we assess the validity of our findings when each of these aspects of IT investment is dropped from the evaluation of the IT strategy and, hence, the subsequent measurement of SITA. To do so, we calculate SITA in five alternative models (R5–R9). We test these models by excluding specific categories of ITI. Model R5 uses a measure of SITA that excludes both hardware and application development, Model R6 uses a measure of SITA that excludes hardware, Model R7 uses a measure of SITA that excludes application development, Model R8 uses a measure of SITA that excludes outsourcing, and Model R9 uses a measure of SITA that excludes miscellaneous (including maintenance) IT investments. As shown in Table C1, the results of all nine models support all five hypotheses.

Due to the possible biasing effect of unobserved heterogeneity across firms on GLS-estimated coefficients, we use firm fixed-effects and firm random-effects estimations. Fixed- and random-effect estimation models remove time-invariant unobserved heterogeneity that is not captured by the study’s control variables (Greene 2011). Models R10 and R11 in Table C2 report the estimated coefficients with fixed- and randomeffects estimation, respectively. Both these models also support all five hypotheses.

Model R12 includes two additional control variables: firm age and industry clockspeed. To calculate firm age, we approximate a firm’s year of birth as the earliest of the years (1) in which the firm is included in COMPUSTAT; (2) in which the firm appears on CRSP; and (3) in which there is a link between CRSP and COMPUSTAT (based on COMPUSTAT data item LINKDT) (Shumway 2001). To calculate industry clockspeed, we follow the approach used by Mendelson and Pillai (1999). Specifically, we first compute for each firm the mean and standard devia tion of quarterly sales over the 42 quarters. The coefficient of variation (i.e., the ratio of the standard deviation to the mean) is then used a an indicator of total variation in sales for that firm over this period. This variation has two components: variation due to deterministic temporal effects (cyclic seasonal uctuations and sales growth), and variation due to random effects (uncertainty and turbulence). For each rm, we perform a regression of log (sales) against time to evaluate the growth trend, while including independent dummy variables for quarters (binary) to control for cyclic seasonal variations. The slope of the regression is used as the growth component of sales variability. The residual value from the regression, which indicate the unpredictable variation in sales, are normalized by dividing by the mean value of log(sales). This ratio is used as a measure of the random component of sales variation, which is used as a proxy for clockspeed.

## Appendix D

## Firm Investment Announcement Analysis

Our main approach to evaluate strategic IT alignment (SITA) rests on assessing business strategy through COMPUSTAT data and IT strategy through InformationWeek data based on the allocation of IT budget to specific categories of R&D on IT, insourcing, outsourcing, hardware, application software, and maintenance. Both aspects, as with most secondary data analyses, have some limitations. Some aspects of IT expenditures may be due to time-invariant legacy costs that occur irrespective of a firm’s existing IT strategy (e.g., maintenance and support costs). Similarly, business strategy assessment based on asset and liquidity measures may be less able to detect managerial aspirations and their year-toyear changes. In an attempt to alleviate these concerns and further assess the robustness of our results, we evaluate business and IT strategies based on public investment announcements made by each firm in each year. Firms utilize public announcements of their strategic investments and actions to provide details on the strategic direction for their stakeholders (McWilliams and Siegel 1997). Thus, by assessing investments publicly announced by a firm, we use an alternative approach to measure business and IT strategies that addresses the potential limitations of high-level budgetary measures in the primary analysis. The next section describes this alternative approach to measuring business and IT strategies, which, to the best of our knowledge has not yet been done in IS research.

## Strategy Attributes

We use prior literature (Sabherwal and Chan 2001; Venkatraman 1989) to identify a different set of business strategy attributes than those used for the main analysis (Appendix B). Specifically, we evaluate the public investment announcements in a given year for a firm based on (1) defensiveness, (2) risk aversion, (3) aggressiveness, (4) proactiveness, (5)analysis, and (6) futurity. Defensiveness focuses on efficiency through cost-saving. Risk aversion reflects making conservative decisions. Aggressiveness reflects a strategic approach that sacrifices shortterm profitability for gaining more market share. Proactiveness reflects the exploration of new ideas and innovations. Analysis reflects an empirical orientation and tendency to make decisions based on factual information. Finally, futurity refers to a strategic approach that empha sizes long-term business effectiveness. The ideal profiles for Defenders, Prospectors, and Analyzers in terms of these attributes are based directly on Table 1 from Sabherwal and Chan (2001).

## Identifying Keywords for Strategy Attributes

We next develop a database containing words associated with each strategy attribute. To reduce the potential bias of generating associated words from only the authors, we first identify a large set of published scholarly articles discussing alignment and the focal attributes through a systematic literature search via Google Scholar. Scholarly articles are obtained by searching for the keyword “alignment” along with “defensiveness” (6,127 html articles), “risk aversion” (12,318), “aggressiveness” (8,873), “proactiveness” (11,884), “analysis” (13,529), or “futurity” (2,814). For each strategy attribute, we combine the html text of scholarly articles using the attribute to form a grand corpus. Next, each tim a sentence within the articles mentions the attribute, we gather the nouns and verbs in the focal sentence, the prior sentence, and subsequent sentence. For example, defensiveness is most frequently associated with efficiency, preservation, and cost reduction. The nouns and verbs most frequently<sup>5</sup> identified in relationship to an attribute are treated as its associated keywords and entered into the associated keyword matrix. ## Identifying Business and IT Strategies for Each Firm-Year Observation

Next, we gather the public investment announcements for each firm in each year from the Lexis/Nexis Academic database and combine them into a firm-year corpus. We amend the firm-year corpus with the text of relative news and blog posts covering the specific public announcements in each year from Factiva. We analyze this final firm-year corpus to identify a count of each of the associated words for the six strategy attributes for each firm in each year. The value of each of the six attributes, for each firm for each year, is calculated as the ratio of words associated with the attribute in the corpus, divided by the total number of words in the corpus. We use the same approach and the same set of keywords for each strategy attribute for business and IT strategies, but use additional search terms related to IT investment and non-IT investments for IT strategy and business strategy, respectively. For both sets of announcements, we exclude news or announcements involving more than one firm. Figure D2 depicts the process of capturing and measuring business and IT strategies from this approach.

## IT Strategy

We estimate each firm’s IT strategy from all the public IT-related announcements. For each year, we form a search string, including the name of the firm; and any of the keywords: “spend,” “invest,” “investment,” or “expenditure”; and any of the key words: “information system,” “information technology,” “computer system,” “communication technology,” or any of the ITs listed in Information Week’s trending ITs in the given year. This provided a total of 8,617 pieces of text from 1,679 unique announcements about IT investments made by 164 firms from 1999-2008, resulting in 506 firm-year corpuses of ITI announcement text.

## Business Strategy

To estimate the business strategy of each firm, we analyze all the public announcements, excluding any IT-related announcements. Specifically, for each year, we form a search string that consists of the name of the firm; and any of the keywords: “spend,” “invest,” “investment,” or “expenditure.” This produces a total of 34,148 pieces of text from 4,665 unique announcements about non-IT investments made by 183 firms from 1999–2008, resulting in 571 firm-year corpuses of non-IT investment announcement text.

## Measuring Strategic IT Alignment

We use the 571 firm-year observations for non-IT investment announcements and the 507 firm-year observations for IT announcements in three alternative approaches for business and IT strategies, as depicted in Figure 4 of the main paper. Two of the three approaches with alternative data (alternative measures 1 and 2) use profile deviation to compute SITA, as in our main analysis. The third measure (alternative measure 3) uses score matching, with SITA for each firm computed using business and IT strategies identified from the public announcements. Due to the need for matched firm-year observations with corpuses of key terms related to both non-IT (571 firm-year observations) and IT investments (507 firm-year observations), the sample for this analysis includes 388 firm-year observations. Having used the same six focal attributes to evaluate business and IT strategies (Sabherwal and Chan 2001), we use a score-matching approach to calculate SITA. For attribute i, we subtract the value of each attribute for IT and non-IT announcements and take the absolute value of the difference, |D |. For example, if a firm’s IT defensiveness is rated high (0.5) and its business defensiveness is rated low (-0.5), the absolute difference of defensiveness between the IT and business strategies would be one (1=|0.5–(-0.5)|). We then compute SITA by averaging the value of absolute differences for all the six attributes and subtracting it from one (1-(3<sub>i</sub>|D<sub>i</sub>|)/6).

## Appendix E

## Analyses of SITA as a Rigidity

In order to further evaluate if SITA reflects a rigidity in less uncertain environments, we conduct three additional analyses. In these analyses, we divide the sample into four quadrants: low uncertainty–high SITA (Q1); low uncertainty–low SITA (Q2); high uncertainty–high SITA (Q3); and high uncertainty–low SITA (Q4).

The first analysis compares the correlation of ITI with firm performance between Q1 and Q2, and between Q1 and Q3. If SITA reflects a rigidity in less uncertain environment, firms in Q1 should be less able to reap benefits from ITI compared to firms in Q2 and Q3.

Further, a rigidity in year t-1 should decrease the ability of a firm to change its level of SITA from year t-1 to year t. Therefore, the second analysis creates the above four quadrants using the values of uncertainty and SITA in year t-1 and compares the mean proportional change in the level of SITA, from year t-1 to year t between Q1 and Q2, and between Q1 and Q3.

Finally, a rigidity in year t-1 should decrease the ability of a firm to change its level of investment in different components of IT, from year t-1 to year t. Therefore, the third analysis creates the above four quadrants using the value of uncertainty and SITA in year t-1 and compare the mean proportional change in the levels of the six components of IT investment (i.e., R&D in IT, IT insourcing, IT outsourcing, IT hardware, software, and IT maintenance), from year t-1 to year t between Q1 and Q2, and between Q1 and Q3.

We conducted these comparisons based on quadrants for each of the three dimensions of uncertainty. Cumulatively, these three analyses involve 48 comparisons (six each for the first two analyses, and 36 for the third analysis).
