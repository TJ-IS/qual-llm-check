# MORE IS NOT NECESSARILY BETTER: AN ABSORPTIVE CAPACITY PERSPECTIVE ON NETWORK EFFECTS IN OPEN SOURCE SOFTWARE DEVELOPMENT COMMUNITIES<sup>1</sup>

Tanya (Ya) Tang Isenberg School of Management, University of Massachusetts, Amherst, 121 Presidents Drive, Amherst, MA 01003 U.S.A. {yatang@isenberg.umass.edu}

Eric (Er) Fang College of Business, Lehigh University, 621 Taylor Street, Bethlehem, PA 18015 U.S.A. {erf219@lehigh.edu}

William J. Qualls Gies College of Business, University of Illinois at Urbana–Champaign, 1206 S. 6<sup>th</sup> Street, Champaign, IL 61820 U.S.A. {wqualls@illlinois.edu}

Previous research has predominantly taken a social network perspective suggesting that building more network connections or becoming deeply embedded in a network provides a better position to access network knowledge in open source software communities.This perspective implicitly assumes that accessed network knowledge automatically gets absorbed and transferred to projects, so that building more and deeper network connections is beneficial: Drawing from an absorptive capacity perspective, this research challenges such conventional wisdom, arguing instead that the benefits depend on a project’s absorptive capacity. Network connections provide access to external knowledge in the community; the absorption and transfer of this new knowledge require appropriate internal knowledge and developer roles. With longitudinal data collected from 4,518 open source software development projects hosted at SourceForge, the authors show that knowledge breadth (depth) helps with the absorption of external knowledge achieved from network depth (breadth), but it inhibits the absorption of external knowledge obtained from network breadth (depth). Further, developer roles (e.g., bridge members, role diversity) can mitigate the negative consequences of suboptimal combinations and facilitate effective transfers of absorbed external knowledge across and within projects. These findings provide important theoretical and managerial implications for managing network connections, knowledge, and developer roles in open source software communities.

Keywords: Open source software development, network breadth, network depth, knowledge breadth, knowledge depth, bridge members, role diversity

## Introduction

Open source software (OSS) development, which represents a version of the open innovation development model (von

Krogh and von Hippel 2006), offers a viable, popular alternative to traditional, firm-based, closed software development approaches (von Hippel and von Krogh 2003), as exemplified by vastly successful open source projects such as the Linux operating system, R for statistical computing, and the Android mobile operating system. The success of OSS development, stemming largely from its greater innovativeness, security, customization capacity, and code quality compared with commercial software development (von Krogh et al. 2012), has contributed to open innovation communities that allow voluntary developers to collaborate, develop the software they need, and share source code. The self-generated, evolving relationships between developers across projects result in complex network connections that support widespread access to knowledge and skills beyond project membership boundaries, thereby facilitating new knowledge creation (Grewal et al. 2006; Singh, Tan, and Mookerjee 2011).

With a social network perspective, most previous research suggests that building more network connections or becoming more deeply embedded in a network provides a better position from which to access key community-held knowledge, thus directly determining OSS success (Grewal et al. 2006; Phelps et al. 2012; Singh, Tan, and Mookerjee 2011). This positive view of social networks emphasizes the benefits of network connections and embeddedness with an implicit assumption that network knowledge accessed through network positions is absorbed and transferred automatically to the OSS project. It equates knowledge access with knowledge absorption and transfer (Daniel and Stewart 2016).

The many failed OSS projects, in practice, have suggested that this assumption should be reexamined. For example, the notable GNU microkernel project, GNU Hurd, was started in 1990 by Richard Stallman, the founder of the GNU project and the Free Software Foundation, together with other famous developers who had tremendous network resources in OSS communities. Even so, it failed to compete with the Linux kernel because the developers were cognitively locked into an idealistic belief in the microkernel architecture that would take a longer time to accomplish than using the monolithic architecture used by Linux.

In this sense, highly connected or embedded OSS projects may fail to benefit from their access to network knowledge if they lack absorptive capacity, namely the ability to identify, assimilate, transform, and apply external knowledge, which is collectively referred to as the ability to absorb external knowledge (Cohen and Levinthal 1990; Roberts et al. 2012).<sup>2</sup> A project’s absorptive capacity depends not only on the absorptive capacities of its individual members, but also on transfers of knowledge across and within the project (Cohen and Levinthal 1990). Thus, a project’s absorptive capacity is formed from two interrelated capabilities: knowledge absorption, which refers to a project’s ability to configure internal knowledge to absorb external knowledge, and knowledge transfer, which refers to the ability to transfer the absorbed knowledge across and within the project (Roberts et al. 2012). This distinction differentiates technical knowledge and skills required in OSS development from mechanisms to facilitate the transfers of such knowledge and skills.

Knowledge absorption capability depends largely on a project’s internal knowledge that enables associative connections with external knowledge to create new knowledge (Cohen and Levinthal 1990). Research in cognitive science (e.g., Bower and Hilgard 1981) clearly establishes the fact that new knowledge creation is a cumulative process that requires both relevant knowledge and an intensive effort to absorb external knowledge. To leverage the network knowledge obtained from the OSS community, developers must expend time and effort to understand the code from the connected projects, add to or modify their code, and find ways to integrate the two sources of code. In extreme cases, a development team without any prior knowledge about software development, even if it were to build network connections to the most successful OSS projects, probably cannot take advantage of these network resources because it would lack the relevant knowledge to realize the value of the external knowledge. Further, across projects with the same level of knowledge absorption capability, project teams may vary in their ability to transfer external knowledge across and within projects to facilitate such knowledge absorption. For example, in OSS development, bridge members (or boundary spanners) who determine how external knowledge is transferred from outside projects to a focal project may increase or decrease the project’s ability to transfer the external knowledge. A project with a greater ability to transfer external knowledge can enhance mutual understanding and comprehension of the absorbed external knowledge, so that it can become better positioned to leverage absorbed external knowledge to improve performance outcomes (Zahra and George 2002).

The previous OSS literature has noted the importance of gaining knowledge access through network connections and embeddedness from a social network perspective, yet less attention has been devoted to the importance of a project’s absorptive capacity in OSS communities. Because OSS projects are self-organizing and the hierarchical structure that exists within firms is not directly manifested in communitybased models, questions about how to absorb and transfer diverse, complex external knowledge through different network connections and embeddedness, thereby enhancing a project’s success, represent substantial challenges to these communities (Singh, Tan, and Mookerjee 2011).

From an absorptive capacity perspective (Cohen and Levinthal 1990; Roberts et al. 2012), we examine how an OSS project can configure its internal knowledge and developer roles to increase its absorptive capacity to absorb and transfer network knowledge in the OSS community. In our proposed model, a project’s network position provides access to external knowledge that is beyond its project boundary based on membership; internal knowledge embodies a project’s ability to absorb external knowledge; the effect of knowledge absorption on a project’s success is contingent upon its ability to transfer the absorbed external knowledge across and within projects, according to developer roles.

We disaggregate network positions and internal knowledge according to their breadth and depth (Carlo et al. 2012; Grewal et al. 2006). Network breadth reflects the scope of direct connections that a project has with other projects in a network; network depth refers to the distance of its embeddedness by which a project reaches all other projects in a network (Freeman 1979; Grewal et al. 2006). Knowledge breadth captures the scope and diversity of internal knowledge associated with a project across different technical domains; knowledge depth represents the level and quality of internal knowledge associated with a project within all technical domains (Carlo et al. 2012; de Luca and Atuahene-Gima 2007). Further, we consider the network and functional roles of developers: bridge members that refers to the number of developers who participate in multiple projects to introduce external knowledge to a focal project, and role diversity that defines the degree to which developers take on different functional roles when collaborating in OSS development.

We conducted a project-level analysis using a full network approach to collect archival data from 4,518 OSS projects across multiple foundries in SourceForge. The results suggest that the network alone has no direct effect on project success, so building more or deeper network connections does not increase the chances of a project’s success. Rather, the outcomes depend on the project’s absorptive capacity. A project’s knowledge can help it absorb external knowledge if it is balanced with external knowledge: knowledge breadth can absorb external knowledge more readily from network depth, whereas knowledge depth can absorb external knowledge more easily from network breadth. Further, a project with a centralized bridge member can mitigate the negative effect of the unbalanced combination between network breadth and knowledge breadth; role diversity helps reduce the negative impact of the unbalanced combination between network depth and knowledge depth.

These results make several contributions to the OSS community literature. First, this paper cautions against misusing the social network perspective to examine the effects of network connections without considering the internal capabilities of a project. The social network perspective is well suited to studying the diffusion of information and social influence in a network but is insufficient for OSS success or related outcomes that demand extensive technical knowledge and effort (Grewal et al. 2006; Méndez-Durón and Garcia 2009; Peng and Dey 2013; Singh, Tan, and Mookerjee 2011; Temizkan and Kumar 2015; Wu et al. 2007). As we illustrate, network connections can help identify valuable external knowledge, but OSS success requires a project’s absorptive capacity to absorb and transfer external knowledge.

Second, we propose absorptive capacity as an alternative theoretical lens through which to study the OSS phenomenon and provide theoretical explanations for mixed findings about the effects of network connections, either positive (Colazo 2010; Grewal et al. 2006; Singh, Tan, and Mookerjee 2011; Temizkan and Kumar 2015; Wu et al. 2007), negative (Colazo 2010; Grewal et al. 2006; Wu et al. 2007), or nonsignificant (Peng and Dey 2013). Knowledge and developer roles constitute two important development resources in OSS and are associated uniquely with a project’s ability to absorb and transfer external knowledge (Cohen and Levinthal 1990). Our study reveals the conditions under which network effects change according to unique configurations in internal knowledge and developer roles.

Third, in a challenge to the traditional “more is better” view, we suggest a balanced perspective on internal knowledge and network connections. Rather than acknowledging the coordination and cognitive costs associated with such relationships, most studies focus on the benefits of network connections. Some exceptions (e.g., Peng and Dey 2013; Peng et al. 2013; Singh, Tan, and Mookerjee 2011) offer empirical evidence of the non-positive effects of network connections on project performance, but they only explore network characteristics. We argue that as the bright and dark sides of network effects always present, a true balance happens between internal knowledge and external network, not the network alone.

Fourth, this empirical study took a full network approach and used snowball sampling to identify the largest connected network (n = 38,079) in an OSS community. Previous studies have relied on selected foundries in an OSS community (e.g., Python in Singh, Tan, and Mookerjee 2011; Perl in Grewal et al. 2006; C language in Temizkan and Kumar 2015), ignoring network connections outside of the selected foundries that may lead to biased measures of network connections and embeddedness (Hanneman and Riddle 2005). Within our full network approach, we provide a more comprehensive picture of the OSS community and enhance the validity and generalizability of the findings.

## Theoretical Background

## New Knowledge Creation Rate

Our theoretical arguments and subsequent hypotheses rely on a project’s capabilities to absorb and transfer external knowledge to create new knowledge; hence, our dependent variable should represent new knowledge created by a project. Because software is a knowledge product and 83% of OSS projects fail due to lack of sustained development activities (Schweik and English 2012), research has suggested that new knowledge creation rate, or how continuously and efficiently a project completes development activities to create new knowledge, provides a good measure of project success (Singh, Tan, and Mookerjee 2011; Singh, Tan, and Youn 2011; Temizkan and Kumar 2015).

Extant research on software development has suggested the completion of modification requests (MRs) as a good measure of project success in commercial software development (Boh et al. 2007). The MRs measure, similar to a work order, represents how efficient a project is to complete MRs to add new functionality or modify old functionality (e.g., fixing bugs) (Temizkan and Kumar 2015). In OSS development, the number of commit transactions, comparable to the MRs measure, measures significant changes to source code and reflects a project’s productivity in the use and reuse of absorbed external knowledge to adapt to existing knowledge to create new knowledge. Following the OSS literature, we theorize a project’s capabilities to absorb and transfer network knowledge to increase project success in terms of new knowledge creation rate (Daniel and Stewart 2016; Grewal et al. 2006; Peng et al. 2013; Singh, Tan, and Mookerjee 2011; Singh, Tan, and Youn 2011; Tan et al. 2007).

## Social Network Perspective on OSS Development

Social network theory is a dominant view used to detail the effects of network connections and embeddedness on various OSS phenomena, including new knowledge sharing and creation (Long and Siau 2008; Singh, Tan, and Mookerjee 2011; Temizkan and Kumar 2015), OSS performance and success (Barbagallo et al. 2008; Daniel et al. 2018; Daniel and Stewart 2016; Grewal et al. 2006; Liu and Iyer 2007; Méndez-Durón and Garcia 2009; Peng 2019; Peng et al. 2013; Sutanto et al. 2014; Tan et al. 2007; Wu et al. 2007), new technology or license adoption (Peng and Dey 2013; Singh and Phelps 2012), project reputation (Hu et al. 2012; Méndez-Durón 2013), and project formation (Hahn et al. 2008) (see Appendix A for a comprehensive review on the social network perspective of OSS development).<sup>3</sup> Social network theory suggests that the network position maintained by a project can have significant effects on its access to external knowledge in the network (Grewal et al. 2006; Kogut 2000). In OSS contexts, software development involves collaboration among developers working on a team that defines their affiliations with the project based on membership. Often, developers participate in multiple projects and can serve as “bridges” that connect different projects, therebycreating development community networks (Fleming and Waguespack 2007). Bridge members’ affiliations with other projects make project membership permeable and fluid (Faraj et al. 2011) and provide them with exposure to knowledge outside the confines of the focal OSS project. Thus, a project’s network connections in the community provide knowledge access to external knowledge possessed by other projects.

Early social network research mostly attempted to provide descriptive statistics of network characteristics. For example, Madey et al. (2002, p. 1806) found that an OSS community exhibits the properties of “self-organizing, collaboration, social networks.” Xu et al. (2005) explored patterns of collaboration in OSS and confirmed that communities display features of the small-world phenomenon and scale-free behavior. More recently, studies have introduced additional theories to predict the impacts of social relations on OSS outcomes. Grewal et al. (2006) applied the concept of embeddedness from organizational sociology to predict how a network’s structural, junctional, and positional embeddedness affect an OSS project’s success. Hahn et al. (2008) argued that prior collaborative ties serve to create cohesion and status cues that influence developers’ choices of OSS projects. Drawing on social capital theory, Singh, Tan, and Mookerjee (2011) examined how a project’s internal and external cohesion with other projects affect its success. With a basis in organizational ambidexterity theory, Temizkan and Kumar (2015) identified patch development and feature requests as exploitation and exploration activities, respectively, then described how the structure of social networks affects the success of two networks in OSS projects.

Although the literature in this stream has grown tremendously in the past decade, generating many interesting insights into OSS community-based development, our literature review reveals some issues and gaps. First, with a few exceptions (Daniel et al. 2018; Daniel and Stewart 2016; Singh, Tan, and Mookerjee 2011), most studies (75% of the articles that we reviewed) assumed that information and knowledge accessed from networks are automatically absorbed and transferred to the project. These studies linked social network variables directly to OSS outcomes without including any variables that affect knowledge absorption and transfer. Such an implicit assumption may be less problematic for studying phenomena such as OSS license adoption (Singh and Phelps 2012), subversion (SVN) adoption (Peng and Dey 2013), or joining a project (Hahn et al. 2008), as these actions do not require the integration of internal and external resources and networks can diffuse information or social influence throughout the community. However, phenomena such as knowledge sharing, knowledge transfer, new knowledge creation (Singh, Tan, and Mookerjee 2011; Temizkan and Kumar 2015), or OSS success (Daniel and Stewart 2016; Grewal et al. 2006; Méndez-Durón and Garcia 2009; Peng et al. 2013; Tan et al. 2007; Wu et al. 2007) all require the tremendous effort of developers to combine knowledge from their internal and external sources. Hence, linking the network variables directly to OSS outcomes but ignoring the project’s ability to absorb and transfer the network knowledge may lead to biased, inaccurate conclusions about the network effects.

Second, empirical findings of network effects are mixed, which makes it difficult to build cumulative learning and advance theoretical understanding. Various concepts from social network theory have been used to study OSS development, including degree centrality (Daniel and Stewart 2016; Grewal et al. 2006; Liu and Iyer 2007; Long and Siau 2008; Méndez-Durón 2013; Peng 2019; Peng and Day 2013; Peng et al. 2013; Temizkan and Kumar 2015; Wu et al. 2007; Xu et al. 2005), betweenness centrality (Grewal et al. 2006; Sutanto et al. 2014), eigenvector centrality (Grewal et al. 2006), prior ties (Hahn et al. 2008), structural holes (Peng and Day2013; Singh, Tan, and Mookerjee 2011), social proximity and equivalence (Singh and Phelps 2012), internal cohesion (Singh, Tan, and Mookerjee 2011; Temizkan and Kumar 2015), and external cohesion (Singh, Tan, and Mookerjee 2011). Degree centrality is the most studied concept, but it produces the most confusing results. Grewal et al. (2006) found that the effects of degree centrality on OSS success are complex—positive in some regimes but negative in others. Colazo (2010) and Wu et al. (2007) offered contradictory results related to OSS project quality and development activities. According to Peng and Dey (2013), the effects of network centrality are significant for SVN technology adoption, but the effects of network closure are not. Thus, theoretical mechanisms appear to be missing from the extant literature, preventing a clear understanding of the mixed network effects on various OSS outcomes.

Third, except for some descriptive studies (Hu and Zhao 2008; Madey et al. 2002; Xu et al. 2005), most empirical research that we reviewed were based on preselected foundries in OSS communities to detail the network effects, including C++ and Java languages in Daniel and Stewart (2016), Perl in Grewal et al. (2006), Java in Peng et al. (2013), Python in Singh, Tan, and Mookerjee (2011), PHP in Tan et al. (2007), and C language in Temizkan and Kumar (2015). This approach makes it easier for researchers to collect data and limit the computational power that increases exponentially with network size. However, in social network analysis, for which the results rely inherently on the definition of network links and boundaries, a preselected foundry approach may ignore critical network connections outside of the selected foundry, leading to biased estimates of network effects on OSS out comes. This limitation provides a plausible empirical explanation for why the previous literature offered mixed results about network effects.

## Absorptive Capacity Perspective on OSS Development

To address these theoretical and empirical issues, we take an absorptive capacity perspective on the largest connected network in an OSS community, and argue that an advantageous network location provides opportunities to access external knowledge, but whether a project can leverage such opportunities depends on its absorptive capacity—that is, its ability to absorb and transfer external knowledge (Cohen and Levinthal 1990; see Figure 1). A project’s absorptive capacity is based on its ability to absorb external knowledge (knowledge absorption) as well as its ability to transfer new knowledge across and within projects (knowledge transfer). Such a distinction separates internal knowledge that helps a project’s absorption of external knowledge from mechanisms that facilitate the transfers of this knowledge, reflecting an important conceptualization of absorption capacity at the project level. As illustrated by Roberts et al. (2012, p. 627),

While a knowledge base enables the associative connections needed for insights related to new knowledge, the organizational assimilation of new knowledge depends more so upon the transfer of knowledge across and within subunits.

## Knowledge Absorption Capability

Internal knowledge forms the content of a project’s knowledge absorption capacity and reflects its ability to absorb external knowledge (Cohen and Levinthal 1990). Research in the cognitive and behavioral sciences (Bower and Hilgard 1981) suggests that relevant knowledge influences knowledge absorption by enabling the assessment of external knowledge, encouraging associative learning by establishing links with preexisting concepts, and enhancing the ability to recall and use that knowledge. The knowledge itself may include “know-what” content, “know-how” problem-solving skills, or “know where and who” network information. In OSS communities, software development requires a range of technical skills related to programming languages, data structures, operating systems, and human–computer interfaces. By participating in OSS project development activities, developers introduce and contribute their own knowledge bases and problem-solving skills across the knowledge domains. If problems are beyond developers’ knowledge domains, they also introduce network knowledge about who knows what, who can help with which problem, or who can exploit new knowledge through social networks in an OSS community. Thus, a project’s internal knowledge constitutes a key component of absorptive capacity that facilitates or inhibits its ability to absorb external knowledge (Roberts et al. 2012).

We decompose internal knowledge into breadth and depth, as they are two major dimensions that influence a project’s propensity to absorb new knowledge (Lane et al. 2006). Knowledge breadth captures a project’s horizontal dimension of knowledge, whereas depth captures the vertical dimension. In an OSS setting, which is marked by uncertainty about where potentially useful knowledge from external networks might emerge, knowledge breadth increases knowledge absorption capability by changing internal knowledge configurations to absorb new knowledge in light of the number of combinatorial possibilities that emerge when more knowledge domains are represented (Carlo et al. 2012). With deeply contextualized knowledge and expertise, developers can leverage knowledge absorption capability by fluently understanding technical development, building stronger associations, and easing the process of deriving insights from the combination of existing and newly acquired knowledge (Zahra and George 2002).

## Knowledge Transfer Capability

The effectiveness of absorptive capacity also depends on social integration factors that determine transfers of knowledge across and within projects (Cohen and Levinthal 1990). Social integration factors build social interactions and shared meaning, promote mutual understanding and comprehension of absorbed external knowledge, and lead to the eventual use of the new knowledge, thus representing a project’s ability to transfer knowledge across and within projects.

Unlike internal knowledge that helps a project absorb external knowledge, social integration factors do not directly absorb external knowledge. Instead, they moderate the relationship between knowledge absorption and performance outcomes by facilitating or inhibiting the transfer of absorbed new knowledge. If common knowledge is not shared between external projects and the focal project, social integration factors offer no means to transfer network knowledge to the development process. As Todorova and Durisin (2007) elaborated, the moderating influence of social integration likely affects knowledge absorption, resulting in either a positive or negative effect on performance outcomes, depending on specific contingencies.

Many factors may influence such social integration processes, and researchers have examined system, socialization, and coordination factors in organizational settings (Van den Bosch et al. 1999). System factors of organizational structures, policies, and routines—for example, organization hierarchy structure (Van den Bosch et al. 1999), level of centralization (Gupta and Govindarajan 2000; Lane et al. 2001), formalization (Jansen et al. 2005), compensation policies (Lane and Lubatkin 1998)—are laid down in formal systems to transfer knowledge through formal exchange mechanisms such as procedures, codes, working manuals, and information systems. Socialization factors capture a firm’s ability to produce a shared ideology with consistent beliefs, values, and behavioral norms resulting from a firm’s culture to facilitate knowledge transfer (Nahapiet and Ghoshal 1998; Todorova and Durisin 2007). Coordination factors—for example, interface function (Cohen and Levinthal 1990), cross-function interface (Cohen and Levinthal 1990; Jansen et al. 2005), job rotation (Jansen et al. 2005), task forces (Gupta and Govindarajan 2000) interactive discussion (Daniel and Stewart 2016)—build lateral interactions and coordination that enhance knowledge transfers across inter- and intra-organizational boundaries.

In an OSS context, because developers are often located in diverse geographies and projects lack the formal and structural hierarchies and routines, system and socialization factors (e.g., organizational structure, centralization, formalization, and culture) that mark commercial software development’s sharing and transfer of new knowledge are not applicable in OSS development (Faraj et al. 2011). We focus on coordination factors in OSS development, primarily developer roles that function as informal mechanisms to facilitate transfers of newly absorbed external knowledge by determining knowledge flows and dictating interaction patterns during coordination (Daniel et al. 2013).

In particular, two OSS developer roles are closely associated with transfers of external knowledge. First, bridge members create connections with outside projects and control the external knowledge that gets transferred into the focal project. Developers who are in this role can function as gatekeepers and introduce useful information and knowledge—including new ideas, algorithms, and development techniques—from other projects to the focal project (Fleming and Waguespack 2007; Peng et al. 2013). Second, developers have been found to take different functional roles that contribute to OSS communities (Setia et al. 2012; Zhang et al. 2013). These functional roles reflect developers’ dependence on one another for meeting development requirements, and their diversity indicates how external knowledge gets transferred across developers during the development and coordination processes (Daniel et al. 2013). Overall, bridge members determine knowledge transfer across projects and role diversity captures knowledge transfer within a project; they represent two important network and functional roles found to be critical to OSS performance (von Krogh and von Hippel 2006).

## Hypotheses

We consider both the positive and negative sides of network connections and internal knowledge and develop hypotheses about how a project’s configuration of internal knowledge can increase or decrease its ability to absorb external knowledge. Following the absorptive capacity literature (Cohen and Levinthal 1990; Lane et al. 2006; March 1991; Roberts et al. 2012), we suggest that internal knowledge needs to be balanced with external networks to leverage the benefits of network breadth and depth while minimizing their associated downsides. If internal knowledge is not optimal for absorbing external knowledge from network positions, we further posit how a project can configure its developer roles to improve its knowledge transfer ability, thus increasing new knowledge creation rate (see Figure 2 for our conceptual framework).

## Knowledge Access Effects of Network Breadth and Network Depth

Projects accumulate different network resources and access varied external knowledge depending on their connections (network breadth) and embeddedness (network depth) in the OSS development community. Network breadth refers to the scope of accessed knowledge through network connections by which a project directly connects to other projects in a network to access external knowledge. According to social network theory, network breadth provides broad access to heterogeneous external knowledge that is owned by connected projects, offering alternative ways to quickly use or reuse that external knowledge to create new knowledge for the focal projects (Tsai and Ghoshal 1998). For instance, OSS development often requires evaluations of alternative solutions to a wide range of problems from deciding on programming languages to designing the web interfaces to porting across different operation systems to building databases. Broad network connections may offer solutions that have already tested by connected projects for similar problems, reducing cognitive effort in development processes and increasing new knowledge creation rate (Peng et al. 2013).

On the flip side, network breadth also increases coordination burdens by competing for developers’ attention across projects and demanding greater effort to integrate knowledge from different paths (Fang 2008). Unlike knowledge that may be shared without being depleted, developers’ attention is finite. They must devote effort to building and maintaining connections with external projects. Switching frequently across projects will cause their development to be less efficient (Daniel and Stewart 2016). For example, Freeplane, a successful project management OSS, connects with four external projects: Lunar Eclipse (a collection of plugins for

Each of these projects pursues different development purposes and demands different sets of technical knowledge. To take advantage of their insights, Freeplane developers must evaluate the reusable components of the four external projects, design interfaces to link them together, and ensure that integrated codes can function properly as a whole package (Peng et al. 2013). Griffin (1993, p. 9) noted, “as the number of different technical inputs to projects increases, stronger coordination within the group is required.” Such coordination effort grows exponentially with the number of external projects to which a project connects, hindering the productivity of development activities and new knowledge creation rate.

Network depth encapsulates the distance of network embeddedness from which a project reaches out to other directly and indirectly connected projects in a network. Network depth allows projects to rapidly access relevant information without any control by others, ensuring “fewer message transmissions, shorter times, and lower costs” (Freeman 1979, p. 225). A project marked by greater network depth has a better overall picture of the community, remains up to date on technical and market trends, gains problem-solving skills quickly, and recognizes “who knows what” easily. Therefore, it can speedily create new knowledge to meet new technical requirements (Maruping et al. 2009).

On the other side, locations that are proximal to other developers may lead to the circulation of similar or redundant information when filtering alternative information, generating a cognitive lock-in that dampens new knowledge creation rate (Leonard-Barton 1992). For instance, SourceForge, which was once the largest OSS communities, uses SVN as a version control system for all its projects. In contrast, Git is a relatively new, decentralized version control system that allows developers to work on code even when they are offline. Because of the benefits of Git, many OSS developers have moved their projects over to the GitHub OSS community. Projects that are deeply entrenched in SourceForge and its SVN system are likely less motivated to stay updated about new developments to Git. Such cognitive lock-in can inhibit developers’ ability to explore ideas from outside the network and hinder their motivation to continue to work on the projects, leading to a decreased new knowledge creation rate (Grabher 1993).<sup>4</sup>

## Knowledge Absorption Effects of Internal Knowledge

Knowledge breadth and depth also display similar positive and negative patterns on new knowledge creation in OSS development. In OSS development, technical knowledge changes quickly. Projects that can configure their internal knowledge to align with their network positions are able to balance the exploration of new development opportunities and the exploitation of existing competencies to guarantee their optimal performance (He and Wong 2004). Dominance in either the breadth or depth dimension of knowledge absorption may eventually make projects dysfunctional (Cohen and Levinthal 1990).

## Knowledge Breadth

Knowledge breadth helps an OSS project absorb external knowledge by increasing the likelihood that external knowledge will be relevant to internal knowledge and providing different configurations for interpreting and contextualizing external knowledge (Carlo et al. 2012; Van den Bosch et al. 1999). However, broad knowledge might also increase the complexity of knowledge integration with external knowledge, leading to inefficient knowledge absorption and new knowledge creation (de Luca and Atuahene-Gima 2007).

Absorbing external knowledge from network breadth. We posit that knowledge breadth diminishes the benefits of broad access provided by network breadth and worsens the negative impact of coordination issues associated with network breadth, with an overall negative effect on new knowledge creation rate. When internal knowledge is highly diverse, the value of greater access to broad external knowledge is likely to be limited; the internal knowledge of the project is already diverse enough to provide different perspectives on and solutions to development problems (Daniel et al. 2013). However, integrating knowledge from diverse sources creates overwhelming coordination difficulties, and internal knowledge cannot provide the common ground that is needed to efficiently incorporate the diverse network knowledge to create new knowledge (Singh, Tan, and Mookerjee 2011; Temizkan and Kumar 2015). Sometimes broad network knowledge suggests development directions that are inconsistent with current knowledge and invokes evaluations of virtually all potential directions to reveal which ones work (e.g., testing which algorithms provide the best outcomes) (Garriga et al. 2013), significantly impairing the speed of new knowledge creation (Leonard-Barton 1992). Therefore, we hypothesize

$\mathbf { H _ { 1 } }$ : Knowledge breadth hinders knowledge absorption from network breadth, such that the interaction between knowledge breadth and network breadth has a negative impact on new knowledge creation rate.

Absorbing external knowledge from network depth. We propose that knowledge breadth can help absorb external knowledge from network depth by strengthening easy, quick access and minimizing the cognitive lock-in associated with network depth, with an overall positive impact on new knowledge creation rate. Being deeply embedded in the network helps the project quickly develop awareness about technical trends and where relevant expertise is located in the community (e.g., new programming languages like JavaScript and Python, non-SQL databases including HBase and MongoDB, mobile operation system, and HTML5 as a new standard for web interface design). A broad knowledge base facilitates the evaluation of development trends from different perspectives and helps the project immediately pinpoint the most relevant or promising development directions to address technical changes (Carlo et al. 2012; Faraj and Sproull 2000). Over time, although a highly embedded project may feature only similar or redundant knowledge, reducing the chances of obtaining unique information (Grewal et al. 2006), broader internal knowledge helps overcome this constraint by providing different perspectives and possibilities in the search for new information and solutions to quickly meet always changing technical requirements. Therefore, we hypothesize

$\mathbf { H } _ { 2 } \colon$ Knowledge breadth helps knowledge absorption from network depth, such that the interaction between knowledge breadth and network depth has a positive impact on new knowledge creation rate.

## Knowledge Depth

Knowledge depth can help an OSS project absorb external knowledge by building stronger associations between existing and newly acquired knowledge, thus easing the process of incorporating transformed knowledge into development to create new knowledge (Zahra and George 2002). However, deep knowledge may also turn core competencies to core rigidities, in that developers rely too much on their existing experience and skill sets and ignore new knowledge that is inconsistent with their current understanding (Leonard-Barton 1992).

Absorbing external knowledge from network breadth. We argue that knowledge depth strengthens the broad access driven by network breadth and minimizes associated coordination burdens, with an overall positive impact on new knowledge creation rate. When broad network connections introduce diverse external knowledge, a deep knowledge base can help a project follow and understand technical developments in connected projects more fluently and, therefore more easily and quickly absorb the broad external knowledge to be integrated with existing knowledge. A deep knowledge base that enhances appreciation of the value of broad external knowledge can help contextualize broad external knowledge (e.g., reuse source code from other projects) and recontextualize absorbed knowledge (e.g., modify source code from other projects to make them compatible with the focal project) to increase new knowledge creation rate (Carlo et al. 2012; Haefliger et al. 2008). Although broad external knowledge accessed from different external projects demands more effort to absorb (Katila and Ahuja 2002), deep internal knowledge provides expertise and confidence to validate and integrate diverse network knowledge more efficiently (McEvily and Chakravarthy 2002). Therefore, we hypothesize

H : Knowledge depth helps knowledge absorption from network breadth, such that the interaction between knowledge depth and network breadth has a positive impact on new knowledge creation rate.

Absorbing external knowledge from network depth. We expect that knowledge depth reduces the benefits of easy and quick access achieved from network depth, but also worsens its associated cognitive lock-in threat, with an overall negative impact on new knowledge creation rate. Although network depth enhances quick awareness of new technical trends, in combination with deep internal knowledge, it can create a self-reinforcing trap that prevents efficient responses to technical changes or opportunities (e.g., adoption of Git to replace SVN) (Leonard-Barton 1992). As Temizkan and Kumar (2015) illustrated, too much network depth hinders a project’s new knowledge creation rate because over-embeddedness in a network provides access only to redundant information and creates a risk of learning myopia, limiting the project’s ability to efficiently explore new knowledge in the network. With deep internal knowledge and deeply embedded networks, the ability to explore external knowledge diminishes even further because a project with deeper internal knowledge has elevated confidence in its own knowledge and, hence little motivation to seek new ideas and solutions from the network to create new knowledge. As the old solution becomes obsolete, developers further lose their motivation to continue working on the project, hindering its development activities and new knowledge creation rate (Grabher 1993; Johnson et al. 2003). Therefore, we hypothesize

H : Knowledge depth hinders knowledge absorption from network depth, such that the interaction between knowledge depth and network depth has a negative impact on new knowledge creation rate.

## Knowledge Transfer Effects of Developer Roles

If internal knowledge is not optimally configured to absorb external knowledge, how might an OSS project mitigate the negative effects? As we discussed, a project can configure its developer roles to improve knowledge transfer ability and increase new knowledge creation rate. Following Cohen and Levinthal (1990), we focus on two important developer roles in the OSS community. The first is the network role of bridge members who connect with outside projects and control the transfer of knowledge from outside projects to a focal project. The second is the functional role of developers that determines how the absorbed knowledge may be distributed and transferred across developers within the focal project.

## Bridge Members

In OSS development, bridge members work on multiple projects and function as conduits for knowledge transfers across project boundaries. They act as gatekeepers or boundary-spanners by monitoring the development progress of external projects, identifying what external knowledge might be useful, and translating technical knowledge that is difficult to absorb in a form that is understandable to other developers. With the same number of connected projects, bridge positions can either be diffused across multiple developers or centralized. For example, if two projects connect to the same ten external projects, those external connections might be diffused through five bridge members or one centralized bridge member. Although both projects are exposed to the same amount of heterogeneous external knowledge, the project with more bridge members is exposed to a relatively broader range of perspectives being interpreted by multiple bridge members. A project with a single, centralized bridge member narrows the interface between the focal and external projects and provides an efficient way to collect, integrate, and transfer external knowledge (Dyer et al. 2004).

We posit that a project with a centralized bridge member, all else being equal, can reduce the coordination difficulty associated with a network breadth and knowledge breadth combin ation, thus mitigating the negative effects of the interaction between network breadth and knowledge breadth on new knowledge creation rate. A broad knowledge base enables a project to readily absorb diverse external knowledge, so it becomes less necessary for bridge members to monitor and translate the external knowledge (Cohen and Levinthal 1990). Instead, centralizing these bridges can narrow the channels of external knowledge and minimize inconsistencies across the different perspectives associated with broad network connections. For example, knowledge obtained from broad network connections may suggest different development directions (e.g., choices of different databases or prioritizing new features or bug fixes). However, a project with a centralized bridge member can quickly compare and evaluate broad external knowledge, resolve inconsistencies, and propose a coherent direction to achieve knowledge transfer efficiency, thus increasing new knowledge creation rate (Cohen and Levinthal 1990). Therefore, we hypothesize

H : The number of bridge members negatively moderates the interaction between network breadth and knowledge breadth on new knowledge creation rate, such that with a centralized bridge member, the negative interaction between network breadth and knowledge breadth has a less negative impact on new knowledge creation rate.

## Role Diversity

Developers join OSS communities with different interests, skills, and capabilities (Daniel et al. 2013; Setia et al. 2012); thus, they often take on different functional roles to coordinate and transfer absorbed external knowledge within a project to meet development requirements. Recent research has investigated different types of functional roles that developers play and how these roles influence developers’ contributions and sustained participation in OSS communities (Setia et al. 2012; Zhang et al. 2013). Yet, limited research has focused on role diversity, or the degree to which developers take different functional roles and collaborate in the OSS community, in ways that are similar to cross-functional teams that have been studied extensively in commercial new product development (Cohen and Levinthal 1990; Jansen et al. 2005). We argue that role diversity can reduce the cognitive inertia associated with network depth and knowledge depth, thus mitigating its negative effects on new knowledge creation rate.

In OSS development, the success of a project relies on core developers who contribute extensive source code based on their technical skills and also on various supportive developers (e.g., support technicians, document writers, translators) to provide feedback, fix bugs, add features, and write documents (Setia et al. 2012; Zhang et al. 2013). Interactions across these different roles help developers more easily become aware of others’ abilities, skills, and competencies, and provide them different perspectives that can be quickly used and reused to absorb and create new knowledge (Zhang et al. 2013). In addition, role diversity can increase opportunities to learn from one another (Hackman 1987), which is a key determinant of sustained participation in OSS communities (Zhang et al. 2013). Supportive developers may tend to possess less technical knowledge and offer fewer contributions, but they can learn technical knowledge and problemsolving skills from the core developers who are the primary contributors to a project (Daniel et al. 2013). Core developers, in turn, can learn from feedback and reports of bugs provided by supportive developers to enhance their technical understanding and programming skills. Different perspectives and increased motivation to learn from role diversity can break through the cognitive inertia caused by the combination of network depth and knowledge depth, thereby enhancing new knowledge creation rate. Therefore, we hypothesize

H : Role diversity positively moderates the interaction between network depth and knowledge depth on new knowledge creation rate, such that, with greater role diversity, the negative interaction between network depth and knowledge depth has a less negative impact on new knowledge creation rate.

## Method

## Data Collection

We consider OSS development using longitudinal data collected from SourceForge that was the largest OSS development community when we collected the data from January to May 2009 (Gao et al. 2007). We collected the independent and control variables in January of 2009, and then we gathered monthly data about the dependent variable between February and May of 2009. With this time lag, we reduced the potential for reverse causality and minimized possible biases that would be due to short-term fluctuations (Jacobson 1990). The four-month window is also consistent with previous research on software release cycles that indicates milestones in software development usually take several months (Hahn et al. 2008). We examined the average lag between projects’ initiation dates and the dates of their first product releases; the average lag was 118 days or approximately four months.

## Measures

New knowledge creation rate. Following the extant literature (Grewal et al. 2006; Méndez-Durón and Garcia 2009; Peng et al. 2013; Wu et al. 2007), we measured new knowledge creation rate as the number of commits. Commits are reviewed and validated by peers, signifying only meaningful changes to the code, and thus are considered a good measure of the productivity of development activities. We averaged monthly project commits from February to May of 2009 to measure new knowledge creation rate. The distribution of commits was skewed to extreme values, so we took a log transformation of (commits + 1) to achieve a normal distribution.

Network Breadth and Depth. We adopted a two-mode affiliation network approach to identify the largest OSS development community network (for details, see Grewal et al. 2006). One mode is the developers and the other is the projects. Developers and projects become connected through developers’ membership affiliations with projects; projects are connected if they share any bridge members who participate in multiple projects (Gao et al. 2007; Grewal et al. 2006). Using a snowball sampling technique (Goodman 1961), we started with a random project and then found projects that were directly connected to it through bridge members. This iterative process continued until no new projects were available that could be added to the network. We repeated the process, but we started with different initial projects to find the convergent, largest connected network that generated a sample of 38,079 interconnected projects in SourceForge.

We measured network breadth as weighted degree centrality or the number of projects to which a focal project directly connects weighted by the average knowledge breadth of directly connected projects. This measure takes into consideration the quantity (number of direct connections) and quality (average knowledge diversity of connected projects) of network connections and is preferable to unweighted degree centrality that only considers the total number of network connections (Nooy et al. 2005). In line with Lin et al. (2009), we measured network depth using closeness centrality<sup>5</sup> or the mean geodesic (shortest) distance between the focal project and all other projects in the network. Freeman (1979) associated closeness centrality with faster and more accurate access to information in the network and defined it as follows:

$$
\text { Network   Depth } = \frac {N - 1}{\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {N} d (g _ {i , j})}, i \neq j \tag {1}
$$

where N is the size of the network, which was equal to 38,079 in our sample and $d ( g _ { i , j } )$ is the geodesic (shortest) path between projects i and j as determined with the fewest number of links between i and j.

Knowledge Breadth and Depth. On SourceForge, project developers report their knowledge skills on five-point Likert scales (1 = lowest, 5 = highest) across four technical knowledge domains: operating systems, programming language, database, and user interface. We averaged all developers knowledge in each domain to obtain project-level knowledge. In addition, we adjusted project-level knowledge by assigning weights to the four technical domains according to their relevance to the project domain. Three experts from IBM and Yahoo, all with more than ten years of software development experience, rated the relative importance of the four technical knowledge domains to the project domain by distributing 100% among them. The interrater reliability, between .739 to .852, indicated good agreement among the experts (Miles and Huberman 1994).

To measure knowledge depth, we averaged the adjusted project-level knowledge across all four technical knowledge domains. For knowledge breadth, we used an inverse Herfindahl index to measure the scope and diversity of project-level knowledge (Cheng and Nault 2012), such that

$$
\text { Knowledge   Breadth } = 1 - \sum_ {j} P ^ {2}, \text { where } P _ {j} = \frac {K _ {j}}{\sum_ {j} K _ {j}} \tag {2}
$$

This measure equals 0 if a project relies on a single knowledge domain and it approaches one as the project spreads its knowledge more evenly over various domains.

Bridge members and role diversity. The bridge members measure is the number of developers who span multiple projects and control the transfer of external knowledge of connected projects to the focal project. For role diversity, we used SourceForge’s classification of 21 different roles for developers, depending on their task functions (see Appendix B). Project administrators (e.g., project managers, Unix administrators, database administrators) control progress and coordinate tasks among other developers; core developers (e.g., developers, graphic designers, user interface designers, Web designers, porters) contribute extensive code, manage software releases, and coordinate with supportive developers; and supportive developers (e.g., support managers and technicians, document writers, and translators) occasionally fix bugs, add features, provide support, and write documents. Similar to knowledge diversity, we used an inverse Herfindahl index to measure role diversity as follows:

$$
\text { Role   Diversity } = 1 - \sum_ {i} P _ {i} ^ {2}, \text { where } P _ {i} = \frac {R _ {i}}{\sum_ {i} R _ {i}} \tag {3}
$$

Control Variables. We controlled for project size, age, status, openness, intended audience, domain, outside degree percentage, and domain knowledge breadth and depth. Project size refers to the number of developers in a project; larger groups tend to have more resources. For project age, we used the number of months that had elapsed since its registration date. Project status is a categorical variable that indicates whether a project is in the planning, pre-alpha, alpha, beta, stable, or mature stage. Even for a stable, mature project, development may continue for feature updates or new version releases. Further, following Lerner and Tirole (2002), we coded project openness that represents the degree of openness to the distribution and modification of source code on a three-point Likert scale: “not open” if it restricted both modification and distribution, “restrictively open” if it restricted either modification or distribution, or “open” if it had no restrictions on either criterion based on license type. In addition, we used two dummy variables to code the project’s intended audience for end users or developers because projects created for these two uses attract different types of interest (Comino et al. 2007). We controlled for project domains using dummy variables and for outside degree percentage using the percentage of direct connections that a project has outside the domain of the focal project. Finally, we controlled for the breadth and depth of a project’s domain knowledge. We measured a developer’s domain knowledge as the number of projects that she or he participated in within each project domain and then we averaged across developers in each project domain to obtain project-level domain knowledge. Similar to technical knowledge breadth and depth, we measured domain knowledge breadth and depth with inverse Herfindahl indexes and average values across all domains (see Table 1 for the descriptive statistics and correlations).

## Model Estimation

We excluded single-developer projects because the development of those projects lacks the coordination process that exists in multiple-developer projects (Singh and Fleming 2010) and those projects without complete information on all variables, resulting in a final data set of 4,518 projects. A key issue for our estimation is the potential for endogeneity in the independent and dependent variables. First, as discussed, we adopted a time lag between the measures of the independent variables (network and knowledge depth and breadth) and the dependent variable (downloads) to reduce reverse causality (Jacobson 1990). Second, there might be interdependence between network breadth/depth and knowledge depth/breadth (e.g., projects with high knowledge/breadth may be more attractive to other external projects), and the failure to control for this factor might lead to an estimation bias. Therefore, we adopted a control function approach to regress network breadth and depth on knowledge breadth and depth lagged by one month, and then included the two residuals in our model estimation as two control variables (Petrin and Train 2010). These residual terms capture the extent to which interdependence exists between network breadth/depth and knowledge breadth/depth; after that, network breadth and depth as independent variables no longer correlated with the error term in the equation (Petrin and Train 2010).

In addition, we screened the data for normality, multicollinearity, and homogeneity in covariances. The skewness and kurtosis of downloads after the log transformation were 1.716 and 4.042, respectively, which suggests that our measure of commits is within the acceptable range of normal distribution. We screened for homoscedasticity using a standardized scatterplot of the predicted dependent variable by the standardized residuals. The residuals, randomly scattered around $0 ,$ provided a relatively even distribution; the homoscedasticity of the variance of errors is a valid assumption. Finally, we assessed the variance inflation factors. They ranged from 1.010 to 5.142, indicating no threat of multivariate multicollinearity. Ultimately,

## Simulation

To develop a clearer picture of the overall effects of different combinations of network and knowledge depth and breadth on new knowledge creation rate, we performed a simulation analysis to compare the four scenarios with two baseline conditions: a “typical” and an “ideal” project. For a typical project in the OSS community, network and knowledge dimensions were set to the sample means, representing a typical project with average network and knowledge resources. For an “ideal” project, network and knowledge dimensions were two standard deviations above the sample means, indicating a project that has accumulated more network and knowledge resources. We kept the control variables at the sample means for all conditions. We next estimated for the four network and knowledge combinations by setting, for each combination, the particular network and knowledge parameters to two standard deviations above the sample means but keeping all other variables at their sample means (see Table 3).

Compared to a typical project, the network breadth and knowledge breadth combination had more resources in network breadth and knowledge breadth but led to the fewest commits for a 30.418% loss. The network depth and knowledge depth combination, similarly, had more resources in network depth and knowledge depth but generated the commits 18.248% lower than those of a typical project.

In addition, we compared the four network and knowledge combinations with an “ideal” project. Compared to the network breadth and knowledge depth combination, the “ideal” project had more network depth and knowledge breadth resources, but it performed 37.523% worse. Similarly, the “ideal” project had more network depth and knowledge breadth resources compared to the network breadth and knowledge depth combination, yet it performed 40.149% worse. These simulation results support our assertion that simply building more network connections and embeddedness does not necessarily lead to better development performance. Instead, the effects of network connections depend on the presence of internal knowledge that can absorb them.

## Discussion

An OSS development model must go beyond access to external knowledge from network connections to account for the absorption and transfer of external knowledge in the community. We investigated how a project can configure knowledge and developer roles to build its absorptive capacity in order to increase OSS success that is validated using both internal development activities and external software downloads. Ou empirical study confirmed that more is not necessarily better; broader and deeper network connections do not always lead to better project success. Rather, the effects of network connections and embeddedness depend on the project’s absorptive capacity to absorb and transfer external knowledge across and within projects. These results have both theoretical and managerial implications.

## Theoretical Implications

First, our findings offer a cautionary tale for researchers who take a social network perspective to examine the effects of network connections without considering the internal capabilities of projects. In multiple disciplines, social network theory frames studies of various networking phenomena such as information or communication flows from one agent to another (Monge and Contractor 2003), the outbreak of viruses and diseases (Monge and Contractor 2003), the diffusion of innovative ideas and new products (Van den Bulte and Wuyts 2007), or advice seeking (Sykes and Venkatesh 2017; Sykes et al. 2014) and impeding network ties (Sykes and Venkatesh 2017) in workplace. These applications consistently prioritize social networks as instruments for diffusing information or influence. For an OSS community that requires tremendous time and effort to read, reuse, and modify the code from other projects, accessing external knowledge through networks is insufficient to create newknowledge without additional stages of knowledge absorption and transfer (Cohen and Levinthal 1990). Controlling for sampling bias, we showed that network breadth and depth have no significant effects on OSS success, supporting our arguments that the network alone does not determine OSS success.

Second, researchers should acknowledge that network connections and embeddedness introduce both positive and negative influences. The early development of social network theory mainly focused on the positive outcomes of network connections (Freeman 1979; Grewal et al. 2006; Tan et al. 2007). As the theory has evolved, new empirical findings have suggested negative consequences as well (Daniel and Stewart 2016; Peng et al. 2013; Singh, Tan, and Mookerjee 2011). Researchers, therefore, should consider both the negative and positive mechanisms of network effects when they build theoretical arguments and conduct empirical tests. The overall effects of network breadth and depth depend on which side dominates in a particular context and sample, which could be positive if the positive side dominates, negative if the negative side dominates, or nonsignificant if both sides cancel each other out. This may explain why the literature has mixed findings of the effects of network connections. In the current work, our use of a snowball sampling technique without imposing an arbitrary definition of the network boundary, our identification of the largest network in the OSS community (n = 38,079), and our inclusion of network links beyond a predefined foundry all helped reduce sampling bias and enabled us to fully capture both the positive and negative effects of network breadth and depth that appear perfectly correlated to cancel out each other.

Third, this work introduces an alternative theoretical lens on absorptive capacity to help examine the effects of network connections on OSS success. Absorptive capacity theory appears in diverse IS research streams, such as those pertaining to IT knowledge, IT knowledge transfer in an organizational setting, IT assimilation, and IT business value (Roberts et al. 2012), but not to OSS thus far. Because OSS development is an emerging, community-based development model, researchers have devoted significant attention to its network characteristics. We contribute to absorptive capacity theory by studying how an OSS project can configure its knowledge and developer roles to build absorptive capacity at the project level. A vast majority of IS research investigates absorptive capacity at the organizational level (65%, 64 papers) or the individual level (26%, 25 papers); only two papers refer to the group (or project) level (Roberts et al. 2012). Considering absorptive capacity at the project level is different from considering it at the individual level—where it refers simply to an individual’s ability to absorb external knowledge without knowledge transfer—or at the organizational level that usually conceptualizes absorptive capacity either as knowledge absorption—for example, R&D intensity (Cohen and Levinthal 1990), R&D staff (Mowery and Oxley 1995), patents (Ahuja and Katila 2001)—or as knowledge transfer—for example, organizational hierarchy (Van den Bosch et al. 1999), formalization (Jansen et al. 2005), job rotation (Jansen et al. 2005)—due to availability of relevant data (Van den Bosch et al. 2003).

Fourth, we offer empirical evidence that internal knowledge and external networks need to be balanced to achieve project success, echoing a balanced theoretical view of absorptive capacity. Taking a quadratic approach, Singh, Tan, and Mookerjee (2011) suggested that because internal and external cohesion include both benefits and drawbacks, a moderate level is better than very low or very high levels. Similarly, Peng et al. (2013) found that the number of network ties has diminishing returns for the success of OSS projects because both benefits and costs are associated with building and maintaining these ties. The quadratic approach optimizes the difference between the positive and negative network effect and suggests that this difference is maximized at the moderate value of network connections. Following absorptive capacity theory, we argue that a true balance happens between internal knowledge and external networks, not in the network alone. The absorptive capacity perspective holds that we need to find the right configuration of internal capabilities that complement network connections to achieve greater performance outcomes. Because the positive and negative effects of network connections always exist, even at the moderate value of network connections, a balanced solution that increases the positive network effect while decreasing its negative effects should be more optimal than the quadratic solution. As illustrated in Table 3, a balanced combination of network depth and knowledge breadth performed 15.026% better than a typical project with moderate levels of network breadth and depth, and a balanced combination of network breadth and knowledge depth performed 17.223% better. Our work extends previous absorptive capacity literature by empirically testing two balanced and two unbalanced combinations between network and knowledge on project success. Thus, it provides important empirical evidence to support the balanced view proposed in previous theory.<sup>6</sup>

## Managerial Implications

Our work offers insights for firms that seek to integrate OSS into their businesses in terms of how they should compose their teams to ensure OSS project success. Approximately 78% of companies from various industries (e.g., computer software, finance, automotive, retailing, healthcare, advertising) rely on OSS (Vaughan-Nichols 2015) and assign employees to participate in firm-initiated OSS projects. For example, Facebook delegates 256 developers to work on OSS projects, from Cassandra (a NoSQL database) to Thrift (a framework for scalable cross-language services development) to Apache Hive (a data warehouse infrastructure). Google allocates 1,289 developers to work on more than 900 OSS projects hosted on GitHub.<sup>7</sup> Other firms, instead, sponsor open source sprints (also called code jams or hack-a-thons), such as Drupal and Plone content management systems, to invite OSS developers with needed technical talent to assemble for a limited time to add features and functionality that meet business requirements.

For those firms that invest heavily in OSS, our work provides managerial guidance on how to configure their knowledge and developer roles to leverage their network connections. A project with balanced internal knowledge and external network connections is more likely to succeed because it has better ability to take advantage of the benefits of code reuse, good testing, and code review from the OSS community-based development model. Specifically, they can establish wide connections in the community while investing in a deep understanding of the chosen domains, or they can become deeply embedded in the community network (i.e., proximity to popular projects) and invest in a diversified knowledge base. If a project is unable to configure its knowledge base to fit its network position, project leaders can assign one or two centralized bridge members to manage all external connections and thus reduce the significant coordination challenges that result from the combination of wide network connections and a broad knowledge base. Alternatively, they could assign developers to take on more diverse functional roles and thus reduce the negative consequences of cognitive myopia suffered from deep knowledge and deep embedding in the community network.

The same guidance applies to the management of voluntary developers but with the caveat, because OSS project managers have less control over these developers. Like commercial software development, most successful OSS projects also feature active project management, but the management is often less visible and more informal, subtle, and low key (Fogel 2005). Instead of passively waiting for projects to grow, project managers should exert their influence to attract new developers or look for developers with the needed skill sets. Research has shown that developers’ decisions to join OSS projects are not random processes. They are more likely to join projects when they have strong collaborative ties with project managers (Hahn et al. 2008). SourceForge’s “Help Wanted” function, for example, allows project managers to broadcast what kinds of developers they need with what specific types of expertise. Project managers should intentionally seek developers who have the needed knowledge to complement their network positions, invite non-bridge members, or strategically assign developers to different roles (usually informally through discussions) to increase their ability to absorb and transfer external knowledge from the network.

## Limitations and Future Research

Some limitations of this study suggest avenues for further research. First, for rigor in our theoretical development and empirical testing, we focused on the technical knowledge of developers but controlled for their domain knowledge. We found that breadth and depth of domain knowledge had strong negative effects on project success, possibly because, unlike technical knowledge that can be reused in different projects, domain knowledge cannot be readily adapted to new projects, especially if they are in different project domains. Further research could investigate how domain knowledge can be absorbed and transferred across and within projects in OSS communities.

Second, we argued that opportunity identification and exploration, as well as coordination difficulty and cognitive inertia, are the positive and negative theoretical mechanisms that affect knowledge absorption and transfer during development processes, but we have not tested these predictions due to the limitations of empirical research using secondary data. Further studies should test those theoretical mechanisms in OSS development.

Third, due to the scope of this study, we focused on bridge members and role diversity as the social integration factors that facilitate knowledge transfer. We acknowledge that other factors may have equal influences. For example, internal cohesion through repeat collaborations (Singh, Tan, and Mookerjee 2011) and interactive discussion (Daniel and Stewart 2016) might reduce coordination difficulties associated with network and knowledge breadth. Job rotation (Jansen et al. 2005), which refers to the lateral transfer of employees between jobs, might mitigate the cognitive inertia with respect to network and knowledge depth that occurs in commercial software development. It would be helpful to explore whether additional social integration factors have equally important influences on reducing the downsides of unbalanced combinations as well as on facilitating knowledge transfer for the two balanced combinations.

## Conclusions

As a successful community-based open innovation model, OSS and its development have received significant attention. This article offers insights to advance a more nuanced view of how a project can configure its knowledge base and developer roles to build its absorptive capacity and, in turn, to leverage its network connections and embeddedness. Our message is clear: More is not necessarily better. Building more and deeper network connections does not ensure project success. Rather, the transformation of external knowledge achieved through network connections depends on internal knowledge and developer roles. We must understand these synergies, and the trade-offs among them, to predict OSS project success.
