# COORDINATING HUMAN AND MACHINE LEARNING FOR EFFECTIVE ORGANIZATIONAL LEARNING<sup>1</sup>

With the rise of machine learning (ML), humans are no longer the only ones capable of learning and contributing to an organization’s stock of knowledge. We study how organizations can coordinate human learning and ML in order to learn effectively as a whole. Based on a series of agent-based simulations, we find that, first, ML can reduce an organization’s demand for human explorative learning that is aimed at uncovering new ideas; second, adjustments to ML systems made by humans are largely beneficial, but this effect can diminish or even become harmful under certain conditions; and third, reliance on knowledge created by ML systems can facilitate organizational learning in turbulent environments, but this requires significant investments in the initial setup of these systems as well as adequately coordinating them with humans. These insights contribute to rethinking organizational learning in the presence of ML and can aid organizations in reallocating scarce resources to facilitate organizational learning in practice.

Keywords: Artificial intelligence, machine learning, human–machine coordination, organizational learning, simulation, agent-based modeling

## Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) continues to transform our society and economy. Machine learning (ML), defined broadly as an approach to deriving patterns from data using algorithms (Mitchell 1997), plays a key role in this development as it enables technology to learn and act autonomously. For companies seeking to leverage the potential of ML, an unresolved challenge is how to coordinate the work of humans and ML systems to ensure their harmonious functioning (e.g., Pentland et al. 2020; Rai et al. 2019; Schuetz and Venkatesh 2020). For example, it is unclear how organizations should readjust humans’ responsibilities now that ML systems can imitate and even outperform them in certain tasks (e.g., Mullard 2017; Thrall et al. 2018). Similarly, human workers can continuously adjust ML systems (e.g., Amershi et al. 2019; Grønsund and Aanestad 2020), but the extent to which such adjustments provide economic benefits remains an elusive issue. To date, discussions on human–machine coordination mainly focus on cost savings, error reduction, and decision quality (e.g., Brock and von Wangenheim 2019; Brynjolfsson and Mitchell 2017; Raisch and Krakowski 2021).

However, these discussions neglect an issue of immense organizational interest: the potential for machine learning to alter what organizations know and how they learn (Argote et al. 2020; Ransbotham et al. 2020). Many examples show that ML can offer novel insights into existing problems by discovering data patterns that are not visible to the naked eye (e.g., Choudhury et al. 2021; Ransbotham et al. 2020). For instance, ML systems used in cancer diagnosis can discover patterns in MRI images that cannot be seen by the human eye (e.g., Thrall et al. 2018), and ML systems used for drug discovery can reveal undiscovered cause–effect relationships of molecules and their medicinal properties (e.g., Preuer et al. 2019). Chess legend Garry Kasparov highlights this potential for humans to learn from ML systems (Silver et al. 2018):

The implications go far beyond my beloved chessboard....Not only do these self-taught expert machines perform incredibly well, but we can actually learn from the new knowledge they produce.

Presently, we understand little about the ways in which ML can disrupt organizational learning. What we do know is that organizational learning depends on a complex system of interacting learners that require coordination (e.g., Argote et al. 2020; Dodgson 1993). Prior research has assumed that such coordination involves only human learners (e.g., Argote and Miron-Spektor 2011; Levinthal and Rerup 2021), but ML undermines this assumption while it differs significantly from human learning (Argote et al. 2020). For instance, humanbased organizational learning is largely a social process (e.g.,

March 1991), while ML is based primarily on data processing (e.g., Mitchell 1997). Thus, theory on how to coordinate human learners cannot readily be applied to coordinate a system that involves both human learning and ML. ML introduces novel issues for organizational learning, such as the need to coordinate the propagation of diverse and even contradictory beliefs offered by humans and ML systems or the challenge of anticipating how human adjustments to ML systems affect organizational learning. To guide organizational learning that involves ML, we therefore seek to study how organizations can coordinate human learning and ML to learn effectively (i.e., accumulate high levels of knowledge) as a whole.

In attempting to answer this question, our aim is to help understand how organizational learning must be rethought in the era of AI. Such understanding is crucial for organizations, given the centrality of learning for organizations’ long-term survival and the significant resource demands associated with organizational learning (e.g., Bushee 1998; Cohen and Levinthal 1989). While existing theory can guide organizations in their allocation of scarce resources to coordinate human-based approaches to learning, it remains silent about ML systems as new sources of knowledge and the need to rethink resource allocation for effective organizational learning with ML. For instance, organizational learning that involves ML might alter the returns on investments coming from human approaches to learning (e.g., Bushee 1998; Kretkowski 1998; Levinthal and March 1993) while requiring new resources for extracting knowledge from ML systems (e.g., Ananny and Crawford 2018) and adjusting ML systems over time (e.g., Breck et al. 2017; Sculley et al. 2015). Without understanding how to coordinate human learning and ML, organizations might make ineffective use of scarce resources.

We conducted a series of agent-based simulations (e.g., Kane and Alavi 2007; Nan and Tanriverdi 2017; Schilling and Fang 2014) to extend March’s (1991) model of organizational learning. We relax its key assumption that humans are the only learners in organizations by incorporating ML into the model. Our simulations offer insights about how organizational learning effectiveness depends on the coordination between human learning and ML, which can help organizations allocate scarce resources to those practices and structures that matter most to effective organizational learning.

## Theoretical Background on Organizational Learning

“Organizations are seen as learning by encoding inferences from history into routines that guide behavior” (Levitt and

March 1988, p. 319). These routines store knowledge from earlier experiences in the form of “rules, procedures, conventions, strategies, and technologies” (Levitt and March 1988, p. 320). Taken together, such routines form a collective organizational memory that is independent of any single organizational member (e.g., Feldman and Pentland 2003; Levitt and March 1988). This organizational knowledge can lead to a sustainable competitive advantage, making organizational learning one of the most important strategic organizational assets (Hatch and Dyer 2004; Levinthal and March 1993; March 1991).

To understand how organizational learning can be managed, we view an organization as a coordination mechanism (e.g., Coase 1937; Kogut and Zander 1996; Marengo 1992), where “learning is one of the activities which needs coordination” (Dodgson 1993, p. 388). Coordination of organizational learning can be viewed from different angles (e.g., Dodgson 1993; Lounamaa and March 1987; March 1991; Nonaka 1994). We follow prior studies that adopt a macro perspective on organizational learning, focusing on the coordination of mutual learning between rather abstract organizational members and the consequences of such mutual learning for organizational learning effectiveness (e.g., Fang et al. 2010; March 1991; Miller et al. 2006).

## Organizational Learning Effectiveness and the Diversity of Beliefs

Organizational knowledge is based on the beliefs of the organization’s members, and the long-term effectiveness of organizational learning depends on the diversity of these beliefs (March 1991). While it is important for organizations to disseminate seemingly correct beliefs among their members, doing so also eliminates the diversity of the members beliefs. To prevent organizations from becoming trapped in inferior routines, a wide dissemination of seemingly correct beliefs should not occur prematurely (Denrell and March 2001; Levinthal and March 1993; March 1991). Instead, prolonged belief diversity allows organizations to experiment with alternatives before homogenizing toward a specific solution. Managing belief diversity by coordinating organizational members’ learning is therefore at the heart of effective organizational learning (e.g., Lavie et al. 2010; March 1991).

One of the central approaches to managing belief diversity is to influence the pace at which organizational members’ beliefs homogenize toward uniform beliefs. To this end, organizations need to balance a tradeoff between exploration and exploitation (e.g., Lavie et al. 2010; March 1991). Exploration maintains belief diversity by fostering a search for and experimentation with new ideas that may yield uncertain, more distant outcomes and entails a shift away from the organization’s current capabilities, while exploitation accelerates belief homogenization by focusing on incremental improvements of existing insights to reap their immediate benefits. Balancing the two forms of learning is crucial for an organization’s survival. If organizations overemphasize the short-term benefits gained by refining and exploiting existing beliefs at the expense of prolonged diversity, they can become trapped in a state of stagnation while ignoring new and useful directions. In contrast, if organizations neglect short-term exploitation and overemphasize having belief diversity over a longer period, they might not survive at all in a competitive environment because they will lack a shared understanding and valuable competence in any specific area (e.g., Lavie et al. 2010; March 1991; Raisch et al. 2009).

## The Role of Information Technology in Organizational Learning

A large body of research on organizational learning and the related issue of knowledge management offers insights regarding the effects of information technology (IT) on organizational learning (e.g., Alavi and Leidner 2001; Choi et al. 2010; Iyengar et al. 2015; Kane and Alavi 2007; Robey et al. 2000; Schultze and Leidner 2002). Overall, IT has the potential to facilitate or hinder knowledge creation, application, and dissemination within organizations (e.g., Alavi and Leidner 2001; Robey et al. 2000; Schultze and Leidner 2002). For instance, while IT may simplify communication between individuals and may enhance an organization’s stock of knowledge, it can also lead to unintended consequences, such as the loss of practical knowledge due to automation (e.g., Iyengar et al. 2015; Robey et al. 2000; Schultze and Leidner 2002).

However, a central issue renders prior research ill-suited to inform our work: The vast majority of studies have assumed that IT is only a tool that supports human learning (e.g., Iyengar et al. 2015; Kane and Alavi 2007; Robey et al. 2000). However, this assumption neglects the ability of ML systems to learn autonomously and contribute their self-learned knowledge to the organization (e.g., Ransbotham et al. 2020; Seidel et al. 2019). Three studies present rare exceptions insofar as they acknowledge self-learning technologies (Bhatt and Zaveri 2002; Elofson and Konsynski 1993; Zhu et al. 1997), but these studies either remain at a purely conceptual level or are limited to very specific organizational tasks and therefore provide little insight into how a complex arrangement involving humans and ML systems might affect organizational learning and its coordination. To develop an understanding of ML and its potential to influence organizational learning, we next discuss the concept of ML.

## Conceptualization of Machine Learning

ML is defined as an approach to deriving patterns from data with learning algorithms to create models that can in turn be used to solve real-world problems (Brynjolfsson and Mitchell 2017; Jordan and Mitchell 2015; Mitchell 1997; Russell and Norvig 2016). At an operational level, there are different forms of ML that realize this general concept in different ways. For instance, the most prevalent forms of ML are supervised, unsupervised, and reinforcement ML (e.g., Jordan and Mitchell 2015; Russell and Norvig 2016), but hybrid approaches as well as new forms have also been discussed (e.g., Berthelot et al. 2019; Lan et al. 2020). In line with our macro perspective of organizational learning that abstracts from operational issues such as humans’ different learning styles (e.g., Fang et al. 2010; March 1991; Miller et al. 2006), we do not distinguish among the forms of ML that share the ability to create knowledge based on data and algorithms (e.g., Mitchell 1997). Instead, our conceptualization of ML, as presented in the next subsection, adheres to the general definition stated above.

## Key Components and Initial Setup of Machine Learning

Our conceptualization of the key components of ML is depicted in Figure 1. The purpose of this conceptualization is to establish a foundation for integrating ML into March’s (1991) model of organizational learning.

Figure 1 shows that ML is used to address a narrow realworld problem that can be represented by a set of data (e.g., Jordan and Mitchell 2015; Mitchell 1997; Russell and Norvig 2016). Thereby, the data have a certain quality and quantity, and thus may or may not provide a good representation of the real-world problem under consideration (e.g., Ackoff 1989; Mingers and Standing 2018; Wang and Strong 1996). ML also requires a learning algorithm (or an ensemble of multiple algorithms; Rokach 2010), such as neural network or decision tree algorithms (e.g., Domingos 2012; Jordan and Mitchell 2015; Mitchell 1997). The learning algorithms include hyperparameters (e.g., the targeted number of layers in a neural network or the maximum depth of a decision tree) that need to be tuned to match the problem (e.g., Goodfellow et al. 2016; Mitchell 1997). Both the algorithm and the data are used to train a model (e.g., a trained neural network represented by weight matrices and mathematical operations) that best reflects the patterns within the data (Jordan and Mitchell 2015; Russell and Norvig 2016). This process can be repeated if new data are gathered (Amershi et al. 2019; Diakopoulos 2016). The resulting model is a more or less faithful representation of the original real-world problem.

ML systems must be set up by humans: A specific real-world problem must be chosen, data need to be provided, and the learning algorithm must be implemented. This initial setup strongly depends on the expertise of those involved (e.g., data scientists, software engineers, domain experts, external consultants), who select, preprocess, and otherwise prepare the data, and who set up and fine-tune the learning algorithm based on their own ideas about the real-world problem (Amershi et al. 2019; Grønsund and Aanestad 2020; Raisch and Krakowski 2021). The more those involved in the development of the ML system know about the problem, the better they can set it up, and the higher its learning capability will be. A lack of human expertise, in contrast, can introduce wrong or biased ideas about the problem into the ML system (Choudhury et al. 2021; Diakopoulos 2016; Schuetz and Venkatesh 2020). A well-known example is the ML-based recruiting system used by Amazon, which was meant to help prioritize application documents for more efficient hiring. As a result of biases in the selected data, however, the system ended up preferring male candidates over female candidates (Logg 2019). Note that, through their setup activities, humans only determine the conditions under which an ML system learns, while the actual learning (i.e., deriving patterns from data and combining them into a model) is performed by the ML system. Therefore, humans do not define the logic of the resulting model that is learned (e.g., Jordan and Mitchell 2015; Mitchell 1997; Ransbotham et al. 2020).

## Coordinating Human and Machine Learning

Having introduced the theoretical background on organizational learning and ML, we now focus on three specific issues associated with the coordination of human learning and ML for effective organizational learning. Using a structurational perspective (DeSanctis and Poole 1994; Orlikowski and Robey 1991), we consider (1) the impact of IT on humans, (2) the influence of humans on IT, and (3) the environmental conditions of the human–IT interaction.

Regarding ML’s impact on humans, humans’ natural preference for known solutions over new but unproven ideas has long plagued organizational learning (e.g., Denrell and March 2001; Lee 2019; Levinthal and March 1993). This tendency is known as learning myopia (Levinthal and March 1993) and is caused by an aversion to the risks associated with the more distant and uncertain benefits that might be gained through exploration (Denrell and March 2001; Fang et al. 2010; Levinthal and March 1981). This myopic bias requires organizations to actively rebalance exploration and exploitation by investing considerable resources in measures such as R&D, employee training, and incentives to engage employees in an exploratory search for new ideas (e.g., Bushee 1998; Kretkowski 1998; Levinthal and March 1993). However, ML systems’ ability to introduce novel insights for existing problems that are for the most part independent of existing beliefs (i.e., ML fostering diversity among beliefs) within the organization could lower the need for human exploration that would otherwise be required for creating and maintaining diversity (March 1991). ML might therefore render some of the measures to promote exploration obsolete, calling into question whether these remain effective investments to improve organizational learning. We need to understand whether human exploratory search is still as important for organizations in contexts where ML systems in addition to humans contribute to organizational learning. Hence, we ask the following research question (RQ):

RQ1: How does the effectiveness of organizational learning depend on human exploration when ML also contributes to organizational learning?

Regarding humans’ influence on ML, human expertise affects many aspects of ML systems beyond their initial setup. As humans’ understanding of the world changes, they can reconfigure productive ML systems to realign them with humans updated problem understanding (e.g., Amershi et al. 2019; Domingos 2012). From a pure computer science perspective, there is no question that extensive and continuous reconfiguration is key to achieving high ML performance (e.g., Amershi et al. 2019; Sculley et al. 2015). Yet, the substantial costs of reconfiguration can prevent organizations from undertaking extensive reconfiguration efforts (e.g., Breck et al. 2017; Sculley et al. 2015). Moreover, reconfiguration can reduce the diversity of beliefs within the organization by indirectly introducing humans’ beliefs into the learning approaches of ML systems. While this alignment can improve ML systems when the human beliefs are largely correct, it is problematic if these beliefs are incorrect or biased (e.g., Diakopoulos 2016; Schuetz and Venkatesh 2020). In sum, we know little about the returns on investments in reconfiguration and how these returns depend on the reciprocal interplay of humans and ML systems. Since the effectiveness of reconfiguration depends on both humans’ expertise within the organization and ML systems’ learning capabilities, determining an appropriate level of investment in ML reconfiguration requires a deep understanding of how different reconfiguration intensities will affect organizational learning under varying contextual conditions. We ask the following question:

## RQ2: How does the effectiveness of organizational learning depend on the intensity of human reconfiguration of ML?

Regarding environmental conditions, the coordination between human learning and ML should take into account the increasing turbulence in organizational environments. An organization’s environment is turbulent if rapid and unpredic table changes can occur, which may threaten economic stability and previous competitive advantages (Grant 2003; Nan and Tanriverdi 2017; Sambamurthy et al. 2003). Indeed, technological advances, geopolitical issues, and global pandemics, among other factors, are making turbulent environments a common feature of the world (e.g., Ancona et al. 2020; Benbya et al. 2020; Nan and Tanriverdi 2017). Dynamics in the environment are particularly problematic for organizational learning, as they might render organizational knowledge obsolete (e.g., March 1991; Posen and Levinthal 2012). Further aggravating the problem, turbulence can also impair organizational learning strategies that work in nonturbulent contexts (e.g., Kane and Alavi 2007; Posen and Levinthal 2012). For instance, while organizations can use knowledge repositories for knowledge sharing among their members, the knowledge stored inside these repositories is rendered increasingly obsolete by turbulence, which nurtures the dissemination of outdated beliefs (Kane and Alavi 2007).

The effectiveness of human-based organizational learning depends on human exploration (e.g., March 1991), which might take too much time in turbulent environments where new insights may expire quickly. Hence, in turbulent environments, ML systems’ ability to swiftly discover patterns in large data sets (Ransbotham et al. 2020), in contrast to human learners’ more time-consuming learning process (e.g., March 1991), can become particularly important. This apparent advantage of ML can entice organizations to prioritize ML when allocating resources. However, downplaying human learning can leave an organization vulnerable to imperfect knowledge provided by ML systems. Take the development of vaccines against virus-related diseases as an example (Mullard 2017; Schneider 2019): As viruses evolve due to antigenic drift, existing knowledge quickly becomes outdated, requiring a rapid search for new vaccines (e.g., Petrova and Russell 2018). However, pharmaceutical firms must know how to take advantage of ML’s rapid learning capability without neglecting the slower yet valuable learning efforts of human chemists to ensure the vaccines’ efficacy. Our final research question is therefore as follows:

RQ3: How does the effectiveness of organizational learning depend on the coordination of human learning and ML in turbulent environments?

## A Model of Organizational Learning with Machine Learning

Our model development begins with a baseline model that is a slightly modified version of March’s (1991) model of organizational learning. We then add ML systems as a new type of learning agent to investigate the behavior of an organizational learning system that involves both humans and ML systems. In agent-based models of organizational learning, agency reflects an actor’s ability to learn (e.g., March 1991; Miller et al. 2006; Schilling and Fang 2014). Humans have obviously been recognized for their ability to learn, and ML, defined as learning from data with the help of algorithms (Jordan and Mitchell 2015; Mitchell 1997), can be attributed an ability to learn as well. In the context of our agent-based model, we therefore use the term ML agents when referring to ML systems that conform with our conceptualization of ML (Figure 1).

## A Baseline Model of Organizational Learning

March’s model reflects the mutual learning process of humans in organizations. In this process, human agents’ beliefs are continually codified into an organizational code that represents the beliefs that the organization propagates to its members through rules, procedures, conventions, strategies, and technologies (e.g., Levitt and March 1988). At the same time, the organization socializes its members with the beliefs included in the code (e.g., through training, policies, and incentives). Through this process, seemingly valuable beliefs are gradually disseminated within the organization, reducing the diversity of beliefs among its members. Fast dissemination of beliefs represents exploitation, while slow dissemin ation of beliefs models exploration. Effective organizational learning seeks to reach the highest level of organizational knowledge possible—that is, to maximize the number of correct beliefs held by the organization.

In March’s model, an organization’s reality is modeled as an m-dimensional vector, with each dimension having a value of either 1 or –1. This vector is unknown to the organization and its members. The organization itself comprises n human agents and an organizational code, which are represented by m-dimensional vectors of beliefs about reality, with each belief being 1, 0, or –1 (0 means there is no belief about this dimension). The percentage of beliefs held by a human agent or the code that correctly match reality determines the agent’s or the code’s knowledge level. In each period, human agents learn from the organizational code: For each dimension of a human agent’s belief vector, the belief changes to the code’s value with probability $\mathbf { \nabla } p _ { 1 } .$ This learning rate $p _ { 1 }$ represents how effective an organization is at educating human agents into its code. If the code has the value 0 for a dimension, no change in beliefs takes place. Further, in each period and for each dimension, the majority belief of the organization’s knowledgeable humans (human agents whose beliefs match reality on more dimensions than the code) is codified in the organizational code depending on the learning rate ${ p _ { 2 } } . ^ { 2 }$ This probability reflects how effective the organization is at codifying its knowledgeable humans’ beliefs.

In addition to this mutual learning process, March introduced personnel turnover, which was modeled by having an agent reset its entire belief vector to a random state with a certain probability. From the perspective of belief diversity, turnover presents an “alternative way of producing variability in an organization” by “introducing less socialized people, [which] increases exploration” (March 1991, pp. 78-79). We build on this notion of creating belief diversity but model a more general idea: that organizations seek input from outside their boundaries through platform strategies, open innovation, and other approaches (e.g., Benbya et al. 2020; Patroni et al. 2020; Trantopoulos et al. 2017). In line with operationalizations of turnover (e.g., Fang et al. 2010; March 1991;

Miller et al. 2006), we model these external stimuli as a probability that human agents acquire fresh beliefs independent of those already in the organization, assuming that these beliefs are on average neither correct nor incorrect. Specifically, in each period and with a probability $p _ { E x t }$ (the rate of external stimulation), each of a human agent’s beliefs changes to a value of 1 or –1 (decided by a coin flip).

Finally, we allow for different degrees of environmental turbulence. Consistent with March and other operationalizations of turbulence (e.g., Fang et al. 2010; Kane and Alavi 2007), we assume that, in each period, each reality dimension changes from 1 to –1 and vice versa with probability $p _ { T u r b }$ (the rate of environmental turbulence).

## Incorporating ML Agents into the Baseline Model

We integrate ML agents and their learning capability into the baseline model presented above via model elements (E1–E4), as follows.

Representation of ML agents: We first introduce ML agents as a new type of learning agent. The model learned by an ML agent is a representation of a single aspect of the real world, which is consistent with March’s view that human agents’ beliefs are representations of aspects of reality. In our baseline model, human agents can hold a belief about each dimension of the m-dimensional reality. This representation of humans’ broad range of beliefs reflects that “individuals have a general intelligence factor that facilitates performance on a wide range of cognitive tasks,” while ML systems “do not possess such a general artificial intelligence” (Argote et al. 2020, p. 4). A human agent may learn how to develop drugs, negotiate with customers, and speak foreign languages, among many other things. By contrast, ML agents are specialized agents with a narrow scope (e.g., Brynjolfsson and Mitchell 2017; Mitchell et al. 2018). This can be seen in real-world applications of ML, where ML agents are only used to perform highly specialized tasks. For example, ML agents involved in drug discovery have a narrow scope, such as predicting the pharmacological and potentially adverse effects of molecules that represent new drug candidates (e.g., Philipp et al. 2018; Properzi et al. 2019; Vamathevan et al. 2019). To represent this specialization of ML agents in our model, we assume that an ML agent can only hold a single belief about a single dimension of reality. As it would be ineffective and highly inefficient for organizations to operate more than one ML agent for any single dimension of reality, due to the significant cost this would entail (e.g., Amershi et al. 2019; Sculley et al. 2015), we assume that organizations do not use more than one ML agent for a given dimension of reality. To incorporate ML agents into our model, we implement the following model extension:

E1: In addition to its n human agents, an organization uses j ML agents, with each ML agent covering a single distinct dimension of the mdimensional reality vector $( 0 \leq j \leq m )$ . Each ML agent is represented by an m-dimensional vector of beliefs in which all beliefs are fixed at 0 (i.e., the agent has no beliefs), except for a single dimension associated with the agent’s specialization, which has a value of 1 or –1 that can change over time.

ML agents’ learning capability: The beliefs (i.e., models) that ML agents learn are the result of their training, which involves the algorithmic processing of data. Thus, an ML agent’s learning capability (i.e., how well the belief learned by an ML agent will represent the real-world problem the agent is meant to address) depends on a variety of different factors. For instance, data serving as a basis for ML can only be an imperfect representation of the real-world problem (e.g., Ackoff 1989; Mingers and Standing 2018; Rowley 2007), as they could be incomplete or biased, low-quantity, or poorly preprocessed (Choudhury et al. 2021; Diakopoulos 2016; Schuetz and Venkatesh 2020). But even with high-quality data, the ML agent’s learning algorithm might not construct a good model of reality, as the algorithm might not fit the problem at hand or its hyperparameter settings might be inadequate (Amershi et al. 2019; Choudhury et al. 2021; Domingos 2012). With regard to drug discovery, for example, models constructed by neural network algorithms showed superior performance in predicting pharmacological and adverse effects of drug candidates compared to models produced by other ML algorithms (e.g., Korotcov et al. 2017; Properzi et al. 2019; Vamathevan et al. 2019).

We model ML agents’ learning capability as a probability q that the belief learned by an ML agent in a given period t matches the value of reality. In this way, q subsumes the variety of different factors associated with the ML agent’s learning mechanism as described above. This is consistent with existing models of organizational learning that abstract from specific factors that may impede or facilitate learning and combine these factors into an overarching construct (e.g., Fang et al. 2010; Miller and Lin 2010; Miller et al. 2006). It is worth noting that the learning capability $q _ { t }$ differs from known performance metrics (e.g., accuracy) that indicate how well an ML agent performs a task with respect to a given set of data (e.g., Mitchell 1997). These metrics only reflect half of the truth because they do not indicate how well the data used to evaluate an ML system represent the actual real-world problem. For instance, an ML agent for predicting pharmacological effects of drug candidates might exhibit perfect accuracy with regard to a set of test data obtained from testtube experiments, but the ML agent may then fail to accurately predict pharmacological effects in living organisms (Schneider et al. 2020). In contrast, an ML agent’s learning capability accounts for both: how well the data actually represent the real-world problem and how well the ML agent’s model represents appropriate patterns captured by the data. In sum, an ML agent’s learning capability is represented by the following extension:

E2: In each period, an ML agent constructs a model of the problem it is meant to address, based on its learning algorithm and the currently available data. In this way, an ML agent’s belief changes to a value that correctly (or incorrectly) reflects the value of the corresponding reality dimension with a probability of $q _ { t }$ (or 1 – q ) that represents the ML agent’s current learning capability.

Reconfiguration of ML agents: As a result of reconfiguration, an ML agent’s learning capability $q _ { t }$ can change over time, as human agents might adjust the ML agent’s initial setup. Thus, the ML agents’ initial setup determines a starting value for their learning capability, which is adjusted over time, as human agents’ beliefs about the ML agents’ specialized dimensions change. For example, ML agents used for drug discovery in a productive setting need to be reconfigured over time if new scientific insights (e.g., about human biology) are developed (e.g., Philipp et al. 2018; Schneider et al. 2020). We model reconfiguration by further specifying the ML agents’ learning capability $q _ { t }$ as a function of human agents’ beliefs that depends on two additional parameters: the ML agents’ initial learning capability $q _ { I n i t }$ and the intensity of an organization’s reconfiguration efforts $\alpha . ^ { 3 }$

The initial learning capability $q _ { I n i t }$ is the result of the ML agent’s original setup and indicates the initial probability that the belief learned by an ML agent correctly reflects the corresponding value in the reality vector. Whether q increases or decreases in relation to $q _ { I n i t }$ due to reconfiguration depends on whether the majority of the human agents hold a correct or an incorrect belief regarding the ML agent’s dimension in period t. If human agents’ knowledge regarding an ML agent’s dimension increases (decreases), their reconfiguration efforts will cause the ML agent’s learning capability to increase (decrease) as well (i.e., the more human agents agree with each other, the stronger the changes in $q _ { t }$ resulting from reconfiguration will be). For instance, if biases creep into human agents’ beliefs about the problem addressed by an ML agent, this should lead to a decline in the ML agent’s learning capability.

Moreover, we control the intensity á of an organization’s reconfiguration efforts in order to represent the amount of resources the organization invests in costly and timeconsuming reconfiguration activities (e.g., Amershi et al. 2019; Domingos 2012; Sculley et al. 2015). The more time and effort the organization spends on reconfiguring its ML agents (e.g., by performing more rigorous data selection and feature engineering to better reflect how human agents understand a problem), the more effectively it can adjust its ML agents’ learning capability in accordance with human agents current beliefs. The higher the value of the parameter á representing an organization’s reconfiguration intensity, the more time and effort the organization will put into reconfiguration activities, and the stronger the influence of human agents’ beliefs on $q _ { t }$ will be. This results in the following model extension:

E3: An ML agent’s learning capability $q _ { t }$ depends on its initial learning capability $q _ { I n i t }$ and the intensity á of reconfiguration efforts. In each period, the more (less) human agents’ beliefs about the ML agent’s specialized dimension match the value in the reality vector, the more the ML agent’s learning capability $q _ { t }$ will increase (decrease). The higher (lower) á is, the stronger (weaker) the influence of human agents’ beliefs on $q _ { t }$ will be.

Codification of ML agents’ beliefs: Up to this point, only human agents’ beliefs are codified in the organizational code and thus contribute to organizational learning. However, ML agents present a new source of potentially valuable knowledge for organizations (e.g., Ransbotham et al. 2020; Seidel et al. 2019). This potential would remain untapped if ML agents beliefs were not fed into the organizational learning process. Organizations that seek to make the most of the knowledge generated by ML agents might want to propagate these agents’ beliefs as part of the organizational code as well. For instance, pharmaceutical companies promote ML agents’ beliefs internally to improve the discovery of drugs (Properzi et al. 2019; Schneider 2019).

How effectively ML agents’ beliefs can be codified by the organization and consequently promoted to its members depends on several factors. Most importantly, the codification of ML agents’ beliefs requires that these beliefs can be made available in a format that humans can understand. Here we must draw a distinction between white- and black-box ML models. Humans should inherently be able to understand an ML agent’s belief if it represents a white-box model (e.g., a decision tree). For black-box models (e.g., neural networks), supplementary techniques are required to enable human understanding of the ML agents’ beliefs (e.g., Letham et al. 2015; Xu et al. 2015). These techniques allow specialists to extract beliefs from black-box models and present them in an understandable format. For instance, black-box models can be extracted as decision rules (e.g., Martens et al. 2007; Ribeiro et al. 2018) or as visual representations (e.g., Lundberg and Lee 2017; Ribeiro et al. 2016). In drug discovery, for example, ML agents are designed to highlight the areas in a molecule that they consider responsible for certain pharmacological effects. These highlighted areas help chemists understand, among other things, the causes of the side effects of potential drugs (Jiménez-Luna et al. 2020; Merck 2020; Preuer et al. 2019). Effective codification of black-box models strongly depends on an organization’s expertise in the field of explainable AI (e.g., Guidotti et al. 2018; Lundberg and Lee 2017). Organizations’ effectiveness in codifying ML agents’ beliefs also depends on a variety of other factors, such as trust in the ML agents and organizational culture (e.g., Bohanec et al. 2017; Ransbotham et al. 2020).

We represent an organization’s effectiveness in codifying ML agents’ beliefs by introducing a new ML-centric code learning rate $p _ { 3 } .$ . This learning rate is similar to $p _ { 2 } .$ , which reflects how effective the organization is at codifying its human agents’ beliefs (March 1991).<sup>4</sup>

E4: In each period and for each belief dimension covered by an ML agent, the ML agent’s belief is codified into the organizational code depending on the code learning rate ${ \mathrm { : } } p _ { 3 }$ . This probability represents how effective the organization is in codifying ML agents’ beliefs.

As a result of our extensions, both the organizational code and human agents now have two potential sources of beliefs. While beliefs from both human and ML agents can be codified into the organizational code, human agents can either learn from the code or generate fresh beliefs based on external stimuli. As organizations should only care about the quality of beliefs, no matter the source, we assume neutral stances regarding these alternative sources of beliefs for both the code and human agents. Specifically, in each period and for each dimension, a coin flip decides whether the organizational code learns through $p _ { 2 }$ or through $p _ { 3 }$ (if an ML agent exists for this dimension). Likewise, in each period and for each dimension, a coin flip decides whether a human agent learns through $p _ { 1 }$ or creates a random belief (i.e., 1 or –1) with probability $p _ { E x t } .$

## Model Validity and Robustness Analyses

We followed established validation strategies to ensure the validity of our model (Brunswicker et al. 2019; Railsback and Grimm 2011; Taber and Timpone 1996). We pursued external validity by grounding the model in prior research and real-world applications of ML. Our baseline model closely resembles March’s widely cited model and is, therefore, strongly grounded. Our extensions are grounded in ML research, and we ensured that they are consistent with ML use in practice, as illustrated by the exemplary case of ML-based drug discovery.

To ensure the internal validity of our model’s behavior and results, we performed robustness analyses (Brunswicker et al. 2019; Railsback and Grimm 2011; Taber and Timpone 1996). We conducted two groups of tests. First, we conducted robustness analyses of the parameter values in our model by varying all parameters used in the model within a meaningful range (see Table 2). We found that our reported results were remarkably robust to reasonable variations.<sup>5</sup> For example, the simulation outcomes for a broader range and finer interval of ML agents’ initial learning capability $q _ { I n i t }$ conform to the effects from variations in $q _ { I n i t }$ on organizational learning effectiveness reported below, indicating that our qualitative insights are insensitive to specific parameter values. Second, we tested the robustness of the simulation results against a relaxation of our assumptions regarding ML. We relaxed the restrictions that (1) ML agents could specialize in only one dimension of reality (allowing ML agents to cover two dimensions), (2) all ML agents have the same initial learning capability (we initialized 20/80, 50/50, and 80/20 percent of ML agents with $q _ { I n i t } = . 2$ and $q _ { I n i t } = . 8$ , respectively), and (3) the reconfiguration of ML agents is performed every period (reconfiguring every second, third, or fifth period). The observed effects on organizational learning effectiveness are consistent with those reported in the results section. These analyses give us confidence in the internal validity of our model.

## Simulation Approach and Parameterization

We implemented our agent-based model in Python using the Mesa framework. We randomly initialized each dimension (m = 30) of the reality vector with the values 1 or –1 and all human agents’ belief vectors with the values 0, 1, or –1 with equal probability (March 1991). The beliefs of the organizational code and the ML agents were initially set to 0. Consistent with prior simulation studies (e.g., Kane and Alavi 2007; March 1991; Miller and Lin 2010), we measured organizational long-term knowledge levels (i.e., the percentage of code beliefs that correctly reflect reality) at the end of each simulation run. We identified 1,000 periods as both computationally feasible and more than sufficient for the organizational knowledge level to stabilize.<sup>6</sup> To derive robust results that reflect the effects of parameter changes rather than effects caused by the model’s stochasticity, the simulation executed 80 iterations for each set of parameter specifications to reach convergence of standard deviations (March 1991). Table 2 shows all model parameters and values used in our simulation and the robustness analyses. Each simulation run represents a single constellation of parameter values. Where possible, we adopted parameter values that were used in prior simulation studies of organizational learning (e.g., Kane and Alavi 2007; March 1991; Posen and Levinthal 2012).

## Research Question 3: Coordinating Human and ML Agents in Turbulent Environments

To understand the impact of environmental turbulence on human–machine coordination strategies, we analyzed how organizational knowledge levels vary with different learning rates $p _ { 1 } , p _ { 2 }$ , and $p _ { 3 }$ under different turbulent conditions $( p _ { T u r b } )$ and with a contribution from ML agents with different initial learning capabilities $q _ { I n i t } .$ A first observation is that, in turbulent environments, intensive human exploration (low $p _ { 1 } )$ and a rapid codification of knowledgeable human agents’ beliefs (high $p _ { 2 } )$ produce organizational knowledge levels superior to those produced by any other setting of the two parameters.<sup>8</sup> Regarding the organization’s codification of ML-generated beliefs $p _ { 3 } ,$ our results show that this codification should depend on the rate of turbulence $p _ { T u r b }$ and ML agents’ initial learning capability $q _ { I n i t ^ { * } }$ Unsurprisingly, exploiting ML agents’ beliefs (high $p _ { 3 } )$ presents a poor option for ML agents with a low initial learning capability $q _ { I n i t } ;$ that is, low values for $p _ { 3 }$ lead to greater organizational knowledge levels than do high values. Thus, we focus on ML agents with a high initial learning capability $q _ { I n i t } .$ For these ML agents, the results depicted in Figure 5 show that organizations can benefit from the rapid codification of beliefs that ML agents offer; that is, high values of the ML-centric code learning rate $p _ { 3 }$ produce equal or greater organizational knowledge levels than do low values of $p _ { 3 } .$ . With increasing environmental turbulence, the rapid codification of ML agents’ beliefs (high $p _ { 3 } )$ with a high initial learning capability $q _ { I n i t }$ becomes more crucial, as the gap between organizational knowledge levels associated with high and low values for $p _ { 3 }$ grows.

In addition, human exploration (low $p _ { 1 } )$ can uphold belief diversity, so that organizational knowledge does not get locked into a past reality. As highlighted in prior work (e.g., Fang et al. 2010; Kane and Alavi 2007; March 1991), diversity helps to generate higher organizational knowledge levels, especially in turbulent environments. ## Discussion

In organizations where ML is increasingly taking over task responsibilities, coordinating humans and ML systems has become a challenging management issue. Our research highlights that the outcomes of organizational learning must not be neglected when coordinating the work of humans and ML systems, given the profound consequences of an organization’s long-term knowledge. Table 3 summarizes our study’s main findings and propositions (P1, P2, and P3) as well as their implications. Next, we discuss how these findings contribute to extant research and how they can help organizations allocate scarce resources among practices and structures that affect organizational learning.

## The Role of Human Exploration in the Presence of Machine Learning

The results pertaining to our first research question contribute to the ongoing conversation on how to deal with the challenge of learning myopia—that is, humans’ natural tendency to favor exploitation over exploration (e.g., Levinthal and March 1993). Prior research has highlighted countermeasures, such as R&D, employee training, and incentives (e.g., Bushee

1998; Kretkowski 1998; Levinthal and March 1993). Our study has identified ML as a new remedial factor, as it can offer humans more freedom to learn according to their own preferences (see P1). This remedial effect becomes stronger as ML systems’ initial capabilities increase, reducing the need for humans to explore new and innovative ideas. The theoretical understanding of the role of human learning in the presence of ML systems offers a rationale for practitioners to reconsider the extent to which organizations allocate their scarce resources to R&D projects and other measures to generate new knowledge and discover new ideas. As ML might be able to take over some of these responsibilities (e.g., understanding how drugs work or learning about unknown consumer needs), this would provide organizations with more slack resources to spend on improving the initial setup of ML systems (e.g., acquiring higher-quality data, bringing in additional ML expertise from outside the organization).

## Reconfiguration of Machine Learning by Humans

Our study adds a business management perspective to earlier tech-centric discussions on the reconfiguration of ML. From a pure computer science perspective, reconfiguration is absolutely key to realign ML with changes in humans’ problem understanding and ensure a high level of ML performance (e.g., Amershi et al. 2019; Sculley et al. 2015). However, the question remains regarding which reconfiguration intensities are economically reasonable. Our propositions (P2a and P2b) offer an answer from an organizational-learning perspective by explaining how reconfiguration translates into increases in organizational knowledge. For practitioners, our results suggest that a moderate intensity of reconfiguration is largely beneficial. However, the beneficial effect can diminish and even become harmful when there is excessive reconfiguration. This largely depends on the initial capability of an ML system as well as the organization’s effectiveness in socializing its human members into the organizational code of beliefs.

Our findings on the benefits of reconfiguration also contribute to a larger discussion regarding whether human workers can be taken “out of the loop” once ML can effectively replace them in their tasks (e.g., Brynjolfsson and Mitchell 2017; Rai et al. 2019; Raisch and Krakowski 2021). As our research suggests, a certain intensity of reconfiguration is required to achieve high levels of organizational knowledge. Reconfiguration requires a deep and current problem understanding that is only attainable through social processes among a group of human learners (e.g., Argote and Miron-Spektor 2011; March 1991). It is unlikely that a few non-domain experts (e.g., data scientists) with a purely technical understanding of reconfiguring ML systems can develop a problem understanding similar to that of a group of human learners who confront the problem in their daily activities. Therefore, delegating reconfiguration activities to the IT department, as is often advocated, seems problematic from an organizationallearning perspective. Our results suggest that humans, especially domain experts, must not be taken out of the loop. Organizations need their solid and up-to-date problem understanding to engage in effective reconfiguration.

## Coordination of Human Learning and Machine Learning in Turbulent Environments

Today’s world is shaped by high dynamics that continually render organizations’ knowledge about the world obsolete (Ancona et al. 2020; Benbya et al. 2020; Nan and Tanriverdi 2017). Organizations struggle to learn effectively in such environments, since the mutual learning process of humans takes time to adapt to change (e.g., Kane and Alavi 2007; March 1991; Posen and Levinthal 2012). Prior research has investigated potential solutions to this problem, including personnel turnover (March 1991) and the use of IT (Kane and Alavi 2007). As a way to introduce new, independent beliefs into the organization, ML also affects an organization’s learning in turbulent environments. Our analysis shows that the more turbulence occurs, the more organizations need to engage humans in exploration and the more quickly organizational routines should absorb the beliefs of both humans and ML systems. At the same time, ML systems must have a high initial learning capability to play a positive role in human– machine coordination (see P3). Therefore, ML is not a panacea for taming turbulent environments. Only when they are adequately coordinated can humans and ML systems reach high levels of knowledge despite turbulence. Given such coordination, the organization might not need other, more radical measures (e.g., forced turnovers) to counteract the detrimental effects of dynamic environments.

## Limitations and Future Research Opportunities

More research is needed to increase our knowledge about the coordination of humans and ML systems for effective organizational learning. Because of the exploratory nature of our study, future research should validate our model and empirically test our propositions under different boundary conditions (e.g., in different industries or cultures) and in more realistic settings. This is important because agent-based simulation models abstract from organizational contexts and thus have an inherent risk of being too simplistic (e.g., a binary representation of reality) or idealized (e.g., all human agents have the same number of belief dimensions).

Alternative modeling approaches exist that could complement the insights gained from our study. For example, we follow March’s (1991) perspective on organizational learning and assume that the organizational code mediates inter-agent learning. This view, which we adopted to keep our model simple and focused (e.g., Bonabeau 2002; Taber and Timpone 1996), does not consider agents’ direct adoption of other agents’ beliefs that are not included in the organizational code. Thus, it abstracts away the impact of different organizational network structures on the diffusion of beliefs (e.g.,

Fang et al. 2010; Schilling and Fang 2014). In the future, however, it could become more relevant to include inter-agent learning in the model, since not only human agents but also ML agents might interact with each other, exhibiting different hierarchical structures. For example, research by DeepMind yielded an ML agent called AlphaZero that achieved state-ofthe-art performance in the games Go, chess, and shogi purely by playing against itself (Silver et al. 2018). Another alternative perspective could assume that ML systems are not modeled as distinct learning agents, as we did in our study, but rather view ML systems as a tool that increases the learning capabilities of specialized human agents (e.g., data scientists). Future research could investigate these alternative modeling approaches and examine their implications.

Furthermore, we have adopted a long-term perspective to analyze organizational knowledge levels. While this allowed us to discover stabilized relationships between learning coordination and learning outcomes, there might be time related (dis)advantages of ML systems that could provide additional insights. It could be a fruitful avenue for future research to adopt a more dynamic perspective and analyze the effect of ML systems on organizational learning processes.

Future research can also use agent-based modeling, as demonstrated in our study, to simulate the implications of ML in organizations. This method allows to model technical aspects of ML systems in organizational setups while enabling rigorous “walk throughs” of the consequences of the complex dynamics of interactions between humans and ML systems (e.g., Bonabeau 2002). Agent-based modeling may therefore play a role in revealing hard-to-foresee dynamics that can drive the bilateral relationships between humans and ML systems toward either a productive or a counterproductive direction. Research questions in this vein could include: How does the algorithmic management of human workers affect individuals’ and organizations’ effectiveness and efficiency? How do team dynamics change with varying roles of humans and ML systems in different team compositions? What are the consequences of replacing or augmenting human workers with ML systems in various organizational contexts? How do psychological factors (e.g., trust in ML systems) affect performance in the presence of ML systems?

## Concluding Remarks

ML systems offer potential for organizations to create knowledge that goes beyond what humans can learn (e.g., Ransbotham et al. 2020). To leverage this potential, ML systems must be incorporated into a system that previously consisted only of human learners. Without guidance on how to coordinate humans and ML systems to enhance organizational knowledge, organizations are likely to misallocate valuable resources, resulting in missed opportunities to benefit from the increasing capabilities of ML systems. Our study takes a first step toward developing theory and guidance for human–machine coordination in the context of organizational learning, but more work is needed to refine organizational learning theory for the AI era.
