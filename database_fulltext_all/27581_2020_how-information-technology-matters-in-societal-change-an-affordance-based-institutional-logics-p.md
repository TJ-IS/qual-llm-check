# HOW INFORMATION TECHNOLOGY MATTERS IN SOCIETAL CHANGE: AN AFFORDANCE-BASED INSTITUTIONAL LOGICS PERSPECTIVE<sup>1</sup>

Isam Faik Ivey Business School, Western University, London, Ontario, CANADA {ifaik@ivey.ca}

Michael Barrett Judge Business School, University of Cambridge, Cambridge, UNITED KINGDOM {m.barrett@jbs.cam.ac.uk}

Eivor Oborn Warwick Business School, University of Warwick, Coventry, UNITED KINGDOM {eivor.oborn@wbs.ac.uk}

While there has been much work on the relationship between information technology (IT) and organizational change, there has been limited research that theorizes the relationship between IT and societal change. This paper draws on institutional theory, in particular institutional logics, to develop a model of IT and societal change, which we argue is critical in an era of large-scale digital transformation. Our approach is based on a view of society as an interinstitutional system, reflecting the multiplicity of logics at the societal level. We conceptualize societal change as shifts in the multiplicity of logics, with a focus on changes in the levels of centrality and compatibility. Our model relates these changes to the materiality of technology through the concept of IT affordances. We propose three mechanisms (sensegiving, translating, and decoupling) through which IT affordances become elements of societal change. We identify three corresponding carriers through which IT affordances gain scale and stability (objects, networks, and platforms). We discuss the implications of our theoretical developments for future research on IT and societal change

Keywords: Information technology, societal change, institutional theory, institutional logics, IT affordances, IT innovation, societal challenges, digital transformation

## Introduction

Information technologies (IT) constitute one of the most visible manifestations of change in contemporary society. Societal changes, including changes to social relations, economic interactions, and political processes, are increasingly entangled with IT. Recent work (Lee 2015; Majchrzak et al.

2016; Yoo et al. 2010) has highlighted the rising interest in the role of IT as a source of both positive societal transformations and complex societal challenges. On the one hand, advances in IT are associated with opportunities for positive changes in societal conditions, including enhanced social inclusion (Andrade and Doolin 2016), poverty alleviation (Jha et al. 2016; Leong et al. 2016), and increased political participation (Selander and Jarvenpaa 2016). On the other hand, IT is linked to a wide range of societal challenges, such as loss of privacy (Conger et al. 2013), increased systemic risks (Tarafdar et al. 2013), the deregulation of employment (Brynjolfsson and McAfee 2012), and the undermining of democratic processes (Allcott and Gentzkow 2017).

Despite the increasing visibility and public attention to how IT matters at a societal level, theoretical developments that account for the complexity of the relationship remain limited. Yet, the need for such developments has long been recognized by analysts of IT and transformations in society:

We feel, apprehensively, that we are on a steeply rising curve of change, so that the historical transition to new levels of technological power is creating a crisis of transformation. But what has been transformed is not always clear. To say, vaguely, “our lives” or, flashily, that we are experiencing “future shock” is of little help in understanding the character of that transformation (Bell 1976, p. 576; emphasis added).

Various theories emerged from these early analyses and they proposed diverse understandings of the character of the ongoing transformation (Giddens 1990). Scholars have repeatedly linked IT with the accelerated rate of change (Barrett and Walsham 1999; Walsham 2001), but they often did so by focusing on proxies of IT, such as changes in economic productivity (Bell 1976) or social configurations (Castells 2010). The materiality of IT (Barrett et al. 2012; Yoo et al. 2010) was largely absent from these theories. The materiality of IT refers to “the ways that its physical and/or digital materials are arranged into particular forms that endure across differences in place and time” (Leonardi 2012, p. 29). It thus reflects a technology’s design choices, adoption configurations, and use patterns. Accounting for this materiality is more important now that the “crisis of transformation” has deepened, and the rapid advancement and increased ubiquity of IT have generated large scale digital transformation in most areas of social life.

In contrast to the early macro-level perspectives, studies of IT phenomena within the Information Systems (IS) and Organization Studies (OS) fields have focused their analyses more directly on the materiality of IT. While these studies are well positioned to develop unique contributions to our understanding of the increasing entanglement between IT and societal change, their focus has been on the organizational and individual levels of change (Winter et al. 2014). Where they have recognized the societal level in their theoretical developments, this was often as a context within which IT processes are embedded (Walsham 2017). Rarely has the materiality of IT been conceptualized as constitutive of societal change. Yet, as a domain of IT-related change, the societal level presents significant differences from the meso (organizational) and micro (individual) levels. In particular, the societal level reflects higher levels of complexity emanating from the multiplicity of rationalities that is characteristic of most modern societies (Friedland and Alford 1991; Merali 2006).

In this paper, we develop a model of the relationship between IT and societal change that accounts for both the complexity of society and the materiality of IT as a constituent of societal change. We draw on institutional theory which has been recognized as a source of rich vocabulary and theoretical grounding for studying how institutions affect and are affected by IT (Currie and Swanson 2009; Mignerat and Rivard 2009). Specifically, we draw on the seminal works on institutional logics and their view of society as an interinstitutional system, constituted of multiple institutional orders, each with their defining logic, including the state, profession, market, corporate, and community logics (Friedland and Alford 1991; Thornton et al. 2012).

The definition of societal change depends on the adopted view of society. In this paper, we build on the view of society as an interinstitutional system to define societal change as shifts in the multiplicity of logics that constitute the interinstitutional system. Shifts in the multiplicity of logics can occur through a change in the centrality of logics whereby some logics become more dominant and others more marginalized. They can also arise when there is a change in the compatibility of logics whereby some logics become conflictual and others blend to form a new logic (Besharov and Smith 2014).

We build our theoretical model in two steps. First, we draw on the concept of technology affordances to theorize the ongoing situated dynamics between IT and institutional logics. Drawing on earlier literature (Faraj and Azad 2012; Fayard and Weeks 2014; Markus and Silver 2008), we define technology affordances as possibilities for goal-oriented action that an actor or group of actors perceive in a technology as they engage with the materiality of its artifacts. We conceptualize technology affordances as an elemental category of institutional logics. We focus our theorizing on IT affordances, which stands out as having significant potential for conceptualizing the dynamics between technology and societal change. Specifically, we suggest that institutional logics focus attention on certain IT affordances. As IT affordances are enacted through IT use, specific institutional logics are activated. Second, we propose that IT affordances gain scale and stability through mechanisms of sensegiving, translating, and decoupling, which build on objects, networks, and platforms as carriers of the scaling process. We suggest that these scaled IT affordances can contribute to societal change when they become the basis of shifts in the prevailing configuration of institutional logics.

In the following sections, we start by examining approaches that have pointed to the importance of the relationship between IT and societal change. We then develop our affordance-based institutional logics approach to the study of this relationship. After that, we present our analysis of the literature that helped us identify IT affordances along with their associated societal level logics. We subsequently examine the mechanisms by which IT affordances are scaled as they (re)produce societal level logics, highlighting how societal change develops through shifts in either the centrality or compatibility of logics. We conclude by discussing the implications of our proposed model for future research on IT and societal change.

## IT and Societal Change

Over the decades, various research traditions have recognized the need to make sense of the relationship between IT and societal change. Earlier studies tended to focus on macro level processes in their analyses of IT and its relationship to the observed societal transformations. Noticeable amongst these is the “information society” literature which highlighted different aspects of the ongoing transformation, such as the rise of “post-industrial production” through shifts in the modes of work and economic exchange (Bell 1976), the shift toward a “network society” through transformations in social configurations (Castells 2010), and the advent of a “control revolution” through increased capacity for information processing (Beniger 1997). The focus in this literature has been on the societal consequences of IT, but it nonetheless reflected limited attention to theorizing how these consequences emerge from the development, deployment, or use of IT. In fact, some studies have explicitly argued for placing the theoretical attention away from the materiality of technology or its use, but rather toward its manifestation in society:

The understanding of those transformations comes less from looking at mere inventions or gadgets, but at a simpler, yet more subtle and profound change, which is one in the axes of work and economics (Bell 1976, p. 576).

Later, a more sociological stream of studies focused on technology practices in theorizing its role in societal transformation. In particular, SCOT (the social construction of technology) studies have built on the traditions of science– technology–society (STS) and the sociology of scientific knowledge (SSK) to investigate the social processes through which technology is constructed (Pinch and Bijker 1987). While this stream of research was first concerned with technological artefacts and systems as units of analysis, the focus has more recently shifted toward a societal level of analysis:

The agenda was broadened to include the issue of technology’s impact on society, which had been bracketed for the sake of fighting technological determinism (Bijker 2010, p. 71).

This shift has taken the form of an increased emphasis on what they call “technological cultures,” reflecting the new reality that “today’s societies…are thoroughly technological and all technologies are pervasively cultural” (Bijker 2010, p. 67). However, these studies have largely considered technological cultures as an explanation for societal outcomes, and consequently paid less attention to how these cultures emerge in relation to the materiality of the technology.

In contrast, studies within the IS and other Management fields that adopt a practice approach suggest a view of IT as performed through material-discursive practices. In so doing, they offered a wider ontological view of the social world as composed of sociomaterial practices (Orlikowski and Scott 2015) or actor–networks (Hanseth et al. 2004), and hence of societal change as unfolding through the entanglement of IT and human agencies. However, while broader in their orientation, these theoretical approaches do not explicitly conceptualize IT and societal change. In particular, these approaches lack a theoretical vocabulary for discussing macro level phenomena, for which they have been criticized as typically “neglect[ing] broader social structures” (Mutch 2013, p. 28). Other studies have highlighted the importance of taking wider societal issues into consideration in order to gain a more holistic understanding of the design, implementation, and use of IT (Avgerou 2001; Currie 2009; Ein-Dor and Segev 1982). Such studies have used social inequalities (Hsieh et al. 2008), political processes (Silva and Hirschheim 2007), and market norms (Koch and Schultze 2011) to explain IT development and use within and across organizations, but the extent to which these societal conditions are themselves being altered by IT is rarely the focus of theoretical development.

Thus, there is a need for theorizing, more explicitly, how IT is constitutive of societal change. For this purpose, we turn to institutional theory, in particular institutional logics, and link them to their micro-foundations as a basis for developing a theoretical framework of IT and societal change.

## An Institutional Perspective of IT and Societal Change

## IT and Institutional Change

Institutional theory offers a unique approach for conceptualizing societal change. One of the main thrusts of the theory has been its view of organizations as embedded in societal environments that shape their decisions and practices beyond the technical requirements of their internal processes (Greenwood et al. 2008; Scott 2014). As such, applications of the theory have focused on institutions—the enduring and taken-for-granted patterns of thinking and acting—as the building blocks of society and, therefore, the means to conceptualizing both societal stability and change (Greenwood et al. 2002; Leung et al. 2014; Zucker 1977).

Most applications of the theory to the study of IT have focused on the effect of institutional forces on IT practices, such as the development, implementation, and use of IT within and across organizations (Butler 2011; Teo et al. 2003). However, some studies challenged the ontological separation between IT and institutions implied by this approach and developed instead a conceptualization of IT as a “carrier” (Gosain 2004; Lyytinen et al. 2009; Scott 2014, p. 49) or “material instantiation” (Gawer and Phillips 2013, p. 1042) of institutions. Building on this approach, Scott (2014, p. 102) suggests that artefacts are carriers of institutions because they embody norms, ideas, and values. He draws on the duality of technology perspective (Orlikowski 1992) to highlight the structuration of technological artifacts, which manifest as an outcome of human agency, but once implemented and routinely used become invisible components of objective reality; in other words, they become institutionalized.

However, in order to explicitly conceptualize the relationship between IT and societal change, we need to relate IT practices to institutional processes at the societal level. To do so, we draw on the institutional logics perspective which offers a valuable and direct conceptualization of institutions at the societal level.

## The Institutional Logics Perspective

The institutional logics perspective started with a seminal article by Friedland and Alford (1991) in which they called for “bringing society back in” to the study of organizational phenomena. Their approach was built on a conception of society as an interinstitutional system; that is a system of multiple institutional orders, each with a defining logic. These institutional orders distinguish between different areas of social life on the basis of their “symbolic systems” and “ways of ordering reality” that make the “experience of time and space meaningful” (Friedland and Alford 1991, p. 243). The market and the family are two such orders in which the sources of meaning differ and reality is ordered differently. Each of the institutional orders has a central logic that is “a set of material practices and symbolic constructions which constitutes its organizing principles and which is available to organizations and individuals to elaborate” (Friedland and Alford 1991, p. 248). The identification and distinction between the major institutional logics that constitute modern society provides a powerful analytical framework for studying societal dynamics by analyzing the interactions between the various logics.

The interinstitutional system has been developed as a typology of institutional logics. Friedland and Alford initially suggested a typology that included five institutional logics, namely the market, the state, democracy, family, and religion. More recently, Thornton (2004, p. 45) built on and modified this typology to be constituted of six logics, including the market, corporate, profession, state, family, and religion, to which “community” was subsequently added as a seventh institutional logic (Thornton et al. 2012). Each of these logics defines an institutional order at the societal level, but they are also reflected at the field<sup>2</sup> and organizational levels, either as subtypes of the societal level logics (e.g., service logic as subtype of market logic) or as hybrids of different logics (e.g., logic of social entrepreneurship as hybrid of market and community logics).

Institutional logics are ideal types, which are heuristic devices for capturing the distinctive features of a phenomenon. This means that

they do not precisely conform to reality because of deliberate simplification to afford comparative analysis and multidimensional classification of phenomena....[They instead] assign a hypothetical meaning that can be used as a yardstick to compare and contrast hypothesized and actual meaning and behavior (Thornton and Ocasio 2008, p. 110).

As ideal types, institutional logics have been defined using sets of concepts that help distinguish between them. Different studies use different sets of concepts to define institutional logics. For example, Thornton et al (2012) define institutional logics by specifying their “elemental categories,” which include basis of norms, sources of legitimacy, sources of authority, sources of identity, and basis of attention. In contrast, Berente and Yoo (2012) define logics by specifying their “dimensions” as organizing principles, assumptions, identities, and domains. On the other hand, Bjerregaard and Jonasson (2014) define logics by specifying their “characteristics” as economic system, structure of field, basis of mission and strategy, sources of productivity, and control of human capital.

The diversity of elemental categories provides the analyst with flexibility in choosing context-appropriate approaches for distinguishing between logics. This flexibility has made the institutional logics perspective a theoretically productive framework for linking individual and organizational practices to societal level phenomena. In particular, the resulting approach to conceptualizing the multiplicity of logics at the societal level has proven generative of valuable insights in the study of institutional change. First, it enables the analysis of practices that generate change from one dominant logic to another. Second, since multiple coexisting logics can either contradict or complement each other, the institutional logics approach enables the analysis of practices that seek to produce new institutional arrangements by building on the contradictions or complementarities of logics (Besharov and Smith 2014; Gawer and Phillips 2013).

## IT and Institutional Logics

Several studies of IT phenomena have found in the institutional logics perspective a suitable framework for analyzing the complexity of the environments in which IT practices unfold. Extant studies have highlighted how the dynamics of institutional logics both influence and are influenced by IT practices. As discussed below, some studies have focused on the role of IT in instigating changes from one dominant organizational logic to another, or what Besharov and Smith (2014) call changes to the degree of centrality of logics. Another set of studies focused instead on the role of IT practices in changes to the degree of compatibility of logics, which represents the extent to which they complement or contradict each other in a given institutional environment (Besharov and Smith 2014).

An example of studies that focused on shifts in the centrality of logics is the work of Gawer and Phillips (2012), which analyzed Intel’s efforts to shift the computer manufacturing industry from a “supply chain logic” to a new “platform logic.” They associated these changes in the field’s logic with changes in architectural design choices toward open interfaces such as the peripheral component interconnect (PCI) and the universal serial bus (USB). Another example is Hultin and Mahring’s (2014) study which investigated how a new logic comes to be a dominant logic as it becomes integrated into an established domain of practice. They drew on a case study of visualization boards being implemented in the emergency services of a hospital to show how the visualization artefacts facilitated the integration and subsequent dominance of a “lean management logic” into existing hospital practices.

Examples of studies that focused on the compatibility of logics include the work of Berente and Yoo (2012) who analyzed how IT practices are affected by contradictions between multiple logics. Building on a case of enterprise system implementation at NASA, they argued that the adopted system reflected the logic of “managerial rationalism” which contradicted other logics at the organization, such as the logic of “scientific professionalism.” This led organizational actors who drew from the other logics to loosely couple their practices with those presumed by the IT system. In contrast to Berente and Yoo’s focus on contradictions between logics, Slavova and Karanasios (2018) highlighted possible complementarities between logics. They built on a case of IT adoption in the Ghanian agricultural sector to describe how IT artifacts enabled the hybridization of the “smallholder logic” and the “value chain logic.”

These studies reveal various mechanisms through which institutional logics shape IT practices, as well as mechanisms through which IT practices produce and reproduce institutional logics. However, these analyses focus on institutional logics at the organizational and field levels, and do not examine how IT practices become integral to societal change. In addition, while these studies describe the mutual influence between IT and institutional logics, they do not develop a framework for integrating IT into institutional logics. Such a framework is important for understanding the enduring effects of IT and its increasingly critical role as constituent of institutional environments. In the next two subsections, we develop such a framework by proposing the concept of IT affordances as a basis for integrating IT into the institutional logics perspective.

## Integrating IT into Institutional Logics: An Affordance-Based Approach

The institutional theory literature suggests various mechanisms for linking institutional logics to practices. Thornton et al. (2012) suggest “focus of attention” as the most direct mechanism through which institutional logics shape practices: “institutional logics guide the allocation of attention by shaping what problems and issues get attended to and what solutions are likely to be considered in decision making” (Thornton et al. 2012, p. 90). In IS studies, the concept that most closely reflects focus of attention as a mechanism for the materiality of IT translating into practices is that of “IT affordances.” The concept of IT affordances highlights the ways in which the attention of the users become intertwined with material elements of IT to produce new possibilities of action. We therefore build our theoretical approach for integrating IT into the conceptualization of institutional logics on the concept of IT affordances.

IT affordances refer to the possibilities of action that users perceive in IT artefacts based on their goal orientation and their use environment (Markus and Silver 2008). As such, affordances are neither a property of the artefact, the user, nor the context of use, but a property of their relationship (Hutchby 2001; Leonardi 2011; Markus and Silver 2008; Seidel et al. 2013; Zammuto et al. 2007). People construct affordances as they “attempt to reconcile their own goals with the materiality of a technology” (Leonardi 2011, p. 154). Affordances have originally been conceptualized at the individual level, but they are increasingly studied at the collective level (Fayard and Weeks 2014; Leonardi 2013; Vaast et al. 2017; Zheng and Yu 2016), reflecting how common goals of multiple users generate common patterns of IT use (collective affordances) as well as diverse patterns (shared affordances) (Leonardi 2013).

Thus, the concept of affordances offers a way for researchers of IT phenomena to account for both the socio-cognitive conditions of IT users and the materiality of IT. Figure 1 presents a model that positions IT affordances as a conceptual link in the recursive relationship between institutional logics and IT-in-use, IT as manifested in everyday practice (Orlikowski and Gash 1994; Vaast and Walsham 2005). As individuals and organizations draw from accessible logics in their interactions with IT-in-use, they focus their attention on specific IT affordances. In turn, IT affordances (re)enact ITin-use and prompt the users to activate specific institutional logics. The figure reprerents the link between the three concepts using verbs (e.g., focus attention) instead of nouns (e.g., focus of attention) in order to highlight the processual nature of their relation.

Thus, users of social media sites who draw on a community logic are more likely to focus their attention on IT affordances that support community building, while users who draw from a market logic are more likely to focus their attention on affordances that enable marketing activities (see Leonardi and Vaast 2016). In turn, when specific affordances emerge from the interaction of users with IT artifacts, they induce the users to activate corresponding institutional logics and enact IT uses that reflect the emerging affordances. For example, when transacting affordances emerge from the interaction of subsistence farmers with e-commerce platforms, the farmers, whose production activities tend to be subsumed under a family or community logic, are likely to activate a market logic in their organizing activities so as to enact uses of the e-commerce platform in ways that enhance the possibilities of transacting (see Leong et al. 2016).

While the recent literature on affordances conceptualizes them as a relation that involves the goals of the users, it does not theorize how these goals are formed to become part of the relation. Yet, by conceptualizing the emergence of affordances in terms of the relationship between institutional logics and IT-in-use, as proposed by the model above, we can analyze the formation of the users’ goals by examining the institutional logics that they draw from. Importantly, by attending to the multiplicity of these logics, we can examine the diversity and potential contradictions of users’ goals as they interact with IT.

We build on Figure 1 in developing our theoretical model linking IT and societal change by proposing technology affordances as an elemental category of institutional logics. As shown in Table 1, multiple elemental categories define the various institutional orders and their corresponding societal level logics (Thornton et al. 2012). We therefore locate technology affordances as constituting another (“Y axis” row) elemental category in conjunction with other categories, such as the basis of legitimacy, norms, identity and authority.

While our proposed elemental category is the broader concept of technology affordances, our focus in this paper is on IT affordances, which represent one of the most visible manifestations of the dynamics between technology and societal change. Multiple IT affordances can be perceived through the activation of the same institutional logic and the activation of different logics can generate the same IT affordance. Therefore, to determine the IT affordances that are central to a logic, we need to identify the set of affordances that are distinctive to that logic as an ideal type, which we discuss below.

## Establishing IT Affordances as an Elemental Category of Institutional Logics

To identify the set of IT affordances that are typical of each societal level logic, we conducted a systematic literature review (Webster and Watson 2002) of the studies that include institutional analyses of IT phenomena. We reviewed articles in the Information Systems literature that adopted institutional theory as the main theoretical lens, as well as articles in the Organization Studies literature that draw on institutional theory to frame an IT-related study. The rationale was to include studies that combine analyses of the institutional environment, allowing the identification of institutional logics, with discussions of IT practices and processes, allowing the identification of corresponding IT affordances.

The scope of our search included the eight Information Systems journals in the Association for Information Systems Senior Scholars’ basket, as well as eight equivalent journals from the Organization Studies field and two journals that were added for their potential to include institutional theory studies of IT, namely Information and Organization and Research Policy (see Appendix A for list of journals and distribution of identified articles). The timeframe of our analysis included publications from 2007 to 2016 inclusive. Instead of aiming for comprehensiveness in the review of the literature, our objective was to attain theoretical saturation so as to identify a set of IT affordances that corresponds to each of the logics. We only included articles that were based on empirical studies as we needed to conduct our own coding for both the logics and the affordances; most existing studies did not explicitly identify logics and corresponding affordances in the same article. Both qualitative and quantitative studies were included, although for quantitative studies the coding was limited to the study background and findings sections. A final list of 53 articles was included in the analysis (see Appendix A).

A pilot set of ten articles was coded separately by two of the authors. Both coders carefully read through the same set of ten articles and identified the relevant logics and affordances presented in the study. Each coder was blind to the response of the other. Affordances and corresponding logics were identified by following a hermeneutic circle (Klein and Myers 1999), moving back and forth between the broader context of the study and the specifics of IT use as described in the accounts and analyses. After some discussion, both coders converged on the same logics in all ten papers. Some papers identified logics explicitly, which we used directly in our coding. In other papers, we inferred the logic from the description of the institutional context of the study, which was present in all studies given that the use of institutional analysis was a selection criteria for our study. When the identified logics were societal level logics, these were used directly in the coding. However, in many papers, the logics discussed were hybrid logics at the field or organizational level. In these cases, we had to tease out from the identified IT affordances those that relate to each of the corresponding societal level logics.

For some papers, both coders chose the same or similar key wording for the affordance(s), such as “controlling” for affordances associated with the corporate logic. In other papers, different wordings and themes were emphasized, such as “controlling funds” and “standardizing costs” or “enhancing social interaction” and “supporting collective identity.” The coders discussed the similarities and differences between the codes across the papers with the other author and agreed on a final list of the different affordances that had been coded for each logic type. The rest of the papers were then divided and coders repeated the process for the remainder of the papers. If either coder was unsure about a code, the paper was read by both and then discussed.

To avoid confirmation bias in the attribution of affordances to logics, we only included IT affordances for which the findings or the analysis of the article indicate an association with the given logic. For example, Jensen et al. (2009) present a case of an electronic patient record (EPR) system implementation in a Danish hospital. They suggest the standardizing of records as one of the main affordances presented in government policy, accessing real-time data as one of the affordances articulated by the hospital management, and facilitating prescription procedures as one of the affordances perceived by the physicians. These affordances were consequently coded under the state, corporate, and professional logics respectively.

In most papers, the concept of affordances is not used directly in the article, but is instead inferred from statements of goals, expectations, and perceptions associated with the implementation and use of IT. In naming the affordances, we adopted the convention of using gerunds or action verbs (Majchrzak and Markus 2012), which has the theoretical advantage of highlighting the ongoing performance of affordances (Weick 1979) and consequently their dependence on the continuous activation of logics.

We analyzed all 53 identified articles based on the above approach. In five of the articles, we could not identify any expression or representation of IT affordances. The results of the coding for the remaining 48 articles are presented in the table in Appendix B. All articles had between one and three logics, but none of the articles included elements of the family or religion logics. While these two logics have been studied in the Organization Studies literature, for example in the context of family businesses (Bullinger 2014) and the management of religious institutions (Creed et al. 2010), no study within the scope of our search has linked them to an IT phenomenon. Our results are therefore limited to the remaining five logics.

The results show that some categories of IT affordances are consistently associated with the same logic across multiple papers. We conducted axial (second cycle) coding (Miles et al. 2013, p. 86) along each of the columns in the table in order to generate IT affordances that are representative of these categories. Our objective was to identify affordances that are typical of the corresponding logics and can therefore be part of their theoretical construction as ideal types. To do so, we needed to select the right level of abstraction for our representative affordances so that they are reflective of how the materiality of IT makes a difference to the logic of action within each of the institutional orders. The intermediate level codes in this process are presented at the end of Appendix B and the resulting affordances are presented in Table 2. These affordances are not suggested as the only ones that can be associated with the corresponding logics, but our review of the literature suggests that they are the most typical of these logics.

The content of the cells in Table 2 specify IT affordances as an elemental category that defines each of the logics. They consequently need to be understood in terms of the recursive relationship between IT and institutional logics. For example, identifying the IT affordances that characterize the state logic as broadening accessibility and traceability means, on the one hand, that when actors draw from a state logic in their use of IT they will mostly focus their attention on the possibilities for broadening access to public services as well as widening the range of actions that can be traced by state institutions. On the other hand, IT systems that are perceived to primarily afford the broadening of accessibility and traceability can make the state logic more salient. Examples of this relationship are given in public health institutions that perceive in IT systems the possibility of expanding the population’s access to healthcare services (Currie and Guah 2007) and of monitoring the spending of public funds on healthcare programs (Davidson and Chismar 2007).

Similarly, presenting the IT affordances that are typical of the professional logic as enhancing knowledgeability and autonomy means, on the one hand, that when professionals enact their professional identity and norms they will focus attention on the possibilities for increasing their knowledge and for projecting that knowledge to others. Professional logic will also focus their attention on the possibility of maintaining their autonomy from bureaucratic control. On the other hand, the professional logic will be activated or undermined in a given context depending on the extent to which the enacted IT systems are perceived to enhance knowledgeability and autonomy. These dynamics were observed, for example, in electronic patient record systems, which allow doctors to maintain and enhance their professional autonomy by helping them to prioritize patients and freeing them from hierarchical pressures (Hultin and Mähring 2014). They were also observed in telemedicine technologies supporting the learning and legitimacy of science-based medicine in remote areas (Miscione 2007).

Suggesting that the IT affordance characteristic of the market logic is stimulating and coordinating transactions means that individuals and organizations that draw from this logic in their use of IT will mostly focus their attention on possibilities for stimulating and managing market relations. This may be achieved by accessing new customers or enhancing the level of transactions with existing ones, as well as by coordinating transactions across value chains. It also means that when actors primarily perceive in IT possibilities for stimulating and coordinating their transactions, the market logic will be more prevalent and more frequently activated. This relationship is reflected in organizational actors perceiving the possibility of attracting attention to their brand or increasing customer intimacy through social media (Miranda et al. 2015). It is also reflected in the providers of mobile services perceiving in advances in mobile technology possibilities for coordinating and integrating the markets for text messaging and data services (Knutsen and Lyytinen 2008).

Likewise, identifying the IT affordances that are typical of the corporate logic as standardizing and controlling operations means, on the one hand, that actors who draw from a corporate logic in their use of IT will focus their attention on the possibilities for enacting standards and maintaining control over organizational operations. On the other hand, it also means that the introduction of new IT into a societal environment can make the activation of the corporate logic more frequent if they are perceived to primarily afford the standardization and control of operations. This recursive relationship is reflected, for example, in organizational actors perceiving in enterprise systems the possibility of increasing accountability and controlling costs (Berente and Yoo 2012), in green IS the possibility of monitoring compliance processes and guiding resource allocation (Butler 2011), or in healthcare IT systems the possibility of establishing new standards for medical practice (Kaganer et al. 2010).

Finally, suggesting that the IT affordance most typical of the community logic is connecting members and opening governance means, on the one hand, that actors drawing from such a logic in their use of IT will most likely focus their attention on possibilities for creating ties between community members, as well as possibilities for supporting open and participatory governance within the community. On the other hand, it also means that perceiving in an IT system possibilities for connecting members and of supporting open governance makes the community logic more salient amongst the system users. This relationship was reflected, for example, in credit unions who made the community logic more salient when they started perceiving in IT-based financial services possibilities for enhancing social inclusion, democratizing control, and connecting union members (Mangan and Kelly 2009).

## IT Affordances and Societal Change

## Scaling IT Affordances

Having shown how IT in use can be linked to societal level institutional logics through the concept of IT affordances, we now examine how IT affordances gain scale and stability so as to enable (or constrain) societal change. To this end, we build on the work of Ocasio et al. (2015) which synthesizes the body of work in institutional theory that addresses the link between local situated action and societal level institutional logics. They identified three key mechanisms through which discursive action produces the structures that shape institutional logics: sensegiving, translating, and theorizing. Since the objective of our theoretical development is not limited to discursive action but aims to account for both the material and the discursive, we supplement their framework to account better for the scaling of IT affordances, as presented in Figure 2.

Sensegiving is the mechanism by which IT affordances are scaled through ascribing stable meanings to actions. When users engage in actions afforded by IT, they inscribe meanings into material artifacts, and turn them into durable objects (Akrich 1992; Leonardi 2010). This results in the typification of actions and turns the ascribed meanings into objects of external reality. The materiality, both physical and digital, of the resulting objects enables the externalization of social meanings, and consequently gives scale and stability to IT affordances. For example, social media users produce texts, memes, and videos that turn ephemeral pieces of information into durable objects that have the capacity to transcend established boundaries of practice. In so doing, they enable the scaling of emerging affordances and create new possibilities for collective action.

Translating is about scaling IT affordances by reproducing practices in new contexts. For communicative actions, translating takes place through institutional narratives that transcend their local context (Ocasio et al. 2015). Such narratives become a means to linking multiple contexts and enabling localized variations of a durable practice to coexist across contexts. However, translating practices across contexts is not limited to narrative discourses. Practices are increasingly reproduced in new context through IT affordances that make the links between multiple variations of practice possible and durable (Czarniawska-Joerges and Sevón 2005). Instead of institutional narratives as the main manifestation of translating processes (Ocasio et al. 2015), studies adopting a materialdiscursive perspective have used the concept of networks to reflect the translating of sociomaterial practices (Takhteyev 2009; Vaast and Walsham 2009). For example, Wasko and Faraj (2005) highlighted the affordances of discussion forums as the basis on which members of dispersed communities translate their local practices to new contexts, producing electronic networks of practice that facilitate knowledge flow. The IT affordances emerging from such practices become scalable by being embedded in stabilizing networks.

Finally, theorizing is about abstracting from specific instances of discursive actions to generate layers of meanings that transcend the local context (Ocasio et al. 2015). Institutional theorists have extensively discussed theorizing as a link between local practices and institutional processes. However, their conceptualization has been focused on the creation of abstract categories that become part of discursive practices. They have paid limited attention to the materiality of everyday practices in understanding how they become abstracted from their local instantiations. To this end, we propose decoupling as an alternative mechanism for scaling IT affordances. Decoupling has been used to reflect the abstraction of practices from a material-discursive perspective (Berente and Yoo 2012). The decoupling of material processes abstracts them from their enabling infrastructures and consequently generates layers of loosely coupled practices. In this regard, Yoo et al. (2010) argue that “pervasive digitization gives birth to a new type of product architecture” that includes “loosely coupled layers of devices, networks, services, and contents created by digital technology” (p. 724). Decoupling makes this layered architecture possible and consequently enables the IT affordances being enacted in practice to be more dynamic and malleable (Barrett et al. 2012; Yoo et al. 2012). In this way, IT affordances emerging from the decoupling of practices across platforms can scale rapidly in unprecedented ways (Huang et al. 2017). For example, Barrett et al. (2016) showed how a digital solution that enabled patients to rate their healthcare providers resulted in the decoupling of the provision of healthcare services from their evaluation. This generated a platform that supported an expanding number of practices, such as performance tracking by healthcare providers, healthcare support by charity organizations, and medical research by pharmaceutical companies.

In sum, sensegiving, translating, and decoupling are mechanisms that give IT affordances scale and durability by building on objects, networks, and platforms as materialdiscursive carriers of scaling processes. Objects make IT affordances transferrable, networks link them horizontally across contexts, and platforms enable them to proliferate across constructed levels of practice. The scaled IT affordances thus constitute a type of infrastructure through which societal processes flow and represent a basis for the (re)production of societal change.

## Conceptualizing Societal Change

In this section, we discuss how to analyze societal change based on the proposed framework. As discussed earlier, the institutional logics perspective suggests an understanding of societal change that is based on a view of society as an interinstitutional system, and therefore a view of change in society as shifts in the multiplicity of the logics that constitute this system. Thus, changes in this multiplicity can be understood across two main dimensions: the centrality and the compatibility of logics (Besharov and Smith 2014). That is, societal change can refer to shifts in the level of centrality or dominance of a logic or in the level of compatibility between multiple coexisting logics.

## Societal Change through Shifts in the Centrality of Logics

The first category of societal change that emerges when scaled IT affordances become integral to a societal context is the shift in centrality from one dominant logic to another. When scaled IT affordances generate new practices, they can make a previously dominant logic less central and a previously marginal logic more central. Over time, these changes can result in a shift from one dominant logic to another or allow for the coexistence of multiple dominant logics.

Such shifts in the centrality of logics were manifested in the 2011 “Arab Spring” uprisings, particularly those events in Tunisia and Egypt, which occurred in an environment where the state logic was dominant. An important element of this dominance was the affordance of Internet technologies for traceability. In Tunisia, for example, the government exerted its influence online by tracking the actions of citizens and censuring them with a view to silencing dissension (Allagui and Kuebler 2011). Similarly, in Egypt, state control over political participation through new media technologies made resistance by local citizens difficult as resistance activities could be traced by government agents and apprehended (Howard et al. 2011). However, in the period leading to the uprisings of 2011, users were able to undermine the centrality of the state logic through new uses of social media that made the government more accountable for their actions and resulted in a relative opening of governance in the country. For example, activists in Egypt created a highly popular Facebook page that used the increasing visibility of police brutality to build the momentum of opposition to the government (Bowen 2013). For similar objectives, social media users in Tunisia circulated pictures of corrupt practices by members of the ruling regime (Howard et al. 2011). In so doing, they generated, from their collective engagement with social media, affordances for opening governance in the public sphere.

These affordances gained scale and durability as the activists used social media to provide a stable meaning (sensegiving) to their struggle by creating images and texts that turned their previously fleeting protest activities into durable objects. They also enabled them to reproduce variations of their local activities in different parts of the country (translating), creating networks of resistance practices that gave momentum to their movement. The new affordances also gained scale as activists were able to maintain loose coupling (decoupling) between their local coordination practices and their mass mobilization activities. This made the interactions between the core groups of activists, along with the social media features they used, a platform for mobilizing large scale participation in the movement.

The scaling of the affordances for the opening of governance eventually resulted in an increase in the centrality of the community logic and a decrease in the ability of the state logic to retain its dominance in the public sphere. However, the tensions of dominance between the two logics were not fully settled following the 2011 uprising. In the subsequent years, the state was able to revert to an even stronger dominance of the state logic in reaction to the uprising, thereby (successfully) challenging attempts by the movement to maintain momentum and effect lasting societal change. These efforts managed to challenge the stability of the newly scaled IT affordances and to inhibit more of the corresponding societal change. They did so by scaling affordances that were conducive to the dominance of the state logic, such as affordances for tracking dissent on social media (Tufekci 2017).

Another example of shifts in the centrality of logics as a basis of societal change, albeit at a slower pace, can be observed in the healthcare field. In various healthcare practices, IT affordances have enabled a shift in the centrality of the professional logic away from its historically dominant position. The affordances of online technologies have enabled new modes of knowledge seeking amongst patients, which has resulted in significant changes to how they seek medical advice. Simple internet searches allow patients to access large amounts of medical information that was, until recently, the sole purview of medical professionals. Patients are also able to compare information across multiple sources and all these changes are producing large numbers of what some have called “expert patients” (Willis 2014). All this undermines the historical dependence on professionals for healthcare advice in modern societies and represents a shift in the centrality of the professional logic from its historically dominant position.

To further elaborate on this example, seeking healthcare advice online has taken a variety of forms, including advice columns from healthcare providers (e.g., NHS Choices in the United Kingdom), online publishers (e.g., WebMD), and patient peers (e.g., PatientsLikeMe). Recent estimates suggest that WebMD receives more unique visits per month than all the doctors working in the United States (Susskind and Susskind 2015, p. 47). The use of these online forums generates affordances for enhancing connectivity amongst patients (community logic) and for increasing transactions with pharmaceutical and insurance companies (market logic). However, they do not afford in a similar manner the enhancing of medical knowledgeability (professional logic), as it is more difficult for professionals to distinguish their voice from the many that compete for attention on these forums, which limits their ability to project their knowledge to others.

Thus, how new IT affordances gain scale and stability is affecting the societal dominance of the professional logic within the medical field. In particular, through the affordances of online healthcare forums, lay individuals can stabilize the meaning of their healthcare advices (sensegiving), which become relatively immutable objects that are accessible and searchable by others at all times. Also, the ability to create communities of patients in these forums enhances the possibility for healthcare practices to spread across different contexts (translating), which creates networks through which new medical practices can gain scale and stability. Finally, the affordances of the healthcare forums, such as those built on anonymity features, allows participants to separate between the practices that shape their identity as patients and those that relate to their other identities in society (decoupling). This turns their material-discursive connections as patients into platforms that enable the proliferation of healthcare advice practices. Thus, the affordances of online healthcare forums are scaled in ways that are rendering the professional logic less central, and are consequently producing societal changes that reflect new meanings and diverse experiences of healthcare.

## Societal Change through Shifts in the Compatibility of Logics

The second category of societal change from an interinstitutional system perspective involves shifts in the compatibility of logics. When IT affordances gain scale and stability, they can enable shifts in the interactions between multiple logics either toward increased conflict, with heightened tensions between competing logics, or toward increased complementarity, with the potential for coexisting logics to blend and form new hybrid logics. For example, in news production and consumption, print newspapers have previously provided space for professional journalists to gather facts and publish their crafted news pieces. In these practices, the professional logic dominated the field of news production, with the community logic being less central, yet compatible. Community members could, for example, engage with the medium through letters to the editor.

More recently, however, IT affordances for connecting members of virtual communities in the creation and exchange of news gained scale through processes of sensegiving around new informational objects, such as blogposts and mashups, which produce and reproduce news stories beyond the professional practices of journalists. This enables internet users to target their news stories to like-minded readers through “sharing” features of social media (Thompson 2014). In addition, the new affordances gained scale and stability as alternative news consumption practices proliferated across networks, appearing in new contexts (translating). They also scaled because of the decoupling of news content from the social media sites on which it circulates. The latter became platforms that enabled the spread of news with limited responsibility over the quality of content, which lead to the proliferation of “fake news” and new threats to established democratic processes (Gant 2007). Thus, the community logic of news production has become increasingly taken up in a way that is more compatible with the market logic of the owners of social media platforms and less with the professional logic of traditional journalism.

The compatibility of logics can also shift in a way that leads to the blending of logics and the emergence of a new hybrid logic that is more conducive to addressing persistent societal challenges. For example, in a case of smallholder farming in India (Jha et al. 2016), the community oriented approach to providing agricultural services, such as advice on the choice of fertilizers and seeds, was initially incompatible with the market logic of commercial agricultural services. Yet, new soil testing devices along with the platforms built around them allowed young micro-entrepreneurs to provide services to the farmers through a sustainable business model, and at the same time contribute to strengthening community interactions by supporting the growth of farmer interest groups amongst their clients.

In this case, the affordances for both coordinating transactions and connecting members resulting from the microentrepreneurs’ interaction with the new IT platform created the basis for increased compatibility between the community and market logics. The scaling of affordances was the result of new objects such as transaction records, which were given stable meanings through the extensive use of the new digital devices (sensegiving). It was also the result of the networks of new farming practices that were spreading across multiple farming communities (translating) by building on the connections of the micro-entrepreneurs and the standards of the devices. Further, the scaling of IT affordances was the result of the decoupling of farming processes from soil testing, which turned soil testing practices into a platform that attracted multiple new stakeholders such as retail outlets, NGOs, and banks. These shifts in the compatibility of the community and market logics represented societal changes that in this case enabled poverty alleviation (Jha et al. 2016), as communities with little initial affinity for market logics took on new roles, including those of entrepreneurs, and diversified their revenue streams while strengthening the bonds in their communities.

## Discussion and Implications for Future Research

In this paper, we contribute a new model for theorizing IT and societal change which is based on conceptualizing IT affordances as integral to institutional logics. The model proposes a recursive relation in which institutional logics focus attention on specific IT affordances, and IT affordances activate particular logics as actors enact IT-in-use. In addition, we propose three mechanisms, namely sensegiving, translating, and decoupling, through which IT affordances gain scale and stability by drawing on objects, networks, and platforms as carriers of scaling processes. The scaled IT affordances can alter or reinforce the centrality and the compatibility of institutional logics, and thus contribute to or inhibit societal change. This relationship between IT and societal change can reflect positive transformations, such as socioeconomic inclusion, increased freedoms, and improved well-being, or adverse consequences, such as new forms of state control, higher risk of privacy infringement, and the deskilling of professional work.

In what follows we discuss how our proposed model contributes to our understanding of such large scale shifts in societal processes that are emerging at a rapid rate, and then suggest potential directions and opportunities for future research. First, our model helps in pushing the conceptualization of IT phenomena beyond the current focus on the organizational and individual levels of analysis and challenges the view of society as a mere context for IT practices. People’s lives are becoming increasingly intertwined with IT in ways that extend beyond work and organizational spheres of action (Carter and Grover 2015) and, as such, individuals, through their use of IT, are more readily drawing from and reshaping the multiplicity of logics that coexist at the societal level. Thus, not only are organizations becoming more institutionally complex (Greenwood et al. 2011), the ubiquitous access to IT has blurred the boundaries between previously defined spheres of activity in society, such as family, community, market, and work (Oborn et al. 2019). As a result, people are increasingly switching between or combining multiple logics across these interconnected activities. Conceptualizing society as an interinstitutional system of different but intersecting institutional logics helps us account for this multiplicity. However, while the current conceptualizations of institutional logics provide a framing for how societal level logics shape IT use, they do not account for how these logics are also increasingly shaped by the materiality of IT use. By theorizing IT affordances as an elemental category of institutional logics, our model unpacks the mechanisms by which logics as higher-order cultural structures (Ocasio et al. 2015) are connected with individual and organizational actions through IT use (Berente and Yoo 2015; Orlikowski 2000).

Second, the model avoids undue emphasis on the situated enactment of IT as the basis of change, which often leads studies drawing from a practice perspective to neglect macrolevel societal structures (Mutch 2013, Walsham 1997). It does so by building on extant literature to propose the three mechanisms of sensegiving, translating, and decoupling, as a basis for understanding how IT affordances gain scale and stability to become collective affordances at a societal level. It also proposes durable objects, extensible networks, and generative platforms as material-discursive carriers of the scaling processes. The scaled affordances that emerge from these processes become a type of infrastructure for societal processes, either changing or reproducing prevalent societal conditions. In this way, our model is able to account for how societal change emerges from IT users’ varied appropriation of technology into new practices that spread across the wider society (see Barrett et al. 2016; Jha et al. 2016; Leonardi et al. 2016).

Third, our model provides an alternative to macro level approaches that focus their analyses of IT and societal change on shifts or evolution toward an “information society” or a “technological culture,” and in which the role of IT in societal change remains abstract and removed from the materiality and diversity of IT use. It does so by presenting an approach to examining the relationship between IT and societal change through analyses of the various possible interactions between institutional logics constituting the interinstitutional system along with their corresponding IT affordances. In particular, the integration of IT affordances into institutional logics enables the analysis of how emerging IT affordances generate conditions for one logic to become more dominant and another more marginal, or for a logic to become more compatible with some logics and less with others. This approach enables us, for example, to examine how the rise of artificial intelligence and big data technologies is allowing the state logic to be dominant in some parts of the world with the salience of tracking and surveillance affordances (Lyon 2014), or how it is blending the market and corporate logics through affordances for real time integration of market information

Finally, our proposed theoretical model presents a number of promising avenues for future research. Table 3 outlines several opportunities for broadening the research agenda on IT and societal change, and points to various illustrative areas for future work.

Key to our proposed model is the integration of IT into the conceptualization of institutional logics based on the concept of affordances. Our literature review identified affordances that are distinctive of five societal level logics as ideal types (Table 2). Future work can usefully validate and refine these affordances and further develop relevant IT affordances at the field and organizational levels (potential research topic 1 in Table 3). For example, while we identify stimulating and coordinating transactions as the affordances characteristic of the market logic at the societal level, future studies can build on this proposition to investigate differences in IT affordances across different subcategories of the market logic, such as distinctions between IT affordances in a service logic compared to a goods logic (Srivastava and Shainesh 2015). They can also identify IT affordances for hybrid logics, such as affordances that can be considered typical of the hybrid between the market and community logics, as reflected in social enterprises (Smith et al. 2013). Future work can also investigate affordances of other (non-IT) technologies involved in shaping societal change, such as transportation and energy technologies. Such work is likely to converge with IT studies because of the increasingly common embedding of IT systems in most forms of technology.

Future studies can also build on the analysis of this paper by examining how IT affordances interact with societal level logics that could not be included in the analysis, namely the family and religion logics (potential research topic 2 in Table 3). Our review of the literature highlights the lack of studies that relate IT use to these two logics. Theorizing this relationship is important for understanding how IT use is increasingly implicated in shaping both family and religion as significant areas of social life, and for making sense of how IT is transforming their interactions with other areas, such as the market, the corporation, and the community.

The institutional logics perspective adopted in our model suggests that prevailing institutions shape actions and beliefs through interactions with various social mechanisms, such as identity, legitimacy, and authority (Fligstein 1996; Thornton and Ocasio 2008). As such, social actors rely on their understanding of rules of the game within a respective logic to guide appropriate action in terms of, for example, social roles, status, or authority. Yet, these elemental aspects of logics are interdependent and work together to constitute the apparatus of each logic. In adding “IT affordances” as a new elemental category that shapes institutional logics, our model leaves open the question of how IT affordances influence, and are influenced by, other elemental categories of a given logic. This opens up an area of research regarding how IT affordances are intertwined with other elemental categories in ways that might bring about societal change. For example, Carter and Grover (2015) illustrate the intertwinement between IT and identity through their concept of “IT identity,” which highlights how individual and social identities are increasingly shaped by IT use. It would therefore be valuable for future studies to account for this intertwinement in exploring how IT affordances activate societal level logics through their effects on identity, or how logics focus attention on specific affordances by calling on corresponding social identities (potential research topic 3 in Table 3).

Similarly, it would be valuable for future research to investigate how sources of authority—including sources of status and power within a logic—are entangled with IT affordances (potential research topic 3 in Table 3). For example, within a market logic, access to resources and competition are central in shaping power and authority (Thornton and Ocasio 1999, 2008). Yet, activating new IT affordances can alter the availability of resources. For example, Vaast et al. (2017) show how microblogging can enable grass root movements to gain new resources through user generated content that can shift power away from prevailing market leaders. What has been referred to as “Twitter Power” (Jansen et al. 2009) can be used to reshape an industry such as oil and gas by users concerned with environmental causes (Vaast et al. 2017) as the taken for granted nature of resources and their ownership or control become questioned and challenged.

Further, our model expands our insights into the scaling and stabilizing of IT affordances to levels at which they start to constitute a type of material-discursive infrastructure for societal change. Our proposed scaling mechanisms of sensegiving, translating, and decoupling, are based on a specific reading of the mechanisms proposed by institutional theorists for linking practices to changes in institutional logics (Ocasio et al. 2015). While their mechanisms focus on discursive practices, our reading helps us account for the materiality of IT practices. Future studies can usefully examine details of how the three proposed mechanisms act and interact at different stages of the change process. The three mechanisms and their associated carriers of objects, networks, and platforms, are set at a relatively high level of abstraction as they account for what makes IT affordances transferrable, linked across contexts, and generative across levels of practice. However, they are not exhaustive and future studies can explore other scaling mechanisms or alternative approaches to framing the scaling processes (potential research topic 4 in Table 3).

Digital transformation is a growing area of IS research with significant consequences for societal change (Hinings et al. 2018; Marjchak et al. 2016). It often takes the form of disruptive transformation stemming from the implementation of radical IT innovations. Studies have for long recognized the potential of IT innovations to be the source of disruptive change (Christensen et al. 2006). However, conceptualizations of disruptive IT innovations have been focused on disruption within organizations and industries (Lyytinen and Rose 2003; Markides 2006). As IT is becoming increasingly associated with broader societal transformation, further theoretical developments are needed to advance our understanding of how IT innovations can be disruptive of whole societal systems. In particular, it is important to understand further the way in which disruptive IT innovations gain scale and stability to become the basis of new ecosystems that change the functioning of society (potential research topic 5 in Table 3).

The model can also provide new insights for studies interested in the design of strategies and policy interventions that aim to address the societal effects of ubiquitous IT use, or leverage IT capabilities to address enduring societal problems (potential research topic 6 in Table 3). Recent work has highlighted the need for design theories aimed at advancing our understanding of how grand societal challenges can or should be addressed through IT (Lee 2015; Majchrzak et al. 2016). In this regard, to build on the model, future work can investigate various areas of design, such as the design of governance systems, legal frameworks, and technology standards, and how they relate to the mechanisms of sensegiving, translating, and decoupling. Understanding how such design interventions affect the scaling and stability of IT practices would help in analyzing their potential in enhancing the desired societal transformations of emerging IT solutions and in limiting their undesired consequences.

The third part of the model relates to the conceptualization of societal change as shifts in the multiplicity of logics, particularly through changes in the centrality and compatibility of logics. In this regard, an important area of future research would be to investigate which societal logics will become dominant and which ones will become marginalized as IT affordances from emerging technologies, such as cloud computing, internet of things, artificial intelligence, and blockchain, gain scale and stability (potential research topic 7 in Table 3). Practitioners and researchers associate several emerging technologies with high expectations for large scale societal transformation. Yet, there is a lack of conceptual models that can help in analyzing the scale and nature of this transformation. Some studies have examined the challenges faced by organizations in estimating the potential and consequences of emerging technologies (Tiwana 2014; Wang 2010), but the focus in these studies has been on investment decisions. Others have focused on specific challenges associated with emerging technologies, such as privacy (Conger et al. 2013) and ethical considerations (Mingers and Walsham 2010). Building on the proposed model, future studies can offer broader conceptualizations by examining the wide range of affordances that emerge from these technologies across different societal orders and their corresponding logics. They can investigate how the scaling of these affordances can make certain logics more dominant and others less salient. In particular, from the perspective of the proposed model, many of the challenges associated with the “dark side” of IT, such as algorithmic control, increased anxiety, and loss of privacy (Tarafdar et al. 2013), can be studied by analyzing how IT becomes the basis of overwhelming dominance of one logic over all others.

Another type of societal change highlighted by the model encompasses shifts in the compatibility of logics. Future research can build on the proposed model by examining how shifts in the compatibility of logics give rise to hybrid forms of organizing and social interaction enabled by IT affordances and their associated practices (potential research topic 8 in Table 3). There has been significant theoretical developments in the study of hybrid forms of organizing (Haigh et al. 2015), including hybrids of financial and social value (McMullen and Warnick 2016), public and private governance (Bishop and Waring 2016), and academic and commercial activities (Powell and Sandholtz 2012). In addition, various studies have investigated hybrid organizing in relation to IT, such as hybrids of open and proprietary software development (Barrett et al. 2013; Deodhar et al. 2012) and hybrids of standardized and nonstandardized interorganizational systems (de Corbiere and Rowe 2013). However, our understanding of how these hybrid forms gain scale and stability to become a basis of change in societal conditions remains limited. Building on the proposed model, future studies can investigate when and how the entanglement of IT affordances with institutional logics increases the compatibility of the latter and support the formation of hybrid logics, and how the mechanisms of sensegiving, translating, and decoupling generate practices that enable the hybridization to occur at a large scale.

## Conclusion

Information technologies have become increasingly entangled with major processes of societal change. This entanglement is generating positive consequences, such as increased effectiveness in responding to societal crises (Kornberger et al. 2018) and higher socioeconomic inclusion of previously marginalized segments of society (Oborn et al. 2019). However, IT is also associated with negative societal changes such as loss of privacy and the spread of hate speech (Chan et al. 2016). In this paper, we have developed an affordance-based institutional logics perspective to examine this dual effect of IT as the source of both positive societal transformations and complex societal challenges. The proposed model provides a basis for IS research to go beyond the current focus on the organizational and individual levels and to explicitly theorize the entanglement of IT use with societal level processes. We hope that the conceptual developments suggested by the model provide a first step toward further work on IT and societal change.

Isam Faik is an assistant professor of Information Systems at the Ivey Business School, Western University. His research investigates the digital transformation of organizations and society with particular focus on the transformation of highly institutionalized practices and structures. He received his Ph.D. in Management Studies as a Gates Scholar from the Judge Business School, University of Cambridge.

Michael Barrett is professor of Information Systems and Innovation Studies and Director of Research at the Cambridge Judge Business School , University of Cambridge. He received his Ph.D. from the University of Cambridge. His research interests include digital innovation and transformation, social inclusion, platform ecosystems, and IT-enabled globalization, with particular emphasis on the role of social and organizational issues in innovation processes. He is Academic Director of Cambridge Digital Innovation and is keen to promote wider impact from scholarly research for policy and practice.

Eivor Oborn is professor of HealthCare Management in the Entrepreneurship and Innovation Group at Warwick Business School, University of Warwick.
