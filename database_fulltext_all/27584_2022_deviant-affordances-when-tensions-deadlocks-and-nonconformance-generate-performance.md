# DEVIANT AFFORDANCES: WHEN TENSIONS, DEADLOCKS, AND NONCONFORMANCE GENERATE PERFORMANCE<sup>1</sup>

Steffi Haag Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, and Friedrich-Alexander-Universität Erlangen-Nürnberg, Nuremberg, GERMANY {steffi.haag@hhu.de}

Andreas Eckhardt University of Innsbruck, Innsbruck, AUSTRIA {Andreas.Eckhardt@uibk.ac.at}

Viswanath Venkatesh Virginia Tech Blacksburg, VA, U.S.A. {vvenkatesh@vvenkatesh.us}

Novel information technologies (ITs), such as mobile devices and third-party cloud services, offer users an increasing variety of action possibilities, i.e., affordances. Organizational IT policies, however, often specify their actualization—i.e., turning those affordances into action—as undesired. Organizations face the challenge that to reach their goals, employees still frequently take advantage of these affordances by using undesired ITs and thereby deviate from IT policies. Although prior work has extensively studied how goaloriented users actualize affordances that are associated with outcomes that support organizational goals, little attention has been paid to the structures, mechanisms, and conditions underlying affordances that deviate from organizational IT policies. We conceptualize these affordances as deviant affordances. Leveraging the orders of change framework and using a multimethod research design integrating interview and experimental studies, we identify three key mechanisms underlying deviant affordances—i.e., tension, deadlock, and actualization mechanisms—that can link together to produce a deviant outcome supporting both the users’ individual goal and an organizational goal. Our work explains the importance of users perceived deadlock in stimulating the generation of deviant outcomes that support organizational goals through improving task, contextual, and innovative job performance.

Keywords: Technology affordances, affordance actualization, deviance, nonconformance, orders of change, multimethod, critical realism, generative mechanisms, performance, shadow IT

## Introduction

An ever-increasing number of information technologies (ITs), in general, and cloud services, in particular, offer goal-oriented users a variety of action possibilities, called affordances (Burton-Jones & Volkoff, 2017; Strong et al., 2014). Dropbox, WeTransfer, and Google Drive, for instance, afford employees quick, easy, and flexible access to share large volumes of data (files) with co-workers, partners, or clients. Similarly, WhatsApp affords employees quick communication and file sharing with individuals and groups of co-workers. However, organizations often have IT policies that specify which affordances they do not desire their employees to actualize (Liang et al., 2013)—i.e., turn them into action through using the IT (Strong et al., 2014). For instance, the use of third-party cloud services is often prohibited due to information security and privacy risks. This creates a tension for employees who are expected to conform to organizational IT policies (Liang et al., 2013) but also wish to support their own goals by using such prohibited cloud services. The outcome and key challenge for organizations is that more than 80% of cloud service use deviates from organizational IT policies (Segal, 2016; Walters, 2013) and this trend is only expected to grow in the future (Panetta, 2017). Despite this deviance and its potentially disastrous outcomes for organizations, such as sensitive corporate data falling into the wrong hands, employees actualizing affordances through the deviant use of cloud services also often report that the outcomes better supported their job goals than acting in conformance with organizational IT policies would have (Segal, 2016). This underscores the need to understand the mechanisms underlying affordances that entail deviance from organizational IT policies but may still support organizational goals. Such an understanding would be valuable for IT managers and supervisors confronted with the complexity of managing deviant IT use that can nonetheless benefit their organizations.

Information systems (IS) scholars (e.g., Burton-Jones & Volkoff, 2017; Fayard & Weeks, 2014; Leonardi, 2011; Strong et al., 2014) have frequently studied the mechanisms of how goal-oriented users<sup>2</sup> actualize affordances to increase the chances of achieving overarching organizational goals. However, these studies have always been under the assumption that organizational structures such as IT policies condition the desired ways of acting. Little attention has thus been paid to affordances that deviate from existing organizational IT policies and conditions but can nonetheless support certain organizational goals. Anderson and Robey (2017) acknowledged that individuals’ actualization of affordances does not always align with the organization’s goals or policies and stressed the need for research to better understand the extent, mechanisms, and outcomes of such deviance.

To address this need, we introduce the concept of deviant affordances, describing affordances whose actualization existing organizational IT policies specify as undesired. In particular, we focus on deviant affordances that have the power to produce an outcome that does not conform to organizational IT policies but nonetheless supports a legitimate organizational goal. Our objective is to explain the structures, mechanisms, and conditions underlying such deviant affordances and how they interact to produce this outcome. The literature on affordances and deviant IT use, as well as the orders of change framework (Watzlawick et al., 1974/2011) from the field of psychotherapy, inspire our investigation. We further follow a key principle of critical realist studies (e.g., Danermark et al., 2019; Wynn &

Williams, 2012) by employing a multimethod research design (Mingers, 2001), including interview and experimental studies, to triangulate the inferences and develop a theory of deviant affordances.

Our work makes two key contributions to the literature on affordances and employee IT use. First, we contribute by developing a theoretical conceptualization of deviant affordances. Deviant affordances can play an important role in understanding employee IT use and its effects because the concept relates to different types of ITs, users, goals, and organizational structures and allows us to explain the mechanisms that interact to produce deviant IT use with potentially constructive or destructive outcomes for the organization. Second, we contribute by retroducting three types of deadlock mechanisms that generate deadlock in specific contexts. We show that the perception of this deadlock, in turn, stimulates the user to actualize deviant affordances to generate an outcome that could not be reached in conformance with organizational IT policies but that nonetheless supports a legitimate organizational goal. Beyond the scientific contributions, explaining the structures, mechanisms, and conditions underlying deviant affordances and their outcomes also benefits practitioners, who can use our findings to anticipate potential organizational structures and conditions that cause actualizations of deviant affordances and thus initiate effective measures to align affordances with organizational goals and IT policies.

## Defining Deviant Affordances

## Concept of Affordances

The concept of affordances from ecological psychology captures “what we perceive when we look at objects” (Gibson, 1979, p. 126). Affordances are action-referential powers of the environment that emerge from relations between objects and actors—whether the actors perceive them or not (Michaels, 2003; Mingers & Standing, 2017). For instance, stairs afford a person climbing. The relational conceptualization indicates that different affordances may arise from an object with different actors. For instance, stairs can afford a different person sitting. Depending on each actor’s goals or capabilities and the respective context, actors can actualize each affordance, i.e., act upon its power, in different ways and thus generate different outcomes (Gibson, 1979).

In the IS field, the concept of affordances can help explain the mechanisms that generate IT-associated outcomes (Bygstad et al., 2016; Volkoff & Strong, 2013). In organizational contexts, affordances describe the action possibilities that IT artifacts and their technological properties (i.e., objects) offer to goaloriented users or user groups (i.e., actors) and that are associated with attaining immediate concrete outcomes, i.e., specific expected outcomes from actualization that support organizational goals (Volkoff & Strong, 2013).

Organizational goals along with sets of norms and IT artifacts are historically based on many earlier individual actions and describe the organizational structure (Wynn & Williams, 2012). Structure and individual actions constitute different levels of social reality that condition and transform each other over time (Archer, 1995; Bygstad et al., 2016; Wynn & Williams, 2012). Coleman (1986) and Hedström and Swedberg (1998) depict this in a cycle of social mechanisms: (1) macro-micro mechanisms describe how structure—i.e., in our work, the organizational structure and its external sociotechnical context—affects individuals; (2) micro-micro mechanisms capture individuals’ specific actions and interactions; and (c) micro-macro mechanisms depict how the network of produced outcomes feeds back into the structure. Archer’s (1995) related morphogenetic model differentiates between social actions that reproduce (morphostasis) and those that transform (morphogenesis) structure. Respective social actions occur in four dimensions (Bhaskar, 1993): (1) interactions with the material and technical world, (2) interactions with other people, (3) interactions with the social structure, and (4) interactions with the individual’s inner self.

The sociotechnical structures of today’s organizations and their external context provide users with a multiplicity of technological properties (Fayard & Weeks, 2014). Goaloriented users are constantly receptive to affordances arising in relation to such technological properties (Jung & Lyytinen, 2014). When users become aware of technological properties that offer them an opportunity to reach a goal they are pursuing, for instance, by transferring affordances from prior usage experiences to other contexts (such as from the personal context to the work context) or by exploring affordances in relation to novel technological properties (Lehrig et al., 2017), they perceive an affordance. The perception of an affordance paves the way for affordance actualization (Jung & Lyytinen, 2014; Lehrig et al., 2017). Affordance actualization describes the individual activities that users perform to realize one or more affordances through their use of technological properties in order to generate an outcome that supports their goal (Burton-Jones & Volkoff, 2017; Strong et al., 2014). However, a misfit between specific technological properties and individual capabilities, such as a discomfort with computers (Anderson & Robey, 2017) or other contextual conditions (Burton-Jones & Volkoff, 2017), may prevent or alter the actualization of affordances and thus the generation of the associated outcome.

In organizational contexts, prior IS affordance research has assumed that users’ intentions or goals support organizational goals due to alignment with their organizational role (Burton-Jones & Volkoff, 2017; Strong et al., 2014; Volkoff & Strong, 2013). That is, it is assumed that the organizational structure affects and conditions individuals, their goals, and actions in such a way that the affordances that arise support organizational goals (Jung & Lyytinen, 2014; Volkoff & Strong, 2013). For example, the condition of conforming to organizational IT policies that prescribe desired and undesired IT-associated activities (Liang et al., 2013)—goal-oriented users would only perceive and actualize affordances that conform to organizational IT policies (Fayard & Weeks, 2014; Leonardi, 2011; Strong et al., 2014).

In contrast to such prevailing assumptions, practice shows that users also perceive and actualize affordances whose actualization the organizational IT policies specify as undesired (e.g., Anderson & Robey, 2017; Behrens, 2009; Boudreau & Robey, 2005). Interestingly, actualizing affordances that deviate from organizational IT policies rather than actualizing those that conform often leads to outcomes that better support not only users’ individual goals, but also, under certain circumstances, certain organizational goals—in particular, increases in efficiency, creativity, and/or innovation have been reported (Behrens, 2009). For instance, Behrens (2009) discusses an innovative web-based learning technology that affords academics support for their own and their university’s teaching and learning goals, even though the organizational IT policies have specified the actualization of these affordances as undesired. Anderson and Robey (2017) acknowledge that affordances do not always conform to all desires or goals of the organization and call for future research to better understand the extent and underlying mechanisms of such deviance. To identify such mechanisms and explain this phenomenon, we introduce the concept of deviant affordances.

## Concept of Deviant Affordances

In the organizational context, deviant affordances describe action possibilities that arise from a relation between a goaloriented user and technological properties and whose actualization the organizational IT policies specify as undesired. Accordingly, actualizing deviant affordances always entails a deviance from, i.e., a nonconformance with (Dodge, 1985; Merton, 1968), organizational IT policies, meaning that the outcome associated with deviant affordances is always a deviant outcome.

We view the deviant outcome as an immediate concrete outcome useful for supporting both a user’s individual goal and at least one legitimate organizational goal. On the one hand, this view parallels prior IS research (e.g., Burton-Jones & Volkoff, 2017; Strong et al., 2014) in that the deviant outcome, which can be defined at both micro- and macrolevels (Strong et al., 2014), can make both goals more likely. However, goal achievement may be a product of mechanisms other than deviant affordances. The deviant outcome is thus helpful to establish a more direct association with the mechanisms underlying deviant affordances. On the other hand, our view breaks with the common but questionable assumption in prior IS affordance studies that the user’s individual goal is aligned with goals that the organization prescribes for their job roles (Strong et al., 2014; Volkoff & Strong, 2013). A key assumption in our work is that the deviant outcome associated with the deviant affordance is objectively assessed as useful to support not only a user’s own but also one or more legitimate organizational goals. Legitimate organizational goals are goals that the organization defines as recognized objectives and promotes as worth striving to achieve (Mainemelis, 2010). We assess the deviant outcome as useful for supporting an organizational goal if it helps achieve or exceed the job goals the organization sets for the user in their job. The assessment of the degree to which an outcome supports an individual’s job goals reflects the outcome-based assessment of individual job performance (Campbell et al., 1993). Individual job performance can encompass various dimensions (Campbell et al., 1993) including task mastery (task performance) and extratask mastery that contributes to organizational goals (contextual performance), for instance, by encouraging a motivated and stable atmosphere in the organization (Borman & Motowidlo, 1993). Even if the deviant outcome is defined as supporting a legitimate organizational goal by supporting an individual’s job goals and thus job performance, it may also enable or trigger other mechanisms that produce potentially negative outcomes for the organization, such as the disclosure of sensitive firm data. Thus, in our work, the overarching outcome for the organization associated with deviant affordances is uncertain a priori.

Notwithstanding the focus of our work, the deviant outcome associated with deviant affordances may a priori be either primarily constructive, destructive, or uncertain for the organization (see rows in Table 1). This outcome-based classification of deviant affordances parallels the classification of workplace deviance in the organizational behavior literature (Mainemelis, 2010; Warren, 2003). Deviant affordances are constructive when the associated deviant outcome primarily supports organizational goals, destructive when the associated deviant outcome primarily harms organizational goals, and uncertain when the associated deviant outcome may either support or harm organizational goals. Note that the rows in Table 1 provide corresponding examples for each class of deviant affordances.

In addition, in this work, we focus on deviant affordances whose actualization the organizational IT policies specify as undesired because they arise from a relation with undesired technological properties<sup>3</sup> (right column in Table 1). Undesired technological properties are not a part of the organizational structure but a part of the sociotechnical context, which is external to the organization. An example is an organizationally unapproved cloud service that affords an employee the transfer of massive customer files. Our focus is based on our expectation that the classes of deviant affordances that arise from a relation with undesired technological properties bear different and higher risks (e.g., in terms of the severity and probability of the deviant outcome for both the organization’s information security and the user’s sanctions) than deviant affordances related to desired technological properties. For instance, if the user stores confidential customer files on an undesired public cloud server and thus violates data protection laws, the consequences for both the organization and the user may be worse than if such files had been stored on a company-wide drive that is not technically designed for confidential data storage. Consistent with Willison and Warkentin (2013), we argue that these different risks require conducting different analyses of the mechanisms underlying deviant affordances related to either undesired or desired technological properties. In sum, we focus on the class of deviant affordances that arises from a relation with undesired technological properties and is associated with a deviant outcome that is a priori uncertain for the organization (shaded in dark grey in Table 1).

Appendix A discusses the differences between the concept of deviant affordances and related concepts that have been previously considered in the IS literature involving deviant and/or adapted IT use, such as shadow IT use (Haag & Eckhardt, 2017), cyberdeviance (Venkatraman et al., 2018), and reinvention (Boudreau & Robey, 2005). Although deviant affordances, if actualized, may share some characteristics with related concepts, our perspective on deviant action possibilities broadens existing theories on deviant IT use because the relational definition allows us to incorporate a variety of deviant affordances that relate to different types of IT artifacts, users, their individual goals and capabilities, and organizational structures (i.e., organizational goals and IT policies).

## Mechanisms Underlying Deviant Affordances

The relational definition of deviant affordances parallels the definition of emergent powers, i.e., potentials that cannot be reduced to their constituting parts (Mingers & Standing, 2017; Sayer, 2010). Explanations of deviant affordances and their deviant outcomes should therefore seek to identify the structural entities (i.e., objects or actors with their properties) that possess emergent powers and the generative mechanisms<sup>4</sup> that produce them (Elder-Vass, 2010).

A mechanism is “that which can cause something in the world to happen” (Danermark et al., 2019, p. 47). Mechanisms consist of structural entities that engage in activities when some cause occurs (start condition). These activities, in turn, regularly cause an outcome or effect (termination condition) (Hedström & Swedberg, 1998; Machamer et al., 2000). The causality is contingent (Elder-Vass, 2010): although a mechanism is everpresent, it may not always be exercised and may operate only when it is triggered (stimulus condition) or when impediments to its exercise are removed (releasing condition) (Bhaskar, 2008). These conditions represent themselves in various mechanisms endemic to the context and are called contextual conditions (Bygstad et al., 2016). Contextual conditions also affect the outcome (Danermark et al., 2019). Some mechanisms enable or reinforce one another whereas others alter or impede the effects of one another. The actual outcome, i.e., the event that can be experienced, is thus a complex composite product generated by different mechanisms (Danermark et al., 2019). Under the same specific contextual conditions, mechanisms tend to behave in certain ways or have certain effects (Mingers & Standing, 2017). Such tendencies depict a “possible, if not plausible, course of action” (Wynn & Williams, 2012, p. 791). For instance, smartphone addicts tend to repeatedly check text messages. However, context determines whether they will actually check their messages at the time a message is received. Figure 1 shows the tendency of a generative mechanism conditioned by its specific context.

Given this background, we sought to identify the fundamental structures, mechanisms, and contextual conditions underlying deviant affordances that generate a deviant outcome useful for supporting a user’s individual goal and at least one legitimate organizational goal by using a multimethod research approach (Mingers, 2001). Three phases offered by Mingers (2001) and Zachariadis et al. (2013) guided our research process, as depicted in Figure 2. In the first phase, i.e., appreciation, we explained the research situation and used extant literature on affordances, deviance, and social mechanisms to abstract the observed events under study and conceptualize deviant affordances. We then collected data using both interviews and an experiment, as these methods enabled an intensive and comparative examination of the phenomenon. In the second phase, i.e., retroductive analysis, we focused, in particular, on cases in which deviant affordances manifested. We constantly iterated between data, literature, and candidate mechanisms to identify the fundamental structures, mechanisms, and contextual conditions capable of explaining deviant affordances and the observed deviant outcomes. In the final phase, i.e., assessment, we triangulated the findings from the interview and experimental studies to draw meta-inferences, assess their quality, and develop a theory of deviant affordances.

Our particular purposes for employing a multimethod approach to data collection and analysis were triangulation, expansion, and compensation (Venkatesh et al., 2013; Zachariadis et al., 2013). Regarding triangulation, the use of a variety of analytical and methodological techniques, data types, and sources to search for generative mechanisms improves not only the discovery of generative mechanisms, but also the validity claims and robustness of meta-inferences (Wynn & Williams, 2012; Zachariadis et al., 2013). Regarding expansion, we conducted an experiment in Study 2 and post hoc interviews in Study 3 to extend the explanations about the mechanisms and contextual conditions obtained in Study 1. Finally, regarding compensation, we conducted an experiment to offset the weakness that interviewees’ reports of deviant actions are prone to the social desirability bias (Griffin & Lopez, 2005; Stewart et al., 2009). ## Study 1: Interviews

## Data Collection

We conducted semi-structured interviews of users employed in different organizations and industries. Collecting data from multiple organizational contexts increased our ability to examine and compare the specific organizational structures and contexts in which deviant affordances and their deviant outcomes arise. This reduced the risk of overlooking potentially important issues and supported the empirical corroboration of the mechanisms we identified, in line with the idea of summative validity (Lee & Hubona, 2009; Wynn & Williams, 2012).

Via email, we invited students in and alumni of part-time programs (MSc, MBA, LL.M) of a private graduate business and law school in Germany to participate in interviews about the digitization of the workplace and incentivized participation by providing the opportunity to win a smart home assistant. Our goal was to attract as many theoretically useful cases—i.e., those that provide us with transparent and varied information about deviant affordances, their outcomes, and the contextual conditions producing it—as possible (Danermark et al., 2019; Eisenhardt, 1989) while, at the same time, accounting for the deviant and sensitive nature of the activities involved. During the coding, analyzing, and comparing of these cases, we were able to narrow the scope of new samples to questions arising in the analysis and thus use theoretical sampling procedures (Danermark et al., 2019).

Guidelines related to interview studies (see Weiss, 1995), as well as the literature on affordances, deviance, and IT use (e.g., Burton-Jones & Grange, 2013; Burton-Jones & Volkoff, 2017; Jung & Lyytinen, 2014; Mainemelis, 2010), informed the development of the initial interview script, which we continuously adapted to insights and ideas gathered from each interview in order to—at times, delicately—uncover potential deviance from organizational IT policies when using ITs. The first and final interview scripts are provided in Appendix C. Appendix C also provides further details on our data collection. In total, in Study 1, we conducted 47 interviews, including seven follow-up interviews, lasting, on average, just under one hour each (2,770 minutes), and identified 48 different deviant affordance cases across 23 interviewees. The 17 interviews in which we did not identify any deviant affordance case served as rival cases in the comparative analysis and helped us delineate relevant contextual conditions. Table C2 in Appendix C summarizes the demographic characteristics and organizational context of the participants.

## Data Analysis

Our data analysis followed the guidelines of Danermark et al. (2019) for conducting an explanatory search for causal mechanisms and was further informed by Wynn and Williams’s (2012) and Bygstad et al.’s (2016) application of these principles to the IS field. Accordingly, we used a fivestep approach that integrated inductive, abductive, retroductive, and deductive reasoning, similar to recent explanatory studies in IS research (e.g., Henfridsson & Bygstad, 2013; Williams & Karahanna, 2013). Table C3 in Appendix C depicts our detailed approach in each step. The findings of each step iteratively fed back to higher and later steps until we arrived at consistent explanations for the structures and mechanisms underlying deviant affordances.

In the first step, for each case, we used open coding to identify and describe key events among the experiences, actions, outcomes, and contexts associated with deviant affordances based on our interpretation and the interpretation of the participants. We discovered three key events (Table 2). First, users perceived deviant affordances as a tension between the two opposing desires of supporting their goals and conforming to organizational IT policies, which we labeled as perceived tension between goal and conformance. Second, we found that, in each deviant affordance case, interviewees described not seeing any recognizable possibility of achieving their goals in conformance with the organizational IT policies; we labeled this perceived deadlock. Finally, we consistently found a deviant outcome associated with deviant affordance actualization that supported at least one legitimate organizational goal through improving task, innovative, and/or contextual performance (see Table C4 in Appendix C for definitions and examples).

In the second step, we identified and abstracted the fundamental sociotechnical entities of the micro- and macrostructures and how they can link together to produce the three key events. At the microlevel, we identified the user and their relevant capabilities and individual goal; at the macrolevel, we identified the organizational goals and IT policies of the organizational structure, as well as the undesired technological properties of the sociotechnical context external to the organization. Table C5 in Appendix C provides empirical manifestations (i.e., observable events) of these relevant entities for each of the 48 deviant affordance cases and inter-rater reliabilities. We also analyzed changes in the structural entities associated with key events and assigned them to the potential layers of social mechanisms (Coleman, 1986; Hedström & Swedberg, 1998) to identify causal influences and the relationships between them (see Table 2).

The third step was retroduction—i.e., we hypothesized several candidate mechanisms that, if existing, could explain the observed key events identified in Step 1. The fourth step was retrodiction—i.e., we examined the relationships between the candidate mechanisms and how they might link together across different layers to cause the deviant outcome associated with deviant affordances. In both steps, alongside previous work on affordances (e.g., Jung & Lyytinen, 2014; Strong et al., 2014) and social mechanisms (Coleman, 1986; Hedström & Swedberg, 1998), the orders of change framework (Watzlawick et al., 1974/2011) from psychotherapy, which describes how the experience of deadlock can arise and, in turn, stimulate individuals to change their activities in deviant novel ways, inspired our analyses (see the Results section). Finally, in the fifth step of empirical corroboration, we examined and compared manifestations of structures and mechanisms within and across concrete contexts of deviant affordance cases to assess and rank the causal and explanatory powers of the candidate mechanisms and specify contextual conditions (see Appendix C, Table C6). We finally arrived at three generative mechanisms with the best explanation, which we discuss in the next section.

## Results: Three Mechanisms Underlying Deviant Affordances

Our data analysis showed three mechanisms underlying deviant affordances that, when exercised in a specific context, may link together, one after another, to produce the observed key events: namely, the tension, deadlock, and actualization mechanisms.

## Tension Mechanism

The tension mechanism describes the tendency of a goaloriented user to engage in activities that generate the perception of a tension between goal and conformance when undesired technological properties occur in a specific context. This perceived tension means that the user experiences a state of conflict regarding possible actions and associated opposing outcomes that either (1) support the user’s individual goal (and thus, based on our definition, also support a legitimate organizational goal) but do not conform to organizational IT policies, or (2) do not support the user’s individual goal (and thus do not support a legitimate organizational goal) but conform to organizational IT policies. For example, Interviewee 10, who was striving to digitize handwritten notes, reported:

If I want to exploit the possibilities of the iPad to write something down in a meeting, then I need to use an app to do it ... Currently, neither Word, Notes, nor any other app has been officially authorized for use ... Well, I feel the tension. (Interviewee 10, Case 21)

The tension mechanism is a macro-micro mechanism because it connects undesired technological properties of the sociotechnical context external to the organization at the macrolevel with the actions and beliefs of a goal-oriented user at the microlevel.

We found two primary activities in which goal-oriented users tend to engage and which generate perceived tension. Goal-oriented users either transfer their own or observed prior experiences, or they envision existing or new undesired technological properties:

Transfer one’s own experiences: Goal-oriented users infer that undesired technological properties afford them support for their goals because they have experienced those technological properties affording them support for similar goals in similar contexts in the past, whether at work—e.g., “I knew that messenger from using it in the business environment when staying in South Korea for my company” (Interviewee 3, Case 6)—or privately, e.g., “I also use [the cloud service] in private life ... I either heard about it from friends or ... read [about] it somewhere. I tried it once and it was amazing.” (Interviewee 4, Case 10)

Transfer observed experiences: Goal-oriented users deduce that undesired technological properties afford them support for their goals by observing that those technological properties have afforded other actors within their social environment support for similar goals in similar contexts in the past. For example, one interviewee reported: “a very innovative supervisor ... who is permanently striving for digital improvement ... He provides lots of ideas [suggestions]” (Interviewee 20, Case 42). Another recounted external service providers who “bring various perspectives, ideas, suggestions: Hey, we use this. Are you familiar with that and do you use it as well?” (Interviewee 16, Case 38). Another interviewee commented: “sometimes you see a solution without being even aware of the problem and you think: oh, yeah, true! Often, this happens among colleagues” (Interviewee 17, Case 39).

Envision-and-explore: Goal-oriented users conceive of and explore the sociotechnical context external to the organization for undesired technological properties that afford them support for their goals. To explore deviant affordances, they may use trial-and-error strategies: “I am like a child who plays. I just have tried it myself a little bit” (Interviewee 11, Case 23); consult various information sources, such as the internet: “So, I ask Mr. Google. Dear Google, [restoring) damaged email files ... And yes, there is a tool! Then, I would like to install it. I don’t want to think about if I am allowed to do that or not” (Interviewee 1, Case 3); or ask colleagues for advice: “The tool is weird. Do you have an idea how I can handle it?” (Interviewee 8, Case 16).

Envision-and-create: Capable users, identified in only six of our 48 deviant affordance cases, conceive of and create novel undesired technological properties that afford them support for their goals: “I simply wrote a small script” (Interviewee 11, Case 22). Such activities usually take users a number of hours to complete. For instance, Interviewee 1 estimated that creating novel undesired technological properties took her around 20 hours: “Well, it’s normal to keep programming into the night because you always think it’ll work the next time. And you realize: [expletive deleted], it doesn’t work yet. Troubleshooting, try again. But, I definitely have it now. And then, again, it’s ah. You can get stuck in this zone sometimes” (Interviewee 1, Case 1).

Although exercising the tension mechanism seems crucial to enabling the generation of the deviant outcome, it appeared insufficient to consistently explain it. As Interviewee 6 succinctly explained: “But, when do I go that very expensive route? Only when my own pressure on myself becomes so immense that I awake from my lethargy.” (Interviewee 6, Case 12). This suggests that there is another internal, i.e., socio/psychological micro-micro mechanism that reinforces a user’s perception of tension up to a certain point that eventually stimulates the generation of the deviant outcome. During our data analysis, we found that, in all deviant affordance cases, interviewees described the same stimulating experience: in the specific organizational context, they no longer perceived any recognizable action possibility of achieving their goals in conformance with the organizational IT policies. Their initial experience of a tension between support of their goal and conformance with organizational IT policies appeared to be reinforced such that it culminated in the perception of deadlock. In the psychotherapy literature, the orders of change framework (Watzlawick et al., 1974/2011) describes how the event of experiencing a deadlock can occur and cause individuals to act in novel deviant ways.

## Deadlock Mechanism

Theoretical redescription adopting the orders of change: In their seminal book on change, Watzlawick et al. (1974/2011) proposed a hierarchical logic of social systems including two distinct orders of change. First-order change represents an incremental change of actions within a given system, but the system structure itself, i.e., the properties and relations of its entities, remain stable (Watzlawick et al., 1974/2011). In contrast, second-order change transforms the system itself into a completely different state. New action possibilities emerge with new powers that are able to change the higher-order system structure and context so that a desired but previously unattainable outcome can be attained (Watzlawick et al., 1974/2011).

During their fieldwork, Watzlawick et al. (1974/2011) observed that second-order changes tend to occur when individuals are confronted with conditions of deadlock. They describe deadlocks as problems created and maintained through three ways of mishandling undesirable conditions: (1) Action is necessary but not taken<sup>5</sup> due to denial that the undesirable conditions are a problem; consequently, the undesirable conditions persist. (2) Action is taken at the wrong level—a first-order change solution is applied to change undesirable conditions that can only be changed from the next higher logical level, or a second-order change solution is applied when a first-order change would have been appropriate; either option results in a game without an end that increasingly intensifies the undesirable conditions. (3) Action is taken when it should not have been because the undesirable conditions are unchangeable or nonexistent.

Thinking of systems and their structures, entities, and properties closely parallels the mechanisms-view of critical realism (Archer, 1995; Mingers, 2010) and the stratified relational view of affordances (Markus & Silver, 2008; Mingers & Standing, 2017; Volkoff & Strong, 2013). In particular, deviant affordances, if actualized, appear to be similar to second-order changes. Deviant affordances describe emerging powers that add new action possibilities to and thus change the action possibilities in which individuals within the organizational structure tend to engage. These action possibilities are associated with deviant outcomes that could not be achieved before but support the goals of the individual and the organization. Following the guidelines of Danermark et al. (2019), we leveraged the orders of change framework to abstract the observed deadlock events in the context of the identified deviant affordance cases and to retroduce the underlying structures and mechanisms that, if existing, would produce perceived deadlock.

Retroduction of three types of deadlock mechanisms: We observed that perceived deadlock arose because the organizational actions did not resolve users’ tension between goal and conformance. We therefore view the user’s perceived tension between goal and conformance as the undesirable condition that—if mishandled by the organization—creates perceived deadlock, and we describe the deadlock mechanism as the tendency of a goal-oriented user to engage in sensemaking activities that ultimately generate the perception of deadlock when perceived tension occurs in a specific context. Consistent with Watzlawick et al.’s (1974/2011) three ways of mishandling undesirable conditions, we further hypothesize the existence of equifinality (i.e., similar end effects are generated by dissimilar initial conditions and mechanisms—Wynn & Williams, 2012) by suggesting three different types of deadlock mechanisms that produce perceived deadlock. Each type, as detailed in Table 3, describes another organizational arrangement that makes it more likely that users will perceive deadlock and thus stimulates the generation of the deviant outcome.

In the Deadlock 1 mechanism, which we encountered most frequently, users perceive that organizational actions that would be needed to resolve the tension between users’ goals and conformance have not been taken. The organization’s lack of action in this regard causes the tension to persist. Making sense of this persisting tension over time generates perceived deadlock:

The depressing thing about it is that they are mainly state-of-the-art tools that the rest of the world has been using for a long time. If you ask, is there something similar? [Central IT says], no, not really. Okay then, we’ll try it ourselves. (Interviewee 13, Case 28)

In the Deadlock 2 mechanism, users perceive organizational actions to be taken at the wrong level. For instance, in Cases 12 and 21, the organization issued tablets, thereby allowing users to perceive the multitude of affordances that the technological properties of the tablet offered, but specified any use of its properties beyond work email and calendar functions as undesired. As a consequence, goal-oriented users who were striving to also read and write customer data on corporate systems from anywhere, experienced a rising intensity of tension between goal and conformance. Making sense of this intensifying tension generated perceived deadlock:

Although, with the iPad, it was a big struggle to get that at all ... we can do virtually nothing with the device, except that we have an application installed on it that we can use to read emails. I can’t use my voice in this application ... nor can I copy anything from it. That’s just extreme ... And if the [company] from its side is anyway not bothered about the new possibilities and says: these are the [only] applications you can use, then you are driven to find workarounds. (Interviewee 10, Case 21)

In the Deadlock 3 mechanism, users perceive organizational actions have been taken when they should not have been because the tension cannot be resolved. In these contexts, the organization has performed actions to ensure that the organizational goals can be attained, but none of these organizational actions have been able to resolve the user’s tension between goal and conformance because of incongruent goals pursued by the user and the organization.

There is always the point where you say that is so restrictive and you could do it better. Precisely, because there are technical possibilities and here simply, for example, there is too much restriction or something has not yet been unlocked, and then you say, forget it. (Interviewee 2, Case 4)

## Actualization Mechanism

The actualization mechanism describes the tendency of a goaloriented user to engage in activities that generate a deviant outcome supporting the user’s individual goal and at least one fundamental organizational goal when a perceived deadlock occurs in a specific context. The actualization mechanism represents a micro-macro mechanism because it links individual actions with effects on organizational structures. Corresponding to affordance actualization (e.g., Strong et al., 2014), we found that the actualization mechanism includes activities of deviant IT use, i.e., goal-oriented users deploying undesired technological properties. For example, Interviewee 12, Case 26 used an undesired personal tablet during work meetings to take and immediately digitize handwritten notes. Although the deviant outcome of digital meeting notes helped Interviewee 12 perform his work tasks more quickly—which in turn also supported certain goals of the organization—using the personal tablet deviated from the organizational IT policies specifying the use of personal devices as undesired. In addition to deviant IT use, we observed that users engaged in further activities to attempt to mitigate the severity and/or probability of the consequences of their deviance:

Severity mitigation: Goal-oriented users take measures to reduce the severity of consequences of their deviance for them—and often for the organization as well. For example, regarding the undesired personal tablet affording Interviewee 12 handwritten note-taking and immediate digitization, Interviewee 12 reported:

But, I always paid attention that, even with my private tablet, I did not write down any account numbers or anything, even if I sometimes really needed it, but I thought, no, I’ll not write it there ... I mean, I shouldn’t be working in IT if I do not know what the risks are. (Interviewee 12, Case 26)

Probability mitigation: Goal-oriented users take measures to reduce the likelihood of consequences occurring for them—for instance, by concealing their deviant IT use from the organization, either explicitly:

Oh, seriously, you can’t find [the USB stick] anyway. Those chips are so tiny today. I can disguise them anywhere and anyway, there’re not allowed to check me. So, I go in and out with the stick anyway. Just not officially. (Interviewee 1, Case 2)

Or implicitly:

I won’t peddle the idea, also for reasons of selfprotection” (Interviewee 10, Case 21) or “no, of course, [the IT security team] didn’t know because it was my private smartphone. (Interviewee 3, Case 6)

In summary, we found that exercising the actualization mechanism tends to integrate activities to actualize both the affordance and deviant component of the phenomenon.

For each of the 48 deviant affordance cases, Table C5 in Appendix C shows the consistency of the empirical events we would have expected to occur had the three mechanisms (i.e., tension, deadlock, and actualization) been subsequently exercised. However, this subsequent exercise in each specific instance is contingent on aspects of the sociotechnical (organizational and external) context. For example, by comparing the entities, structures, and contextual conditions of the deviant affordance cases with those in which deviant affordances did not manifest (see detailed findings in Appendix C, Table C6), we found that 95.4% of the identified deviant affordance cases occurred in conditions in which users expected their organizations to impose less severe and/or probable sanctions in reaction to their deviance from IT policies. By contrast, when organizations threatened users with severe and/or probable sanctions for deviance from organizational IT policies, the actualization mechanism was not exercised. For instance, Interviewee C30, working in the movie industry, stated: “[A data leak] would be a huge problem ... that must be prevented ... Once a customer demanded that the person be kicked out without notice” (Interviewee C30).

Based on the results of our comparative analysis, the consistency of events, and the concept of summative validity (Lee & Hubona, 2009; Wynn & Williams, 2012), we believe that the three proposed mechanisms underlie deviant affordances and produce deviant outcomes that support the user’s individual goal and at least one legitimate organizational goal. ## Study 2: Experiment

To support our analysis of the structures, mechanisms, and contextual conditions underlying deviant affordances and, more importantly, to observe typical manifestations and patterns, we next conducted a laboratory experiment. The experiment compensated for the limitation regarding the interviews—i.e., because of the deviant component, deviant affordances are especially prone to social mechanisms that counteract themselves in such a way that the three generative mechanisms, even if exercised, may not always manifest empirically (Griffin & Lopez, 2005; Stewart et al., 2009). The experiment enabled us to challenge normally prevailing contextual conditions by removing some of the counteracting social mechanisms while provoking the mechanisms underlying deviant affordances and their deviant outcomes to reveal themselves (Danermark et al., 2019; Mingers, 2002). We made use of our empirical observations in the interview data and our theoretical interpretations of them and provoked deadlocks in the context of less severe and less probable sanctions for deviance. Screen recordings capturing the development of activities in response to events and outcomes (Bakeman & Quera, 2011) then enabled us to systematically observe how participants handled these deadlocks. Thus, in addition to the interviewees’ own reports, the experiment produced different kinds of knowledge about the mechanisms and contextual conditions underlying deviant affordances and their deviant outcomes. We provide the key details of the experiment next and Appendix D provides the full details.

## Data Collection

The participants in the experiment were 169 undergraduate and graduate students at a German university. We excluded 26 participants from the analysis: seven dropped out because of a technical problem and 19 did not encounter the deadlock provocation because of a lack of time and/or technical skill. The final sample included 143 participants. The average age was 21.4 years and 65.7% of participants were women. On average, each participant had 2.74 email accounts and 4.19 years of experience using email.

We selected a file-sharing task based on a web-based mail system within the organizational structure of a university. The assigned task comprised searching for and downloading two scientific MIS Quarterly articles from EBSCOhost’s journal database and subsequently using a university email system to send both PDF articles separately to a given addressee by email. We set the successful performance of this task as the job goal for the participants so that the achievement of the job goal could, according to our definition, be considered useful for supporting a legitimate organizational goal. To provoke tension and the deadlock mechanisms, we manipulated the university email system such that it impeded the job goal of successfully sending both PDF articles: the PDF of the second article exceeded the maximum file attachment size. In addition, we randomly assigned participants to different deadlock conditions in order to account for the equifinality of the generated deadlock. Deadlock 1 conditions involved the establishment of an experimental IT policy (see Appendix D, Table D1) specifying the actualization of affordances in relation to the constraining email system as the desired affordances for the task. Consequently, to overcome the constraint, actions would have needed to be taken by the experimenters; as they were not taken, we provoked perceptions of Deadlock 1 in participants due to persisting tension. Deadlock 2 conditions involved additional explicit specifications regarding undesired technological properties (see Appendix D, Table D1), increasing the chances that the participants would perceive Deadlock 2 because of intensifying tension. Finally, for Deadlock 3 conditions, we observed all participants’ actions before the constraint provocation as well as six cases without any constraint provocation. The Deadlock 3 conditions thus left room for deadlocks that participants might perceive due to incongruence between their own goal and the job goal, i.e., due to an unresolvable tension.

To motivate participation, we offered the incentive of the opportunity to win a tablet in a raffle to participants who sent us both PDF articles on time and thus met the job goal. Each participant conducted the task alone in a separate room in an office at the university equipped only with one PC with an internet connection. The isolated setting minimized the potential interplay of other social mechanisms and the internet access gave participants the means to perceive deviant affordances related to undesired technological properties from the sociotechnical context external to the university. In addition to screen recordings made during the task, we used a paper-based questionnaire following the experiment to gather data about demographics and control variables. In total, each participant spent about 45 minutes on the experiment (i.e., from arrival to departure). Data collection took place over three weeks.

## Data Analysis

Similar to the approach of Ortiz de Guinea and Webster (2013), we used systematic observation (Bakeman & Quera, 2011) to analyze the screen recordings. We first defined and piloted a coding scheme and then used it to analyze timestamped actions and events for patterns. The basic unit of analysis was each individual participant’s interaction with the computer. We observed these interactions both between and within subjects before and after the provocation of deadlock. Appendix D (Table D2) presents the coding manual with category definitions, examples for respective empirical manifestations, and inter-rater reliability κ.

Based on our definitions and interview findings, we next analyzed the actions and events with respect to the potential manifestations of the three mechanisms underlying deviant affordances. We also assessed the frequency with which these manifestations occurred and the plausible activities and outcomes involved. To investigate whether there was a pattern of plausible activities that would be more likely to lead to job goal achievement, we examined the relationships between activities typical for the three mechanisms and their outcomes (Figure 3), similar to Henfridsson and Bygstad’s (2013) configurational analysis of mechanisms. Finally, we qualitatively examined the concrete contextual conditions by comparing the cases and patterns in which deviant affordances emerged, led to job goal achievement, or did neither.

## Results

We identified 33 cases in which 26 participants<sup>7</sup> used technological properties that the IT policies established in the experiment specified as undesired. For instance, participants used a different email system to receive the small PDF article, a different email system to send the large PDF article, or a downloaded or web-based PDF compressing tool to reduce the size of the large PDF file (see Appendix D for further examples). As those activities of deviant IT use typically manifest when the actualization mechanism is exercised, we primarily focused our subsequent analysis on those 33 cases to analyze the mechanisms underlying deviant affordances and their deviant outcomes. Similar to the interview findings, the exercised actualization mechanism manifested not only in activities of deviant IT use but also in activities mitigating the consequences of deviating from the experimental IT policies. In 17 of the 33 cases (51.5%), participants concealed their activities, thus reducing the probability of consequences. For example, participants discarded circumstantial evidence in the recycling bin and emptied it, deleted the browsing history, deleted newly downloaded technologies, or closed browser tabs or windows with revealing content. Almost half of these concealment activities occurred after the job goal was met when there was ample time left in the experiment.

We therefore observed that user capabilities and available time conditioned probability mitigation activities, consistent with prior observations in organizational behavior (Criscuolo et al., 2014). Due to the experimental context of less severe and probable sanctions for deviance, we could not identify any clear manifestations of severity mitigation activities. Regarding the deadlock mechanisms, nine of the 33 cases (27.3%) occurred under the Deadlock 1 conditions, and 19 occurred under the Deadlock 2 conditions (9 formal, 10 informal conditions; 57.6%). Deviant affordances emerged five times (15.2%) before the constraint was provoked, i.e., when the experimental structures would have still had enabled job goal achievement. In these cases, participants cc’d and bcc’d the small PDF file to a personal email account. Consistent with our interview findings, we assume that a Deadlock 3 mechanism was exercised in these five cases because these participants pursued individual goals that were incongruent with the job goal prescribed in the experiment. In the six cases in which neither of the two PDF articles exceeded the maximum attachment size, we found no evidence for the existence of either the deadlock mechanism or the actualization mechanism. Table 4 shows the distribution of identified deviant affordance cases across the three deadlock conditions.

Envision-and-explore was the predominant way of exercising the tension mechanism in the experiment. In 22 cases (66.7%), participants searched on Google for undesired technological properties affording them PDF file size reduction. In the remaining 11 cases (33.3%), participants transferred prior experiences<sup>8</sup> by (1) purposefully using another undesired email system known to afford them the receipt of emails/PDF files (five cases), (2) purposefully opening another undesired email system known to afford them the sending of PDF files larger than 2 MB (five cases), or (3) purposefully searching for concrete undesired technological properties commonly known to afford users data files compression and thus the reduction of the PDF file size. None of the participants created novel undesired technological properties that afforded them support for their goal. We recorded 117 cases where the tension mechanism was not observably exercised; none of these cases exhibited any manifestations of deviant affordances.

Overall, the exercise of a single mechanism, be it tension or deadlock, was insufficient to invoke the actualization mechanism and its deviant outcome. This empirically corroborates that the three mechanisms are linked because they generated a deviant outcome that supported the job goal only in the case of subsequent concatenation. We observed three different deviant outcomes: “small PDF file received,” “large PDF file sent,” and “size of large PDF file reduced.” We abstracted three respective deviant affordances that participants actualized to generate those outcomes in the specific experimental context: “an undesired email system affords participants the receipt of the small PDF file,” “an undesired email system affords participants the sending of the large PDF file,” and “an undesired data compression technology affords participants the reduction of the large PDF file size.”

The experiment also allowed us to observe the specific contextual conditions and patterns of deviant affordance manifestations that made the achievement of the deviant outcome and/or the job goal more likely. Overall, in almost half of the cases (16 of 33 cases; 48.5%), exercising the three mechanisms underlying the deviant affordances generated successful goal achievement, i.e., the recipient received both PDF files and participants solved the task. The job goal was achieved in nine of the 17 cases (52.9%) in which we observed activities concealing the deviant IT use (i.e., probability mitigation), in all five cases in which participants used another email system to send the files (i.e., transfer one’s own experiences), and in 11 of the 22 cases (50.0%) in which participants searched online (using Google) for deviant affordances (i.e., envision-and-explore). The job goal was not achieved in the five cases in which the Deadlock 3 mechanism was exercised and participants pursued their individual goals by receiving the small PDF files on their private email systems. In the remaining 12 of the 17 (70.6%) deviant affordance cases in which the goal was not achieved, the reasons almost always appeared to be a lack of user capabilities so that the exercised actualization mechanism did not generate its regular deviant outcome. For instance, in Deviant Affordance Case 14, in which an undesired file archiver utility (WinRAR) afforded the participant PDF file size compression, the participant successfully searched for and installed the IT but was ultimately unable to sufficiently compress the file below 2MB. In Deviant Affordance Case 61, the actualization mechanism generated its regular deviant outcome (i.e., “size of large PDF file reduced”), but a lack of time prevented the participant from sending this compressed PDF file and achieving the task goal. Study 3: Post Hoc Interviews

Study 1 and 2 suggest that the severity and probability of sanctions that users expect from their deviance from the organizational IT policies may act as an important contextual condition. To expand our results and gain a richer understanding of contextual conditions that either release or impede the exercise of the three key mechanisms, we conducted post hoc interviews of the consequences that users expect to bear due to either deviating from organizational IT policies or failing to achieve their goals.

## Data Collection and Analysis

Overall, we conducted 26 interviews (Table 5) using two different purposive sampling approaches. First, we went back to the interviewees in Study 1 who either provided us with deviant affordance cases or rigorously advocated conformance with the organizational IT policies. The 22 interviews from this first sampling approach enabled us to test and refine our previous comparative analyses of structure, key mechanisms, and contextual conditions in Study 1 (see Appendix C, Table C6). Second, we conducted new interviews until we concluded that we had reached theoretical saturation, which happened after four new interviews. Two of these four interviews provided five new deviant affordance cases that corroborated our proposed structures, mechanisms, and contextual conditions underlying deviant affordances, whereas the other two interviews expanded our comparisons of cases and contextual conditions that impeded manifestations of deviant affordances. The focus of our questions was on the consequences that the interviewees did or did not expect both from deviating from the organizational IT policies, or, if applicable, failing to achieve their goals. We also asked interviewees to what extent and when they considered those consequences. In addition, related to each identified deviant affordance case, we asked adapted questions from the Study 1 interview script (see Appendix C, Table C1). We conducted all interviews via video/audio conference. A raffle for a smartwatch was used as an incentive to encourage participation. Table E1 in Appendix E summarizes the interviewees’ demographic characteristics and organizational contexts.

To analyze the post hoc interviews, two authors independently coded the interview transcripts regarding the expected consequences for the user (see Appendix E for details). We then compared the contexts that gave rise to deviant affordances to those that did not. Based on this comparative analysis, we abstracted and retroduced the contextual conditions and their (releasing or impeding) relationships with the key mechanisms of deviant affordances.

## Results

We found that the perception of deadlock, and thus the stimulation and exercise of the actualization mechanism, is conditioned on users’ evaluation of the expected consequences of deviating from organizational IT policies versus supporting their goal. This evaluation helped users better assess whether they favored supporting their goal over conforming with organizational IT policies, which in turn determined how likely they were to perceive deadlock. Hence, the evaluation of consequences acts indirectly as a contextual condition that influences the user’s sensemaking activities in the deadlock mechanism and, depending on the outcome, either impedes or releases the perception of deadlock.

In contextual conditions in which the severity/probability of the consequences the interviewees expected from deviating from the organizational IT policies was stronger than the benefits they expected to gain from supporting their goal, they tended to favor conforming with IT policies over supporting their goal and thus postponed their goal and accepted the tension for the time being. As a consequence, in such conditions, the deadlock was not perceived and the actualization mechanism was neither stimulated nor exercised. For instance, Interviewee C41 favored avoiding a potential note in her personal file resulting from deviance over quickly and easily communicating with colleagues via WhatsApp:

There was the instruction from the headquarters that [WhatsApp] is not allowed ... and I got a very personally addressed email ... it was very clearly stated to whom [a violation] would be forwarded. And then, it was clear to me: Okay, no, of course I don't want that any superior gets notified about it with my name attached ... Of course, my direct supervisor would also get that note. And then, I thought to myself: It doesn't necessarily have to be that it might be noted in my personnel file or something ... Okay, if I can’t use WhatsApp, I’ll have to contact people through other channels. (Interviewee C41)

By contrast, in contextual conditions in which the severity/probability of the consequences the interviewees expected from deviating from the organizational IT policies was weaker than the benefits they expected to gain from supporting their goal—including the effects of severity or probability mitigation activities typically occurring during the actualization mechanism (see Study 1)—they tended to favor supporting their goal over conforming with IT policies. Therefore, after making sense of their tension, users were more likely to perceive deadlock, which in turn stimulated the actualization mechanism. For instance, Interviewee 24 expected huge financial gains for the organization through using WhatsApp, which the organizational IT policies specified as undesired. Hence, he prioritized pursuing his goal, despite the potentially severe consequences resulting from this deviance. Making sense of the organizational actions, he perceived deadlock:

Of course, I agree that there can be consequences. That's why I pay careful attention to what I share on this channel WhatsApp ... But, it is extremely difficult to get along without this WhatsApp. I would prefer it personally ... The employer ought to provide a platform where I can establish fast, transparent communication ... It has to be said, I believe that without this WhatsApp channel we would have enormous financial losses ... Because if I am not allowed to use this channel, WhatsApp, which is simply available at all providers and systems, a lot of communication or a lot of important information would not reach our employees fast enough ... In my opinion, this can have massive consequences. (Interviewee 24)

The majority of interviewees reporting deviant affordance cases actually rated the consequences of deviating from organizational IT policies as mild and playing, at most, a minor role in their evaluations. Thus, the potential gains to be realized from supporting their goals or the potential losses realized from failing to support them primarily conditioned how important interviewees found their goals to be and thus how likely they were to perceive deadlock. For instance, Interviewee 4 expected severe consequences in terms of his personal reputation and future career if he failed to achieve his job goal, which is why he favored supporting his own goal over conforming with the organizational IT policies:

I think [the consequences of deviance] would have been a conversation with the supervisor, you would have looked into it once and then you would have agreed that you would not use [WeTransfer] anymore and would have thought of other ways ... You always have to weigh the benefits; you have to weigh for yourself: What have I gained? I gained time, it went fast, it was easy for me, and I could meanwhile do something else. It was actually good for the employer from my point of view. So, it was actually a win-win, I’d say ... My boss [thinks] that it's actually good that I find such fast, uncomplicated ways, ways that he perhaps doesn’t know, because he says: “Man, [name deleted] is a digital native, he manages everything here wonderfully.” And that’s what fires you up a little bit ... The boss expects you to find a solution ... In the worst case, it is harmful for my career ... If I don't have a [solution], then I will not be promoted by the boss to the same extent as now. (Interviewee 4, Case 10)

In fact, some interviewees confessed that they never evaluated any potential consequences of deviance or that they were unaware of them, focusing only on the gains associated with supporting their goal. For example, Interviewee 12 reported:

Good question. I’m not sure about the consequences ... I don’t know. What are they gonna do? Well, I myself ... didn’t think it was critical. That’s why I did it, because I thought that it would bring me more benefits than running around with such a paper pad all the time. (Interviewee 12, Case 26)

Interviewee 20 confessed that potential performance gains obliviated any evaluation of the consequences that might arise from deviating from organizational IT policies, such as potential security issues:

No plaintiff, no judge. Well, I’d say, if I put my personal notes on [Evernote] and share that with my colleagues, who also put their personal notes on there, then I see that as less critical. I can also write the notes in my book, and the book falls out of my pocket and the competitor gets it. There is no difference ... The only thing you have to be honest about is that it’s an app. I just get it from Google Play or from the App Store. And as a user, I don’t go into the background check in such detail about where the servers are located, who has access to my data, what is passed on to third parties in the background, and so on. Let’s face it, that’s not what I deal with when I download an app. (Interviewee 20, Case 42)

To conclude, we expect the mechanisms underlying the deviant affordances and their deviant outcomes that support the user’s individual goal and a legitimate organizational goal to be contingent on contexts in which the user evaluates the consequences of supporting their goal versus conforming with organizational IT policies. This evaluation can (but does not need to) include assessing the respective consequences potentially resulting from deviating from organizational IT policies. Contextual conditions of relatively severe and/or probable consequences of deviance tend to discourage users from goal pursuit, causing them to accept the tension, which would in turn impede the perception of deadlock. Contextual conditions in which such impediments are removed are conditions of relatively less severe and/or probable consequences of deviance. In such contexts, the perception of deadlock would be released, in turn stimulating the exercise of the actualization mechanism. Finally, by comparing the deviant affordance cases of the same interviewees, we found that the evaluation of expected consequences is specific to each deviant affordance case. Interviewee 2, Case 4 says this in a nutshell: “Well ... it’s really a deliberate risk assessment on a case-by-case basis.”

## Meta-Inferences: Theory of Deviant Affordances

We collected data using interviews and an experiment to support our search for the structure, mechanisms, and contextual conditions underlying deviant affordances that produce deviant outcomes supporting the user’s individual goal and at least one organizational goal. To develop metainferences, we triangulated the various data types, sources, methods, and investigators in our study (see Venkatesh et al., 2013; Wynn & Williams, 2012). In particular, we integrated the findings from our interview and experimental studies to theorize how tension, deadlock, and actualization mechanisms interact in specific contexts to bring about the observed outcomes (Figure 4). In this section, we describe the inferred theory of deviant affordances. Appendix F depicts how we assessed the quality of meta-inferences and why we consider them to be credible.

The starting point is changes in the technological properties of the sociotechnical context external to the organization that cause undesired technological properties to arise. When goaloriented users who are constantly receptive to such changes in their sociotechnical context either engage in envisioning these undesired technological properties or transferring them via their own or observed experiences, the (macro-micro) tension mechanism is exercised and deviant affordances emerge. In specific contexts, the observable outcome is that users perceive a tension between supporting their goal and conforming with organizational IT policies: the undesired technological properties afford users a deviant outcome that supports their goal—and, in turn, a legitimate organizational goal. Perceiving the tension provides evidence for the existence of a deviant affordance. The perception of tension, however, depends on contextual conditions. Relevant contextual conditions intervening in or impeding this perception include, for instance, the lack of user capabilities essential for transferring or envisioning undesired technological properties that afford them support for their goals. In addition, users are less likely to perceive tension when the number of undesired technological properties in the external context is low (whether due to a small number of useful technological properties in the sociotechnical context and/or due to the paucity of organizational IT policies specifying the undesirability of actualizing affordances related to technological properties of the sociotechnical context).

Figure 4. Generative Mechanisms Underlying Deviant Affordances

The perception of tension between the users’ goal and conformance with organizational IT policies enables and causes the (micro-micro) deadlock mechanism. The deadlock mechanism is exercised when users perceiving the tension start to make sense of it. Following Bhaskar’s (1993) range of four social actions, the users’ sensemaking activities may include reflecting upon (1) alternative affordances in relation to desired technological properties, (2) their interactions with the organization, (3) organizational IT policies specifying the actualization of affordances related to the technological properties as undesired, or (4) their individual goal. The sensemaking activities of the deadlock mechanism resemble a feedback loop: users constantly assess how the outcome of their sensemaking activities helps to resolve the perceived tension between their goal and policy conformance. If the tension has not been resolved (morphostasis), users continue to engage in activities to make sense of the tension and reflect on the outcome. If the tension is resolved, the loop is exited (morphogenesis). In this case, contextual conditions produced a resolution of the tension, preventing the deadlock mechanism from generating its regular outcome—perceived deadlock. For instance, the organization might have respecified the organizational IT policies, making the previously undesired technological properties now desired, or users might have changed their (individual) goal. By contrast, in the absence of such contextual conditions, time goes by and the tension remains unresolved because neither users’ desire to support their goal nor the organization’s desire for IT policy conformance goes away. Eventually, the tension is dealt with in one of two ways.

First, users may accept or try to ignore the tension. For example, in some contexts, users may acknowledge the importance of conforming to organizational IT policies and freely subordinate their individual goals to organizational goals. In other contexts, the organization may force users to conform by either technically constraining access to undesired technological properties or severely sanctioning any deviance. Both these contexts (and the respective consequences of deviance/conformance) cause users to favor conformance over deviance. The acceptance of the tension produced by these contextual conditions, however, keeps users in the loop and may be only temporary because the tension is not resolved (but represents merely a first-order change in users’ beliefs).

Second, users may perceive deadlock, i.e., the impasse of their sensemaking activities in the loop. They perceive deadlock because organizational actions to resolve the tension between supporting their goals and conforming to organizational IT policies are either necessary but not taken, taken at the wrong level, or taken when they should not have been with the result that users perceive the tension as persisting (Deadlock 1), perceive the tension as intensifying (Deadlock 2), or perceive the tension as being unresolvable (Deadlock 3), respectively. The perception of deadlock is more likely to occur in contexts in which users prioritize the support of their goal and thus favor deviance from rather than conformance with organizational IT policies. One reason for this may be that users expect more severe consequences for failing to achieve their goals than for deviating from the IT policy. Since perceived deadlocks can only be escaped through secondorder changes (Watzlawick et al., 1974/2011), users are provoked to escape the tension and the loop.

Under specific contextual conditions, such as when users accept justifications regarding their deviance from organizational IT policies, the perception of deadlock stimulates and causes the (micro-macro) actualization mechanism.<sup>9</sup> The actualization mechanism is exercised when users engage in activities of deviant IT use and mitigating the consequences (in terms of severity and probability) of the deviance. The product is a deviant outcome that, in the context of our work, supports not only the user’s individual goal but also at least one fundamental organizational goal. However, contextual conditions, such as insufficient capabilities to use the undesired technological properties, can alter and/or impede the regular generation of this deviant outcome. The resulting actual outcome may not help users escape the perceived deadlock but may provide feedback for potential adjustments and iterations of their activities, causing the exercise of the actualization (or even the tension) mechanism once again. Finally, the deviant outcome may serve to stimulate the exercise of further actualization or tension mechanisms associated with the same or related goals, respectively. These feedback mechanisms, however, are not the focus of this work.

## Discussion

Our objective was to understand the structures, mechanisms, and conditions underlying deviant affordances, whose deviant manifestations and outcomes are increasingly observed in organizations. Inspired by the literatures on affordances, deviant IT use, and the orders of change framework (Watzlawick et al., 1974/2011), we conceptualized deviant affordances and retroducted three key mechanisms and the ways in which they are linked together to produce uncertain outcomes for the organization. Our multimethod research approach (Mingers, 2001; Zachariadis et al., 2013), leveraging a dynamic mode of inquiry (Zachariadis et al., 2013), and the relevant properties of mixed methods research guidelines (Venkatesh et al., 2013, 2016) add robustness and validity to our explanations. Our findings underscore the importance of organizational structures and actions in creating deadlock perceptions that, in specific contexts, stimulate users’ deviant IT use, which results in deviant outcomes that support organizational goals through improving task, contextual, or innovative performance.

## Theoretical Implications

Our research makes key contributions to the literatures on affordances and employee IT use. We advance knowledge on affordances by adding deviance as a key component and developing a theoretical conceptualization of deviant affordances. Deviant affordances challenge the assumption in existing IS affordance literature that affordance actualizations consistently support organizational goals and IT policies (e.g., Anderson & Robey, 2017; Strong et al., 2014). This is particularly important in the current digital age in which novel technologies are increasingly arising in users’ sociotechnical contexts and questioning traditional organizational IT policies that specify their use as undesired (e.g., Panetta, 2017). We show that it is this change in the undesired technological properties of the sociotechnical context external to the organization that causes the tension mechanism. A frequent outcome is that employees perceive tension between supporting their individual goal—and, in the context of our work, also a legitimate organizational goal—and conforming with organizational IT policies. This, in turn, enables one of three types of deadlock mechanisms. In specific contexts, these deadlock mechanisms generate perceptions of deadlock for users seeking to attain their goal under the demand of conformance with organizational IT policies, which in turn, stimulates the actualization mechanism as a means of escaping the deadlock. We found that the concatenation of these three mechanisms increases the likelihood of a deviant outcome that supports users in achieving their goals and also furthers a legitimate goal of their organization. Our work thus helps improve the understanding of the previously unexplained mechanisms underlying recently observed manifestations of affordances that deviate from organizational IT policies (Anderson & Robey, 2017) and their outcomes, which often support not only users’ individual goals but also certain organizational goals, such as increases in efficiency and/or innovation (Behrens, 2009).

Our concept and theory of deviant affordances that, if actualized, manifest in deviant IT use enrich existing theories on deviant IT use with uncertain organizational outcomes, such as shadow IT use and reinvention (Boudreau & Robey, 2005) (see Appendix A). Deviant affordances offer a relational perspective on deviant IT use, and our theory relates to a variety of deviant affordances associated with different types of ITs, users, goals, and organizational structures (i.e., organizational goals and IT policies). By explaining the structures, mechanisms, and conditions underlying deviant affordances and how they interact to produce the observed deviant outcomes, we identify technological, individual, and organizational factors that can drive the actualization of deviant affordances and thus deviant employee IT use and its effects.

Deviant affordances and their underlying mechanisms further help to explain the impact of organizational actions and structures on users’ beliefs and actions—in particular, when organizational actions, or the lack thereof, backfire and stimulate individual outbreaks and structural transformations in response to deadlocks. Leveraging the orders of change framework (Watzlawick et al., 1974/2011) from psychotherapy to abstract observed deadlock events revealed three types of deadlock mechanisms that cause users to perceive deadlock because the organization’s actions (or the lack thereof) have failed to resolve the tension between achieving one’s goals and conforming with organizational IT policies. In specific contexts, this perception of deadlock, in turn, stimulates the actualization mechanism, which entails a second-order change that enables users to not only escape the deadlock but also produce a deviant outcome that supports both their individual goals and certain organizational goals better than outcomes associated with affordances conforming to organizational IT policies.

## Future Research Directions

Our research focused on goal-oriented users that, at a minimum, understood that the organizational IT policies specified the actualization of deviant affordances as undesired. Thus, in the course of the actualization mechanism producing the deviant outcome, users deliberately deviated from organizational IT policies to reach their goals. This focus allowed us to exclude such deviant affordance cases in which users recklessly follow and imitate goal-oriented users acting on deviant affordances. In such cases, users may perceive the affordance in relation to the technological properties but likely do not perceive the deviance from organizational IT policies. However, by simply imitating the use of the technological properties without also transferring deviance, followers’ activities aim to actualize affordances that they deem to be conforming to organizational IT policies and would thus be similar to activities involved in classic affordance actualizations analyzed in the extant research (e.g., Strong et al., 2014). In such cases, for instance, we would not expect any actualization mechanism activities that are oriented toward mitigating the severity and probability of potential consequences resulting from deviance from organizational IT policies. As a result, these deviant affordance cases might not only produce differing deviant outcomes but may also bear a greater risk of producing destructive outcomes for both the user and the organization. Our work thus suggests the importance of future research analyzing the mechanisms underlying deviant affordances that followers who are unaware of the deviance actualize and how they affect individual and organizational goals.

We focused on goal-oriented individual actions and action possibilities and thus did not consider networks of deviant affordances. Affordance networks describe interrelations between multiple affordances and associated outcomes that together support broader organizational goals (Burton-Jones & Volkoff, 2017). Our interviews indicated that deviant affordances may also emerge and manifest across multiple levels of analysis. Future research should unpack the key mechanisms of deviant affordance networks that co-produce deviant outcomes, supporting goals at multiple levels of individuals, groups, and organizations.

In the experiment, we made use of observations from our interviews in Study 1 and provoked empirical manifestations of deviant affordances in contextual conditions of relatively less severe/probable sanctions for deviance. This was useful to observe the combinations of mechanisms and contextual conditions causally relevant for producing deviant outcomes. Although insights from the post hoc interviews (Study 3) substantiated our design by holding the releasing condition “expected consequences of deviance” constant at less severe/probable levels, we were not able to identify any manifestations indicating severity mitigation activities in the experiment. We also did not observe how highly severe/probable consequences interact with the deadlock mechanism. Future research should address these limitations.

Finally, we focused on the class of deviant affordances whose actualization organizational IT policies specify as undesired because these deviant affordances arise from a relation with undesired technological properties and produce a deviant outcome that leads to uncertain outcomes for the organization. This helped us to explain outcomes and events associated with deviant affordances that are increasingly observed in practice (Anderson & Robey, 2017; Segal, 2016) and allowed us to consider the exposure to different risks, such as those related to information security and privacy (e.g., Willison & Warkentin, 2013), which are inherent to the various deviant affordance classes. We encourage future researchers to identify the structures, mechanisms, and conditions that underpin other classes of deviant affordances (Table 1) and their deviant and long-term outcomes.

## Practical Implications

Our research opens up a novel perspective for managers seeking to develop purposeful strategies to deal with the everincreasing variety of manifestations of deviant affordances emerging in organizations (Segal, 2016). First, organizations (management/leadership within organizations) should attempt to identify and resolve users’ tensions between their goals and IT policy conformance before any deadlock perceptions arise. There may be different ways to achieve this. For instance, organizational policy makers should ensure that organizational IT policies afford users full attainment of those goals that are expected from their role in the organization. Organizations should also be responsive to users’ needs. Our findings suggest that organizations should observe manifestations of plausible user activities indicating the exercise of the tension mechanism because these activities may reveal affordances that users desire. We offer three possibilities for how organizations can observe and identify such affordances. First, IT staff members can listen and carefully pay attention to what users ask them, their colleagues, or supervisors about deviant affordances. Second, they can establish an official online platform to discuss novel affordance ideas and scan the log files of users’ intranet search queries regarding envisioned deviant affordances. Third, organizations can monitor and analyze users’ search queries on search engines. Note, however, that this third possibility may infringe on users’ privacy and would depend on the organization’s monitoring policy. IT managers could then possibly infer potential tensions perceived by users and develop countermeasures to avoid perceptions of any type of deadlock.

After users perceive deadlocks, empirical manifestations of the actualization mechanism can also provide a source of learning. The IT team should compare deviant affordances with the affordances conforming to organizational IT policies to identify, for instance, why the use of third-party cloud services helps users overcome deadlocks and achieve their goals. Building on this, IT managers and developers could, in turn, provide or create novel desired technological properties and/or adapt their IT policies so that novel affordances can arise, offering users to support their goals in conformance with organizational IT policies and the organization to reap the full benefits of the multitude of technological properties available today. However, since our findings also indicate a tendency for users to conceal deviant affordance manifestations, organizations should seek strategies to bring manifestations of deviant affordances to light. One way to do this would be to incentivize employees to suggest novel affordance ideas. Finally, in terms of Deadlock 3 mechanisms with underlying incongruence between the goals of users and the organization, managerial actions to raise user awareness of the reasons for this incongruence may help restrain Deadlock 3 perceptions and thus minimize the stimulation of the actualization mechanism.

## Conclusion

Our work provides a key step in explaining how outcomes of prohibited cloud service use that support organizational goals are produced. We analyzed users and their goals, organizations’ structures and contexts, and technological properties external to and not desired by organizations, and identified three key mechanisms that, if exercised in specific contexts, entail activities that do not conform with organizational IT policies but generate a deviant outcome that can nonetheless support a legitimate organizational goal. Our findings inform future research on the organizational actions, structures, and conditions that elicit actualizations of deviant affordances. They can help organizations better channel and regulate employees’ deployment of third-party cloud services that they do not desire but from which they may still benefit.

## Details on Interview Data Collection

We let participants choose their preferred mode of communication for the interview: face-to-face on-site at their organization or at the authors school, via video conference, or by telephone. To reduce biases due to social desirability, we sought to gain interviewees’ trust by guaranteeing their anonymity and starting each conversation by chatting superficially about ITs used at home and at work. Two authors conducted the first nine interviews together to establish a consistent interview style and debriefed after each interview to discuss the emerging themes. Each interview was recorded and transcribed for in-depth analysis.

We conducted seven follow-up interviews to clarify our understanding and iteratively adjust our abstractions of deviant affordances that emerged by going back and forth between relevant theoretical explanations in the literature and the interview data within and across different cases (Danermark et al., 2019). We continued additional interviews until they no longer added new knowledge relevant to explain the mechanisms underlying deviant affordances and their deviant outcomes.

In total, in Study 1, we conducted 47 interviews and identified 48 different deviant affordance cases across 23 interviewees. The interviews with those 23 interviewees and the seven follow-ups lasted, on average, 54 and 28 minutes, respectively. The remaining 17 interviews in which we did not identify any deviant affordance case lasted, on average, 79 minutes.

## Appendix D

# Experimental Data Collection and Analysis of Study 2

Details on Experimental Data Collection

## Sample

The 169 participants in the experiment were undergraduate and graduate students enrolled in an IS course at a German university and earned 2% of their total grade for their participation. These students experience a multitude of affordances arising from a relation with diverse technological properties to support personal or work goals. They may thus be especially prone to perceiving tension between their goals and conformance with organizational IT policies when they start their jobs. For reasons of privacy and to minimize social desirability biases, we guaranteed the anonymity of all participants. In addition, after finishing data collection, we obtained students’ consent to evaluate the screenrecording data.

## Experimental Task, Manipulations, and Deadlock Conditions

We selected a file-sharing task based on a web-based mail system within the organizational structure of a university. People are generally quite capable of sending emails and sharing files because both are routine and relevant tasks at work and for personal (nonwork) activities. The assigned task comprised searching for and downloading two scientific MIS Quarterly articles from EBSCOhost’s journal database and subsequently using a university email system to send both PDF articles separately to a given addressee by email.

To provoke tension and deadlock mechanisms, we manipulated the email system such that it constrained the job goal of successfully sending both PDF articles from being reached. The technological properties of the email system afforded participants only the attaching and sending of files of a maximum size of 2.0 MB. Although the PDF of article 1 had a file size of 0.4 MB and could be attached and sent without any difficulty, the PDF of article 2 had a file size of 3.0 MB, thus exceeding the maximum file attachment size of the emai system by 1.0 MB. Participants perceived the constraint when they either realized that the second file was too large to attach and send or when they actually tried to attach the second file. In the latter case, each attachment process was interrupted by a system e rror message highlighting in red that the attached file was too large. This error message served as a symbolic expression communicating the const raint to the users (Markus & Silver, 2008). The constraint served two purposes. First, it provoked goal-oriented participants to be more receptive to deviant affordances in the sociotechnical context associated with supporting their goal, thus triggering the tension mechanism. Second, it helped provoke participants who perceived tension to also perceive deadlock. The reason is that, although we constrained the university email system, we established an experimental IT policy specifying the actualization of affordances in relation to this constraining email system as the desired ones for the task. In addition, we were available only at the beginning of each session to briefly demo nstrate the basic functions of EBSCOhost and the email system, and provide both a comprehensive description of the task procedure that played the role of the experimental IT policy (see Table D1) and a detailed manual on how to use the database and the email system. Howe ver, during task performance, when goal-oriented participants were facing the constraint, we were not there to help and react. Our actions, or the lack thereof, thus simulated Watzlawick et al.’s (1974/2011) description of action is necessary but not taken, in turn, provoking participants to perceive Deadlock 1 due to persisting tension.

In addition to those Deadlock 1 conditions, we randomly assigned participants to two more conditions in order to account for the equifinality of the generated deadlock. Deadlock 2 conditions involved additional explicit specifications that the actualization of any affordances in relation to any other hardware such as private USB sticks, mobiles, or smartphones, any other email client, any other web and cloud services, as well as to downloaded software from the Internet was undesired. Those specifications on undesired technological properties were either included in the IT policy in writing (formal condition) or verbally communicated by the experimenters (informal condition) (see Table D1) The additional specifications drew participants’ attention to a variety of undesired technological properties/deviant affordances, increasing the chance that participants perceived Deadlock 2 due to intensifying tension.

Finally, in Deadlock 3 conditions, we observed all participants’ actions before the provocation of the constraint as well as six cases without any constraint provocation. In these conditions, neither of the two PDF articles exceeded the maximum attachment size and participants were able to achieve the job goal by using the desired email system. These conditions thus left room for deadlocks that participants perceived due to incongruence between their goal and the job goal—i.e., when the tension is unresolvable.

## Pilot Study

We conducted three pilot studies to refine the experimental design and procedure. We pretested different time frames and 12 minutes was found to be enough time to observe manifestations of deviant affordances and their outcomes. On average, people took 5:32 minutes to experience the constraint, leaving an average of 6:28 minutes to perceive tension, deadlock, and produce a deviant outcome.

## Details on Coding the Experimental Data

To analyze the screen recordings using systematic observation (Bakeman & Quera, 2011), we first defined and piloted a coding scheme. Our coding scheme was developed in an iterative process to increase the reliability of data collection (Bakeman & Quera, 2011). One of the authors watched all records and captured each participant’s sequence of behavioral events after the deadlock was provoked. A comprehensive list of codes was identified comprising themes that we had identified to be relevant in our interview studies, existing behavioral schemes of studies with similar interests (e.g., Skinner et al., 2003), as well as newly discovered themes. Guided by the identified structural entities and hypothesized causal mechanisms underlying deviant affordances, we then aggregated and combined these codes into dichotomous categories with 1= presence and 0 = absence of plausible activities and outcomes indicating the exercise of tension, deadlock, or actualization mechanisms as well as various contextual conditions to enable descriptive analysis (Bakeman & Quera, 2011). The final coding manual with category definitions and examples for respective empirical manifestations is provided in Table D2.

After testing these categories against the three pilot recordings, one author coded all data for all categories and two other independent assistants each coded all data for distinct categories. We assigned coders to distinct categories to reduce coding biases in patterns discovered between the codes of different categories (Bakeman & Quera, 2011), e.g., in patterns between plausible activities manifesting the tension and actualization mechanisms. Each Cohen’s kappa κ, which reflects the degree of agreement among raters, was greater than the threshold (Bakeman & Quera, 2011) (see Table D2).

## Details on Analyzing the Post Hoc Interviews

Similar to our approach in Study 1, two authors independently coded the interview transcripts in order to detect contextual conditions and respective sociotechnical entities. Classical expectancy value theories (e.g., Rogers, 1983) inspired our coding categories: an expected low/high value, i.e., in our case low/high severity, of consequences for the user resulting from deviating from the organizational IT policies (inter-rater reliability κ<sub>lsevdeviance</sub> = 0.869; κ<sub>hsevdeviance</sub> = 0.914) and a high/low probability that these consequences would affect them (κ<sub>lprobdeviance</sub> = 0.895; κ<sub>hprobdeviance</sub> = 0.750) as well as an expected low/high value of consequences for the user resulting from supporting their goal $( \kappa _ { \mathrm { l v a l u e g o a l } } = 0 . 7 6 2 ; \kappa _ { \mathrm { h v a l u e g o a l } } = 0 . 8 7 6 )$ that would occur most likely in case of deviance from the organizational IT policies. In addition, ignorance of the consequences resulting from deviating from the organizational IT policies emerged from the data (κ<sub>igncons</sub>= 0.875).

## Quality of Meta-Inferences

The employment of multiple approaches supporting our causal analysis strengthened the power of our explanations (Wynn & Williams, 2012; Zachariadis et al., 2013) and lent preliminary credibility and confidence in the quality of meta-inferences (Venkatesh et al., 2016). To assess the quality of our inferences, we analyzed the design, analytical, and inferential validity of the interview and experimental studies, as well as the design validity and explanatory quality of the meta-inferences, as proposed by Venkatesh et al. (2013, 2016) and Zachariadis et al. (2013) (see Table F1). We deduced from those assessments that the selected multimethod approach appears appropriate to credibly explain the mechanisms underlying deviant affordances and their deviant outcomes. The integration of interview and experimental findings added value beyond each of the individual studies because they helped us to establish confidence that the proposed mechanisms consistently explain the empirically observed events and that we sufficiently addressed the full context of generative structures underlying those mechanisms. In addition to triangulation, the multimethod approach helped us to compensate for threats stemming from potential social desirability biases in the interviews through objective systematic observations in the experiment (weakness minimization legitimation; Onwuegbuzie & Johnson, 2006) as well as to adequately integrate the perspectives of users inside the organization and of objective observers (inside-outside legitimation; Onwuegbuzie & Johnson, 2006). Another potential threat is that our data analysis relied on abductive and retroductive reasoning that lacks fixed criteria to definitely assess inferential validity (i.e., threat to multiple validities) (Danermark et al., 2019). As remedial actions, we used counterfactual thinking, compared different cases in which deviant affordances did and did not manifest, and provoked deadlock situations to study how people handle it. These are all recently proposed strategies of powerful retroduction (Danermark et al., 2019), thus increasing our confidence in the inferential quality.
