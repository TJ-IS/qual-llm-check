# PEER PRIVACY CONCERN: CONCEPTUALIZATION AND MEASUREMENT<sup>1</sup>

Nan (Andy) Zhang Department of Management Science and Engineering, School of Management, Harbin Institute of Technology, Harbin, CHINA, {andyzhang@hit.edu.cn}

Chong (Alex) Wang Guanghua School of Management, Peking University, Beijing, CHINA, {alexwang@gsm.pku.edu.cn}

Elena Karahanna MIS Department, Terry College of Business, University of Georgia, Athens, GA, U.S.A., {ekarah@uga.edu}

## Yan Xu

Department of Information Systems, Business Statistics and Operations Management, Business School and Division of Public Policy, Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, HONG KONG {xuyan@ust.hk}

Privacy needs on today’s internet differ from the information privacy needs in traditional e-commerce settings due to their focus on interactions among online peers rather than merely transactions with an online vendor. Peer-oriented online interactions have critical implications for an individual’s virtual presence and self-cognition. Yet existing conceptualizations of internet privacy concerns have solely focused on the control of personal information release and on online interactions with online vendors. Drawing on the theory of personal boundaries, this study revisits the theoretical foundation of online privacy and proposes a multidimensional peer-related privacy concern construct, that focuses on privacy violations from online peers. We term this new construct “Peer Privacy Concern” (PrPC) and define it as the general feeling of being unable to maintain functional personal boundaries in online activities as a result of the behavior of online peers. This construct consists of four dimensions comprised of a reconceptualization of information privacy concerns to also reflect privacy concerns with respect to peers’ handling of self-shared information and with respect to peer-shared information about one’s self, and three new dimensions that tap into the arising privacy needs from virtual interactions (i.e., virtual territory privacy concern and communication privacy concern) as well as from the need to maintain psychological independence (i.e., psychological privacy concern). These new dimensions, which are rooted in the theory of personal boundaries, are prominent privacy needs in online social interactions with peers. However, they are absent from previous privacy concern conceptualizations. Scales fo measuring this new construct are developed and empirically validated.

Keywords: Privacy concerns, online peers, personal boundaries, online social networking, scale development

## Introduction

Advances in information technology are challenging individuals’ ability to maintain privacy. As human social interactions have become increasingly digitized, our ability to control self-exposure has declined, especially on the internet (Attrill 2012). This erosion of privacy is threatening our autonomy as consumers and citizens (Cohen 2000) and depriving us of the space necessary for free thought and free expression. As such, privacy concerns are at an all-time high, leading scholars to call privacy “the issue of our times” (Acquisti et al. 2015, p.509).

Westin (1967) defined information privacy as the claim of individuals, groups, or institutions to determine for themselves when, how, and to what extent digital information about them is communicated to others (Westin 1967, p. 7). Leveraging this definition, previous information systems (IS) research about internet privacy has equated privacy with the control over the digitized personal information that is necessary to facilitate online e-commerce transactions (Hong and Thong 2013; Malhotra et al. 2004; Smith et al. 2011) and investigated the collection, dissemination, and usage of factual personal information, such as addresses, demographic data, transaction information, and financial records, by online vendors.

The penetration of the mobile internet and the proliferation of social media and social networking platforms have expanded the domain of internet use beyond e-commerce. This has fundamentally altered the context of online interactions. Today, the internet encompasses more social aspects. Online social networking services, such as Facebook, Twitter, and Instagram, have become the most popular tools for creating and maintaining social connections (Statista 2020), and online peers have become an indispensable part of many people’s lives and also a critical component of the digital economy.

E-commerce has also become more social regarding the convergence of online social networking with the internet’s commercial aspects. Companies analyze social media posts, make personal recommendations on social media, and recruit social media opinion leaders to promote products. Consumers are provided with product information through online social connections and consult peer-generated reviews before making purchases. Crowdfunders receive funds from peers who use social network information to evaluate creditability. Sharing platforms enable peer-to-peer transactions in which community engagement and social reputations play essential roles. Clearly, online peer connections form an integral component of the modern internet and shape the pattern of various types of online social interactions, both commercial and noncommercial. While researchers have made significant efforts to understand the impact of peer-related online social interactions (e.g., Bapna et al. 2019; Karahanna et al. 2018; Ren et al. 2012; Rhue and Sundararajan 2019), research on peer-related privacy concerns continues to lag.

Privacy needs are present in all contexts where social interactions occur and the conceptualization of privacy is context-dependent. When the context of social interaction changes, the conceptual domain of privacy changes accordingly (Bennett 1992; Smith et al. 2011). As Smith et al. (1996) acknowledged, “the dimensionality [of privacy] is neither absolute nor static since perceptions of advocates, consumers, and scholars could shift over time” (p. 190). Similarly, Stewart and Segars (2002, p. 37) suggested that “the theoretical and operational assumptions underlying the structure of constructs such as concern for information privacy (CFIP) should be reinvestigated in light of emerging technology, practice, and research.” As such, the expansion of the domain of digitized social interactions on the modern internet necessitates a reexamination and extension of the conceptual domain of internet privacy.

Social interactions on the modern internet are different from earlier commerce-focused internet interactions in ways that are consequential to the way we conceptualize privacy concerns. The differences from a user’s point of view are stark. First, the target of the interactions differs. With the proliferation of online social networking, the main target of online social interactions has shifted from vendors to peers,<sup>2</sup> with whom the focal user establishes and maintains social connections. Peer-oriented interactions are absent in the traditional e-commerce environment, in which prior conceptualizations of information privacy are rooted. Thus, we maintain that conceptualizations of privacy concerns need to be extended to peers. Second, the purpose of interactions is different. While business-toconsumer interactions in e-commerce are instrumental in facilitating commercial transactions, online peer-to-peer interactions entail rich symbolic meanings to maintain social connections and online identities (e.g., Ludwig et al. 2014). Thus, it is clear that privacy needs to be extended to protect the virtual self (Gefen et al. 2003). Third, the content of interactions is different. Today, internet users share a wider spectrum of personal information and opinions across platforms, covering all aspects of personal life; this thus differs from information that is passively shared in an e-commerce context, which is limited to transaction details such as demographics, product/service preferences, and purchases. The extensive range of information that is shared online makes it increasingly challenging to evaluate privacy implications before sharing. Fourth, the pattern of interactions is different. Social interactions are multifaceted and complex (e.g., Acquisti et al. 2014; Debatin et al. 2009). Online social communications are decentralized, bidirectional, and multichanneled, while personal information transmissions in e-commerce interactions are centralized and unidirectional. Online peers are more than passive receivers of others’ shared content; they are equally empowered to share, respond, comment, and disseminate others’ personal information. These critical differences in the context of social interactions warrant a reexamination of the conceptual domain of internet privacy, considering the dynamic boundary between the public and the private in online social interactions (Tufekci 2008).

The prevalence of the voluntary sharing of personal and often sensitive content online does not imply that privacy is less of an issue. On the contrary, privacy concerns are at an all-time high (i.e., the “privacy paradox,” Acquisti and Gross 2006), and privacy issues are becoming increasingly complex. There are many examples of public outcry regarding manipulative comments, fake news, and unintended peer disclosure through features such as tagging and retweeting (e.g., the “Cambridge Analytica”<sup>3</sup> and “Europe vs. Facebook” cases<sup>4</sup>). Such privacy violation incidents highlight the limitations of the current conceptualizations of internet privacy, as many of the issues address how online social interactions with peers, rather than with platform vendors, are managed. However, a comprehensive review of the privacy literature suggests that scholars have mostly focused on the risks of sharing information with vendors. Only rarely have measures of privacy concerns (risks) associated with online peers been included (Ozdemir et al. 2017), and only one paper (Lin and Armstrong 2019) has studied peer-related privacy concerns beyond information privacy. How peers handle online privacy is an important topic that nevertheless remains underexamined in the literature (Bélanger and Crossler 2011; Ozdemir et al. 2017; Strutin 2011).

The goal of this paper is to examine the conceptualization of internet privacy in the context of online peer-oriented interactions. To do this, we revisited the personal boundary regulation theory of privacy (e.g., Altman 1975, Burgoon 1982, Margulis 1977a, 1977b), and used it as a basis for a proposed privacy conceptualization that focuses on privacy concerns with respect to peers. We define this new concept, which we call Peer Privacy Concern (PrPC), as the general feeling of being unable to maintain functional personal boundaries in online activities as a result of the behavior of online peers. This paper posits that users achieve personal boundary regulation with online peers through interface regulation and interaction regulation, manifesting in the following four privacy dimensions: information privacy, psychological privacy, communication privacy, and virtual territory privacy. Based on this multidimensional conceptualization, we developed and validated measurement scales following a multistage, iterative scale development process (MacKenzie et al. 2011). The new construct, PrPC, and its measurement items complement existing information privacy scales that solely focus on vendors. While the presented scales were developed in the context of online social networks, the items can be easily adapted to any online context where peer interactions play a significant role.

We make several contributions to privacy research. First, we broaden the internet privacy conceptualization from a focus on vendors dealing with personal factual information to a focus on online peers. We highlight differences between peer-related privacy concerns and vendor-related privacy concerns and bring clarity to discussions about privacy issues that confound the two. Second, the new conceptualization of PrPC achieves comprehensive coverage of privacy needs in peer-oriented online interactions. In addition to issues related to how online peers deal with personal information shared online (which conceptually aligns with information privacy concerns in the literature), PrPC includes concerns related to whether personal virtual territory rights are respected, the management of direct online communication, and the freedom to both express and form opinions, all of which are critical for maintaining effective personal boundaries online. We hope that these new dimensions will inspire future privacy research. Third, the absence of a proper theory-driven conceptualization of peer-related privacy concerns and a validated measurement scale represent critical obstacles to cumulative research in this area. This study develops a reliable and valid measurement scale for PrPC that can serve as the foundation for future theoretical and empirical research on peer-related privacy.

## Theoretical Background

## Privacy and Personal Boundary Regulation

Privacy means different things for different people in different circumstances. Privacy has been described as the protection of one’s personal space and the right to be left alone (Brandeis and Warren 1890), the control over and safeguarding of personal information (Westin 1967), the selective control of access to the self (Altman 1975; Margulis 1977a, 1977b), and an aspect of dignity, autonomy, and ultimately human freedom (Schoeman 1992). While seemingly different, these definitions are related and pertain to controlling the interactions that define the boundaries between the self and others, between the private and the shared or public (Acquisti et al. 2016; Burgoon 1982).

As a form of interpersonal transaction control, privacy can be understood as being subject to a personal boundary regulation process (Altman 1975). Boundaries occur at points of discontinuity in space, time, or function. Such discontinuities become personal boundaries if individuals require control over transactions across these boundaries (Miller et al. 1978). Personal boundaries reflect the need and effort to differentiate oneself from the surrounding social environment in order to establish and maintain an intact selfconcept and to develop a sense of individuality (Morrison et al. 1985). People establish and maintain boundaries because of the need for intimacy and psychological respite as well as the desire for protection from social influence and control (Margulis 2003). Personal boundary regulation defines the self as a unique individual by articulating who the self is, what belongs to the self, what the self will do, and how close others can get to the self. That is, personal boundaries enable people to develop their sense of individuality. By being able to alter the degree of openness of personal boundaries, individuals can control their physical interactions with others and the information transactions between the self and the environment (Altman 1975).

## Privacy Concerns: Existing Conceptualizations, Measurements, and Contexts

The control-based definition of privacy serves as the foundation of privacy discussions and measurement scales in the IS literature (Smith et al. 2011). These conceptualizations and measures of information privacy have served as the foundation of prior research on internet privacy. Existing discussions about information privacy have deepened the understanding of the nature of privacy concerns regarding the collection and misuse of digitized personal information by vendors (intentionally or otherwise). While insightful, existing conceptualizations are constrained in that they equate internet privacy with information privacy. The dimensions of information privacy have evolved to also accommodate the evolving context of online interactions. However, the construct still mainly focuses on interactions with vendors in the context of ecommerce (internet storefronts, retail platforms, and mobile commerce). In these interactions, privacy concerns focus on personal factual information shared in the process of online transactions and services.

However, few studies have examined peer-related privacy concerns and included either direct or indirect measures of concerns or perceived risks in the context of peers (Ozdemir et al. 2017)<sup>5</sup>. With a primary focus on information privacy, these studies have mostly focused on self-disclosure in various contexts of online social interaction and have primarily examined risk and benefit antecedents of selfdisclosure (e.g., Jiang et al. 2013; Kordzadeh and Warren 2017; Posey et al. 2010).

## Peer Privacy Concern

## Conceptualization: A Personal Boundary Regulation Perspective

According to Altman (1975), privacy is a dynamic and dialectic boundary regulation process with respect to one’s social interactions and serves two primary goals: interface regulation and interaction regulation. In the offline context, interface regulation refers to the control over information transactions and regulates the bidirectional information flow between the self and one’s environment. In other words, interface regulation controls both personal information about the individual that can be accessed by others and information from the environment that reaches the individual. Interaction regulation refers to the control of cross-boundary activities and includes interactions with the self and interactions with objects within the personal territory of the self (e.g., in one’s home). In other words, it represents the control of personal social interactions and of others’ ability to use ones’ personal belongings or personal space.

In online social communication, users maintain social connections with online peers through information sharing and regular online interactions. To form and protect their (virtual) self-identities, users require privacy to control their personal boundaries. In other words, in online social interactions with peers, privacy aims to achieve both interaction regulation and interface regulation and thus to maintain functional virtual personal boundaries.

The interface regulation of bidirectional online information transactions includes both the dissemination of personal information that is shared online either by the individual or by peers, as well as the information that reaches an individual through the social network (e.g., items shared by one’s connections). As such, online privacy, as a boundary regulation process, achieves control over (1) information dissemination about the self in social networks (i.e., information shared by the focal user and by peers), which entails what personal information about oneself others have access to (which is termed peer-related information privacy), and (2) what information reaches oneself from peers (which is termed psychological privacy).

Interaction regulation refers to the control of online interactions that cross virtual personal boundaries. The virtual personal boundary includes both the self and items that are deemed personal (e.g., personal profile pages and creations).

Drawing on the personal boundary regulation perspective, We define the Peer Privacy Concern (PrPC) as the general feeling of being unable to maintain functional personal boundaries in online activities as a result of the behavior of online peers. PrPC has four dimensions: peer-related information privacy, psychological privacy, virtual territory privacy, and communication privacy, which are summarized in Table 4. The four dimensions present a theory-driven, comprehensive coverage of individual privacy needs in the context of online peer-oriented interactions. PrPC includes three new dimensions (i.e., psychological privacy, virtual territory privacy, and communication privacy) in addition to information privacy. While information privacy remains an important component of PrPC, its focus shifts to the role of peers in the dissemination of personal information.

## Dimensions of the Peer Privacy Concern

## Peer-Related Information Privacy Concern (PIPC)

As possessors of others’ private information, and as secondary dissemination channels, peers in social networks pose significant threats to information privacy (e.g., Debatin et al. 2009). In online social networks, individuals share a broad spectrum of personal information, which, in addition to their demographics, may include more sensitive/subjective information, such as life and work experiences that are shared in the form of pictures and weblogs (Aggarwal et al. 2012), political views (Wade et al. 2020; Wattal et al. 2010), sexual orientation (Acquisti and Gross 2006), and comments on others’ postings (Efimova and De Moor 2005). Although the shared information has a specific intended audience, it is challenging to precisely know who can access the information shared, let alone how to maintain control over how this information is disseminated. The inability to regulate personal sharing “leaks” can lead to serious problems, raising concerns about information privacy.

Peer-related information privacy concerns differ from general information privacy concerns with respect to the platform vendor. Privacy concerns with respect to vendors focus on the collection, storage, and use of personal information that has become available to the vendor during online service interactions. Unlike vendors, online peers are participants, rather than facilitators of online social interactions, and they are equally empowered to share, collect, and disseminate information. Peer-related information privacy concerns not only emanate from private information a person shares themselves (i.e., self-shared information) but also from one’s private information that peers share, either intentionally or unintentionally (i.e., peer-shared information).

Self-Shared Information Privacy Concern (SSIPC): In terms of self-shared information, secondary usage of information by online peers is of primary concern. Users expect their self-shared information to be handled properly by their online peers, and users commonly assume that peers will not use it for other purposes. However, examples of personal sharing misuse are abundant. For example, several years ago, a school superintendent recommended that a teacher be dismissed after the teacher made remarks online that the superintendent (who was her online friend) perceived as racially insensitive.<sup>6</sup> Users also have concerns about secondary usage that are unique to online social networking: namely, unintentional secondary dissemination by authorized online peers (i.e., online friends). For example, the focal user’s sharing of political viewpoints may be unintentionally disseminated beyond that person’s close social circle through online peers liking, reposting, retweeting, or commenting on posts.

Peer-Shared Information Privacy Concern (PSIPC): Online peers can also disclose information about a focal user without consent. In this case, online peers are the source of primary disclosure of one’s private information. For example, one could be tagged by peers in photos or mentioned in peer users’ online postings on platforms such as Instagram and Facebook (e.g., Debatin et al. 2009; Wisniewski et al. 2015). Thus, peer sharing can result in private information leaks or shadow profiles in online social networks. For instance, based on data collected from Friendster, Garcia (2017) showed that it is possible to predict a user’s sexual orientation and relationship status from peers’ sharing. In principle, as “coowners” of private information, peers are responsible for the protection of this information (Petronio 2002). However, it is difficult to assess the commitment of peers to protect others’ privacy and prevent them from posting sensitive personal information online (Chen et al. 2015; Rosenblum 2007). A report by the Pew Research Center showed that 45% of respondents reported removing their names from photos they were tagged on in an effort to manage their privacy. <sup>7</sup> Furthermore, even if information shared by peers is not sensitive, it may be considered inaccurate by the focal user (Shin et al. 2018).

In summary, peers pose a significant threat to user privacy. Based on Westin’s definition (1967), we define the Peer-Related Information Privacy Concern (PIPC) as the control of when, how, by whom, and to what extent information about an individual is communicated to others by online peers. Considering the distinct nature of privacy threats related to peer behavior in the context of self-shared information and peer-shared information about the user, PIPC has two subdimensions. First, the Self-Shared Information Privacy Concern (SSIPC) arises because of unauthorized secondary usage by online peers who have authorized access to information on the focal user (i.e., online connections) and unintentional secondary dissemination by retweeting, commenting, and sharing by the online connections of the focal user. Second, the Peer-Shared Information Privacy Concern (PSIPC) arises from the focal user’s lack of control over peers’ sharing of such focal user’s personal information online, and the lack of control over the accuracy of this peershared personal information.

## Psychological Privacy Concern (PPC)

In social networks, informational interactions are bidirectional. In addition to controlling others’ access to personal information, interface regulation also involves the control of what information one receives from others. Prior literature has commonly omitted this aspect of interface control because information transmission in the e-commerce context is mostly unidirectional. When it is not unidirectional, the types of information received from the vendor are mostly aimed at influencing one’s consumption behavior (e.g., recommendations and targeted advertising). However, the internet is now a prominent information channel. Users are constantly exposed to information shared by socially significant others or recommended by the platform, and their public postings are being judged by an extended social circle. For example, Stella et al. (2018) found that Twitter social bots were used to influence human behavior by increasing exposure to inflammatory and negative narratives. The Facebook and Cambridge Analytica privacy incidents show that opinions are subject to manipulations. Therefore, many people are now aware that their opinions are influenced by their social networks and can be subject to manipulation attempts.

We term this aspect of interface regulation as psychological privacy. Psychological privacy maintains control over what kind of information reaches an individual. By maintaining relative solitude, psychological privacy helps to protect an individual’s psychological independence. This refers to their ability to make decisions and reach conclusions independently, without other people’s thoughts and ideas unduly influencing them, either implicitly or explicitly. Psychological privacy is important because the quality of independent thought, the diversity of views, and voluntary conformity (or nonconformity) are considered desirable traits (Westin 1967).

The need for psychological privacy in the presence of online peers is significant. Information flow through online connections can overwhelm individuals, thus challenging their cognitive capacity (Yates and Paquette 2011). Furthermore, online peers influence focal users by changing their information exposure (e.g., Goes et al. 2014) and by exerting peer pressure and social influence through shared and publicly expressed comments (e.g., Wang et al. 2018). For example, 12% of Facebook users reported that someone has asked them to “unfriend” another user in their friend network.<sup>8</sup> A multitude of information inputs and potentially judgmental comments may compromise both an individual’s psychological independence and well-being. As Bates (1964, p. 432) suggested, when receiving overwhelming information, a person needs a mechanism to “assess the flood of information received, to consider alternatives and possible consequences so that he may then act as consistently and appropriately as possible.” In other words, people require psychological privacy to filter out irrelevant or unwanted information with the goal of remaining free from outside interference or manipulation to formulate attitudes, beliefs, and values and to develop identities, assimilate personal experiences, and self-evaluate. Psychological privacy protects individuality and the awareness of individuals of their choices in life. Because of intentional and unintentional interference from online peers, the loss of control over inbound information thus raises concerns about psychological privacy.

## Communication Privacy Concern (CPC)

Communication privacy focuses on control over when, through which communication channel, and with whom individuals have a direct online conversation. Violations of communication privacy may include forcing an individual into a conversation at an inappropriate place or time or involving people in a group conversation without their prior consent.

Communication privacy is a significant concern in the presence of online peers because of the elevated sense of connectedness that results from the virtual presence of a user. Unlike offline communications, where temporal and spatial discontinuities create natural boundaries, the boundaries of online conversations are vague. They can take place anywhere with an internet connection and at any time, which essentially create the expectation of availability. Users may feel pressured to respond to online communication requests. Furthermore, online platforms, such as LinkedIn and Facebook, implement many virtual communication tools/channels with different capabilities and options. Users may feel compelled to simultaneously engage in multiple online conversations. In certain cases, they may even be unaware of who the participants are in an online group meeting. The augmented expectation for availability, uncertainty about the audience, and the difficulty of simultaneously engaging in multiple conversation channels, among other issues, weaken users’ control over when, how, and with whom online communications take place, and raise concerns about communication privacy.

## Virtual Territory Privacy Concern (VTPC)

Virtual territory privacy, defined as the control over others’ interactions within one’s own virtual space, helps individuals claim ownership over their virtual belongings within their personal territory. People have an innate need to maintain territoriality, i.e., they need to possess a certain space and set aside a part of the environment for themselves (Ardrey 1966; Lorenz and Leyhausen 1973; Porteous 1976; Weil 1952). This need leads to the creation of personal territories in social environments (Ardrey 1966; Duncan 1981; Kron 1983; Porteous 1976). Personal belongings within personal territories are considered private. Territory privacy maintains control over cross-boundary interactions by regulating others’ interactions with personal belongings within these personal territories. The undermining of this control by others—e.g., by visiting a person’s house uninvited, snooping on one’s work desk, or using a person’s clothes without the person’s permission—is considered a violation of territory privacy.

The need for personal territory is also present in a virtual social environment (Karahanna et al. 2018). Online platforms enable the creation of personal virtual properties such as profile pages, blog posts, and digital creations. Users invest effort in the creation and maintenance of digital properties and often experience both psychological ownership and emotional attachment. As such, they feel the need to exercise control over others’ interactions with these digital properties. Unauthorized interactions (e.g., the addition, deletion, or alteration of content) are considered intrusions into their virtual territory, which, as shown by Lin and Armstrong (2017), is of concern to the users of social networking sites. This concern was also evidenced by Jeong and Kim (2017), who found that Facebook users are concerned about online peers posting on their timeline. The prospect of such virtual territory intrusions raises concerns about virtual territory privacy.

It is worth noting that most online platforms emphasize supporting the ownership of user-generated digital content technically. Users are guaranteed to have sole control over the creation, deletion, and alteration of their digital belongings (e.g., their profiles). However, the interactive nature of many online platforms mandates that online peers interact with the user’s virtual properties. For example, peers may post comments on a user’s profile page or tag a picture that has been shared online. These capabilities to interact with others digital properties may be exploited. For example, peers may use friends’ profile pages as an advertising venue and post things that are not relevant to the owner (which would be akin to posting an advertisement on the lawn of someone else’s house). Thus, the Virtual Territory Privacy Concern is an important component of PrPC.

## Peer Privacy Concern: A Multidimensional Construct

In summary, the four-dimensional conceptualization of PrPC is derived from a comprehensive discussion of the personal boundary control view and extends original privacy conceptualizations in the psychology literature into the online context. Peer-related information privacy and psychological privacy both enable the regulation of the information interface (input and output), while virtual territory privacy and communication privacy help individuals regulate others’ interactions with the self and personal belongings. PrPC manifests in all dimensions and represents the general feeling of being unable to maintain functional personal boundaries in online activities as a result of the behavior of online peers.

In this study, PrPC is conceptualized as a second-order reflective construct. A reflectively measured construct is one where the construct causes variance in its reflective indicators (e.g., Cenfetelli and Bassellier 2009). According to the theoretical definition of PrPC as applied in the present study, a common theme for the concept is personal boundary control. Consistent with this theoretical conceptualization, PrPC represents shared variance across its subdimensions, which reflects concerns over boundary control emanating from peer behaviors. In other words, concerns over losing personal boundary control because of peer behavior manifest in the subdimensions of PrPC. Consistent with a reflective conceptualization, we expect the four subdimensions to be highly correlated with each other. Figure 1 illustrates the structure of PrPC.

In empirical studies, measures of privacy commonly focus on the cognition and perception of privacy control rather than on assessments of privacy (Smith et al. 2011). The present study follows this practice and proposes items that measure privacy concerns as a proxy for privacy cognition.

## Generate Items that Represent the Construct

To create the item list, we used Facebook, which has over 1.7 billion daily active users, as the empirical context. Using Facebook as a target online platform allowed us to capture a broad set of interpersonal interactions among peers that can infringe upon one’s privacy. The developed items can be easily customized to other contexts, such as Twitter, Instagram, TikTok, or e-commerce environments featuring deep social interactions (e.g., Airbnb and Kickstarter).

As suggested by MacKenzie et al (2011), we derived our initial list of items based on (1) our theoretical descriptions of PrPC and its dimensions, (2) several focus groups with Facebook users, and (3) an extensive survey of existing privacy concerns scales in the IS literature, which we reworded to fit the context of peer interactions in online social networks. The latter provided us with only a few candidate items as many of the reworded items from extant scales do not make sense for peer-related privacy concerns, and the new dimensions of PrPC are not covered by the reworded items (see Appendix B, Table B2). A few illustrative examples of how the initial item list was derived are presented below:

The Psychological Privacy Concern focuses on maintaining independence from the influence of others. Based on this definition, we included an item “I am concerned that my opinions are unduly influenced by postings from my Facebook friends.”

The Peer-Related Information Privacy Concern, with respect to self-shared information, focuses on unintentional secondary dissemination by authorized online peers. Participants in our focus groups expressed concerns about their posts being forwarded to peers who are not their direct connections. We thus included the item “I am concerned that my Facebook peers can unintentionally (through their “like”, “tagging”, and “sharing” activities) expose pictures I post to people who are not in my “Friends” circle.”

The Peer-Related Information Privacy Concern, with respect to other-shared information, touches upon the accuracy of private information shared by peers. Given that the IUIPC scale (Malhotra et al. 2004) contains items that measure concerns about accuracy, we modified these scale items to our context (e.g., “I am concerned that my Facebook friends may post information about me on Facebook that is not correct”).

## Assessing the Content Validity of Items

The objective of this step was to ensure that the items that measure each dimension correspond to the conceptual definition of the dimension. We conducted a qualitative assessment of the content validity of the initial item pool by discussing the items in face-to-face unstructured interviews with a convenience sample consisting of three researchers whose work focuses on social media privacy and seven Facebook users.

Based on their feedback, several items were revised and new items were created. The resulting pool of 40 items included nine items regarding virtual territory privacy concerns, six items regarding communication privacy concerns, 20 items regarding the two dimensions of peer-related information privacy concerns, and five items regarding psychological privacy concerns.

We next conducted two rounds of item sorting, in which participants received the items and definitions of the dimensions and were asked to sort each item under the dimension they believed it represented. The objective was to (1) qualitatively assess the convergent and discriminant validity of the scales, (2) to identify items with ambiguous meaning, and (3) assess whether each dimension was represented in our items. Based on the results of the first round of item sorting, which involved nine doctoral students, we revised items that were identified as confusing and items that were consistently misclassified. This resulted in the removal of nine items. The second round of item sorting involved another nine doctoral students and showed strong agreement on correct item classification (the average Cohen’s Kappa was 0.88 across all dimensions). After deleting a number of redundant items, we kept 33 items in the item pool.

## Pilot Study

We next conducted a pilot study, with 70 Facebook users recruited from a university in Hong Kong. The aim was to refine the scales by trimming the item pool, change item wording as necessary to remove confusing or ambiguous phrasing, and conduct an initial quantitative assessment of reliability and validity. We paid each participant 50 HKD. We assessed convergent and discriminant validity using exploratory factor analysis and reliability using Cronbach’s alpha (Netemeyer et al. 2003). This resulted in additional item modifications.

After deleting a few problematic items, the Cronbach’s alpha for each dimension and subdimension ranged from 0.91 to 0.94, indicating good reliability. The average variance extracted (AVE) for each dimension ranged from 0.53 to 0.81, indicating adequate convergent validity. Self-loadings were significant and higher than cross-loadings, showing adequate discriminant validity. We deleted some additional redundant items and kept 21 items in the item pool. The resulting item list that was used for the main data collection is shown in Appendix B, Table B1.

## Scale Purification and Refinement

To further refine our scales (MacKenzie et al. 2011; Netemeyer et al., 2003), we collected another dataset from a Hong Kong university using online questionnaires. We carried out the data collection in two waves (Podsakoff et al. 2003). The first questionnaire was used to collect data on the independent variables, i.e., the new PrPC scale and relevant control variables (i.e., misrepresentation of identity and privacy violation experiences (Malhotra et al. 2004)). Participants who completed and provided valid responses in the first questionnaire were invited to complete a second questionnaire two weeks later. The second questionnaire captured variables that are commonly included in information privacy studies (specifically, risk belief [Hong and Thong 2013; Malhotra et al. 2004], as well as age, gender, and Facebook experience [Malhotra et al. 2004]). A total of 355 out of 409 students completed both questionnaires. No significant differences were found in construct mean scores between participants who did not complete the second survey and those who did. We assessed scale validity using the steps for scale purification and refinement described in MacKenzie et al. (2011), which led us to drop one item.

## Gathering Data from a New Sample and Reexamining Scale Properties

We next conducted another study with an alternative sample to affirm scale properties and the generalizability of the scale to other samples (MacKenzie et al. 2011). Following the procedure suggested above, we collected data in two waves using Amazon Mechanical Turk (Podsakoff et al. 2003).<sup>9</sup> The first questionnaire collected data on independent variables. Participants who completed and provided valid responses to the first questionnaire received 4 USD and were invited to complete a second questionnaire two weeks later. The second questionnaire collected data on dependent variables. Participants who completed and provided valid responses to the second questionnaire received 6 USD to encourage participation in both studies. A total of 272 individuals signed up for the survey; 268 completed the first questionnaire and 232 completed the second questionnaire. We found no significant differences in construct mean scores between participants who did not complete the second survey and those who did. The response rate across both waves was 85.3% (44.0% females; average age = 38.91 [range, 20-72 years]; average Facebook experience 9.13 years [range, 3-12years]).

Since these are our final scale items, we present the scale validation results of this study as evidence of scale validity. We used covariance-based structural equation modeling in AMOS (version 24) to assess psychometric properties. Following the scale purification and refinement guidelines of MacKenzie et al. (2011), we evaluated the goodness of fit of the measurement model, assessed the validity and reliability of the set of indicators at the construct level, and evaluated individual indicator validity and reliability. Specifically, we assessed the following items: (1) the measurement model to examine the reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity of each of the dimensions of PrPC disaggregated, (2) the proposed higher-order factor structure of PrPC, and (3) the proposed higher-order factor structure of PIPC.

## Measurement Model for PrPC Disaggregated

We conducted a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to assess reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity (Hair et al., 2012; McKenzie et al., 2011; Netemeyer et al., 2003). The proposed scales exhibit good reliability (all exceeding the 0.707 threshold—Hair et al. 2011) and achieved good convergent validity (all AVEs exceeding 0.5—Fornell and Larcker 1981) (see Table $\begin{array} { r } { 6 ; } \end{array}$ fit indexes shown in Table 8). Support for discriminant validity is provided by the fact that (1) all items loaded higher on their respective constructs than on other constructs, and the crossloading differences far exceeded the suggested threshold of 0.1 (Gefen and Straub 2005), and (2) the square root of the AVEs exceeded the intercorrelations between constructs (see Table 7). To further assess discriminant validity, we used the pairwise chi-square difference test method (e.g., Anderson and Gerbing 1988; Wright et al. 2012), where we created a model with two (three) dimensions and ran a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) where these two (three) dimensions were allowed to freely covary. Next, we constrained their covariance to 1 and evaluated the change in $\chi ^ { 2 } .$ A significant decline in model fit for the constrained model provides evidence for discriminant validity. We repeated this process for all possible combinations of two (three) dimensions. The results, summarized in Table 8, show that all changes of $\chi ^ { 2 }$ are significant, providing evidence for discriminant validity.

It should be noted that, although distinct, the correlation between the Virtual Territory Privacy Concern and the Peer-Shared Information Privacy Concern is high. This may originate from the fact that the two dimensions are indeed highly correlated. Users who are concerned about others’ inappropriate conduct regarding their personal information also likely worry about intrusion into their personal space. Meanwhile, although both dimensions are conceptually distinct, their item wording has similarities as both concern other users’ posting and editing behavior. This is not a major concern, given that PrPC is reflectively measured by these dimensions and, thus, the dimensions are expected to covary. High correlations between firstorder privacy dimensions have been commonly observed with previous privacy measurement scales (e.g., Hong and Thong 2013; Malhotra et al. 2004).

## Assessing the Higher-Order Factor Structure of PrPC

We then empirically evaluated the proposed higher-order factor structures. We first examined the structure of the Peer-Related Information Privacy Concern (PIPC) dimension to assess the validity of having PIPC as a higher-order construct rather than the two subdimensions only. We then assessed the validity of the multidimensional structure of PrPC to assess the validity of this higher-order factor. Following the literature (e.g., Hong and Thong 2013; Stewart and Segars 2002), we compared the model fit of the proposed theoretical higher-order model to the model fit of a disaggregated model. Table 9 shows that the models are comparable on fit indices that compare each model with a null model (e.g., CFI, GFI) and on fit indices focusing on discrepancy functions (e.g., relative chi-square and RMSEA). Their goodness-of-fit indices (such as AIC) differ; however, theoretically, a higher-order model cannot improve in terms of goodness of fit compared with a disaggregated model because the higher-order model explains all covariance among the first-order factors with fewer parameters (Marsh and Hocevar 1985). Marsh and Hocevar suggested calculating the T-value, which is a ratio between the model fit $( \chi ^ { 2 } )$ of the proposed higher-order model and that of the disaggregated model. This practice has been implemented by Stewart and Segars (2002) for validating CFIP and by Hong and Thong (2013) for validating IPC. A value of 0.90 or above suggests that the higher-order factor provides a good explanation for correlations among lowerorder factors (Marsh and Hocevar 1985), which is supportive of the higher-order factor structure. Table 9 shows the fit statistics for each of the three models involved in this assessment, as shown in Figure 2.

(1) Factor Structure of the Peer-Related Information Privacy Concern Dimension: Figure 2 shows the proposed higher-order factor structure for the PIPC dimension (Model 1b) and the benchmark disaggregated model (Model 1a). The T-value of 0.981 (T = 227.601/232.102, see Table 9) identifies PIPC as the proposed higher-order factor.

(2) Factor Structure of the Peer Privacy Concern: Having established the factor structure for PIPC, we next validated the proposed higher-order structure of the PrPC construct. The Tvalue of 0.962 (T = 232.102/241.351; comparison between the PrPC higher-order model [Model 1c] and the benchmark disaggregated model [Model 1b] in Figure 2), supports the appropriateness of the proposed higher-order factor structure of PrPC. Table 10 shows the factor loadings of the PrPC dimensions. We provide additional support for the higherorder structure of PrPC in cross-validation study 2 below.

Nomological validity (also, concurrent validity) is defined as “the degree to which predictions from a formal theoretical network containing the concept under scrutiny are confirmed” (Bearden et al. 1993, p. 5). To validate the newly proposed PrPC construct as well as the scales, we conducted two tests. First, we developed a unidimensional general privacy concern with respect to peers (GPCP) scale based on the unidimensional general privacy concern scale used by Smith et al. (1996) and Malhotra et al. (2004) to assess concurrent validity (see Table B1). GPCP provides a summary measure for privacy concerns regarding peer interactions in online social networks. If PrPC captures peer privacy concerns well it should correlate highly with the unidimensional GPCP scale, thus indicating good concurrent validity (Meng et al. 1992, Malhotra et al. 2004). The high correlation between PrPC and GPCP of 0.775 (p < 0.001) suggests strong concurrent validity.

Second, we empirically estimated a structural model based on a tested theory in the information privacy literature. Following Hong and Thong (2013) and Malhotra et al. (2004), we examined the relationships between PrPC and risk belief regarding online activities with Facebook peers. Based on prior research, we expected PrPC to have a positive relationship with risk belief. The model also includes several controls that may impact risk belief. Previous studies have suggested that demographic characteristics and personal experiences can influence people’s privacy concerns (Dinev et al. 2015; Hong and Thong 2013; Malhotra et al. 2004; Smith et al. 2011). Therefore, the following were included as controls: (1) the two demographic characteristics of age (Culnan 1995; Milne and Rohm 2000; Wang and Petrison 1993) and gender (Milne and Rohm 2000), as well as (2) the following three variables related to personal experience: internet experience (Milne and Rohm 2000; Phelps et al. 2000), whether the subject’s privacy had been invaded in the past (Culnan 2000), and how often subjects provide falsified identity information (Hoffman et al. 1999; Pew Internet Project 2000). Results are shown in Figure 3 (see Appendix C for descriptive statistics for control variables). The fit indices shown in Figure 3 indicate good fit with the data and, as hypothesized, the path coefficient in the model from PrPC to risk belief (0.506) was found to be significant. Hence, we conclude that PrPC exhibits good nomological validity.

## Scale Cross-Validation

The final step was the cross-validation of the scale using new samples. We collected data from two new samples from Amazon Mechanical Turk to cross-validate the psychometric properties of PrPC and to provide additional evidence of nomological validity. In these two studies, we tested PrPC in a nomological network derived from Jiang et al. (2013), who proposed that privacy concerns mediate the relationship between perceived intrusiveness and selfdisclosure. As in prior research (e.g., Hong and Thong 2013; Ozdemir et al. 2017), we used an abridged version of the model that included the constructs relevant to our context. In addition to assessing nomological validity, we used the two validation studies to provide evidence of the performance of the PrPC scale vis-à-vis (1) existing scales reworded in the context of peers, and (2) existing scales that capture privacy concerns with respect to vendors. The measurement models and the test details of these two studies are presented in Appendices D and E.

(1) PrPC vis-à-vis Existing Scales Reworded in the Context of Peers: In Study 1 (N=231), we first compared the PrPC scale to the Internet Privacy Concern (IPC) scale (Hong and Thong 2013) with modified wording to fit the peer-oriented interaction context. The objective of this comparison was to show that simply changing the wording of previous privacy concern scales fails to fully capture privacy concerns in an online social networking context. The results of the nomological test, as shown in Figure 4, suggest that, compared with IPC, the PrPC model has a better model fit, as indicated by the significant difference between the chisquared values of both models $( \chi ^ { 2 } ( 3 ) = 1 6 8 . 1 2 4$ , p < 0.001). While the PrPC model explains more variance in self-disclosure than the IPC model (26.2% vs. 21.8%), a Chow (1960) test shows that the path coefficient between PrPC and self-disclosure did not significantly differ from the path coefficient between IPC and self-disclosure (F (2, 458) = 0.170, p = 0.843).<sup>10</sup>

(2) PrPC vis-à-vis Existing Privacy Concern Scales with Respect to Vendors: Study 2 (N=186) examines the additional predictive power of PrPC beyond IPC with respect to the vendor, which is the focus of extant scales. Figure 5 and Table 11 report the comparison between the following four models: Model 1 includes controls only, Model 2 includes controls and IPC, Model 3 includes controls and PrPC, and Model 4 is the full model with both PrPC and vendor IPC. We first note that perceived intrusiveness only exerts a significant effect on PrPC but not on IPC with respect to vendors. Second, although both exert significant effects on self-disclosure, the effect of PrPC is significantly higher than the effect of IPC (path coefficients: -0.233 vs. -0.145, significant path coefficient difference based on Chow’s test: F (2, 368) = 36.389, p < 0.001), indicating the importance of capturing privacy concerns with respect to peers. PrPC and IPC together, with respect to vendors, generate higher predictive power than either one alone (10.3% and 6.6% each, respectively, versus 10.4% jointly), indicating that they capture distinct privacy concerns.

## Limitations

We conducted multiple rounds of data collection to assess our scale and we believe it offers a solid foundation for future research. However, as with all studies, our study has limitations that call for future work. First, our scale development focused on Facebook. However, the items could be easily adapted to other contexts (e.g., Twitter, Instagram, blogs) where online social interactions with peers are prominent. Second, although we created temporal separation between our independent and dependent variables in the nomological network by collecting two waves of data (as recommended by Podsakoff et al. 2003), we have not ruled out all common method bias concerns. We encourage future studies to further alleviate such concerns by collecting measures of each dimension using different approaches.

## Discussion and Contributions

Ample evidence indicates that online peers (e.g., in online social networks, sharing economy platforms,) cause privacy concerns and that peer privacy violations have consequences for online users. The integration of e-commerce and online social networks has also added to the significance of such peer-related privacy issues. However, research on peer-related privacy is still limited. We believe that a significant obstacle for the development of research in this area lies in the absence of a proper theory-driven conceptualization of PrPC and a validated measurement scale. Existing internet privacy conceptualizations and scales primarily focus on vendors and on self-shared information that facilitates online transactions. The few available studies that utilize peer-related privacy concerns scales reword these scales to focus on peers without rethinking how privacy concerns regarding peers may differ.

Drawing on the personal boundary regulation theory of privacy (Altman 1975), we propose a theoretically grounded multidimensional conceptualization and definition of PrPC. Our conceptualization of PrPC expands the domain of online privacy concerns to also consider the multifaceted and complex nature of online peer interactions as follows. First, both the creation and maintenance of the self-concept in an online social environment require comprehensive privacy control beyond regulating the dissemination and use of personal posts. Second, virtual presence involves both digital accounts and digital personal belongings. Setting personal boundaries can help to protect these virtual personal belongings and assert control over digital communications. Third, peer-related concerns related to information privacy are different from vendor-related information privacy, which has been the focus of prior work. Simply contextualizing existing measurement scales results in less content-valid scales than scales designed to fit the information privacy needs of online peer interactions. Fourth, social networking platforms are essential information channels for information access. As such, control over the inflow of information (i.e., psychological privacy) is a critical component of online peer privacy because it enables independent thoughts, the diversity of views, and voluntary conformity (or nonconformity). Our conceptualization of PrPC incorporates all of these.

We suggest that peer privacy concerns manifest in four dimensions: peer-related information privacy concerns, psychological privacy concerns, communication privacy concerns, and virtual territory privacy concerns. While these dimensions are correlated, they use distinctive mechanisms to signal what state of privacy exists and what strategies should be used to restore privacy or demand more privacy. Our PrPC scale complements existing scales of information privacy concerns with respect to vendors and demonstrates its utility for comprehensively capturing the totality of online privacy concerns that span both vendors (via existing scales) and peers (via PrPC).

Our scale development work provides important contributions to the IS literature on internet privacy. First, we expand the conceptualization of internet privacy from its earlier focus on vendors’ use of personal factual information to peers’ behavior with respect to all manner of self-shared and peer-shared information. This reconceptualization and distinction clarify discussions about privacy issues that confound the two, and call for more privacy research focusing on peer interactions and online social networks. For example, privacy calculus models (e.g., Dinev and Hart 2006; Kehr et al. 2015) can be extended to examine the influence of online peers, as well as the characteristics of one’s social network, such as tie strength, density, network commonality (Choi et al. 2015), and can clarify how these influence and are influenced by PrPC.

Second, the new conceptualization of PrPC offers comprehensive coverage of privacy needs in peer-oriented online interactions. In addition to issues related to how online peers deal with personal information that is shared online (which aligns conceptually with information privacy concerns in the literature), PrPC includes concerns related to whether personal virtual territory rights are respected, the management of direct online communications, and the freedom to express and form opinions, all of which are critical for maintaining effective personal boundaries online. As our cross-validations illustrate, the multidimensional structure of PrPC offers a stronger explanation of peerrelated privacy issues in online environments. It opens up novel frontiers for privacy research regarding the control of virtual interactions, the constraints of free expression, and the management of the virtual self. Such dimensions of PrPC can be studied separately and, consequently, researchers can examine antecedents and consequences of specific subdimensions (e.g., PIPC with respect to peershared information) that may be of interest in their specific research setting.

Third, this paper develops a reliable and valid scale for PrPC and demonstrates its nomological validity. While our empirical study focuses on Facebook, the scales can be easily adapted for research on other online social networking services. Given that the development and validation of scales for new constructs spearheads research in a domain and contributes to the accumulation of knowledge, we hope that our scale helps to advance the current understanding of the nature, causes, consequences, and mitigation of privacy issues emanating from the behavior of peers. We hope that the development of PrPC will encourage researchers to develop novel theoretical frameworks of the antecedents and consequences of PrPC, such as new theories with different sets of outcome variables, antecedents, and mediators than those offered by existing privacy research.

Our work also has a number of practical implications. Social network service providers, as well as other online providers that integrate social network functions, can use our conceptualization to discuss, develop, and provide features that address the different aspects of users’ privacy concerns on their platforms. Since such user privacy concerns are multidimensional, it is unlikely that any single feature could fulfill all privacy protection requirements. Our study also reminds online vendors that peer-related privacy concerns are critical and that they differ from vendor-related concerns. Effective privacy policies and protection mechanisms should therefore address the behavior of online peers regarding privacy issues and should enable users to better manage their online social networks. The PrPC conceptualization provides a comprehensive map of areas that require privacy protection functionality or policy. Finally, social platform providers can use these new PrPC scales to evaluate the peer privacy concerns of their users. With an improved conceptual and quantitative means of capturing users’ privacy concerns, platform providers and governments can implement fine-tuned policies, mechanisms, and tools that target specific dimensions of privacy threats, thus creating a more desirable online social environment.

## Literature Review

We conducted a comprehensive review of articles on privacy that were published in the eight journals included in the Senior Scholars’ Basket (AIS, 2016) between 2000 and 2019. We also reviewed other related journals and conference proceedings. Most of the scales used by previous studies were designed to measure privacy concerns with respect to vendors rather than peers, focused on information privacy, and were conducted in the context of e-commerce. Of the 71 articles we found that addressed individual privacy concerns, only 11 included direct or indirect measures of concerns or perceived risks associated with peers. These are shown in Table A1. As shown in Table A1, with the exception of Lin and Armstrong (2019), the measures of privacy concerns with respect to peers focus solely on information privacy and adap existing scales that have been developed to capture privacy concerns with respect to peers.
