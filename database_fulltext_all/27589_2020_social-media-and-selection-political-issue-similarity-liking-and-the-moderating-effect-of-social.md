# SOCIAL MEDIA AND SELECTION: POLITICAL ISSUE SIMILARITY, LIKING, AND THE MODERATING EFFECT OF SOCIAL MEDIA PLATFORM<sup>1</sup>

Julie T. Wade Johnson College of Business and Economics, University of South Carolina Upstate, Spartanburg, SC 29306 U.S.A. {jtwade@uscupstate.edu}

Philip L. Roth Management Department, College of Business, Clemson University, Clemson, SC 29603 U.S.A. {rothp@clemson.edu}

Jason Bennett Thatcher Fox School of Business, Temple University, Philadelphia, PA 19122 U.S.A. {jason.thatcher@temple.edu}

Michael Dinger Johnson College of Business and Economics, University of South Carolina Upstate, Spartanburg, SC 29306 U.S.A. {mdinger@uscupstate.edu}

In this work, we investigate how social media has changed hiring processes, an important internal activity of organizations. Specifically, we probe how viewing job-relevant and job-irrelevant social media content influences hiring managers’ ratings of job applicants. To do so, we conducted an experiment that manipulated the presence of social media content on political issues and job-relevant information as well as the social media platforms on which they appear. We balanced job-relevant and job-irrelevant content because we were interested in assessing whether information about political issues continued to have effects even in the presence of information relating to a job applicant’s knowledge, skills, and abilities. We found that social media posts that convey information about political issues do have effects, even in the presence of job-relevant information. We also found that, for some issues, the source of social media content matters, with platform effects impacting the assessment of job applicants. This work has timely implications, suggesting that managers be made aware that both social media content and the platform on which it is viewed can contaminate hiring processes. We suggest a need for future research at the intersection between social media and hiring policies.

Keywords: Social media, social media platform, political affiliation, cybervetting, hireability

## Introduction

Social media has radically changed how firms gather information and interact with their environments. Information systems (IS) research has extensively examined how social media can enhance traditional business processes like customer service activities (Gunarathne et al. 2018) and predict or enhance firm value (Luo and Zhang 2013; Luo et al. 2013). It has also considered the impact of social media on a broader, societal scale—for example, the use of social media to organize collective action for prosocial or political purposes (Selander and Jarvenpaa 2016; Vaast et al. 2017). These studies suggest that social media tools and access to social media content are changing how firms make decisions and how people interact with one another.

Whereas most studies focus on the positive impact of social media on firm decisions and strategies (Heavey et al. 2020), we direct attention to how accessing social media content may negatively impact an important firm process: hiring new employees. Evidence suggests that hiring managers use social media to access personal aspects of job applicants’ lives. A survey of 600 hiring managers revealed that most hiring managers screen job applicants’ social media accounts, with 85% using Facebook and 66% checking LinkedIn (Hill 2018). Such social media assessments can have a powerful impact on hiring—a study of 2000 hiring managers in the United Kingdom reported that one in five eliminated applicants based on social media content (Smith 2017).

Using social media content to assess job applicants departs from long-standing practices in selection and hiring, which have traditionally focused on job-relevant information. To minimize discrimination, the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission has long advised firms to collect and view only information relevant to determining applicant qualifications such as job-relevant experiences and credentials (like skills and abilities) and to ignore job-irrelevant information, such as demographic variables (e.g., gender, see Koch et al. 2015) and ethnicity (King et al. 2006).<sup>2</sup> However, social media assessments expose hiring managers to new forms of job-irrelevant information, such as applicants’ political views, values, and beliefs (which may be considered to be types of deep-level diversity) (Smith 2017). As such, these social media assessments can contaminate selection processes in ways that differ from traditional demographic biases (Marlowe et al. 1996; Ziegert and Hanges 2005).

Exposing hiring managers to job-irrelevant, political social media content may be problematic for organizations, particularly those committed to equitable hiring practices, because it may introduce bias based on factors related to values, attitudes, and beliefs (e.g., political views) into the selection of new employees. For example, one high-level executive noted, “Even if I really liked a job applicant, the current administration is so opposed to my own beliefs that I don’t think I could take a Trump-supporting job applicant seriously, even if they acted civilly” (Buscaglia 2017). The tendency to negatively evaluate applicants with opposing views may be becoming even more problematic because deep political divisions in our society appear to be growing, as recently indicated by polarized political dialogue in the United States, extensive civil unrest in Hong Kong and India, growing antiimmigrant parties in Germany and Italy, and rioting in France over public policies (e.g., Dalton and Kostov 2019; Huddy et al. 2015; Purohit 2019).

Understanding how exposure to social media content influences hiring managers is further complicated by the presence of social media platforms, with varying platform designs, intended uses, and user bases. Given that users engage with and present themselves in unique ways on different platforms (van Dijck 2013), information found on one social media platform may influence hiring managers differently than information found on other platforms. Although research generally recognizes distinctions and dichotomies between major social media platforms, such as Facebook and LinkedIn (see Caers and Castelyns 2011; Chang et al. 2017; van Dijck 2013), little is known about how information on different social media platforms may moderate the impact of social media content on hiring managers.

Examining how exposure to social media—in terms of access to political information—influences hiring managers’ beliefs about job applicants is a timely and relevant topic for IS research, particularly because social media assessments represent an IT-induced sociotechnical change (e.g., the introduction and use of social media) in a core firm process: namely, hiring new employees (Sarker et al. 2019). Hence, this study investigates

(1) How does viewing job applicants’ posts about political issues influence hiring managers’ hireability evaluations?

## (2) How does the specific platform on which the content is viewed influence hiring managers’ evaluations?

We root our investigation of social media and hiring in the similarity-attraction paradigm (Byrne 1971). This theoretical perspective helps to explain how hiring managers react to viewing applicants’ social media activity. We contextualize this explanation by examining how the specific social media platform shapes hiring managers’ reactions to applicants. In this way, we seek to explain how not only the content on social media platforms, but also the source of information influences hiring managers’ assessments of applicants. By doing so, we are better able to offer guidance for hiring managers (and organizations) on how to manage potential biases introduced to selection processes by social media assessments. To answer our research questions, we conducted an experiment that manipulated job-irrelevant political and job-relevant ability-related content on social media.

This research makes three major contributions to our understanding of social media’s impact on people and organizations—a topic of particular interest in the IS literature (Chen et al. 2012; Dewan and Ramaprasad 2014; Kane et al. 2014; Oh et al. 2013). First, we contribute to the IS literature by integrating social media research with the similarity-attraction paradigm to demonstrate how the use of social media in hiring processes can contaminate hiring managers’ perceptions and create new ways of forming biases for or against job appli cants. This contribution suggests that social media use by managers may lead to suboptimal employment decisions, such as liking less qualified applicants or disliking more qualified applicants, and it directs IS research toward the role of social media usage in organizational decision-making. Second, we contribute to organizational behavior/human resources (OB/HR) literature by demonstrating that social media has distinct impacts compared to traditional hiring tools. Jobirrelevant information found on social media platforms impacts hiring manager perceptions even in the presence of job-relevant information, an effect not often found in the traditional hiring literature (McCarthy et al. 2010; Olian et al. 1988). Third, we contribute to IS and OB/HR research by expanding on the similarity-attraction paradigm (Byrne 1971; Roth et al. 2016) to demonstrate the moderating impact of the information source. We show that the influence of jobrelevant/irrelevant information is moderated by the social media platform on which it appears, and we offer a theoretical explanation suggesting that unexpected information attracts greater attention which strengthens its impact on hiring manager perceptions. In this way, we also make a general contribution to IS research and theories of information processing by showing how unexpected information on an IT platform may draw more attention from users and magnify impacts on decision-making. Together, these contributions demonstrate that information found on social media platforms can impact hiring managers’ perceptions and suggest a need for future research on how social media content may influence additional organizational processes, decisions, and performance outcomes.

## Social Media and Selection

Social media is an umbrella term used “to describe digital social software that facilitates information sharing, usergenerated content, and collaboration across people” (McFarland and Ployhart 2015, p. 1654). Viewing social media content likely impacts hiring managers through evoking feelings of similarity and liking, which affect perceived hireability. When hiring managers share job applicants’ views, reports from practice suggest that they rate them as a better fit with organizational culture and more likely to engage in organizational citizenship behaviors (Fisher 2016; McGregor

2016). Evidence implies that hiring managers who view job applicants’ political posts consider them to be more assertive, argumentative, disruptive, and willing to engage in counterproductive workplace behaviors (Bercovici 2014; Kassing and Avtgis 1999). While practice provides mixed evidence of social media’s impact (Forbes 2018; Sedghi 2014; Živkoviæ 2018), we lack a rich theoretical explanation for how viewing such social media content impacts assessments of job applicants (Roth et al. 2016).

Social media has expanded hiring managers’ access to jobrelevant and job-irrelevant information. Social media assessments involve hiring managers reviewing applicants’ social media posts on platforms such as Facebook or LinkedIn, ostensibly to find information to assess applicants; however, this practice often also results in exposure to job-irrelevant content (e.g., political affiliation or religion). For example, Zhang et al. (2020) analyzed profiles of graduating college seniors. They found that while 57% of the profiles included work experience, the profiles also included job-irrelevant information on students’ nationalities (73%), marital status (71%), age (64%), sexual orientation (64%), religion (46%), and political affiliation (56%). Additionally, some profiles (27%) displayed negative behaviors, such as drug/alcohol use or vulgar language. Although social media assessments afford hiring managers opportunities to view job-relevant information, they increase the potential for hiring managers to view job-irrelevant information that may shape selection decisions. Job-relevant and job-irrelevant information revealed by social media assessments may evoke a mix of cognitive and affective responses in hiring managers. Cognitive responses imply controlled and intentional assessments of an applicant’s social media content, whereas affective responses are often quick and emotional (Edwards 1990; Forgas 2001). Traditional selection practices encourage cognitive, rational assessments of information by evaluating applicants’ qualifications and suitability based on job-relevant individuating information. Types of individuating information include personality factors (Caldwell and Burger 1998), cognitive ability (Le et al. 2007), and prior performance ratings (Landy and Farr 1980). Social media assessments introduce more “types” of job-irrelevant information, such as politics, religion, and social activities, that are likely to evoke affective responses (Zhang et al. 2020). Considering affective responses to content is important because research has found that cognitive judgments are significantly impacted by affective factors and that affectivebased attitudes are often stronger than those based on cognition (Edwards 1990).

By exposing hiring managers to job-relevant individuating information and job-irrelevant information, social media assessments break from decades of sound selection research and practice that focus on integrating (Le et al. 2007), excluding, or controlling for job-irrelevant information (Ployhart and Holtz 2008). Selection research has established that by structuring (standardizing) the topic, questions, and rating scales, human resource processes maximize the impact of jobrelevant information and minimize the contaminating role of extraneous, job-irrelevant information on assessments (Campion et al. 1997; Huffcutt and Roth 1998). However, most organizations lack policies that structure the gathering and integration of social media information into applicant assessments (SHRM 2016; Van Iddekinge et al. 2016). Researchers have observed that virtually all hiring managers analyze social media in an unstructured manner (Van Iddekinge et al. 2016). Given the lack of structure of social media assessments, it seems possible that hiring managers’ affective responses to job-irrelevant social media posts influence their evaluations of job applicants, thus threatening to disrupt the carefully constructed equitable hiring processes employed by many firms (Petty and Cacioppo 1986, 2012).

## Explaining the Influence of Job-Irrelevant Social Media Information

Similarity theories shed light on why viewing job-irrelevant social media posts may introduce bias for or against job applicants. The similarity-attraction paradigm suggests that when hiring managers perceive similarity with job applicants, they exhibit a positive affective response to the applicant, or, in other words, “liking” (e.g., Byrne 1971; Goldberg 2005). This paradigm suggests that people mentally organize themselves and others into categories based on cues elicited from messages such as physical appearance (e.g., gender or ethnicity), socialization (e.g., education or religion), or other variables (Harrison et al. 1998; Sacco et al. 2003). That is, people categorize others into in-groups and out-groups (see also Goldberg 2005; Quattrone and Jones 1980). Such mental organizing tends to reinforce one’s identity, resulting in people preferring those with whom they perceive similarity (Reskin et al. 1999; Tajfel 1981).

By expanding the range of available job-irrelevant information, social media assessments have changed the hiring context (Johns 2006) and expanded the likelihood that hiring managers categorize applicants based on in-group and outgroup preferences (Goldberg 2005). For example, a study of college professors, based partially on blogs, revealed (stated) intentions to discriminate against individuals of different political values when hiring and reviewing their work (Yancey 2011). These impacts may be especially strong given unstructured exposure to deeper types of diversity (such as political affiliation and the values it connotes) that may be charged with negative affect (Drouin et al. 2015).

In addition to the range of information, social media assessments can change with the source of information. Selection research focuses attention on information from sources designed to predict job performance such as objective tests, panel interviews, and resumes (Gatewood et al. 2016). Most social media assessments use sources that are not designed by an organization to predict job performance but are designed to project hedonic or utilitarian purposes (e.g., selfpromotion), which are well-known drivers of technology use (e.g., Lin and Bhattacherjee 2010; van der Heijden 2004). Accordingly, hiring managers may form expectations about the content that users post to a social media platform. Personal content found on a more hedonic platform (i.e., Facebook) where managers might expect to find job-irrelevant information, such as political opinions, may impact perceptions of similarity differently than comparable content from utilitarian platforms (i.e., LinkedIn) designed to support online labor markets.

## Political Affiliation, Social Media, and Deep-Level Diversity

By making applicants’ job-irrelevant information, such as political affiliation, available to hiring managers, social media meaningfully changes the hiring context (see Johns 2006), and social media often makes applicants’ political affiliation far more transparent than many other attributes (Zhang et al. 2020). It is important to consider this change in available information because, over the last four decades (Iyengar et al. 2012), members of political parties have expressed increasingly negative views about the opposing party members (Abramowitz 2010; Abramowitz and Saunders 2008; Abramowtiz and Webster 2015; Iyengar and Westwood 2015; McConnell et al. 2017). Perhaps due to the value-laden nature of political attitudes, political affiliation appears to be associated with high levels of negative affect (Yancey 2011).

Political affiliation is a type of deep-level diversity (Klein and Harrison 2007) and it differs from surface-level demographic variables because it highlights deeply held differences in values, attitudes, and beliefs (Roth et al. 2017). For example, one political party might prioritize compassion toward immigrants whereas another party might prioritize national security and crime prevention. These value differences are magnified by social media and traditional news outlets that act as “echo chambers” that amplify one view and seek to increase harsh political rhetoric (Bail et al. 2018). The influence of deeplevel diversity, in the form of values, attitudes, and beliefs, is more likely than surface-level diversity values (e.g., gender, race) to evoke strong reactions, because variables like values, attitudes, and beliefs inherently contain assessments of what is right, wrong, or socially preferable (Klein and Harrison 2007).

It is important to note that viewing deep-level diversity on social media may have a more powerful impact than viewing traditional surface-level demographic variables because raters may view variables such as political affiliation, values, and norms as a choice. Political affiliation at the party level confers agreement with the political party on many issues and political affiliation at the issue level is an individual’s personal belief on a specific political topic. This contrasts with observable, biologically driven surface-level demographic variables such as gender and race. Because political affiliations are viewed as personal choices, they result in greater negative affect in the form of stigmatization and exclusion (Goffman 2009; Kurzban and Leary 2001).

While we know that political affiliation at the party level may evoke strong negative affect, we know less about whether viewing social media posts at the political issue level evokes similar negative affect (Roth et al. 2017). For example, after the universally negative responses to the Parkland, Florida, shooting of 17 school children (Kramer and Harlan 2019) and the terrorist violence at the Bataclan Theater in France (Snapes 2018), participants fought over a key political issue: gun control (Plott 2018). An applicant’s position on such political issues may shape a hiring manager’s assessments, not unlike how politicians are assessed by their constituencies regarding their beliefs about hot button issues, such as affordable healthcare (Goodnough and Kaplan 2019), abortion (Rovner 2019), and environmental issues (Worland 2019), which are mentioned as frequently as party affiliation in the press. The preponderance of polarizing issues suggests the importance of considering the impact of similarity or differences at the political issue level, rather than simply at the broad political party level.

## Social Media and the Similarity-Attraction Paradigm

As noted above, political information likely contaminates social media hiring assessments. To understand this contamination, we develop a contextualized model of social media and hiring (see Figure 1 for our research model and Table 1 for construct definitions). We used a cross-context theory replication (see Hong et al. 2014) to evaluate the impact of job-irrelevant and job-relevant (i.e., individuating) information from different social media platforms on hiring manager perceptions of similarity, liking, and hireability. Our model integrates principles from the similarity-attraction paradigm, which directs attention toward perceived similarities and parallel-processing theories that help to explain the impact of multiple information sources (Chaiken and Trope 1999; Forgas and George 2001). Our model assumes that some jobirrelevant information may quickly trigger affective responses, whereas job-relevant information is processed more systematically (Petty and Cacioppo 1986). In the following pages, we elaborate on the constructs’ relationships and variables in our model.

## Dependent Variable

We operationalize our key dependent variable as hireability evaluations (i.e., assessments of job applicants by hiring managers), with a focus on two major dimensions of job performance that capture task and contextual factors. First, task performance refers to the hiring manager’s evaluation that a job applicant will effectively accomplish assigned responsibilities and job duties (Borman and Motowidlo 1997). Second, organizational citizenship behaviors (OCBs) focus on the hiring manager’s evaluation that a job applicant will go “above and beyond” job expectations to help others or the whole organization (Organ 1997; Organ and Ryan 1995). OB/HR theory suggests that together these constructs cover the major aspects of job performance (Brief and Motowidlo 1986; Motowidlo and Van Scotter 1994).

## Hypothesis Development

To understand the influence of social media assessments on hiring managers’ perceptions, we begin by examining the relationships among similarity, liking, and perceived hireability suggested by the similarity-attraction paradigm (Byrne 1971; Goldberg 2005) in the social media setting. We focus on the affect-laden, volitional “deep-level” variable of political affiliation and anticipate stronger relationships than have been found based on surface-level demographic variables (Goldberg 2005; Graves and Powell 1995; Graves and Powell 1996; Lin et al. 1992; McCarthy et al. 2010; McFarland et al. 2004; Sacco et al. 2003).

Social media content across platforms conveys a wealth of information about applicants’ political opinions (Ceron et al. 2014; Conover et al. 2011; Halpern and Gibbs 2013). Social media platforms form a readily available conduit through which hiring managers can access such content, resulting in strong affective reactions toward applicants based on deeply held political attitudes, values, and beliefs (Roth et al. 2017). The public nature of content on social media platforms, combined with the intensely value-laden nature of political attitudes (Yancey 2011), creates a dramatically altered hiring context wherein new forms/indicators of deep-level diversity (Klein and Harrison 2007) can more heavily factor into perceptions of job applicants. Issue-level political affiliations may evoke quickly formed judgments or stereotypes (Graham et al. 2012; Scherer et al. 2015), resulting in hiring managers making biased judgments for or against applicants. In other words, social media platforms enable hiring managers to form biases not often possible in traditional hiring formats.

We propose that when hiring managers view social media content indicative of a political affiliation different than their own, they may quickly form a negative affective attitude (disliking) toward the applicant. Similarly, we argue that when hiring managers find social media content from an applicant that supports a political affiliation similar to their own, they quickly form a positive affective attitude (liking) toward the applicant. We note that this is a variable that was not available using traditional selection tools and so the novelty of this contribution rests in the access to new information afforded by social media. Accordingly, we hypothesize:

## H1: Perceived political similarity positively influences liking of job applicants.

While social media content may exert an effect on the likinghireability relationship, we do not know whether the information conveyed via social media fundamentally changes the liking to hireability relationship. This relationship is important to investigate because we do not know whether liking, a feeling based on content gleaned from an online social media platform, will translate to impact a more tangible offline organizational outcome used in the selection process: perceptions of applicant hireability. We expect the social media context does not change this part of the similarity-attraction paradigm and that liking positively influences hireability evaluations of a job applicant (Goldberg 2005). While the injection of social media content into the hiring context is novel, it should not change how positive perceptions of a job applicant (e.g., liking) shape evaluations of that applicant. Hence, we hypothesize:

H2a: Liking of job applicants positively influences hireability ratings for expected task behaviors.

H2b: Liking of job applicants positively influences hireability ratings for expected OCBs.

Although individuating information is often related to hiring judgments/ratings (Olian et al. 1988), the social media context is unique, in part because of the large volume of personal information made available to recruiters (Roth et al. 2016). Research on decision-making has long recognized that an overabundance of information may degrade the ability to predict individual performance (Bartlett and Green 1966) due to information overload (Hiltz and Turoff 1985; Jones et al. 2004). Whereas traditional selection tools manage information overload by isolating job-relevant individuating information using mechanisms such as resumes, work samples, or structured job interviews, social media-based assessments often intertwine job-relevant and job-irrelevant content. This may cue affective, heuristic, category-based judgments (i.e., a cue such as an applicant’s views on marijuana legalization) that, due to information overload, either become diluted or persist in the presence of individuating information.

In the context of social media assessments, we maintain that hiring managers systematically, cognitively process jobrelevant individuating information (Maheswaran and Chaiken 1991) because the factors involved in evaluating the relationship between individuating information and expected performance are less affectively laden and less likely to quickly invoke mechanisms such as stereotypes or prejudices (e.g., Devine 1989). We also argue that individuating information requires more intentional, cognitive assessment to determine the extent to which information might predict work performance, such as considering whether a job applicant’s past sales success might foreshadow success as a manager. Finally, research suggests decision makers can potentially inhibit the effects of stereotypes and prejudices when engaged in controlled mental processing (Devine 1989), especially concerning job-relevant information (McCarthy et al. 2010). In the social media context, we argue that job-irrelevant, political content may engender an affective response due to perceived similarity, but that job-relevant, individuating information nevertheless impacts applicant assessments through controlled, systematic assessment of the applicant. Hence, we hypothesize:

H3a: Individuating information influences hireability ratings for expected task behaviors.

H3b: Individuating information influences hireability ratings for expected OCBs.

## Social Media Platform’s Moderating Influence

Social media assessments typically involve hiring managers viewing content on several social media platforms (Richey et al. 2018; SHRM 2016). The purpose of such platforms may potentially shape managers’ expectations of what is “normal” or expected content such as personal content on Facebook and professional content on LinkedIn (Chang et al. 2017). IS research has regularly highlighted that disconfirmed or unmet expectations have powerful effects on beliefs and behavior (Brown et al. 2014; Lankton et al. 2014; Lankton and McKnight 2012; Venkatesh and Goyal 2010). Accordingly, we examine whether content that is incongruent with platform expectations impacts hiring managers’ response to applicants.

When information found on a platform is incongruent with expectations, we anticipate a powerful influence on hiring managers’ assessments. Neuroscience research finds that information that violates viewer expectations heightens brain response (Strange et al. 2005) and draws increased attention (Baldi and Itti 2010; Itti and Baldi 2009). Specifically, it is the violation of expectations, not just novelty, that increases attention (Vachon et al. 2012). We argue that these findings imply that the violation of expectations (e.g., surprise) regarding content found on a social media platform draws increased attention.

For example, although it is possible to share political content on LinkedIn, the platform is generally more structured and work-focused than Facebook (Caers and Castelyns 2011; van Dijck 2013). As a result, we argue that political issue content found on LinkedIn is unexpected and violates expectations for the professionally focused utilitarian platform (Zide et al. 2014). We suspect that unexpected political content on LinkedIn should draw increased attention (Baldi and Itti 2010; Itti and Baldi 2009; Vachon et al. 2012) and magnify a hiring manager’s affective response (liking) to politically similar job applicants.

A similar logic applies to finding incongruent information on Facebook, which is generally more hedonic and personal than Linkedin (Lallmahomed et al. 2013; Turel 2015). Facebook creates opportunities for personal engagement with friends and family (Chang et al. 2017; van Dijck 2013) but has been appropriated by some users and businesses for utilitarian purposes (van Dam and van de Velden 2015). Because the platform’s primary focus is personal, we argue that unexpectedly finding work-oriented information draws greater attention (Baldi and Itti 2010; Itti and Baldi 2009; Vachon et al. 2012). Richey et al.’s (2018) work supports this intuition. In their study of Indian IT professionals, they found instances in which this attention-grabbing effect was intentionally used to subvert expectations by sharing professional attributes in a nonprofessional social media setting (Facebook) in order to attract attention from employers. We anticipate that this increase in attention may trigger a deeper consideration of the individuating information and may positively impact perceptions of hireability (O’Sullivan and Durso 1984; Stangor and McMillan 1992).

In sum, we believe that where a hiring manager finds information (i.e., a platform intended to host social content versus one hosting work-related content) moderates the relative impact of that information. Our logic is that finding unexpected content on a platform may draw increased attention and magnify the impact of that content. Accordingly, we hypothesize:

H4: The social media platform moderates the perceived similarity and liking relationship. Viewing content indicating similar political issue affiliation on a utilitarian platform results in the highest liking levels.

H5a: The social media platform moderates the relationship between individuating information and hireability ratings for expected task behaviors. Viewing individuating information on a hedonic platform results in the highest evaluation levels.

H5b: The social media platform moderates the relationship between individuating information and hireability ratings for expected OCBs. Viewing individuating information on a hedonic platform results in the highest evaluation levels.

## Method

To evaluate our hypotheses, we conducted an experiment that manipulated user-generated content, in the form of political issue affiliations and individuating information, on different platforms. We isolated platform effects by manipulating what website the content appeared on. For this, LinkedIn and Facebook were selected because, as noted above, though both platforms are well-established and popular, they differ in intended user purposes and expectations (Chang et al. 2017). After an extensive and iterative development process with multiple phases of testing, we created social media profiles that incorporated political issue affiliations, individuating information, and platform differences.

We designed a posttest-only randomized experiment with random selection that assigned participants to different conditions (i.e., participants viewed different social media profiles) (Campbell et al. 1963). Previous social media studies have used similar designs (Kluemper and Rosen 2009; Mazer et al. 2007). The experiment employed a $2 \times 2 \times 2$ design, with one within-participant factor. Political issue-related content (“for” or “against” one of three political issues consisting of legalizing marijuana, gun control laws, and the Affordable Care Act) and individuating information (“high” = containing job-relevant information, or “low” = containing other, job-irrelevant information) were used as betweenparticipant variables and the platform (i.e., Facebook or LinkedIn) served as a within-participant factor. Individuating information was manipulated by varying profiles that contained individuating information (high) or did not contain individuating information (low—these profiles contained innocuous, job-irrelevant content) and was dummy coded to reflect profiles that were low in individuating information (= 0) and high in individuating information (= 1).

We created LinkedIn and Facebook profiles with high and low levels of political attitudes and individuating information. Profiles reflected the three political issue affiliations (legalizing marijuana, gun control laws, and the Affordable Care Act), though we assumed and tested with empirical evidence (structural invariance, detailed later in this section) that there was no variation between political issue contexts (so, the design is a $2 \times 2 \times 2$ design, as opposed to a $2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 3$ design). ## Developing Social Media Profiles

Developing the social media profiles was an iterative process. First, we reviewed over 50 LinkedIn and Facebook profiles of actual college students, paying particular attention to pairs of profiles reflecting individuals that had both a Facebook and a LinkedIn profile. We recorded similarities and differences between the social media platforms, including information cues and how they were displayed on each profile, taking note of the most common ways that content was presented by platform and also noting content types that were consistent on both Facebook and LinkedIn. Next, we created profiles in LinkedIn and Facebook using actual, publicly available profile photographs (with user consent). Then, we modified these profiles to include our political issue and individuating information manipulations. Our final social media profiles were viewable without being “intrusive” (without our participants asking for a social media password or “friending” the job applicant; instead, our participants viewed publicly accessible [fictionalized] social media profile pages) and presented content consistently across both profiles; for example, applicants posted the same article on both platforms, profile pictures were the same in all conditions within each study, etc. See Appendix A for sample profiles.

## Experimental Manipulations

We manipulated job-relevant (individuating information) and job-irrelevant (political issue affiliation) content. To do this, we embedded political content supporting and opposing important political issues as well as high and low levels of individuating information for the fictionalized job applicants. In each manipulation, we used text, links, and images that were replicated across platforms. The manipulated content was presented in similar formats within Facebook and LinkedIn profiles. In all, we created 12 LinkedIn profiles and 12 Facebook profiles; that is, we developed 2 × 2 × 2 designs for each of our three political issue contexts (see Appendix

A). To ensure that our results were not specific to a particular political issue, we manipulated content on three political issues that were polarizing in the US and were relevant to work-related stereotypes. Under these criteria, we selected marijuana legalization, gun control, and the Affordable Care Act as political issues in our experiments (see Table 3).

The content on political issues reflected fictionalized job applicants who expressed support for (i.e., the high condition) or opposition to (i.e., the low condition) these issues. Further, the political issue affiliation content was manipulated by sharing articles in status updates; “liking” politically charged posts, pictures, and articles; and joining political groups. For example, one fictional profile contained a link stating “Five Reasons Legalizing Marijuana is Great!” for the high condition.

The content on individuating information was either high or low in job-relevant information. This meant that job-relevant content was either present (high) or not (low). So, for instance, for the high condition of individuating information, we presented job-relevant content about a fictional job applicant, where, on each platform, the fictional applicant posted a status update about their job saying, such as “I was named Employee of the Month for top performance numbers for June!”

We included manipulation checks to ensure our participants processed information presented on the profiles. Our manipulation checks were consistent with established conventions for social media-based experiments (Kluemper and Rosen 2009; Mazer et al. 2007). After respondents viewed the profiles and completed our survey, we then asked participants: “Does this applicant support legalizing marijuana/the National Rifle Association/the Affordable Care Act?” For the manipulation checks, 92.3% (n = 176) of respondents reported noticing the marijuana legislation manipulation, 87% (n = 166) respondents reported noticing the gun control manipulation, and 98.4% (n = 188) reported noticing the Affordable Care Act manipulation, with remaining respondents reporting “I don’t know.” Hence, we concluded that the participants perceived our manipulations as intended, supporting that our manipulations were successful.

## Sample

Our participants consisted primarily of graduate students from a university in the southeastern United States and hiring managers from a local company (n > 300 employees) (Table 4). Out of 270 invited participants, a total of 191 participated in our study (71% response rate). Of our participants, 122 (65.9%) had direct experience with conducting hiring interviews and there were no significant differences between the graduate students and employees (F(1,126) \$1, p = .733). Only 22 (11.9%) reported being trained to use social media to evaluate applicants. In general, our participants were older, more educated, and more experienced with actual HR hiring practices than many employed in laboratory studies (Kluemper and Rosen 2009).

## Measures, Reliability, and Validity

Established items and scales were adapted to measure perceived similarity (á = .96) (Tepper et al. 2011), liking (á = .86) (Wayne and Ferris 1990), and hireability–expected task performance (á = .92) (Williams and Anderson 1991) and hireability–expected organizational citizenship behaviors (á = .89) (Williams and Anderson 1991). Our confirmatory factor analysis suggested acceptable model fit; overall, the model Satorra-Bentler chi-square statistic was 2023.2 with p = .01 (CFI = .96, RMSEA = .05, RMSEA intervals of .05, .06, and SRMR = .08) (Satorra and Bentler 2001). Scale reliabilities exceeded .80 and their average variance extracted (AVEs) exceeded .63, indicating convergent validity. The square root of the AVE was higher than the construct correlations, suggesting discriminant validity (see Table 5). See Appendix B for the scales, Appendix C for the confirmatory factor analysis, standardized loadings and scale reliabilities, and Appendix D for details of pretests and pilot tests.

Since this study examined three separate political issue affiliations, comparison between groups was important. Before moving into our structural model, we used a stacked model approach to confirmatory factor analyses to assess whether the three political conditions had the same pattern of fixed and free factor loadings (that is, that the three conditions were equal, or showed “metric invariance”; Horn and McArdle 1992; Vandenberg and Lance, 2000). Testing metric invariance requires comparing the Satorra-Bentler chisquares of the study’s factor structure of three stacked models to a constrained model (with constrained factor loadings, a more restrictive model). The Satorra-Bentler chi-square for our model (2023.2 with 1155 degrees of freedom) was compared to the Satorra-Bentler chi-square of the constrained model (2000 at 1184 d.f.), yielding a scaled difference of 29.49. This difference was not significant (p > .2), providing evidence of metric invariance—the factor structures across each condition have the same factor structure, or are equivalent (Bryant and Sartorra 2012; Vandenberg and Lance 2000).

## Common Method Bias and Social Desirability Bias

To control and test for common method bias, we used procedural and statistical remedies (Podsakoff et al. 2003; Podsakoff et al. 2000). First, where possible, we measured constructs using multiple methods. For example, to measure “liking,” we used Likert scales and varied the questions and anchors. We also varied the range of the anchors; for example, with hireability, which was evaluated with a sevenpoint Likert scale and a five-point overall evaluation. Additionally, we implemented manipulation checks after completing the core construct measures to avoid priming the participants. Next, we protected subject anonymity to reduce evaluation apprehension (Podsakoff et al. 2003). The recruiting material and IRB informed consent statement promised participants that responses would be anonymous and confidential. To further reduce apprehension, we told participants that there were no right or wrong answers and instructed them to respond to all questions as honestly as possible. A latent variable marker test revealed no significant issues with common method bias (Chin et al. 2013). Because we manipulated political attitudes, we controlled for social desirability bias.

## Assumption Tests

We also conducted assumption tests. Skewness and kurtosis values fell within the required bounds (values were < 3 on skewness, and kurtosis fell under the conservative “rule of thumb,” SI < 10.0) (Kline 2015). Also, we estimated Mardia’s coefficient and found that our normalized estimate exceeded the recommended statistic of 3, even after deleting one outlier, suggesting nonnormality (Ullman 2006). Hence, we used the ML robust method in EQS and the Satorra-Bentler chi-square statistic (Byrne and Stewart 2006).

## Results

We estimated structural equation models to evaluate the measurement and structural models (Anderson and Gerbing 1988) in EQS (version 6.1, build 97) (Bentler 1995). We estimated responses to our social media profiles while controlling for social desirability bias, age, education, and HR experience. Our results reflect an overall model fit (for all three stacked models) as well as individual parameter estimates for hypotheses. Overall, the model Satorra-Bentler chi-square statistic was 1882.35 with (p = .04) (Satorra and Bentler 2001) (see Table 6).

Hypothesis 1 states that perceived similarity influences liking of job applicants. We tested this hypothesis by showing participants social media profiles of job applicants who posted content supporting or opposing legalizing marijuana (LM), gun control (GC) and the Affordable Care Act (ACA). Our analysis revealed support for Hypothesis 1 in all three contexts (LM: b = .49, â = .72, SE = .06, t = 8.74, p < .001; GC: b = .37, â = .77, SE = .06, t = 7.68, p < .001; ACA: b = .51, â = .78, SE = .06, t = 9.47, p < .001). Hence, when job applicants post about legalizing marijuana, gun control, or the Affordable Care Act participants appear to like applicants they perceive as more similar to themselves.

Hypothesis 2a states that liking job applicants influences hireability ratings of expected task behaviors. Our model shows a significant relationship with ratings for expected task behaviors (LM: b = 1.15, â = .67, SE = .21, t = 5.57, p < .001; GC: b = 1.26, â = .5, SE = .32, t = 3.97, p < .001; ACA: b = 1.64, â = 1.02, SE = .23, t = 7.02, p < .001), implying that Hypothesis 2a was supported in all three contexts. Hypothesis 2b, regarding the influence of liking on expected OCBs, was also supported for all three contexts (LM: b = .99, â = .67, SE = .18, t = 5.59, p < .001; GC: b = 1.15, â = .57, SE = .27, t = 4.32, p < .001; ACA: b = 1.13, â = .85, SE = .17, t = 6.62, p < .001). In all three contexts, participants gave likeable job applicants significantly higher hireability ratings for both expected task performance and expected OCBs.

Hypothesis 3a and 3b, which state that individuating infor mation (job-relevant content about the job applicant) leads to more positive hireability ratings, was not supported in this model in the legalizing marijuana context for expected task performance (LM: b = .08, â = -.04, SE = .14, t = -.59, p > .05) or expected OCBs (LM: b = .14, â = .07, SE = .13, t = 1.10, p > .05), but was supported for the gun control and Affordable Care Act contexts both for task performance (GC: b = .80, â = -.33, SE = .15, t = -5.34, p < .001; ACA: b = .83, â = .4, SE = .12, t = 7.13, p < .001) and OCBs (GC: b = .43, â = -.22, SE = .12, t = -3.52, p < .001; ACA: b = .66, â = .37, SE= .10, t = 6.44, p < .001). Although individuating information did not significantly influence hireability ratings in the legalizing marijuana context, individuating information did significantly increase these ratings in the gun control and Affordable Care Act contexts.

To test Hypotheses 4, 5a, and 5b, we ran a general linear model in SPSS 25 using composite variables, followed by examining the simple slopes of the significant interactions. We also tested the interactions using latent variables in our structural model by running a multilinear model using composite variables. We created interaction terms by dummycoding the platform measure (0 = Facebook, 1 = LinkedIn) as individuating information (0 = low individuating information, 1 = high individuating information). Hypothesis 4 posits that the social media platform (Facebook or LinkedIn) moderates the perceived similarity and liking relationship. We examined the standardized residuals for outliers (values higher than 3 are considered outliers) and removed one case from this analysis that was considered an outlier.<sup>3</sup> Using the interaction term, we found that this relationship was not significant in the legalizing marijuana context (F(1,126) \$ 1.0, p = .72), in the gun control context (F(1,118) \$ 1.0, p = .67), or in the Affordable Care Act context (F(1,128) = 1.09, p = .37). Overall, we found that the platform—whether Facebook or LinkedIn—did not moderate the relationship between similarity and liking in a social media context. That is, the effect of similarity on liking generalized across platforms.

## Mediation Testing

We used bootstrapping in EQS and a Sobel Test in SPSS 25 to evaluate if the perceived similarity to hireability relationship was mediated by liking (Hayes 2009). We found evidence of mediation in all three contexts (for the legalizing marijuana, gun control laws, and Affordable Care Act contexts, t = 5.58, 3.50, and 6.33, p-values of < .001, < .001, and < .001). Accordingly, evidence suggests that the participant’s liking of an applicant fully mediated the effect of perceived similarity on perceived hireability.

## Structural Invariance

Because comparison between groups was important (that is, we were looking to see that the hypothesized relationships in our research model held up, regardless of the political issue), we tested to determine whether the three political conditions had the same pattern of parameters (showed “structural invariance”; Horn and McArdle 1992; Vandenberg and Lance

2000). We tested for structural invariance by comparing the Satorra-Bentler chi-squares and independent chi-squares of the study’s structural model to a constrained model (i.e., with constrained relationship, a more restrictive model). The goal was to find tests of nonsignificance (that is, nonsignificant tests between our model and a constrained model suggest that the relationships are not influenced by the political issues). For the most part, our results indicated that the hypothesized relationships are significant, regardless of the political issue our respondents associated with the fictionalized job applicants. See Appendix E for a more thorough breakdown of our tests for structural invariance.

## Discussion

Motivated by a desire to understand how viewing social media content regarding political affiliation shapes recruiters and hiring managers’ ratings of job applicants, we manipulated information about gun ownership, universal healthcare, and drug legalization in social media profiles to assess its impact on assessments of applicants’ hireability. We found evidence that viewing information about political affiliation impacts ratings of potential employees.

First, our experiment demonstrates that viewing information related to political affiliation (at the issue level) on social media platforms affects hiring managers’ ratings of job applicants’ similarity, liking, and hireability. In the context of political issues and social media, we demonstrate that feelings of similarity, or the lack thereof, positively or negatively impact liking and ratings of hireability. This finding has broad implications for IS and related research because analysis indicates that many users create content that deals directly with politics (Zhang et al. 2020). Our results (1) suggest a need for research that examines how political party or issue-level affiliation might manifest on social media platforms and influence not only hiring evaluations, but also other personnel decisions such as promotion or retention, and (2) imply a need for research that examines how polarizing social media content on any number of topics, ranging from political issue-related information to sports fandom, may influence hiring manager assessments of information and information sources and the decisions that such assessments impact (Roth et al. 2016).

Second, we found that viewing job-relevant individuating information on social media influences hiring-related evaluations. For two of our three political issue affiliations, we found that individuating information on social media directly influences both task and OCB hireability assessments. Whereas studies in OB/HR demonstrate how individuating information impacts hiring decisions in structured situations such as job interviews (e.g., McCarthy et al. 2010), we demonstrate that individuating information impacts judgments in a new, often relatively unstructured context for employee selection. Our participants’ assessments were influenced by job-relevant individuating information, even in the presence of job-irrelevant social media content. Notably, in one of our three contexts (legalizing marijuana), we found a nonsigni ficant relationship between individuating information and perceived hireability. We suspect that this nonsignificant outcome may have been influenced by work-related stereotypes of marijuana supporters/users (Hirst et al. 2017) that characterize them as lazy, poorly dressed, or generally lacking in hireable attributes.

Third, in three out of six instances (combined for H5a/b), the social media platform moderated the individuating information to hireability relationship. In two (legalizing marijuana and gun control) of our three contexts, the hedonic platform (Facebook) significantly moderated this relationship more often than the utilitarian platform (LinkedIn), and in the third context (Affordable Care Act), there was no significant difference between the two platforms in this respect (i.e., relating to H5a/b). The interactions appeared to be ordinal such that the Facebook condition with high individuating information had the highest score while the other conditions yielded lower scores. This finding suggests that the unex pected job-relevant information found on Facebook may “surprise” hiring managers and draw more attention, thus magnifying the impact of the individuating information on perceived hireability.

Fourth, we note that the moderating effect of the social media platform (H4) on the perceived similarity and liking relationship was not significant in all three contexts (legalizing marijuana, gun control, and the Affordable Care Act). A plausible explanation for the unexpected H4 finding is that the psychological processes involved in perceived similarity and liking are affective in nature, potentially making the impact of surprise and the violation of expectation less powerful in comparison to cognitively based processes. It is also possible that hiring managers using LinkedIn are more engaged with the task at hand and focus more heavily on job-relevant, individuating information, making hiring managers less vulnerable to the potential impact of political content on their liking of a job applicant in this context. Or, it may be the case that political content appears more commonly on LinkedIn than we thought and is therefore less surprising to hiring managers than is professional content on Facebook. Ultimately, these findings suggest an opportunity for research that examines the interplay between expectations, information source (e.g., platform), social media content, and the perceptions of hiring managers.

Fifth, we found effects for job-irrelevant political issue affiliation on both platforms, even in the presence of jobrelevant individuating information. This finding directly contrasts with human resources and staffing studies that find that individuating information typicallyovershadows the effects of job-irrelevant information on similarity and liking in hiring assessments (McCarthy et al. 2010; Sacco et al. 2003). Given that political affiliation information is volitional, involves indicators of values, attitudes, and beliefs, and represents a type of deep-level diversity (Klein and Harrison 2007), this finding could imply that high levels of political polarization and its impact on feelings of similarity exert more robust and impactful effects than other (surface-level) job-irrelevant factors such as gender and race. Given that staffing research has largely focused on demographic rather than political differences, future research should directly compare the relative power of social media content on demographic differences and political attitudes with individuating information (Huddy et al. 2015; Iyengar and Westwood 2015; Roth et al. 2017). Our finding that political issue affiliations have effects across different platforms suggests that future work should consider user expectations related to the social media platforms used for assessments and decisions.

## Theoretical Implications

We contribute to IS literature in several ways. By examining howsocial media impacts and potentially contaminates hiring, an internal firm process, we lay a foundation for IS research to examine social media posts in terms of organizational judgments and decision-making regarding hiring, retention, promotion, and other managerial decisions. IS research has begun to address how social media impacts organizations (e.g., Chen et al. 2012; Dewan and Ramaprasad 2014; Kane et al. 2014; Oh et al. 2013) and our work contributes to this research stream by demonstrating how the new information source provided by social media potentially alters traditional organizational processes. Further, this work relates to the literature on privacy and self-disclosure (Joinson et al. 2010; Posey et al. 2010) by showing specific ways that posting about political interests and activities may bias job search outcomes for social media users.

Our research also contributes to the literature by demonstrating the moderating impact of the information source (i.e., different social media platforms). Our moderation hypotheses (H4, H5a/b) build on the logic that surprising, incongruent, or unexpected content attracts attention (Baldi and Itti 2010; Itti and Baldi 2009; Vachon et al. 2012). Notably, while unexpected information moderated the cognitively based individuating information to hireability relationship (H5a/b) for both platforms, there was no significant effect for either platform on the affectively based perceived similarity to liking relationship (H4), indicating that the impact of surprise may be less powerful for affectively based processes, such as liking, than for cognitively based processes, such as hireability evaluations.

Further, since we found that the effects of unexpected content were much stronger on Facebook than on LinkedIn, the moderating effect of the social media platform suggests that outcomes of self-disclosure (Joinson et al. 2010; Posey et al. 2010) on social media are not only a matter of what content is disclosed, but also where the content is disclosed. As such, we provide initial evidence that social media users form expectations regarding the type of content they may find on a platform and demonstrate that surprising content may exert a more meaningful impact on the viewer.

## Limitations and Future Research

Like all studies, our work has limitations. First, our sample consisted of graduate students and employees of a firm, roughly two-thirds of whom reported experience conducting employment interviews. Additionally, although our moderation hypotheses (H4, H5a/b) were hypothesized as occurring partly due to the incongruence of unexpected content on a hedonic/utilitarian platform, we did not actually measure the expectations for these platforms or the extent to which expectations were confirmed/disconfirmed (incongruence). Further, this work is limited in that we only measured perceptions of hireability but not actual hiring decisions.

Our study is also limited by the number of political issues that we included. Additional work is needed on political issue affiliation that considers a wider variety of issues, such as immigration, economic policy, social justice, etc., that polarize populations and evoke distrust (Chapman and Ciment 2015). Also, future research should evaluate opinions that fall along a continuum, such as how managers react to applicants who express politically disinterested, neutral, or centrist views. Additionally, more work is needed to investigate the complexities of polarizing positions themselves, such as support for legalizing marijuana exclusively for medicinal purposes, or support for increased gun control regulations in the form of expanded background checks only. Finally, there are cultural/ national differences that should also be investigated in future research, such as how managers react to support for specific political candidates in multiparty elections such as those found in Canada and Europe.

Findings should also be considered in light of media synchronicity theory (Dennis et al. 2008); future studies should probe the impact of more static versus more dynamic social media content, perhaps by comparing static content, such as a LinkedIn profile, to more dynamic feeds, such as Facebook’s front page. Beyond that, researchers should investigate more ephemeral platforms, such as Twitter or Snapchat, where content quickly appears and disappears.

While our work focuses on social media’s intersection with firms’ employee selection processes, work is still needed to consider how viewing political social media content shapes managers’ human resource decisions regarding existing employees, such as who to train, who to promote, and who to separate from the firm. Since it is likely that the availability of social media content exerts a pervasive impact across human resource decisions in many organizations, we have left unexamined how social media users’ awareness of monitoring by current or potential employers might change their online behavior, perhaps in terms of minimizing or hiding polarizing activity or, alternatively, by curating their online presence to optimize their appeal to employers. We have also left unexamined how users sharing various types of content, such as politicized memes (Dean 2018; Glãveanu et al. 2018), may change employer predictions of employees’ future behavior, such as attributions of organizational citizenship or counterproductive work behaviors.

Further, given that we found evidence that political issue affiliations influence perceptions, future research should consider how to design interventions that train or cue hiring managers to recognize and overcome similarity/differencebased biases. For example, research suggests that biases based on stereotypes can be inhibited through controlled, active thought processes (Devine 1989). Given that applied research has yielded effective interventions that limit the impact of demographic diversity, researchers should consider whether similar interventions help to control for or overcome biases based on social media assessments.

Another important research avenue would be to consider the impact of different types of deep-level diversity, such as political affiliation, vis-à-vis surface-level diversity like race or gender. In order to isolate and examine deep-level diver sity, our experimental design controlled for extraneous variables that would have created additional noise and potentially confounded our results; that is, our fictionalized job applicants were all, by design, the same gender, race, and general physical attractiveness/health level (all were white, male, brunette, and mid-20s). Though we focused on the effect of deep-level diversity and only controlled for surface-level variables, the complex relationship between surface- and deep-level diversity deserves more explicit evaluation regarding the interplay of deep-level diversity variables with surface-level variables. For example, are a hiring manager’s perceived differences with a job applicant magnified when the applicant has opposing political views and is a different race or gender? Alternatively, can political affiliation similarity overcome demographic differences in terms of their impact on perceived similarity and liking?

Finally, regarding our moderation hypotheses, we argued that unexpected content (job-relevant/job-irrelevant) found on platforms (hedonic/utilitarian) magnifies the impact of that content on ratings. In our case, the individuating information to hireability relationship was often moderated by platform. In this area, future research should consider elaborating on how content on social media platforms that is incongruent with users’ expectations shapes decision-making. For example, examining how norms and expectations for content on given platforms might be manipulated to realize a goal—such as attracting an employer’s attention (Richey et al. 2018) or promoting a product—could yield theoretical or practical insight for social media content managers. Additionally, future IS research in this area should consider how user expectations of a platform form and change over time, especially in terms of how social media users actively create content that shapes platform norms (van Dijck 2013). Investigating the associations between changing norms and behavior and assessments is crucial because our findings suggest that unexpected or incongruent social media content is more powerful; however, if many users start leveraging the social media platform in a similar way (e.g., Richey et al. 2018), then such content may exhibit diminishing impact.

## Implications for Firms

The study has important practical implications for hiring managers seeking to integrate social media content into job applicant assessments. First, since we found that jobirrelevant political content affects assessments of job applicants, we recommend that organizations articulate clear, transparent policies and training for hiring managers. Structuring how hiring managers sift through social media content may ensure that they focus on job-relevant information and ignore job-irrelevant content that could bias assessments. For example, hiring managers might be instructed to focus attention on applicant qualities that are directly related to performance (e.g., writing skills, languages spoken, and so on) in a specific job.

Additionally, organizations should develop standardized criteria for if, when, and how to evaluate social media content. An organization’s social media hiring policy should define the conditions under which posts should be collected, document how posts are collected, and afford some insight into how to evaluate job applicants’ social media profiles. While we found that hiring managers could detect individuating information, it is not clear that social media assessments are necessary to evaluate all applicants for all jobs. As such, company policy should also articulate whether social media assessments are necessary.

## Implications for Job Seekers

Our study also has implications for job applicants who are active social media users. Job applicants should be aware that a broad variety of opinions, beliefs, and behavior—not just posts about bad behavior—may influence job search outcomes. Further, our results suggest that users should also consider the implications of posting content on different types of social media platforms, particularly hedonic platforms. Our findings suggest that posting job-relevant content on hedonic platforms may be particularly powerful when job searching because job-relevant individuating information on hedonic platforms exhibited significantly stronger impacts on hireability assessments than did individuating information on utilitarian platforms. Finally, content evoking political issues influenced hiring ratings even in the presence of individuating information. Pragmatically, although we do not suggest that job applicants withdraw from expressing opinions on social media, we do suggest they be sensitive to the positive aspects and negative implications of posts and carefully evaluate what information they choose to post on social media platforms.

## Conclusion

Our work provides evidence that social media content on political affiliation (at the issue level) exerts influence on hiring managers’ assessments of similarity, liking, and, more importantly, hireability. This occurs even in the presence of job-relevant individuating information. In the context of political issues, our results suggest that managers need to understand that viewing job-irrelevant content can color their assessments of similarity, liking, and hireability of job applicants. We found that these effects often occurred across platforms (e.g., LinkedIn and Facebook) and suggest that organizations would be well-served in creating policies and training programs that encourage raters to be mindful of how social media assessments may create liking-based biases that impact the hireability ratings of job applicants. Additionally, we suggest that job seekers should be mindful of how their social media content may draw scrutiny from hiring managers and potentially create positive or negative biases.

## Control Variables

We controlled for relevant demographic and psychological variables. Though research results concerning the impact of gender and ethnicity are mixed in psychology, they are popular variables in many studies using demographic similarity theory (McCarthy et al. 2010). We measured these demographic factors, as well as sexual orientation.

We also measured individual cognitive absorption, a user’s deep involvement with social media, using the cognitive absorption scale (see Table B4) developed by Agarwal and Karahanna (2000). We were concerned that a more “entertaining” platform, such as Facebook, might engage our participants more, possibly influencing our results, so this scale was used to account for how engaged participants were with the two different platforms, LinkedIn and Facebook. The scale consists of five components (control, focused immersion, curiosity, heightened enjoyment, and temporal disassociation), all with reliabilities ranging from .83 to .93

In the study’s instructions, we clearly informed participants that there was no right or wrong way to answer our survey items. We also suggested that viewing the social media profiles could be seen as a “fun” activity, in an effort to alleviate pressure participants might feel to answer in a socially desirable way. We also anticipated that our participants might be more honest, since they were judging others rather than reporting on their own behaviors, (Collerton et al. 2007).

However, since the political beliefs we manipulated are generally considered polarizing, some applicants might still attempt to respond in a way they deem more socially appropriate. As such, we controlled for social desirability—i.e., an individual’s proclivity to respond to items in a way they might feel is socially admired—using a shortened version of the Marlow and Crowne social desirability scale (Reynolds 1982). The items are reported in Table B6.

As a procedural control, we also controlled for information presented in the profiles that was not directly relevant to the manipulations. All profiles not including the experimental manipulations contained the same information in forms appropriate for the platform condition. For example, for each political issue, the same profile picture was used for every experimental condition. All fictional job applicants were business graduates of the University of Colorado with similar work experience (note: across experiments the wording was varied as were the names of the companies for which the applicants currently worked).

Finally, we controlled for professional and educational experience. Our participants were asked, “Have you ever interviewed anyone before?” “Have you been trained in how to evaluate social media?” and “Have you served in a human resources management position before?”

## Measurement Equivalence in Confirmatory Factor Analyses

Before evaluating our structural model, we tested to ensure that our confirmatory factor analyses of the three political contexts had the same pattern of fixed and free factor loadings (that is, that the three contexts were equal, or showed “metric invariance”; Horn and McArdle 1992; Vandenberg and Lance 2000). To do so, we evaluated the Satorra-Bentler chi-squares of the study’s factor structure to a constrained mode (with constrained factor loadings, a more restrictive model). The Satorra-Bentler chi-square for our model (2023.2 with 1155 degrees of freedom) was compared to the Satorra-Bentler chi-square (2,000 at 1184 d.f.), yielding a scaled difference of 29.49. This difference was not significant (p > .2), providing evidence of metric invariance—that the factor structures across each context have the same factor structure o are equivalent (Bryant and Satorra 2012; Vandenberg and Lance 2000).

## Appendix D

## Pretest and Pilot Testing Results

We extensively pretested the initial survey instrument in order to ensure the validity of the experimental manipulations; in particular, we sought to determine that differing information cues (“liking” the National Rifle Association versus posting an article about legalizing marijuana) presented a valid level of stimuli, ascertain whether the profiles appeared authentic to participants, and evaluate the experimental procedure and questionnaire (Dennis and Valacich 2001). The supporting and opposing conditions were written to be unambiguously on one side of a political issue while seeming like natural content for a social media platform. For example, the content supporting legalizing marijuana was a shared link on the sample profile called “Five Reasons Legalizing Marijuana is Great!” and the content opposing was a link called “Five Reasons Legalizing Marijuana Stinks!” (see Table A1 in Appendix A for screenshots of all manipulations and accompanying graphics).

The pretesting and pilot testing processes refined development of the social media profiles (as illustrated in Figure 2). Our pretesting sample consisted of business graduate students who had experience selecting new employees (n = 10). Participants indicated that the initial profiles were “vanilla looking” and appeared inauthentic. This prompted the research team to include innocuous information to heighten the appearance of authenticity without drawing focus from the experimental manipulations, leading to a second iteration of profiles that we used in the experiments. We present a sample social media profile in Figure A1, which was created for Experiment 1: Legalizing Marijuana and contains a condition of positive political attitudes about legalizing marijuana and a low instance of individuating information. Note, in this particular profile, sources of innocuous information populating the applicant’s profile include his list of likes (the Denver Broncos, YouTube, Starbucks, etc.) and places he has visited (Prentup Field, Longhorn Steakhouse, etc.) provide additional authenticity, the status updates all have a numbe of “likes” to them and a few of the status updates may also be mapped correctly with the “places” the applicant has visited. The remaining socia media profiles are included in Appendix C of this document.

After developing our preliminary instrument and social media profiles, we conducted a pilot study to simulate the full experiment (n = 35 undergraduate business majors). In particular, we had two major objectives for conducting a pilot test: to uncover logistical problems with our survey and to establish an estimate of the total time required to complete the survey (Dennis and Valacich 2001). Due to our relatively small sample size for the pilot test (n = 35), we were unable to run statistical analyses of our research model. However, we did determine that the experiment ran smoothly and required around 20–25 minutes to administer. We determined that showing the profile at the bottom of each question set was ideal for survey participants. Further, to ensure participants were responding to experimental manipulations, as well as providing manipulation checks, we asked an open-ended question, “What did you notice most about this social media profile?” with each profile. In all, for each political issue, we determined that the experimental manipulations, regardless of the cue, were visible to our participants (using descriptive statistics and frequency charts, we determined 100% of applicants noticed our manipulations in the legalizing marijuana content, 98% for gun control, and 95% for the Affordable Care Act). We also examined the reliability of our scales, finding acceptable reliability for all of them (ranging from .85 to .93). On the basis of these results, we determined no significant changes needed to be made to our social media profiles or the survey instruments, and that we could continue with our experimental process as planned.

## Structural Invariance in Research Models

Comparison between groups was important to ensure that our results were not specific to one particular political issue and were pervasive acros all three. We tested to determine whether the three political contexts had the same pattern of parameters (that is, whether the three contexts were equal, or showed “structural invariance”; Horn and McArdle 1992; Vandenberg and Lance 2000). Structural invariance can be tested by comparing the Satorra-Bentler chi-squares and independent chi-squares of the study’s structural model to a constrained model (with constrained relationship, a more restrictive model). We performed this test to determine whether the relationships in our model were moderated by the political context (or not).

We first compared Context 1 (legalizing marijuana) to Context 2 (gun control laws). Two equality constraints significantly harmed model fit, indicating that these relationships were not equal across contexts. The equality constraints for liking ö hireability relationships for task (b = 1.15 and 1.26 in the legalizing marijuana and gun control laws contexts) and organizational citizenship behaviors (b =1.15 and 1.13 in the legalizing marijuana and gun control laws contexts) resulted in significant chi-squares of 18.17 (p < .001) and 10.572 (p < .001), respectively, where the results for our legalizing marijuana context were significantly higher than the gun control laws context for these particular relationships. No other relationships across models tested as significant, indicating the relationships did not differ significantly across contexts.

For Context 2 (gun control laws) and Context 3 (the Affordable Care Act), three constraints harmed model fit, indicating that these relationships were not equal across contexts. The liking ö hireability relationship constraints for task (b = .1.26 and 1.64 for the gun control and Affordable Care Act contexts, indicating a significantly higher relationship in the Affordable Care Act context) and organizational citizenship behaviors (b = 1.15 and 1.13 for the gun control and Affordable Care Act contexts, showing a significantly higher relationship for the gun control laws context) resulted in significant chi-square values of 5.89 (p = .015) and 9.747 (p = .002). The individuating information ö platform ö hireability-OCB constraints harmed model fit as well (b = .41 and .22 for legalizing marijuana and gun control laws, chi-square = 16.57, p = .001), showing a significantly higher relationship for the legalizing marijuana context. No other relationships across models tested as significant, indicating the relationships did not differ significantly across contexts.

Finally, for Context 1 (legalizing marijuana) and Context 3 (The Affordable Care Act), two constraints harmed model fit, including the individuating information ö hireability (b = .08 and .83 for the legalizing marijuana and Affordable Care Act contexts) link for task behaviors (chi-square = 15.15, p = .02, with a significantly higher relationship in the Affordable Care Act context) and for the individuating information ö platform ö hireability-task behaviors (b = .41 and .18 for the legalizing marijuana and Affordable Care Act contexts; chi-square = 5.802, $\mathbf { p } = . 0 3 )$ . This relationship was significantly higher in the legalizing marijuana context. No other relationships across models tested as significant, indicating the relationships did not differ significantly across contexts.
