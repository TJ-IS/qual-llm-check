# AMBIVALENCE IS BETTER THAN INDIFFERENCE: A BEHAVIORAL AND NEUROPHYSIOLOGICAL ASSESSMENT OF AMBIVALENCE IN ONLINE ENVIRONMENTS<sup>1</sup>

Akshat Lakhiwa Department of Management Information Systems, Terry College of Business, University of Georgia, Athens GA, U.S.A {akshat.lakhiwal@uga.edu}

Hillol Bala Department of Operations and Decision Technologies, Kelley School of Business, Indiana University, Bloomington, IN, U.S.A. {hbala@indiana.edu}

Pierre-Majorique Léger Department of Information Technologies, HEC Montreal, Montréal, Québec, CANADA {pierre-majorique.leger@hec.ca}

Information representations such as ratings and reviews play an important role in assisting users in making decisions in online environments. Prior information systems (IS) research has mostly focused on the role of extreme valence, i.e., the positivity/negativity of information, portrayed by such representations. Yet this bipolar approach discounts how the coexistence of positivity and negativity (i.e., ambivalence) or their absence (i.e., indifference) is formed and leads to distinct attentional processes and outcomes such as purchase decisions. We theorize how and why the valence of information projected through such representations may elicit mixed feelings and influence decision-making in online environments. We conducted four randomized controlled experiments, including an electroencephalography (EEG) study, to disentangle the influence of ambivalence and indifference on decision-making in an online shopping context. We found that ambivalence and indifference to online information distinctly influenced attention and purchase decisions relative to positivity and negativity. Our findings further suggest the inability of incumbent bipolar representations, such as the widely implemented star rating system, to capture the mixed feelings expressed in online content. We propose a bivariate intervention that overcomes the limitations of bipolar representations by not only discerning ambivalence from indifference but also amplifying purchase decisions for products with ambivalent information by at least 50%, compared to incumbent bipolar representations. Our findings advance ongoing research on the role of information valence in online environments and offer implications for practice.

Keywords: Ambivalence, indifference, decision-making, NeuroIS, information representation, valence, digital platforms

## Introduction

Information representations (henceforth, representations), such as ratings and reviews, are important considerations for decision-making in online environments (Li, 2018; Yin et al., 2021). Digital platforms such as Amazon, Etsy, Tripadvisor, and Yelp rely heavily on such representations to display user preferences, curate offerings, and provide recommendations (Adomavicius et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2020). Due to the pervasiveness and importance of representations in online environments, there has been a growing interest in identifying and remedying aspects that may cause unintended and potentially erroneous deviations in users’ preferences and purchase decisions, as well as platforms’ recommendation strategies (Ananthakrishnan et al., 2020; Garg & Johari, 2021; Klein et al., 2018).

Representations draw attention and signal information valence, which is characterized by specific emotional responses, often categorized as positive or negative (Ahn et al., 2018). Valence elicits perceptions, forms attitudes, and influences decisions, where positive valence/higher ratings facilitate purchase decisions and negative valence/lower ratings often lead to rejection (Yin et al., 2021). By focusing on the bipolar (positivenegative) aspects of information valence, representations simplify the role of extreme positive or negative attitudes in decision-making. But this bipolar distinction disregards how nonextreme attitudes may affect decision processes and outcomes such as attention and purchase decisions. For instance, the first review in Figure 1 and the sequence of positive and negative reviews noted in Figure 2 reveal coexisting and conflicting dispositions, termed ambivalence. Ambivalence differs from both extreme attitudes and indifference, defined as the absence of evaluative disposition (together, nonambivalence). Despite the prevalence<sup>2</sup> of content with nonextreme valence, prior research has mostly assessed antecedents of decision-making in online environments with a bipolar yardstick, e.g., the positivity or negativity of representations (Godes & Mayzlin, 2004; McAlister et al., 2012; Smith et al., 2012). This bipolar approach limits the understanding of how and why nonextreme attitudes such as ambivalence disparately affect users’ preferences and decisions.

The ubiquity and abundance of information in online environments further make decision-making reliant on information received from various sources such as ratings and reviews, often in different sequences (Xu et al., 2020). As individuals appraise the discrete valence of such sequentially received information, they may experience mixed feelings that influence the eventual emergence of positive or negative decisions (Figure 2). Yet the question of how this sequential appraisal of information manifests ambivalence and influences decisions made in the presence of ambivalence is yet to be answered. We seek to develop this understanding by examining how ambivalence and indifference, in contrast to extreme attitudes, influence users’ attentional processes and purchase outcomes in online environments. We draw upon the two most widely used representations, i.e., reviews and ratings, to theorize how the sequential presentation of conflicting information elicits ambivalence and how the different order in which such information is appraised may alter decision-making for ambivalent individuals. In particular, we seek answers to the following research questions:

RQ1: How does decision-making differ for individuals experiencing ambivalence relative to those experiencing positivity, negativity, and indifference in online purchasing contexts?

RQ2: How does the order of sequentially presented information valence influence decision-making for individuals experiencing ambivalence in online purchasing contexts?

The bipolar interpretation of information valence further renders a parsimonious positive-negative continuum to incumbent graphical representations such as star ratings (Figure 1). Such an approach contradicts the bivariate structure, i.e., the nonreciprocity of positive and negative perceptions and attitudes, oversimplifies information valence, and obstructs the discernibility of nonextreme feelings, such as ambivalence and indifference (Snyder & Tormala, 2017). This indiscernibility caused by bipolar representations potentially distorts users’ preferences and may affect the accuracy of recommendations, thereby impacting platform strategies. In our third research question, we compare the incumbent bipolar representation approach (e.g., the widely used star rating system) with a bivariate approach and theorize how and why the distinct realization of ambivalence and indifference between bipolar and bivariate representations affects users’ preferences in online environments. Thus, we ask:

RQ3: How does bivariate representation of product ratings in online environments influence decision-making compared to incumbent bipolar representations for products with ambivalent ratings?

We theorize how ambivalence, evoked by sequentially appraised conflicting information valence, elicits higher attention and distinct purchase intentions relative to nonambivalence. In order to objectively measure users’ attention, we draw upon prior NeuroIS research and designed an electroencephalography (EEG) experiment to examine our hypotheses. Among those experiencing ambivalence, purchase intention and attention were higher when the leading valence of sequentially processed information was positive rather than negative. Although ambivalence and indifference distinctly affect purchase decisions, Experiment 2 reveals how incumbent bipolar representations fail to discern the two.

We theorize how bivariate representations, compared to bipolar representations, overcome this limitation, stimulate higher cognitive involvement, and increase the purchase of products with ambivalent information. We find support for our hypotheses in Experiments 3 and 4. Indeed, as suggested by our findings in Experiments 2, 3, and 4, the proposed bivariate representation approach leads to a ≥50% increase in the purchase of products with ambivalent information, relative to a bipolar approach. In Experiments 3 and 4, we also address concerns regarding representation novelty and consistently consolidate our findings.

This research contributes by broadening the understanding of how the nonextreme valence of ambivalence and indifference discernibly affects decision-making in online environments. We theorize how information valence appraised from diverse sources influences the emergence of decision outcomes in the presence of ambivalence, thus advancing discussion on the use of multiple sources in online environments. We draw upon prior NeuroIS research to suggest how cognition affected by the appraisal of information valence in various sequences resolves ambivalence, and contribute to research on the resolution of mixed feelings in online environments. By highlighting weaknesses in the incumbent representational strategy, we postulate that a bivariate intervention can reduce distortion in users’ preferences, amplify positive outcomes under ambivalence, and potentially motivate informed decisionmaking. Our findings contribute to ongoing research on information representations and discussions on the role of mixed feelings in improving decision-making and platforms’ strategies. We thus advance knowledge on information representations, which has implications for research and practice.

Related Literature and Theoretical Background

## Information Representations and Attitude Formation

Representations often aggregate peer-generated feedback, thereby easing decision-making by drawing users’ limited attention and reducing information overload (Lee et al., 2012). Reviews and ratings are two of the most widely used representations<sup>3</sup> that initiate interpretation, interact with memory and cognition, allow sensemaking, and guide decision outcomes in online contexts (Cheung et al., 2017). They provide an assessment of quality, modify attitudes, present emotions, and provide feedback to online platforms’ recommendation strategies (Adomavicius et al., 2021; Yin et al., 2021). Nevertheless, representations are often subject to a tension between brevity and the accurate portrayal of the underlying information (Adomavicius et al., 2013). While prevalent representations exhibit a bipolar approach that considers the positivity and negativity of information valence as a single continuum, affect and attitudes are inherently characterized by a bivariate paradigm with nonreciprocal (i.e., independent) cognitive activation for positivity and negativity (Snyder & Tormala, 2017; Yin et al., 2014). A bivariate approach may thus potentially permit a distinct conceptualization of attitudes such as ambivalence, which are otherwise indiscernible from indifference using a bipolar approach.

## Ambivalence and Indifference

Users often experience mixed feelings such as ambivalence in information-rich online environments (Lakhiwal et al., 2020; Moody et al., 2017). However, ambivalence can be mistaken for indifference from a bipolar endpoint, e.g., star ratings. Ambivalence constitutes simultaneous, strong, and opposing cognitive and affective orientations that may result from interpersonal, dispositional, or external artifacts (Armitage & Conner, 2000; Petty et al., 2006). In contrast, indifference constitutes weak or no cognitive or affective orientations (Schneider & Schwarz, 2017; Thompson et al., 1995). While ambivalence may manifest as strong concurrent positive and negative associations, indifference constitutes neither positivity nor negativity (Schneider & Schwarz, 2017). The coexistence or lack of evaluations makes both ambivalence and indifference weaker (with an attenuated effect on intention) than extreme attitudes (Conner et al., 2003). In evaluative contexts, conflicting evaluations due to ambivalence can lead to elaboration and aversion, reducing this attenuation, relative to indifference (Van Harreveld et al., 2015). Indifference neither invokes conflict nor heightens information processing, but it can weaken decisionmaking motivation (Willemsen & Keren, 2003).

The gradual and temporally dissociated processing of information affects the cumulative formation of attitudes (Conner et al., 2002; Xu et al., 2020). For example, the sequential processing of new information triggers assimilative processes and updates beliefs and evaluations (Ein-Gar et al., 2012). Sequential interaction with conflicting valence thus provides a context for the emergence of ambivalence and the examination of its effect on decision-making. The appraisal of conflicting information, or appraisals that contradict existing evaluations, may occur in positive-first or negative-first sequences, depending on the valence of the preceding evaluation. In such conditions, new evaluations typically interact with and adjust the cognitive environment anchored to existing or leading attitudes (Li et al., 2020; Petty et al., 2006). Thus, we discuss the characteristics of the leading positivity or negativity of information that may contribute to the emergence of ambivalence and subsequently affect decision outcomes in the presence of ambivalence.

## Negativity and Negativity Bias Theory

We first draw on negativity bias theory and discuss the characteristics of leading negative evaluations that can influence the formation and resolution of ambivalence. Cognitive and behavioral reactions that emerge from negative evaluations may influence an individual’s response to subsequently appraised information. Early studies have discussed dissimilarities in users’ responses to negative/positive evaluations (e.g., Kanouse et al., 1972). Recent studies on negativity bias theory have posited that negative information generally elicits strong negative attitudes and sparks immediate action tendencies (Baumeister et al., 2001). Negativity produces stress by arousing fear, anxiety, sadness, or anger (Taylor, 1991). Negative information further enhances the behavior inhibitory system that motivates aversion and leads to a narrowminded approach to preserving resources and avoiding harm during decision-making (Chen & Lurie, 2013). Therefore, emotions aroused by negative information typically follow immediate responses as individuals’ attention narrows and they focus on aspects that elicit negativity (Yang et al., 2018). This behavior is often attributed to the unexpected or repulsive nature of negative information and forms the basis for fast decision-making (Meffert et al., 2006).

## Positivity and Broaden-and-Build Theory

Broaden-and-build theory allows us to theorize how the cognitive consequences of leading positive evaluations may influence the formation and resolution of ambivalence. This theory suggests that positivity broadens people’s thought and action repertoires and encourages them to consider more expansive ideas, actions, and solutions (Fredrickson & Losada, 2005). In contrast to negative valence, positive valence points to the affective quality of goodness and often elicits increased exploration, the ability to absorb new information, and positive expectations (Fredrickson & Branigan, 2005; Kong et al., 2020). Positivity activates the behavior approach system that guides behavior in response to reward signals and is followed by active engagement with the environment and information processing with an adaptive bias to approach and explore (Gable & Reis, 2010). When individuals process information with positive valence, they relax autonomic or quick reactions and instead exhibit nonspecific and nonimmediate action tendencies (Fredrickson et al., 2000). Fredrickson et al. (2000) found that prolonged positive feelings can potentially undo negative feelings. This broadening function of positivity improves individuals’ approach toward new information and can reduce lingering negative feelings. Taken altogether, positive evaluations generally activate cognitive and behavioral reactions that broaden one’s approach to subsequent information (Lyubomirsky et al., 2005).

## Research Overview

We develop and test our hypotheses in four experiments (Table 1). In Experiment 1, we theorize how sequentially appraised information elicits ambivalence and use EEG to examine the effect of ambivalence on attention and purchase intention, relative to nonambivalence (e.g., indifference). In Experiment 2, we intervene with a bivariate representation approach that accurately discerns ambivalence from indifference. We hypothesize how this bivariate representation affects purchase decisions in online environments relative to incumbent bipolar representations, i.e., the star rating system. We designed Experiment 3 to address potential confound due to representation novelty and theorize how bivariate representations influence decision-making. Experiment 4 consolidates findings and offers additional robustness.

## Experiment 1

## Experiment 1: Research Hypotheses

## Ambivalence and Attention

Relative to indifference, ambivalence is characterized by stronger cognitive and emotional inconsistency, which can lead to a heightened state of arousal marked by discomfort that motivates the need to achieve consistency (Schneider & Schwarz, 2017; van Harreveld et al., 2015). We argue that such motivation could affect individuals’ allocation of cognitive resources to filter information and resolve ambivalence (Shen et al., 2015). That is, the strong or opposing evaluations experienced by ambivalent individuals could invoke conflict, which demands more attention in decision-making than indifference, as indifference is characterized by no evaluation (Yoo, 2010).

The attentional resources required by ambivalence are also higher than that of extreme positive/negative attitudes, which involve the consideration of a single valence orientation and thus avoid inconsistency and ease decision-making (Nohlen et al., 2019). According to negativity bias theory, negativity activates the behavior inhibitory system, leading to the withdrawal of cognitive resources and creating less demand for attention relative to ambivalence. Positivity, however, activates the behavior approach system, which motivates approach-based behavior, as suggested by broaden-and-build theory. Yet positivity elicits more selective attention to mull over unidimensional valence than ambivalence, which requires simultaneous consideration of dichotomous aspects to disentangle conflict (Qahri-Saremi & Turel, 2020). Ambivalence therefore would be expected to require more attention than nonambivalence due to the simultaneous consideration of conflicting affective and cognitive evaluations. Compared to non-ambivalence, ambivalence would thus lead to greater cognitive involvement and information processing, as well as heightened individual attention.

In EEG research, event-related potentials (ERPs) are distinct voltage fluctuations in the EEG signal that represent cognitive processes in response to external stimuli. Such fluctuations are time-locked to a precise marker representing the stimuli. The amplitude of the P300 ERP at the midline over the parietal cortex of the brain<sup>4</sup> (Figure 3), i.e., the Pz electrode, has been shown to be an index of attention (Coull, 1998; Luck, 2014). Heightened attention would thus be expected to lead to higher activation of the brain’s parietal cortex, triggering a larger amplitude of the P300 ERP at the outset of ambivalenceinducing stimuli relative to nonambivalence-inducing stimuli.

H1: Ambivalence needs more attention (measured as amplitude of the P300 ERP) than positivity, negativity, and indifference.

## Ambivalence and Purchase Intention

Individuals’ intention, which can predict their behavior, is updated based on new information and would thus vary as attitudes are updated by sequentially appraised information (Cenfetelli & Schwarz, 2011; Venkatesh et al., 2008; Yi & La, 2004). Extreme attitudes typically exhibit a strong and consistent association with intention; positive valence leads to higher intention while negative valence leads to lower intention (Chevalier & Mayzlin, 2006). Extreme attitudes are thought to be cognitively less demanding and believed to facilitate faster decisions than nonextreme attitudes (Nohlen et al., 2019). Ambivalence and indifference have a weaker and less-extreme association with intention, leading to greater variability in outcomes relative to extreme attitudes (Nohlen et al., 2019; Thompson et al., 1995). However, in evaluative contexts such as online shopping, relative to indifference, dichotomous evaluations due to ambivalence lead to higher cognitive elaboration, which strengthens the association between ambivalence and intention, relative to indifference (Van Harreveld et al., 2015). Nevertheless, any conflict or discomfort caused by discrepant orientations associated with ambivalence can invoke a negative affect that would lower purchase intention (Van Harreveld et al., 2015). Thus, due to its aversive effect, ambivalence may reduce purchase intention.

H2a: Ambivalence and indifference lead to higher purchase intention than negativity.

H2b: Ambivalence and indifference lead to lower purchase intention than positivity.

H2c: Ambivalence leads to lower purchase intention than indifference.

## Information Sequence and Ambivalence

We suggest that the positive-first or negative-first sequences of information appraisal could elicit ambivalence in two ways. First, we theorize that the steady appraisal of information from discrete sources results in a continuity of moments that each represent the interaction of newly formed and existing attitudes, even though some moments may lead to intermittent evaluative conflict. Second, the appraised valence from discrete sources might continue to update positive/negative cognitions independently and accumulate as ambivalence. In both cases, the coexistence of conflicting attitudinal orientations would be characterized as ambivalence, where the leading attitude sets up the cognitive environment for decision-making and influences the resolution of ambivalence.

Information sequence, ambivalence, and attention: We posit that simultaneous opposing dispositions would elicit ambivalence in both positive-first and negative-first sequences. However, the leading positivity initiated by the positive-first sequence would be expected to create a cognitive environment that seeks continued action, exploration, and assimilation due to the broadening effect of positivity (Gable & Reis, 2010). Hence, the conflicting valence would be salient and fully experienced with an approach-based mindset. In contrast, in a negative-first sequence, the leading negativity would be expected to initiate the behavior inhibitory system, leading to a more narrow-minded cognitive environment with a resourceconserving reaction toward subsequently appraised information (Chen & Lurie, 2013). We expect the negativefirst sequence to thus attenuate individuals’ interest in subsequent information, deterring the full experience of opposing dispositions or the investment of the cognitive resources needed for a thoughtful resolution (Baumeister et al., 2001). Experiencing ambivalence in a positive-first sequence would thus be expected to trigger an exploratory mindset and the need for continued action, whereas a negative-first sequence would initiate decision-making and the desire to minimize resource expenditure. Users would thus be expected to invest greater attention resources when experiencing ambivalence in a positive-first sequence versus a negative-first sequence. Hence, the activation of P300 ERP should be stronger when ambivalence is experienced in a positive-first versus a negative-first sequence.

H3: The information sequence moderates the effect of ambivalence on the amplitude of P300 ERP (attention) such that the positive effect of ambivalence on attention is greater when positive information is presented first, resulting in a higher amplitude of P300 ERP.

Information Sequence, Ambivalence, and Purchase Intention: Negative affect makes avoidance or aversive behaviors cognitively less demanding than positive outcomes (Nohlen et al., 2019; Taylor, 1991). Thus, we anticipate that with a negative-first sequence, the behaviorinhibiting effect would set up a cognitive environment that seeks to conserve resources and avoid potential harm, motivating individuals to withdraw interest (Chen & Lurie, 2013). In contrast, a positive-first sequence would activate the broadening function of positive feelings before the negativity bias could trigger harm-avoidance behavior. Positivity would thus be expected to provide individuals with resources to constructively cope with adversity and fuel psychological resilience instead of resorting to cognitively less-demanding outcomes (Fazio et al., 2004), while its broadening function should reduce negative feelings (Fredrickson et al., 2000).

When ambivalence is experienced with a negative-first sequence, we expect decision-making to be anchored to a self-protective and behavior-inhibiting stance. A negativefirst sequence would thus be expected to reduce the importance of later appraised dispositions, emphasize harm avoidance, and facilitate cognitively less-effortful outcomes. In contrast, a positive-first sequence would be expected to create a positivistic and exploratory cognitive environment. In this case, actions would thus be driven by an approachbased mindset, greater cognitive involvement, and meticulous evaluation, which would likely lead to delayed decision-making (Van Harreveld et al., 2015). With a positive-first sequence, individuals’ overall evaluation would thus be catalyzed by their exploratory mindset, undermining any inhibiting or aversive stance toward the focal product, which would facilitate the increased intention to purchase, relative to a negative-first sequence. Individuals experiencing ambivalence with a negative-first sequence would thus be expected to exhibit lower purchase intention than individuals experiencing ambivalence with a positivefirst sequence.

H4: The information sequence moderates the effect of ambivalence on purchase intention such that the negative effect of ambivalence on purchase intention is lower when positive information is appraised first, resulting in higher purchase intention.

## Experiment 1: Methodology

Scholars have discussed the potential of neuroscientific methodologies for IS research, i.e., NeuroIS (Dimoka et al., 2011; Vance et al., 2018). NeuroIS methods such as EEG, fMRI, and eye-tracking offer rigorous, objective, and unbiased complementarity to surveys, lab and field experiments, interviews, secondary sources, and the simulated models conventionally used in IS research (Tams et al., 2014). NeuroIS methods allow for the localization of brain regions and processes associated with IS constructs and capture otherwise difficult-to-measure mental processes. They also complement other methods in identifying antecedents of latent cognition and use behaviors, the disentanglement of temporal sequences, and theory building (Dimoka et al., 2011). The literature has thus suggested that NeuroIS concepts, methods, and tools can effectively capture how individuals process information, make economic and social decisions, manage uncertainty, and respond to rewards/punishments (Jenkins et al., 2016; Léger et al., 2014).

In NeuroIS research, EEG has been deployed to measure the neurophysiological association of emotional and behavioral constructs with cognitive activity within the brain (Moravec et al., 2019). It is less obtrusive than other NeuroIS methods such as fMRI, and offers superior temporal precision, ecological validity, and the unprompted diagnosticity of users’ attention. We drew on NeuroIS and associated literatures to design a within-subject controlled EEG experiment. Our design is consistent with prior work that has examined ambivalence using EEG (Nohlen et al., 2014; van Harreveld et al., 2009). Despite being a resource-intensive methodology, EEG offers a rigorous and objective measurement of P300 ERP, which is associated with users’ attention and time-locked with external stimuli.

## Participants

We recruited 23 participants,<sup>5</sup> at a major university in Canada (aged 18 to 30; 33% were women). The average duration for the study was \~90 minutes, including set-up time. Participants received a gift card worth CAD30 for participation. We used data from 18<sup>6</sup> participants for the final analysis.

## Procedure

We presented participants with 80 trials in a random sequence on a blank white screen. Each trial included a product with an image, followed by two attributes, shown sequentially with a separator screen and a fixation cross (Figure 4). The order of attributes within each trial was randomized. The product image was shown for 2.5 seconds, followed by a fixation cross for 1.5 seconds, followed by two attributes for 2.5 seconds each, and separated by a fixation cross for 1.5 seconds<sup>7</sup> (Nohlen et al., 2014; Osterhout & Holcomb, 1992). The measures followed this sequence. We presented trials in batches of 20, with an interval of 90 seconds between successive batches.

## Treatment

We conducted a pretest<sup>8</sup> to identify 80 products relevant to college students in North America. Each product was randomly assigned two adjectives as attributes to ensure that the overall valence of the product was positive, negative, indifferent, or ambivalent (Table 2). The sequence was randomized and recorded for ambivalent products to be positive-first or negative-first. There were five treatment groups with 16 products per group.

## Stimulus Design

Attributes were identified using product reviews from Amazon<sup>9</sup> (Table 2). We preprocessed reviews to remove punctuations, stop-words, symbols, and emoticons, and stemmed reviews for consistency. We tokenized the reviews and used vectorized n-grams of up to two tokens to train two logistic regression classifiers.<sup>10</sup> We tested the classifiers on 3-star rated reviews to separate reviews that would classify as both positive and negative (i.e., ambivalent) from those classified as neither negative nor positive (i.e., indifferent). Attributes were then manually extracted using the coefficients of the classifiers (Table 3).

## EEG Acquisition and Preprocessing

EEG data were acquired using a 32-electrode EEG apparatus. The LiveAmp (Brain Products GmbH) amplifier and an actiCAP (Brain Products GmbH) cap tracker localized electrodes with landmarks that were placed on the nasion and the superior junction between the ear and skull. We based the electrode positions on the 32-channel standard cap layout for actiCAP.<sup>11</sup> We presented and administered stimuli using E-Prime 2.0 software (Psychology Software Tools). Stimulus onset coincided with a TTL (transistor-transistor logic) pulse registered in an EEG recording via the BrainVision Recorder (Brain Products GmbH). The BrainVision Recorder and E-Prime were thus synchronized.

Data were analyzed using a combination of BrainVision Analyzer (Brain Products GmbH)<sup>12</sup> and MATLAB (MathWorks, Inc.) using guidelines suggested by Duncan et al. (2009) and Müller-Putz et al. (2015) for preprocessing. The sampling rate was 256 Hz. We used an infinite impulse response Butterworth filter for preprocessing with a bandpass of 1-15 Hz<sup>13</sup> (Duncan et al., 2009; Léger et al., 2014). Artifacts during trials were visually inspected and cleaned using the artifact subspace reconstruction (ASR) algorithm from MATLAB (Chang et al., 2018). We segmented EEGs from -200ms to 1500ms of the onset of the second attribute for each product in the trial. Altogether, we had 16 epochs per treatment, on average, for the 18 participants. A jackknife procedure was then used on these averaged waveforms to improve the signal-to-noise ratio for each condition for each participant (Luck, 2014). The final 18 jackknifed waveforms for each condition were sampled in the 300-500ms time window to find the peak amplitude of the P300 component at the Pz electrode. The statistics were adjusted using the sample size to prevent any inflation of standard errors (Luck, 2014).

## Measures

We measured positive and negative affect using two items on a 5-point scale (see Appendix) and used the following formula to calculate ambivalence (Oreg & Sverdlik, 2011):

$$
A m b i v a l e n c e = \left(\frac {\text {Positive} + \text {Negative}}{2}\right) - | \text {Positive} - \text {Negative} | (1)
$$

The two items were followed by a single item to measure purchase intention (see Appendix). Attention was measured using the P300 ERP component, which peaks in the parietal cortex of the brain between 300 to 500 milliseconds after the stimulus is introduced (Coull, 1998; Müller-Putz et al., 2015).

## Experiment 1: Results and Analysis

## Behavioral Results

We checked the manipulation and found that ambivalence was higher for products with ambivalent attributes<sup>14</sup> than for those with nonambivalent attributes $( F \ : ( 3 , 1 4 3 6 ) = 3 8 6 . 5 , p$ < 0.01). We found no significant difference in ambivalence for products with positive-first and negative-first ambivalent information. Post hoc tests (with Bonferroni correction) confirmed significant differences in ambivalence among all treatment groups. Purchase intention was highest for products with overall positive attributes, followed by those with indifferent and ambivalent attributes (F (3, 1436) = 432.77, $p \ < \ 0 . 0 1 \rangle$ ). Ambivalence led to lower purchase intention than indifference but higher purchase intention than negativity. Post hoc results support H2a, H2b, and H2c. ## Experiment 1: Discussion

Through Experiment 1, we address limitations in the understanding of the role of nonextreme valence of information on users’ purchase-related decision-making in online environments. The findings from Experiment 1 enrich the knowledge of such decision-making by answering the first two research questions. We focused on the sequential presentation of recommended reviews and used NeuroIS-based assessment to illustrate how individuals making decisions in online environments experience significant heterogeneity in terms of cognitive (i.e., attention) and behavioral (i.e., purchase intention) consequences due to the distinct realization of ambivalence and indifference relative to positivity and negativity. The order in which users process information such as recommended reviews further contributed to how they resolved decision-making conflicts due to ambivalence. Overall, the findings from Experiment 1 suggest that a bipolar interpretation of the valence of online information may potentially misrepresent nonextreme valence and fail to discern ambivalence from indifference. These findings thus raise the question: Could incumbent bipolar representations be distorting decision makers’ preferences? More importantly, what happens if we replace these representations with a more holistic yet succinct representational intervention? We make this inquiry in Experiment 2.

Graphical representations such as the star rating system summarize the affective evaluation or valence expressed in online content and encapsulate the diverse aspects of peergenerated feedback. ## Experiment 2: Research Hypotheses

## Information Representations and Discernibility of Nonextreme Ratings

By displaying extreme ratings as global opposites, incumbent bipolar representations mismatch the mental representation of positivity and negativity that is guided by distinct bivariate cognitions (Snyder & Tormala, 2017). This approach thereby creates visual ambiguity between ambivalent and indifferent information (Figure 2). Such a mismatch between perceived and actual information presentation can result in a cognitive misfit, which can corrupt problem-solving and explain the inability of bipolar representations to differentiate between ambivalence and indifference (Bačić & Fadlalla, 2016). A simple bivariate approach visualizes the strength of positive/negative valence on separate and salient scales, thus matching the mental representations of positive and negative cognitions. Bivariate representations can thus enable the perceivable distinction between ambivalent and indifferent information. Bipolar representations, on the contrary, limit the simultaneous realization of positive and negative dispositions and restrict the distinct perception of ambivalent and indifferent information.

H5: Bivariate representations lead to a greater perceivable difference in ambivalence for ambivalent and indifferent information than bipolar representations.

## Information Representations and Decision-Making Complexity

Information representations’ visual salience can heighten information processing (Cheung et al., 2017; Yantis & Egeth, 1999). By increasing the visual salience of positive and negative valence and matching their cognitive representations, bivariate representations can attract greater nontemporal attention and motivate deeper processing of positive/negative valence, relative to bipolar representations (Goswami et al., 2008; Hong et al., 2013). Users would thus be expected to engage higher cognitive resources to assess product attributes presented by bivariate representations, which would make them better informed about diverse information aspects and hence improve their decisionmaking, relative to bipolar representations. Under such conditions, heightened decision-making complexity may be aligned with users’ increased cognitive load (Broniarczyk & Griffin, 2014). Therefore, we argue that by heightening information processing, bivariate representations would motivate informed decision-making better than bipolar representations. However, more effortful deliberations may increase cognitive burden (Broniarczyk & Griffin, 2014), making decision-making more complex as individuals recognize the granular details associated with decisionmaking.

H6: Decision complexity experienced by individuals for products with ambivalent information will be higher when information is presented using a bivariate scale versus a bipolar scale.

## Information Representations and Purchase Outcomes

The visual salience of information draws attention and increases its utility, improving individuals’ favorability toward their environment and decision-making (Jiang & Benbasat, 2007; Yantis & Egeth, 1999). In this sense, a bivariate scale would make the strength of both positive and negative aspects of information visually salient and draw users’ attention, allowing them to fully experience and process the conflict between the positive/negative aspects of information under conditions of ambivalence. In fact, the salience of visual artifacts with nontemporal characteristics, such as rating scales, can heighten cognitive involvement, which diverts attention toward the decision task instead of temporal artifacts such as the passage of time (Lee et al., 2012). Such conditions can deepen thinking, heighten information processing, and generate positive affect (Agarwal & Karahanna, 2000). When ambivalent information is presented using bivariate representations, the general positivity and cognitive involvement would thus motivate investment of cognitive resources to resolve decision-making conflicts related to purchase intention.

The positive aspect of ambivalence coupled with the general positive affect of bivariate representations would hence alleviate autonomic reactivity and increase flexibility in decision-making (Fredrickson, 2001). Bipolar representations, in contrast, make no distinction between the respective strengths of positivity and negativity, and would thus lack such an effect. As bivariate representations demand higher cognition, they may lead to decision-making delays that can enhance positive outcomes and could thus undo the negative effect of ambivalence (Fredrickson et al., 2000). When confronted with a choice between products with ambivalent or indifferent information, individuals would thus be more likely to purchase the choice with ambivalent information when it is represented using a bivariate scale instead of a bipolar scale.

H7a-b: The effect of ambivalence on (a) purchase intention and (b) the purchase decision is moderated by the type of rating scale, such that the negative effect of ambivalence would be lower when information is represented with a bivariate scale rather than a bipolar scale.

## Experiment 2: Methodology

## Participants

We recruited participants from the undergraduate and graduate subject pool of a large midwestern university in the United States. Subjects received extra course credit for participation. The average duration of the study was \~30 minutes. We received responses from 65 participants (average age was 22; 40% were women) after dropping participants who incorrectly answered attention-check questions.

## Procedure

The study emulated an online purchase setting, i.e., ecommerce. The task was to purchase three items—a laptop computer, an office chair, and a coffeemaker—in three randomly ordered scenarios. In each scenario, participants had four randomly ordered choices. Each choice (shown in random sequence) had an average rating, a brand indifferent image, and five description points.<sup>16</sup> We manipulated the rating to represent the average valence of peer-generated reviews associated with that choice, and measured choicelevel outcomes after each choice. At the end of each scenario, all four choices were shown to participants, who had to purchase one choice; they could view information for all choices again (Figure 7).

The experiment had a 2×4 mixed design. The bipolar vs. bivariate representation scales were the between-subject manipulation. The within-subject manipulation was the rating of each choice, i.e., positive (5-stars), negative (1- star), ambivalent (2.5 stars), and indifferent (2.5 stars). For two of the three scenarios, the four choices were balanced between the within-subject manipulations of positive, negative, ambivalent, and indifferent. We call these the mix scenarios. For the remaining scenario, the rating composition of four choices was balanced between ambivalent (2 choices) and indifferent (2 choices); we call this the mid scenario. The mid scenario was added to simulate a purchase context in which participants must decide among choices with nonextreme ratings. All choices were randomized.

## Measures

Responses were measured at choice and scenario levels. After viewing each choice, participants reported their ambivalence using the items employed in Experiment 1. Purchase intention and decision complexity were measured using a single item from Valenzuela et al. (2009). Purchase decision was measured at the end of each scenario using a single item (see Appendix). Choice-level measures were recorded for each of the 12 choices, and scenario-level measures were recorded for each of the three scenarios (Table 4).

## Decision Complexity

We found support for H6 in that participants who received the bivariate scale experienced significantly higher decision complexity, compared to those who received the bipolar scale for products with ambivalent ratings (F (1,259) = 6.84, p < 0.05, d = 0.32).

## Purchase Intention

Purchase intention was highest for products with 5-star ratings and lowest for products with 1-star ratings. For choices with an ambivalent rating versus those with an indifferent rating, no significant difference in purchase intention was observed when the bipolar scale was used. However, purchase intention was significantly higher for choices with ambivalent ratings versus indifferent ratings when the bivariate scale was used (F (1,255) = 26.02, p < 0.01, d = 0.63), supporting H7a.

## Purchase Decision

For the mix scenarios, both bipolar and bivariate representations led to the highest purchase of choices with 5- star ratings. Altogether, with the bipolar scale, participants purchased choices, i.e., products, with indifferent ratings 18 times, compared to 15 times for choices with ambivalent ratings. With the bivariate scale, choices with ambivalent ratings were purchased 25 times, relative to 16 times for choices with indifferent ratings. We did not find statistical support for H7b. However, among all purchase decisions, the average purchase for choices with ambivalent information increased by \~73% when the bivariate scale was used, relative to the bipolar scale (F (1,193) = 3.57, p = 0.06, d = 0.27).

## Experiment 2: Discussion

In Experiment 2, we highlight the inability of incumbent bipolar representations to discern nuanced yet extremely prevalent nonextreme valence in peer-generated content. Our findings underscore a potential oversight that can influence the sale of products in online environments and misinform decision makers. By substituting a bipolar scale with a simple bivariate scale, the accuracy of representing information with nonextreme valence significantly improved and discernible purchase outcomes were observed, complementing our findings in Experiment 1. The bivariate representation approach is most impressive because of its economically substantial effect, i.e., a medium to large Cohen’s D of 0.63, in mitigating the negative effect of ambivalence on purchase intention. Albeit nonsignificant, bivariate representations yielded a >70% increase in the final purchase of products with ambivalent ratings. The bivariate scale significantly improved users’ decision complexity with a small to medium effect size of 0.32. In light of other findings, this increased decision complexity indicates a deeper cognitive involvement elicited by bivariate versus bipolar representations. Hence, this effect suggests informed decision-making that results from using bivariate instead of bipolar representations.

There are four concerns that challenge the generalizability of our findings in Experiment 2. First, the novelty of bivariate intervention may explain changes in individuals’ behavior relative to the more popular 5-star rating scale. Second, by itself, the bivariate approach allows individuals to realize ambivalence and thereby increases the purchase of choices with ambivalent ratings. But in practice, individuals may realize ambivalence due to other information, such as reviews accompanying the ratings. This raises the question of whether bivariate representations would continue to increase the purchase of ambivalent choices in the presence of actual reviews. Third, although H7b is not empirically supported, the effect was observed in the hypothesized direction. A potential cause for the empirical nonsupport could be statistical power, which is only marginally above the suggested threshold of 0.80 for the observed effect size (James et al., 2019). Fourth, in Experiment 2, we argue that users would more likely experience heightened cognitive involvement with bivariate representations versus bipolar representations. We conducted Experiment 3 to provide additional support for this argument and address the other concerns.

Experiment 3 was designed by making two changes to Experiment 2: a nonpopular bipolar representation (henceforth, novel bipolar) was added as the third betweensubject manipulation (Figure 8); and each choice in Experiment 2 was appended with two reviews selected from Amazon.com. The rating of the two reviews was averaged to equate the manipulated rating of each choice (Figure 9).

By presenting the latent positivity and negativity in peer-generated content on two separate scales, we show that bivariate representations increase the visual salience of information valence and potentially attract more attention, relative to bipolar representations (Hong et al., 2004).

## Experiment 3: Results

## Manipulation Check

Participants received two concurrent reviews along with average ratings; thus, ambivalence was significantly higher for choices with ambivalent ratings than for choices with indifferent ratings for the bipolar (F (3,863) = 36.16, p < 0.01, d = 0.43), novel bipolar (F (1,871) = 42.44, p < 0.01, d = 0.41), and bivariate (F (1,879) = 133.83, p < 0.01, d = 0.74) groups.

## Decision Complexity

H6 is supported. Decision complexity was significantly higher for the bivariate scale versus the bipolar scale (F (1,2614) = 35.85, p < 0.01, d = 0.23) and novel bipolar scale (F (1,2626) $= 1 6 . 0 7 , p < 0 . 0 1 , d = 0 . 1 5 )$ , respectively, for choices with ambivalent ratings.

## Purchase Intention

Purchase intention was highest for choices with 5-star ratings and lowest for choices with 1-star ratings. No significant difference in purchase intention was observed between choices with ambivalent and indifferent ratings with the bipolar or novel bipolar scales. With the bivariate scale, purchase intention was higher for choices with ambivalent ratings than for those with indifferent ratings (F (1,879) = 18.48, p < 0.01, d = 0.32), supporting H7a.

## Purchase Decision

Most of the participants purchased choices with 5-star ratings for all three between-subject manipulations in the mix scenarios. With the bipolar scale, participants purchased choices with ambivalent ratings 61 times, compared to 59 times for choices with indifferent ratings. With the novel bipolar scale, participants purchased choices with ambivalent ratings 57 times, relative to 70 times for choices with indifferent ratings. With the bivariate scale, participants purchased choices with ambivalent ratings 98 times, relative to 34 times for choices with indifferent ratings. Overall, when the bivariate scale was used, the purchase of products with ambivalent ratings increased by more than 50% and 70%, respectively, relative to the bipolar (F (1,649) = 10.07, p < 0.01, d = 0.25) and novel bipolar scales (F (1,656) = 13.96, p < 0.01, d = 0.29), supporting H7b. No significant difference was observed in the purchase of choices with ambivalent ratings among participants who received either the bipolar or novel bipolar scale.

## Decision Response Time

On average, participants took 1.64 and 1.58 more seconds to express their purchase intention when they were presented with ratings using the bivariate scale relative to the bipolar scale $( F \left( 1 , 2 6 1 3 \right) = 5 . 6 5 , p < 0 . 0 1 , d = 0 . 0 9 )$ and the novel bipolar scale $( F ~ ( 1 , 2 6 2 5 ) ~ = ~ 7 . 3 3 , ~ p ~ < ~ 0 . 0 1 , ~ d ~ = ~ 0 . 1 0 )$ , respectively, providing support for H8.

## Experiment 3: Discussion

Experiment 3 provides strong support for the findings in Experiment 2. Bivariate representations assisted decision makers in holistically realizing nonextreme attitudes and thus prevented the loss of meaningful information that occurs with bipolar representations. The bivariate intervention consistently mitigated the negative effect of ambivalence on purchase behavior, even after controlling for design novelty. The increase in purchase intention persisted even when participants discerned ambivalent choices from indifferent choices. This was not the case with bipolar or novel bipolar representations. We observed a reduction in effect size (d = 0.32) for this increased purchase intention relative to Experiment 2 (d = 0.63), perhaps indicating an adjustment due to the presence of reviews along with ratings. Nevertheless, the economic significance of this effect is substantial considering the growing prevalence of decisionmaking in online contexts.

Participants took much longer to purchase when using the bivariate versus bipolar scale, suggesting increased complexity and potentially higher cognitive involvement. Although the effect was small (d = 0.09, 0.10), it has significant implications considering users’ limited attention span and the ubiquity of online information (Ahn et al., 2018). With a considerable increase in statistical power compared to Experiment 2, we found empirical support for H7b. The bivariate representation scale led to an increased purchase of products with ambivalent information with a small to medium (d = 0.25, 0.29) but economically important size. Experiment 4 seeks a consistent verdict on H7b and examines the role of information sequence (H4) in a richer technology-experience setup due to the presence of reviews.

## Experiment 4

Experiment 1 reveals how the distinct realization of ambivalence and indifference influenced online purchase decisions, and how information sequence explained outcome heterogeneity due to ambivalence. Experiments 2 and 3 extend this understanding by highlighting limitations in incumbent bipolar representations, suggesting a bivariate intervention. These findings are consolidated in Experiment 4 with two objectives. First, the statistical significance of the effect of bivariate representations on purchase decisions (H7b) for products with ambivalent ratings is tested for consistency. Second, the role of information sequence (H4) is tested in a more generalizable user-experience context.

## Experiment 4: Methodology

## Procedure

The design for Experiment 4 was the same as that for Experiment 3, with two changes. First, the two reviews displayed for each choice in Experiment 3 were presented sequentially after the rating information. Review order was randomized but recorded for choices with ambivalent information. Second, the novel bipolar representation was dropped.

## Participants

Participants were undergraduate and graduate students at a large midwestern university in the United States. They received extra course credit for participation. We received 83 responses. Participants’ average age was 22, and 45% were women.

## Measures

We used the choice- and scenario-level measures from Experiment 2. Since the reviews were presented sequentially after the rating information, the time between the presentation of ratings and the measurement of purchase intention was significantly longer than in Experiment 3 and thus does not accurately capture decision response time. ## Experiment 4: Results

## Manipulation Check

No significant difference in ambivalence was observed for choices with ambivalent or indifferent ratings when information was presented using a bipolar scale. This finding could be because of the separation between ratings and the presentation of reviews, indicating the rating scale’s strong influence on participants’ attitudes. When information was presented using the bivariate scale, ambivalence was significantly higher for choices with ambivalent ratings than for those with indifferent ratings (F (1,311) = 22.90, p < 0.01, d = 0.54).

## Decision Complexity

Our results support H6. Decision complexity was significantly higher when ratings were presented using a bivariate scale versus a bipolar scale (F (1,331) = 24.04, p < 0.05, d = 0.53) for choices with ambivalent ratings.

## Purchase Intention

We found support for H7a. With the bivariate scale, purchase intention was significantly higher for choices with ambivalent ratings than for those with indifferent ratings (F (1,311) = 13.47, p < 0.01, d = 0.41). No difference in purchase intention for choices with ambivalent and indifferent ratings was observed with the bipolar scale.

## Purchase Decision

Most participants purchased choices with 5-star ratings for both bipolar and bivariate representations in the mix scenarios. Overall, with the bipolar scale, choices with indifferent ratings were purchased 28 times, compared to 19 for choices with ambivalent ratings. With the bivariate scale, choices with indifferent ratings were purchased seven times, compared to choices with ambivalence ratings, which were purchased 42 times. Altogether, the bivariate scale led to a significant increase of more than 100% in the purchase of choices with ambivalent ratings (F (1,247) = 16.40, p < 0.01, d = 0.51), providing support for H7b.

## Information Sequence

Both positive-first and negative-first sequences enabled participants to experience ambivalence. Purchase intention was much higher when reviews were presented in a positivefirst sequence than negative-first sequence (F (1,154) = 4.35, p < 0.05, d = 0.33), supporting H4.

## Experiment 4: Discussion

Findings in Experiment 4 provide strong support for the findings in Experiments 1, 2, and 3, with statistically significant and practically important effects. We found additional evidence for the role of information sequence in explaining outcome heterogeneity for individuals experiencing ambivalence in an ecologically stronger context than Experiment 1. The effect was statistically significant and meaningful with a small to medium size of d = 0.33. Furthermore, by demonstrating how a bivariate scale increases purchase decisions for choices with ambivalent information, Experiment 4 reconciles findings from Experiments 2 and 3.

## General Discussion

Table 7 presents a summary of our findings. Extant discussions on information valence in online environments have broadly embraced a bipolar viewpoint, limiting the discernibility of perceptions and attitudes to a positivenegative continuum (Adomavicius et al., 2019). Such limitations are also reflected in incumbent representations such as star ratings that aggregate valence with a bipolar distinction. This research confirms that this bipolar approach constrains the understanding of decision-making in online environments, potentially misrepresenting information, and distorting users’ preferences.

In four experiments, we theorized and examined how ambivalence and indifference distinctly affect attention and purchase outcomes, and how sequentially appraised information valence elicits and assists ambivalence resolution. We found that bipolar representations suppress the realization of important nonextreme attitudes and intervened using a simple bivariate approach that permits the realization of such nonextreme attitudes. Our findings demonstrate how ambivalence and indifference, when discernible, significantly and disparately impact decision outcomes in online environments relative to positivity/negativity. We found an impressive and practically substantial increase in the purchase of products with ambivalent ratings of 70%, 50%, and 100%, respectively, across three studies when information was represented with a bivariate scale rather than the incumbent bipolar scale (i.e., star ratings).

In Experiment 2, weak statistical power restricted statistical confidence for H7b. Nevertheless, the effect was in the hypothesized direction. We thus raised the statistical power in Experiment 3 and found support for our conjectures. Compared to a bipolar scale, the bivariate scale consistently motivated more informed decision-making by increasing users’ cognition, as seen in the increased decision complexity and decision response time. Altogether, the bivariate representation motivated cognition, mitigated the negative effect of ambivalence on purchase outcomes, and led to a discernible increase in the purchase of products with ambivalent information.

## Theoretical Contributions

Our key contributions are summarized in Table 8. We found that treating the positivity and negativity of information valence as opposites may simplify the relationship between purchase behaviors and information valence. However, this simultaneously conceals how complex yet prevalently experienced attitudes determine decision-making in online environments (Moody et al., 2017; Xu et al., 2020). We corroborate this premise by theorizing how ambivalence and indifference elicit discernible attentional and purchase outcomes. We thus extend prior research that confirms the prevalence of nonextreme attitudes by theoretically distinguishing their influence relative to extreme attitudes in online environments (Moody et al., 2017). By using a bivariate interpretation of valence, we broaden the framework for this research stream.

Decision-making in online environments often involves multiple sources that often compete with users’ limited attentional resources (Gerlach & Cenfetelli, 2021). Information appraised from myriad sources, such as reviews and descriptions, is likely to evoke mixed feelings such as ambivalence, and affect decision-making (Akhtar et al., 2019; Moody et al., 2017). We extend this by theorizing how the mixed feelings evoked in such conditions are not only formed but also explained by the order in which users receive and process information. The cognitive effect of the leading information valence sets the tone for ambivalence resolution; our findings suggest that ambivalent users purchase more when positive rather than negative information is appraised first. Our research responds to the continued call to examine how ambivalent users resolve decision-making conflicts (van Harreveld et al., 2015).

The prevalence of diverse digital platforms, such as ecommerce and video streaming platforms, stresses the importance of understanding factors that draw users attention (Ahn et al., 2018). Based on NeuroIS methods and prior psychology literature, we theorize that individuals are more cognitively invested when experiencing ambivalence relative to other attitudes in online environments. We found that attention is even higher when the leading valence of the ambivalence-eliciting information sequence is positive. We thus broaden the understanding of determinants of users’ attention in online environments and invite scholars to probe how ambivalence may influence other important behaviors such as information search in such environments (Li et al., 2020).

Information portrayed by graphical representations assists online decision makers and influence platforms’ recommendation strategies (Adomavicius et al., 2021). Knowledge of the role of information valence in users purchase behavior is based on these representation approaches. We theorized and found that incumbent bipolar representations inaccurately aggregate nonextreme valence of information. We intervened with a bivariate representation that accurately expressed the holistic structure of information valence and elicited discernible purchase outcomes. Our findings thus provide an enriched framework for the examination of users’ response to information portrayed on online platforms. The presented bivariate representation approach should also motivate a nuanced reexamination of online platforms’ strategies, such as recommendation accuracy (Adomavicius et al., 2019, 2021).

We contribute to research on the biases and errors in representations that focus on misinformed decision-making in online environments (Ananthakrishnan et al., 2020; Garg & Johari, 2021) by documenting how a bivariate representation approach improves representational accuracy and promulgates informed decision-making. Recent research has emphasized how cognitive elaboration can improve online users’ response to misinformation (Moravec et al., 2019). We found that a bivariate representation approach can motivate cognition and nudge users to spend more time making decisions than a bipolar approach. By answering our three research questions, we thus broaden the understanding of how users experience and resolve mixed feelings as they appraise information from representations. Our findings further advance the discussion on approaches that overcome the limitations of incumbent representations and provide implications for future research.

## Practical Implications

Knowledge of users’ response to the valence of information portrayed by representations often guides how platforms deploy such representations. Based on such knowledge, platforms often conduct experiments and A/B tests to optimize representations and recommendation strategies. Our findings motivate consideration of a wider palate of user attitudes about decision-making to estimate the true impact of information valence on platform metrics. While positivity/negativity result in a higher/lower likelihood of purchase, respectively, our findings suggest how purchase behavior emerges for products with a nonextreme information valence. For example, some 3-star-rated products may be sold more than others, and the order of displayed or processed information explains purchase outcomes for such products. These insights help to determine aspects that can explain variability in product purchase and can potentially affect recommendation strategies.

Insights related to information order, ambivalence, attention, and purchase outcomes from this research can provide a yardstick to ascertain how users’ mixed feelings are transformed into outcomes. Platforms often track real-time activity and clickstream information (Montgomery et al., 2004). Our findings motivate recommendation strategies to provide real-time indications of users’ potential behavior as they sequentially process information. While representations are important decision aids for users and provide vital input for recommendation systems, inaccurate representations can bias recommendations (Adomavicius et al., 2019). We show that bipolar representations can lead to the inaccurate summarization of nonextreme valence. On the contrary, bivariate representations provide a granular expression of information valence, thereby offering a wider pool of emotions that may improve existing recommendation strategies.

Products and vendors add important complementarity to digital platforms. By maximizing their welfare, platforms can improve the value of the overall platform ecosystem. The bivariate representation approach allows for the differentiation of products with ambivalent or indifferent information. Therefore, such an approach can prevent product rejection due to misrepresented indifference or ambivalence, potentially affecting the long tail of ecommerce purchases. Altogether, our findings equip platforms with nuanced insights related to users’ decisionmaking in online environments and advance novel approaches to improving platform strategies. Our findings offer insights to practitioners on how to conduct detailed testing in realistic settings on the use of bivariate representations and their effect on decision-making and the strategies of online platforms.

## Limitations And Future Research

Our examination has limitations that can motivate future research. While the experimental design we employed may cause concerns regarding generalizability, it allowed for control over exogenous factors. We conducted multiple experiments to bolster our findings and addressed various threats to generalizability. We used randomization to control for potential confound due to prior beliefs (Nohlen et al., 2014). Using student subjects is a limitation related to the difficulty of running such studies directly in the field (Koriat et al., 1980). However, the stimuli we used were designed to create an experimental setting that was familiar to student subjects. Although we presented only two attributes per product, our findings should generalize to longer information sequences because, as argued above, users’ cognitive environment is anchored to the valence of leading information.

We invite future researchers to investigate the boundaries and heterogeneities of our findings, perhaps in contexts with longer information sequences. We hope that our research will motivate the exploration of other interventions that may influence the resolution of mixed feelings in online settings. Other NeuroIS methods such as eye-tracking could be used to better understand how users weigh different sources for the resolution of ambivalence and clarify the role of mechanisms such as cognitive absorption, temporal dissociation, or System 2 thinking that may underpin changes in cognition. Our findings could motivate future research on how bivariate representations influence important recommendation metrics. Whether and how this broader valence range can improve platforms’ strategies, such as the accuracy of recommendations, is also a question for future research.

## Conclusion

Users often experience mixed feelings while making decisions in online environments. They not only consume but also generate content that reports their mixed feelings. We conducted this research to examine the role of mixed feelings in online environments to broaden the knowledge related to the role of valence, perceptions, and attitudes in the IS literature. Our experiments reveal how the incumbent representation of information may misguide users’ decisionmaking and platform strategies. We discuss limitations and suggest improvements. Our research thus bridges the gap between theoretical understanding, i.e., the bivariate structure of attitudes and cognition, and practical approaches to representations on digital platforms.

## About the Authors

Akshat Lakhiwal is an assistant professor of management information systems at the University of Georgia’s Terry College of Business. He received an MBA from the Indian Institute of Technology at New Delhi and a PhD in information systems from the Kelley School of Business, Indiana University. His research uses methodologies such as experimentation, econometrics, and machine learning to examine unintended changes in individuals’ reactions and subsequent outcomes emerging from their interaction with information technologies (ITs). He served as the managing editor for MIS Quarterly Executive and was also awarded the Doctoral Student Service Award by the Association of Information Systems in 2021. Akshat’s work has been accepted for publication in MIS Quarterly and Journal of Management Information Systems, and has also been presented at several premier conferences including the Conference on Information Systems and Technology, the Annual INFORMS Meeting, and the Neuro IS Retreat 2020.

Hillol Bala is an associate professor of information systems, the Conrad Prebys Professor, and the co-director of the Institute for Digital Enterprise (IDE) at the Kelley School of Business, Indiana University, Bloomington. His Ph.D. (information systems) is from the Walton College of Business, University of Arkansas. His research interests are related to enterprise systems implementation; digital transformation in organizations (including healthcare organizations); and technology use, adaptation, and impacts. His work has appeared in premier academic journals, such as MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Management Science, Production and Operations Management, Journal of Management Information Systems, Journal of Operations Management, IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering, Journal of the Association for Information Systems, Decision Sciences, European Journal of Information Systems, Information Systems Journal, The Information Society, Communications of the ACM, and MIS Quarterly Executive. He has held editorial roles for major information systems journals (e.g., MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, and Journal of the Association for Information Systems) and conferences.

Pierre-Majorique Léger is a professor of information technologies at HEC Montreal and he is the chairholder of the Research Chair in User Experience (UX). He holds a Ph.D. in industrial engineering from École Polytechnique de Montréal and has done postdoctoral studies in information technologies at HEC Montréal and NYU Stern School of Business. He is the co-director of the Tech3Lab, a NeuroIS research laboratory devoted to the investigation of human factors in information technologies. He is also the director of the ERPsim Lab and the principal inventor of ERPsim, a simulation game to teach ERP concepts now used in more than 40,000 students per year in 250 universities worldwide. He is also the co-founder of the NeuroIS Society. He is currently financed by The Natural Sciences and Engineering Research Council of Canada, Social Sciences and Humanities Research Council, and Fonds de Recherche du Québec—Société et Culture.
