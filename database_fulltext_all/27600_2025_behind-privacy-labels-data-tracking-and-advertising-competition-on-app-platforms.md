# BEHIND PRIVACY LABELS: DATA TRACKING AND ADVERTISING COMPETITION ON APP PLATFORMS<sup>1</sup>

Yi Gao Area of Information Systems and Quantitative Sciences, Rawls College of Business, Texas Tech University Lubbock, TX, U.S.A. {Yi.Gao@ttu.edu}

Meilin Gu

Department of Information Systems, W. P. Carey School of Business, Arizona State University Tempe, AZ, U.S.A. {Meilin.Gu@asu.edu}

Dengpan Liu Department of Management Science and Engineering, School of Economics and Management, Tsinghua University Beijing, CHINA {liudp@sem.tsinghua.edu.cn}

Recently, a controversial new privacy policy on mobile app platforms, which requires app developers to display privacy labels and explicitly request data-tracking permissions from users, has sparked a heated discussion among practitioners in digital advertising. In this paper, we build a game-theoretic model to examine how this new policy impacts the key stakeholders of a mobile app platform (i.e., app developers, the platform, and consumers). The model captures how the new policy prompts developers to expand their strategies beyond pricing by introducing data-tracking levels as an additional competitive dimension, which in turn affects the intensity of price competition. We find that while implementing a policy restricting developers’ tracking of consumers may increase the platform’s advertising revenue, it can also put the platform at a disadvantage. This is because it may incentivize developers to lower their prices and engage in more intense price competition, consequently reducing the platform’s commission revenue. Regarding app developers, the new policy may prove beneficial despite its restriction to tracking only consumers who have opted in. Another noteworthy finding is that while platforms have asserted that the new policy is aimed at safeguarding consumers’ privacy, it does not always serve consumers’ best interests. Our paper provides meaningful implications for all key stakeholders of mobile app platforms regarding the implementation of the new privacy policy.

Keywords: Privacy labels, data-tracking level, app platform, digital advertising, analytical modeling

## Introduction

In the era of big data, data privacy has emerged as one of the most critical yet challenging issues faced by society as a whole (Angst & Agarwal, 2009; Meehan, 2019). This issue holds particular significance in the realm of digital advertising (Tucker, 2014), a rapidly expanding market valued at \$567.49 billion in 2022 (Statista, 2023). On the one hand, numerous companies have achieved significant operational enhancements (e.g., improved targeted advertising) by harnessing valuable business insights derived from vast amounts of consumer data, often at the expense of consumer privacy (Chen & Stallaert, 2014). Consequently, these enhanced operational efficiencies benefit consumers by providing them with precisely tailored products or services (Johnson et al., 2020). On the other hand, there is a growing awareness among consumers regarding data privacy protection (Hu et al., 2010; Kummer & Schulte, 2019). Some individuals are so apprehensive about falling victim to privacy breaches that they may even choose to avoid companies that collect excessive personal information (Gal-Or et al., 2018). Meanwhile, a global trend is emerging toward the implementation of stringent data protection regulations. Notable examples include the General Data Protection Regulation, enacted by the European Union in 2016, and the California Consumer Privacy Act, passed by the California state legislature in 2018. These regulations aim to empower users to maintain control over their data, obligating companies to act responsibly when utilizing users’ personal information (Keane, 2021).<sup>2</sup>

## Motivation

Under stringent regulations, technology firms are generally obligated to disclose how data is collected and used, while also granting users the right to decide whether to allow data tracking. App platforms have swiftly adapted to this evolving landscape of privacy protection. Recently, Apple introduced several privacy-related changes in its operating system with the release of iOS 14 (Miller, 2020). These changes are expected to disrupt the in-app digital advertising business, a fast-growing market projected to reach \$544.93 billion by 2028 (Verified Market Research, 2023). Under the new privacy policy, users can access privacy settings through the “App Privacy” menu. As shown in the left panel of Figure 1, app developers are now required to display privacy labels that explicitly describe the types of data their apps may collect, including data used to track users, data linked to users, and data not linked to users. The primary aim of these privacy labels is to provide users with standardized and easily understandable information about how apps collect and utilize their data.

In addition, under this new policy, when users launch an app for the first time, a tracking notification window will appear, requesting their consent for data tracking (as shown in the right panel of Figure 1). Users then decide whether to grant tracking permission, with estimates suggesting that only a small fraction —less than 10% of users—will be willing to grant app developers access to their data (Statt, 2021). Consequently, if a developer decides to track more data, the quantity of information collected from each consenting user will increase, but the number of users permitting tracking will decrease (Gal-Or et al., 2018). Faced with this trade-off, developers must optimally choose their data-tracking levels under this new privacy policy (Whitney, 2020).

Before the implementation of the new privacy policy, app developers typically provided users with lengthy and complex privacy policy documents. In such cases, consumers were essentially opting into tracking by default, as only a small fraction of them would carefully review the policy document (Guynn, 2020) and exercise their rights to control how their data was collected and used by developers. For ease of exposition, we hereafter refer to this scenario as the opt-in regime. In contrast, under the new privacy policy, app developers are now obligated to present privacy labels that are easily understandable to users and explicitly seek their permission before tracking their data. In this case, users, now aware of how their data is collected and used by app developers, are essentially opting out of tracking by default and exercising caution when deciding whether to grant developers access to their data. We henceforth refer to this scenario as the opt-out regime.

The app platforms have asserted that this new privacy policy will benefit users by ensuring they have a clear understanding of how their data is collected and used. However, certain app companies, such as Facebook and Snap, whose revenue heavily relies on online advertising, have voiced concerns that the privacy policy change could diminish the effectiveness of targeted ads and potentially disrupt the online advertising industry (Leswing, 2021b). Moreover, some app developers have gone so far as to accuse app platforms of engaging in anticompetitive behavior. They argue that these platforms leverage control over application stores in a way that benefits their own bottom line at the expense of app developers (Graham, 2021).

The concerns raised regarding the new privacy policy are not unfounded, especially in the context of mobile app advertising. Many professionals in the digital advertising industry believe that this policy change could potentially bring side benefits to app platforms, particularly in relation to their search ad business (an example of such ads is shown in the left panel of Figure 2) (Murphy & McGee, 2021).<sup>3</sup> Such side benefits mainly stem from the fact that this policy change will not adversely affect platforms, as they typically rely solely on first-party data and refrain from cross-site tracking to identify their target consumers (Leswing, 2021a).<sup>4</sup> In contrast, app developers typically rely on cross-site tracking, which involves tracking users across other companies’ apps or websites, to identify their target audience (Leswing, 2021a). Under the new privacy policy, the number of users granting permission for data tracking is expected to drop significantly, posing challenges for app developers in delivering accurate and customized ads. This, in turn, will reduce the effectiveness of their in-app ads, as illustrated in the right panel of Figure 2. For convenience, we hereafter refer to the ads displayed on platforms as platform ads and those displayed within apps as in-app ads. Since advertisers seeking to promote their apps might consider both platforms and apps as viable

The impact of the privacy policy on advertising competition between platforms and developers has drawn considerable attention. News articles (e.g., Leswing, 2021a; Murphy & McGee, 2021) suggest that Apple’s privacy policy may pose challenges for app developers, potentially reducing the effectiveness of their in-app ads and boosting Apple’s own ad business.

In addition to its impact on advertising competition, the new policy is expected to have implications for the pricing strategies of app developers. There have been predictions that certain free apps may begin to charge users for their services as a result of the new policy (Graham, 2020). This change in pricing strategy could potentially benefit app platforms by enabling them to generate additional commission revenue from app sales.

In this paper, we conduct an in-depth investigation of the aforementioned controversial issues, focusing on the impact of the new privacy policy on digital advertising competition, particularly in mobile app advertising. Specifically, we analyze how this policy affects advertising competition between platforms and app developers, as well as its effect on app pricing competition. Moreover, we examine the impact of this policy change on consumer welfare. Our analysis provides valuable managerial insights for key stakeholders of mobile app platforms.

## Research Questions and Key Findings

Some app platforms (e.g., Apple) have indicated that their implementation of the new policy is driven by altruistic motives, particularly to safeguard consumer privacy (Graham, 2021). However, recent articles (e.g., Leswing, 2021a) suggest that the adoption of this new policy could potentially benefit app platforms by boosting their own advertising business. To explore this contentious issue, we first ask: Will the app platform benefit from adopting the privacy policy? Interestingly, we find that the platform can sometimes be worse off under the privacy policy. The underlying rationale is as follows. With the new privacy policy, in addition to app prices, consumers factor in apps’ data-tracking levels when making download decisions. This prompts developers to expand their strategies beyond pricing by incorporating data-tracking levels as an additional competitive dimension. Under the privacy policy, developers may track fewer consumers compared to the optin regime, potentially increasing the platform’s advertising revenue. However, intensified price competition may drive developers to lower their prices, resulting in a decline in the platform’s commission revenue. Consequently, the platform’s potential gain in advertising revenue is insufficient to offset the loss in commission revenue, ultimately leaving the platform worse off.

Another aspect of the heated discussion surrounding the implementation of this new policy lies in its potential impact on app developers’ pricing strategies and profits. One ongoing debate centers on whether app developers should raise their prices or even convert their free apps into paid versions (Graham, 2020). Furthermore, companies like Facebook have raised concerns that the privacy policy change could harm their profitability and potentially disrupt the entire advertising industry (Leswing, 2021b). This naturally gives rise to two important questions: Will app prices be higher under the optout regime compared to the opt-in regime? And will app developers benefit from this new privacy policy? Our findings suggest that the answer to the first question can sometimes be affirmative. Specifically, when consumers are highly sensitive to data tracking, developers tend to raise their prices under the new policy. This is because, in such cases, when the privacy policy is implemented, developers set lower data-tracking levels. Consequently, they generate less advertising revenue per consumer, leading to reduced app price competition and elevated app prices. As a result, certain apps that were previously offered for free under the opt-in regime are no longer free under the opt-out regime. While practitioners often attribute this potential increase in app prices to developers’ inability to afford to offer free (or low-priced) products supported by advertising under the opt-out regime (Graham, 2020), we propose an alternative explanation. Specifically, this price increase may also stem from the intricate interplay between developers’ price competition and the additional dimension of competition in data tracking. In terms of app developers’ profitability, our analysis reveals that, in some cases, developers can still benefit from the privacy policy despite tracking fewer consumers.

The controversy surrounding the opt-out regime also extends to its potential effects on consumer welfare. App platforms (e.g., Apple) often position themselves as champions of consumer privacy, claiming that their adoption of the privacy policy stems from a belief that “privacy is a fundamental human right” (Kelly, 2021). However, there are ongoing concerns that the privacy policy might lead consumers to begin paying for certain apps that were previously free, thereby negatively affecting their surplus (Sokol & Zhu, 2021). These debates prompt questions about whether the policy change truly serves to enhance consumer welfare. Thus, the question arises: Will consumers benefit from the new privacy policy? Surprisingly, the answer to this question is not always positive. Although consumers gain more control over their data, they may also face the downside of paying higher app prices under the opt-out regime, which could diminish their surplus. Furthermore, our research reveals that the interests of key stakeholders are not always aligned. For example, although the platform may benefit from adopting the new policy, developers and consumers can be worse off under certain circumstances.

In addition to the aforementioned research questions, we explore a set of “what-if” normative questions for further insights. In this regard, we extend our analysis to various scenarios, including those where app developers have heterogeneous app quality, the platform implements a revenue-sharing model, the market is not fully covered, and app developers display both mobile app ads and product ads. Our analysis demonstrates that the key insights derived from the main model still hold under these extensions.

## Contributions

Our research makes substantial contributions from both theoretical and practical perspectives. Theoretically, first, our work contributes to the literature on in-app advertising by being the earliest analysis of the new privacy policy’s impact on mobile app platform stakeholders, addressing an underexplored area: advertising competition between the platform and app developers in the context of data privacy. We also analyze the platform’s decision to adopt this policy and its impact on strategic interactions with app developers, revealing a novel mechanism through which the platform can gain an advantage.

Second, our study is among the first to examine the changes in the consumer decision structure resulting from the platform’s adoption of the privacy policy. Specifically, under the opt-in regime, consumers make download and app-usage decisions sequentially—choosing, after downloading, whether to discontinue use due to excessive data tracking. In contrast, under the new privacy policy, consumers observe data-tracking levels via privacy labels and make decisions about downloading and granting datatracking permissions, thereby optimizing the combined utilities of data tracking and app usage.

Third, we contribute to the data privacy literature by endogenizing developers’ data-tracking decisions. We uncover the significant changes that policy adoption introduces to the trade-offs developers face in setting datatracking levels and explore the interplay between their price competition and this new dimension of competition in data tracking. We further contribute to this stream of literature by offering an alternative explanation for the negative relationship, as documented in previous studies, between app prices and data-tracking levels. Lastly, we contribute to the literature on multichannel advertising by examining how the sequence of consumers engaging with ads across channels (outlets) affects interoutlet competition within the context of data privacy.

From a practical standpoint, our study provides important implications for all key stakeholders involved in mobile app platforms regarding the new privacy policy. First, while Apple has already implemented this privacy policy, some other app platforms have yet to do so. Therefore, our findings can serve as guidance for app platforms in determining whether and when to adopt the privacy policy. Moreover, our findings convey a distinct message to platforms that raising consumers awareness of data tracking and restricting developers’ data tracking may not always align with their best interests. They must remain mindful of the evolving competitive strategies of developers, which, under the new privacy policy, include not only pricing but also data tracking. Second, our results suggest that although developers may experience a decline in advertising revenue, they can still benefit from the new policy. Thus, developers need not be overly concerned about this privacy policy change. They should, however, make informed decisions about the types and amounts of data they track, as it is not advantageous to engage in excessive data tracking. Third, consumers should be mindful that policies aimed at protecting their privacy can, in fact, hurt them. Therefore, they should exercise caution when choosing which platform to engage with. Finally, policymakers should devise appropriate regulatory policies on app pricing to safeguard consumer interests when the new privacy policy is implemented.

## Literature Review

Our work is mainly related to three streams of literature: (1) economics of privacy in information systems (IS), (2) multichannel advertising, and (3) in-app advertising. Next, we provide a brief review of the related work and highlight our contributions to each stream.

## Economics of Privacy in IS

In the following two subsections, we briefly review the literature on privacy issues in online advertising, as well as research on incentives for privacy information disclosure.

## Privacy Issues in Online Advertising

Growing concern over privacy in online advertising arises as advertisers often adopt approaches like behavioral targeting to track consumers, thereby risking the leakage of personal data (Gao et al., 2020). Prior studies (e.g., Tucker 2014; Johnson et al., 2020) have empirically investigated how users’ privacy concerns affect ad effectiveness. From an analytical perspective, researchers have investigated the impact of targeted ads and information disclosure on firm profitability. For example, Chen and Stallaert (2014) studied whether a monopoly online publisher should adopt behavioral targeting and examined its impact on the publisher’s and advertisers payoffs, as well as on social welfare. To the best of our knowledge, there is scant literature that endogenizes the firm’s decision on data-tracking levels. One of the most closely related works is Gal-Or et al. (2018), where the authors endogenized firms’ data-tracking levels and study the impact of users’ privacy concerns on the competition between two online advertising firms. Unlike their model setup, we consider a mobile app context where app developers compete with the app platform in advertising and must provide users with privacy labels. We also investigate the price competition between app developers and how such competition may be affected by their decisions regarding data tracking levels.

## Incentives for Privacy Information Disclosure

A growing body of literature has examined the incentives users have for disclosing their private information. For example, Casadesus-Masanell and Hervas-Drane (2015) analyzed the impact of consumer privacy on competition in a marketplace in which consumers can choose which firm to patronize and how much personal information to disclose. More recently, Kummer and Schulte (2019) examined the money-for-privacy trade-off in a mobile app market in which developers charge lower app prices in exchange for greater access to personal information. Their results show that less expensive apps typically ask users for more privacy-sensitive permissions. Using a natural experiment, Pu et al. (2020) investigated the effect of private information disclosure (i.e., identity disclosure) on users’ content-generation behaviors in an online community. Unlike this stream of literature, our study takes it a step further by focusing on firms’ decisions regarding data-tracking levels. Specifically, we fill the gap in the literature by examining how apps’ data-tracking decisions affect their price competition in a mobile app market when the new privacy policy is implemented.

## Multichannel Advertising

Online advertisers often employ multiple channels to display ads to their users, a strategy commonly referred to as multichannel advertising (Li & Kannan, 2014). There is a growing body of literature on this topic, with most studies focusing on its effectiveness from an empirical perspective (e.g., Olbrich & Schultz, 2014; Danaher et al., 2020). As an example of the limited analytical research in this stream of literature, Zhang (2009) examined when online retailers should adopt the multichannel strategy and advertise their instore prices in a competitive context, finding that multichannel retailing is not necessarily the best strategy for all retailers. More recently, Berman (2018) examined a situation where information is symmetric between advertisers and publishers and analytically studied how inefficiencies from externalities and uncertainty impact advertisers’ profits across different channels.

Unlike this stream of literature, which focuses on the effectiveness of multichannel advertising strategies, our work is among the earliest attempts to use an analytical method to examine multichannel advertising within the context of data privacy. Specifically, we analytically investigate how the sequence in which consumers engage with ads across different channels (outlets) shapes the impact of the new privacy policy on advertising competition among the platform and developers.

## In-App Advertising

Mobile in-app advertising, a highly prevalent form of digital advertising, has attracted considerable scholarly attention (e.g., Sun et al., 2017; Aseri et al., 2018). Of particular relevance to our study are the existing analytical works exploring the dynamics between app platforms and developers, most of which focus on scenarios in which the app platform attains advertising revenue and then shares a portion with the developers (e.g., Hao et al., 2017; Ji et al., 2019). Specifically, Hao et al. (2017) examined how platform owners should optimize advertising revenuesharing contracts under agency pricing for app sales. More recently, Ji et al. (2019) employed a differential game model to investigate when the platform should adopt an in-app advertising program and how the platform and developers should optimally adjust their advertising efforts.

Our study differs from the existing literature on in-app advertising in several notable ways. First, unlike prior studies of in-app advertising, which have primarily focused on the revenue-sharing contract between platforms and app developers, our research delves into the impact of the privacy policy change on the advertising competition between the platform and app developers. This issue, central to the controversy surrounding the privacy policy change (e.g., Lovejoy, 2021), remains relatively underexplored in the existing literature. Second, our study also addresses a novel trade-off related to competition strategies among developers, encompassing two dimensions: app pricing and data-tracking levels. In particular, with the advent of the new privacy policy, consumers can observe developers’ datatracking levels and face an additional decision—whether to allow data tracking—when selecting which app to download. Consequently, developers can compete not only based on app prices but also on data-tracking levels. This introduction of a new competitive dimension has significant implications for pricing and data-tracking strategies, as well as for the payoffs of key stakeholders, including developers, the platform, and consumers, which cannot be directly derived from existing models in the literature

## App Developers’ Problem

Following prior literature (e.g., Hao et al., 2017), we assume that app developers can generate revenue by selling apps (referred to as transaction revenue). Each developer offers an app and sets its price $p _ { i I }$ . Developers share a proportion ?? (the platform’s commission rate) of the transaction revenue with the platform, while incurring no additional costs. Consistent with the literature $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , Zhou & Zou, 2022), we assume that the commission rate is fixed and exogenously determined.<sup>12</sup>

In addition to the transaction revenue, app developers also generate revenue through displaying in-app ads to consumers who use the apps (referred to as advertising revenue). In practice, advertisers typically target consumers through multiple channels to reach as many consumers as possible. In our model, we assume that ads are displayed through all outlets (i.e., apps A and B, as well as the platform). As suggested in many studies (e.g., Krämer et al., 2019), the first ad impression holds the highest significance, whereas subsequent impressions contribute significantly less value. Thus, we assume that all outlets can only generate advertising revenue from the consumers’ first ad impressions,<sup>13</sup> thereby engaging in direct advertising competition for consumers.

As for consumers who download app i but stop using it, they cannot be targeted by in-app ads. We assume that these consumers will not contribute to developers’ advertising revenue and may only be potentially targeted by the platform’s ads. In contrast, consumers who continue using app i can engage with both the app itself and the platform during their daily usage. The sequence in which they engage with these two outlets can vary depending on the context, and this variation has important implications for the advertising competition between the platform and developers. For example, consider a user deciding to order food delivery. This user might first open the App Store (i.e., the platform) to search for and download a delivery app like Uber Eats. Alternatively, if the app is already installed and frequently used, the user may bypass the platform altogether and launch the Uber Eats app directly from their smartphone. When consumers interact with app i more frequently before accessing the platform, the probability of being targeted by in-app ads surpasses that of platform ads. As a result, app i gains an advantage over the platform in terms of advertising revenue. ## App Developers’ Problem

First, our results align with practitioners’ concerns that apps that were previously free may now decide to charge consumers for services due to the new privacy policy. However, we offer an alternative explanation for this price hike, suggesting that it may result from developers’ reduced data-tracking levels and intensified competition in data tracking, driven by the presence of privacy labels. Consequently, even if developers can afford to offer their apps for free, they may still be incentivized to raise app prices under this new policy. Moreover, Proposition 1 suggests that consumers’ data-tracking sensitivity plays a crucial role in determining the change in app prices across the two regimes. In practice, developers can estimate their consumers’ datatracking sensitivity based on app categories and the types of data they collect. Moreover, developers can conduct randomized experiments to investigate other factors influencing consumers’ sensitivity to data tracking.

From a theoretical standpoint, our finding that app prices increase when data-tracking levels are reduced aligns with previous research on the disclosure of privacy information. Specifically, Kummer and Schulte (2019) demonstrated, through empirical evidence, that more expensive apps typically collect less information from consumers. However, unlike the setup and methodology adopted by Kummer and Schulte (2019), we analytically investigate this issue by considering the intricate interplay between developers’ price competition and the additional dimension: competition in data tracking. This allows us to provide an alternative explanation for the negative relationship between app prices and datatracking levels. Moreover, while the extant literature (e.g., Kollnig et al., 2022) has found that developers may not always reduce data-tracking levels under Apple’s new privacy policy, our study delves deeper to uncover the underlying mechanism and identifies the specific conditions under which this phenomenon is likely to occur.

Consequently, the platform may achieve higher overall revenue by setting a data-tracking level lower than the maximum, as this incentivizes developers to adopt higher data-tracking levels, thereby allowing the platform and developers to collectively exploit the advertising potential of consumers.

For instance, when the market includes a large number of privacysensitive consumers, the platform should adopt a relatively low level of data tracking.

## Concluding Remarks

Recently, a new and controversial privacy policy in the mobile app market has emerged, which requires app developers to display privacy labels and explicitly request data tracking permissions from users, sparking broad discussion among practitioners. Although platforms have claimed that their primary motivation for adopting this new policy is to safeguard consumer privacy, it remains unclear whether their actions are driven by self-interest and how this policy change will impact both app developers and consumers. In this paper, we built a game-theoretic model to examine these important issues.

Our study yields several interesting findings. First, our results demonstrate that under certain conditions, the adoption of the new privacy policy can result in increased app prices, leading some previously free apps to begin charging for services. Additionally, we find that adopting the new privacy policy may not always be beneficial to the platform. In fact, embracing the new policy can lead developers to increase data-tracking levels and lower prices, which negatively impacts the platform’s profitability by reducing both its advertising and commission revenue. Moreover, despite tracking fewer consumers with the platform’s new privacy policy in place, app developers can still end up better off. Another noteworthy finding is that although the platforms assert the new policy will safeguard consumers’ privacy, it may not necessarily benefit them, as they might end up paying higher app prices.

Our paper contributes to the literature in several aspects. First, our work contributes to the literature on in-app advertising by analytically investigating the impact of the new privacy policy, which requires app developers to display privacy labels and explicitly request users’ tracking permissions, and by examining the strategic interplay between app developers and the platform. Second, our study is among the first to examine the changes in the consumer decision-making structure triggered by the implementation of a privacy policy. Third, we contribute to the literature on data privacy by endogenizing app developers’ decisions regarding datatracking levels. Our study examines the significant changes in developers’ trade-offs resulting from policy adoption when determining these levels. Further, it investigates the intricate interplay between their price competition and the additional dimension of competition in data tracking. Moreover, our research offers an alternative explanation for the negative relationship, as reported in the literature, between app prices and data-tracking levels. Fourth, our study is among the first to examine issues related to multichannel advertising within the context of data privacy. Specifically, we explored how the sequence of consumers engaging with ads across various channels (outlets) affects inter-outlet competition.

Our results provide valuable insights that can help app platforms decide whether and when to adopt the new policy. Our results also convey an important message to platforms: Imposing restrictions on developers’ data tracking may inadvertently harm platforms, especially when consumers exhibit a relatively low sensitivity towards data tracking. Notably, there is a discrepancy in privacy concerns among users of various platforms, with iOS users typically having greater concerns about privacy than users of other platforms (Hatamoto, 2020). This, along with our findings, could shed light on why Apple has been at the forefront of adopting the new privacy policy. Furthermore, while app developers may face a decline in advertising revenue due to the new policy, they can still benefit under certain circumstances, especially for apps that collect non-sensitive data (e.g., weather apps). Another key takeaway from this study is that policies claiming to protect consumers’ privacy may actually harm them. Thus, consumers need to be fully aware of the downsides of the new privacy policy and strategically choose which platforms to engage with. Additionally, since app prices may increase under the new privacy policy, policymakers should devise appropriate regulatory measures on app pricing to better protect consumers’ interests.
