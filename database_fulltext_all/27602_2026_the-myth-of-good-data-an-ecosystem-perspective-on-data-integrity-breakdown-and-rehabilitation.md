# THE MYTH OF GOOD DATA: AN ECOSYSTEM PERSPECTIVE ON DATA INTEGRITY BREAKDOWN AND REHABILITATION<sup>1</sup>

Kathleen H. Pine College of Health Solutions, Arizona State University Los Angeles, CA, U.S.A {khpine@asu.edu}

Melissa Mazmanian

Department of Informatics, University of California, Irvine Irvine, CA, U.S.A. {mmazmani@uci.edu}

In recent years, a huge amount of excitement has surrounded the potential to transform organizations and institutions through evidence-based or data-driven decision-making. However, such promises are often premised on a stable view of data quality. In this paper, we take a practice perspective on data integrity, asking how attempts to leverage data at one point in a data ecosystem can ripple unexpectedly, triggering a breakdown in the integrity of data resources at another point in the ecosystem. We further outline the intensive efforts involved in rehabilitating data when integrity is undermined. Through a multisited ethnographic study in multiple healthcare organizations, we trace four empirical examples of situated data integrity breakdown (negotiated category breakdown, category expansion breakdown, data mismatch breakdown, and data disaggregation breakdown), linking the triggers, responses, and rehabilitation efforts in each case. We argue that organizations need to take the ongoing work of retraining personnel, modifying IT systems, and/or shifting data collection practices into account to rehabilitate data whose integrity will be inevitably undermined as a result of shifting needs, goals, expectations, and/or IT systems across a connected data ecosystem. In so doing, we offer insights into the often unpredictable impacts of attempts to leverage data, thereby enriching the discourse on data-driven decision-making and its implications for organizational strategy and practice.

Keywords: Data-driven decision-making, data analytics, data management, data integrity, data quality, data work, data rehabilitation, ethnography

## Introduction

In recent years, a huge amount of attention and excitement has been paid to the potential of predictive analytics, big data, machine learning, and generative AI to help organizations by using data to identify and solve problems, complete decision process tasks, and make decisions (i.e., Li et al., 2022; Nzeako et al., 2024; Rana & Shuford, 2024). Evidence-based management celebrates decisions based on “the hard facts and truth about what works and what doesn’t” (Pfeffer & Sutton, 2006: p. 13). Similarly, data-driven decision-making has gained prominence broadly as organizations strive to make “data-driven decisions,” which are decisions that are made “on the basis of evidence, rather than intuition” (McAfee & Brynjolfsson, 2012 p. 62).

The promises of data-driven decision-making lie in an organization’s ability to extract insights from data resources to make strategic and process improvements (Bharadwaj et al., 2013). Current advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) further increase the power of datadriven innovations to provide meaningful insights to organizations (Bader & Kaiser, 2019; Chintala & Thiyagarajan, 2023). The wave of enthusiasm for what data can achieve has been promoted by popular management outlets (Agrawal et al., 2024; Anderson, 2015; Bean & Gupta, 2021; Greeven et al., 2023; Leonardi & Contractor, 2018; McAfee & Brynjolfsson, 2012) and is reflected in a burgeoning stream of scholarly work across domains (Bhatia & Mittal, 2019; Brynjolfsson et al., 2011; Côrte-Real et al., 2017; Grover et al., 2018; LaValle et al., 2011; Sleep et al., 2019; Souravlas, & Roumeliotis, 2019; Troisi et al., 2020; Blohm et al., 2024).

Despite the promise of data to determine the best course of action, diagnose an organizational issue, or anticipate a future need, studies estimate that investments in data-driven initiatives add little to no value for organizations (OECD, 2015; Yeoh & Popovič, 2016). The failure of initiatives in data-driven decision-making results in tremendous cost to organizations (Liu et al., 2018; Redman, 1995) and often does not yield expected benefits and results (Božič & Dimovski, 2019; Ranjbarfard & Hatami, 2020; Villamarín-García, 2020). Thus, organizations are faced with a critical question: What is getting in the way of reaping the benefits of datadriven initiatives? We address this question by looking at situated ramifications of using data-driven insights to make strategic decisions and shift organizational practice in the context of the U.S. healthcare system.

Through a multi-sited ethnographic engagement with a hospital system (Hospital System) in the western United States, a mesolevel healthcare policy organization, and additional stakeholders in the broader healthcare field, we examined four empirical examples of data-driven decision-making (called evidence-based medicine in this context) leading to data breakdowns within local settings. Within the domain of healthcare, our field sites were particularly concerned about cultivating data resources in a way that would be in line with federal requirements for data reporting and would accurately reflect performance, knowing that poor performance on certain measures would lead to financial loss, institutional/public sanction, and/or loss of competitive advantage.

We find that the answer to why data initiatives struggle lies in developing a new understanding of what data is and how it operates. Conceptualizing data as neither “good” nor “bad,” we theorize data as having integrity (i.e., the alignment between data resources and the situated needs, expectations, and organizational and technological infrastructures of a particular use scenario). In so doing, we show that the integrity of data is entwined with situated contexts of data in use. Further, by examining moments where data loses integrity in sudden and unexpected ways, we theorize what we have come to call an ecosystem perspective on data in use. This perspective reveals how data-informed decisions made at one location in a data ecosystem can undermine data integrity at another location. Finally, our findings suggest that the immense efforts required by organizations to rehabilitate the integrity of local data when it is undermined by shifting contexts of use represent one reason that data-driven initiatives struggle to achieve a return on investment.

Specifically, we argue that data rehabilitation is not about fixing data itself. Instead, just as rehabilitating the body after an injury may require strengthening the muscles around the site of injury or rewiring the nervous system, rehabilitating data often requires multi-pronged efforts that go far beyond traditional ideas of what it takes to “clean” data resources. We find that the organizational response to breakdowns in data integrity reflects a number of elements, including the degree to which breakdowns could be anticipated or detected; the perceived stakes of the breakdown; and possible pathways for rehabilitating data (i.e., cleaning existing data, retraining personnel responsible for collecting data, and/or building or reprogramming technological systems.

Further, we find that efforts to rehabilitate data integrity often involve retraining personnel to collect data in new ways, convincing people to adjust their local definitions of data, or supporting the development of new data capture technologies. These practices require an enormous amount of time, labor, expertise, social capital, and financial investment. In tracing both the triggers and the organizational responses to situated data integrity breakdowns, we shed light on the fragility of data and the often hidden and underappreciated costs of continually rehabilitating data resources.

## Related Work

## Data Quality: A Garbage in, Garbage Out Perspective

The power of data analytics to improve organizational functioning (by making complex decisions on the basis of data rather than intuition) has been touted as beneficial for organizational functioning in such diverse domains as business, manufacturing, building maintenance, city government, education, and healthcare (Brynjolfsson & McElheran, 2016; Kriegova et al., 2021; Kurilovas, 2020; Ma et al., 2020; Mandinach, 2012; Marsh & Farrell, 2015; Troisi et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2020). Engaging in data-driven decision-making is expected to improve organizational and worker performance (Masha, 2014; McAfee & Brynjolfsson, 2012; Nguyen, 2016). Despite growing adoption of data tools to enhance data-driven decision-making, drawing a direct connection between investment in data-driven decision-making and organizational value has proven difficult (Côrte-Real et al., 2017).

Scholarship has shown that one of the key reasons that organizations fail to derive value from adoption and investment in data-driven tools and practices is poor data quality (Baesens et al., 2016; Jensen et al., 2019; Jetzek et al., 2014; Lennerholt & van Laere, 2019; Ermakova et al., 2021); according to the popular adage “garbage in, garbage out,” the results produced by data tools are only as good as the data that is fed into them in the first place. Indeed, scholars have long been interested in identifying the dimensions of data quality.

As outlined by Jayawardene et al. (2013) and Zhang et al. (2019), the dimensions of data quality are as follows. Completeness is the extent to which a dataset contains all of the records and data points (i.e., data fields in a record are complete and do not contain null values) required to characterize a thing of interest. Validity is the extent to which data are collected in accordance with particular rules or specifications, such as particular measurements or formulas applicable to those data. Validity denotes the existence of standardized rules for data collection and the extent to which data conform to these rules; it is present when data are collected according to standardized rules and protocols. Consistency is the degree to which data are recorded following the same measures and notations, making data comparable. Where validity requires adherence to protocols for measurement and data entry, consistency rests on consistent data recording practices across data recorders, data recording sites, and time. Thus, different and disparate people across sites measure, document, enter, and code data in consistent ways over time. Accuracy denotes the extent to which data agree with the original phenomenon that was rendered as data. For example, in a clinical medical setting, that means that the data contained in various datasets reflect the events that actually occurred in a healthcare case—i.e., a patient’s vital signs, history, diagnoses, treatments, assigned clinicians, etc., are reflected accurately in datasets.

From this perspective, dimensions of data that contribute to their quality are seen as fixed elements (intrinsic or contextindependent data quality) that are relatively stable. Cultivating data resources for data analytics is a matter of assessing, cleaning, and using the “good” data on hand in organizations, while weeding out the “bad.” These scholars tend to assume that the quality of data can be assessed using preexisting measurement criteria mapped to the predetermined needs of the dataset consumer (Strong et al., 1997; Ge & Helfert, 2007). In other words, “bad” data can be assessed independent of context and can be turned into “good” data (through processes of data cleaning). Further, once the data is processed, it will continue to exhibit the properties of high-quality data, independent of context. In sum, this work tends to focus on outlining procedures for assessing high-quality data and managing the effort and costs of cleaning data (e.g., Ballou & Pazer, 1985; Batini et al., 2007; Cai & Zhu, 2015; Ge & Helfert, 2007; Kahn et al., 2016; Martens & Provost, 2014; Nelson et al., 2005; Shankaranarayanan & Cai, 2006; Wang & Strong, 1996; Wang, 1998; Wixom & Watson, 2001).

Data quality scholarship recognizes that data possessing each of these properties (i.e., high-quality data) is rare and the processes for assessing and cleaning data are increasingly complex and intensive—even if they are key to realizing the organizational benefits of data-driven decision-making (Sadiq et al., 2011; Shankaranarayaan & Blake, 2017). This literature emphasizes that the lack of standardized data stores is a key managerial problem. However, it also recognizes that achieving “total” standardization of data stores should not be the goal of data management. These scholars argue that not only is total standardization an unrealistic goal, but even reaching for “almost total” standardization is resource intensive and should only be undertaken when the business value of producing such data is worth the cost (Goodhue et al., 1988). As noted by Aaltonen et al. (2021) even though scholarly work about data quality discusses the challenges of achieving high-quality data, it broadly assumes that data are faithful, objective representations of facts. Thus, from this perspective, the biggest question for organizations is to decide how many resources to deploy in the service of cleaning and harvesting data.

## Data in Use: A Practice Lens

In parallel to data quality scholarship, a burgeoning stream of literature on the situated and subjective elements of data quality has emerged in information systems and adjacent disciplines such as science & technology studies, critical data studies, and information science. Scholarship in this tradition integrates a practice theory lens with critical studies of data and data analytic practices. As such, this research has focused on the inherent subjectivity of what is considered data, how it is produced, and how it is used in practice.

For example, studies of data reuse have shown that simply creating repositories of data accomplishes little in terms of practical value because data are never complete, harvestable entities sitting in a ready repository (Dourish, 2017; Moser & Law, 2006; Loukissas, 2019; Ribes & Jackson, 2013; Winthereik, 2024). Thus, data are never actually “raw” and ready to be plugged into analytics and algorithms (Bowker, 2005; Elish & Crawford, 2018; Gitelman, 2013). Further, scholars have attended to the ways in which data change as they are passed around, become part of larger systems, and are taken up in local contexts (Moser & Law, 2006; Pink et al., 2018; Ribes & Jackson, 2013). As part of the recognition that data shift, scholars have recently begun to explore in some depth the complex, situated, and effortful “data work” that permeates all facets of data production, governance, and use (Bossen et al., 2019; Edwards, 2018; Fischer et al., 2017; Fiske et al., 2019; Islind et al., 2019; Meng et al., 2019; Møller et al., 2020; Østerlie & Monteiro, 2020; Pedersen & Bossen, 2024; Pine, 2019; Pine & Bossen, 2020; Sambasivan et al., 2021; Benfeldt & Persson, 2025).

This body of literature applies a critical and constructivist approach to data, conceptualizing data resources as always situated, contextual, and emergent in practice. Despite the rich body of literature that is developing in the data-in-practice vein, minimal attention has been paid to data quality from a practice perspective (or what we have come to call data integrity). This lack of attention is striking, given that a practice perspective challenges the implicit assumptions that permeate literature on data quality.

Building on the critical and practice perspectives on data quality described above, we argue that organizations attempting to reap value from data must figure out how to work with data whose integrity is tied to a shifting data ecosystem and be ready to invest in rehabilitating data when conditions undermine data integrity.

## Data Integrity: An Ecosystem Perspective

Our perspective aligns with work by Aaltonen et al. (2021), which attends to the process by which data are produced and made into data commodities—meaning, objects that are portable and tradeable across an organizational field. These scholars argue that data production practices unfold not just within an organization, but as “part of broader organizational arrangements that make them matter in a particular business and industry” (p. 402). Data production, they argue, faces an ongoing threat; since the data or the algorithms used for measurement are constantly changing, the organization must constantly work to stabilize its production of industry-accepted measures.

Drawing on the data-in-practice and data quality literatures, we extend studies of data production practices to show how the integrity of data resources (specifically the properties of completeness, consistency, validity, and accuracy) at Hospital System was destabilized by conditions that originated both internally and externally to the organization. Thus, we extend Aaltonen et al.’s (2021) work by identifying the specific conditions under which data integrity breaks down and tracing the causes of breakdowns in the broader data ecosystem. Then, building on prior conceptualizations of data production processes by Jones (2019), we describe what we call efforts around data rehabilitation, the ongoing work that Hospital System (in concert with other organizations in the ecosystem) engages in to detect breakdowns in data integrity and rehabilitate data to a sufficient, if ever-ephemeral, integrity visà-vis a changing landscape.

Our use of the term data ecosystem is loosely based on Baker and Bowker’s term “information ecology” (2007, p. 138) and their framework conceptualizing the assemblage of data, knowledge, and information practices situated in data centers, learning centers, and global networks. However, in biological sciences, the term “ecology” is reserved for the study of ecosystems, whereas the ecosystem itself is a community of organisms interacting with each other and the physical environment. Thus, we developed the term data ecosystem in order to encompass the suite of IT tools that interact to produce digital resources (data); the various people and coordination mechanisms involved in collecting, curating, managing, and deploying data; evolving goals and strategies that motivate specific lines of action and provide templates for assessment and benchmarking; and decisions and policies reliant on datadriven insights. Further, a data ecosystem perspective brings into relief the interactions and dependencies between multiple levels of analysis. In our case, these include individual hospitals, Hospital System, state-wide quality improvement programs, and federal bodies that benchmark and regulate data in use. While the IS community has recently begun to use the term “data ecosystems” to refer to the environment for “creating, managing, and sustaining data sharing initiatives” (Oliveira & Farias Lóscio, 2018, p. 3), this literature is focused on building connected IS systems that facilitate data sharing across organizations rather than interrogating the relationships between tools, people, coordinative mechanisms and evolving goals and decisions (Oliveira & Farias Lóscio, 2018; Oliveira et al., 2019; Lefebvre et al., 2025).

## Methods

## Research Setting

This research is part of a multi-sited ethnographic study. Figure 1 outlines the relevant organizations within the health care ecosystem from the perspective of Hospital System—the darkened circles are our field sites. Our field sites included three hospitals that were part of Hospital System, a western U.S. hospital system comprising six hospitals and a number of ambulatory clinics (at the time of writing). Three of Hospital System’s six hospitals provided obstetrical services and were thus the focus of this study. These hospitals varied in the volume of obstetrical patients they served. “Big Hospital” served over 5,000 obstetrical patients per year, “Medium Hospital” served around 2,000 obstetrical patients per year, and “Small Hospital” served less than 600 patients per year. In addition to the obstetrical unit, each of these three hospitals had additional departments or teams that were key to obstetrical data in use, including medical records departments, birth certificate offices, and various teams charged with managing quality improvement processes in their hospital.

As part of Hospital System, each of the hospital field sites shared a variety of systemwide services, which were located at a separate headquarters (“Shared Services”). Shared Services housed the electronic health record (EHR) system used by all the system’s hospitals and clinics. Shared Services also housed a broad suite of IT tools used for data-driven decision-making and an interdisciplinary Information Services team tasked with overseeing system-wide information systems.

At the time of our study, Hospital System had recently hired a CIO to oversee the development of specialty-specific data warehouses expected to serve the entire system. The data warehouses were supposed to pull data from the EHR and other sources and layer these data with SAP software to facilitate strategic data-driven decision-making. Because Hospital System centralized data-driven resources and initiatives in Shared Services, implementation of data-driven initiatives occurred across all three hospitals.

We also engaged in ethnographic research with a state-level Obstetric QI, a multi-stakeholder organization housed at a university and funded by hospital membership fees and grant funding. Obstetric QI operates at the regional level, providing infrastructure, research, and structured learning for data-driven quality improvement to hospitals providing obstetric services up and down the west coast of the U.S. Obstetric QI provides a variety of support services focused on data-driven approaches to improving obstetrical care, such as developing quality improvement toolkits, leading data-driven learning collaboratives, and providing training on data quality.

A key part of Obstetric QI’s operation is Data Center, which developed a web-based tool that uses a novel process to link various datasets and provide rapid-turnaround obstetrical data and quality measure results to hospital subscribers. Data Center’s obstetrical quality interface allows subscribers to easily generate quality measures, compare measure results to national benchmarks (as well as to other hospitals nationally), identify data quality issues, and identify opportunities for quality improvement. Hospital System was an avid user of Data Center’s tools.

The broader ecosystem in which Hospital System is positioned is depicted in Figure 1. Quality measures are used as a key mechanism of regulatory control over healthcare organizations, which must collect and report quality measures to accreditation organizations (i.e., The Joint Commission) and state and federal governments that purchase healthcare for those on public insurance plans (i.e., Centers for Medicaid and Medicare Services). Hospitals are required to report certain quality measures to these external oversight organizations in order to maintain accreditation and the ability to accept patients with federal and state public insurance programs (Medicare and Medicaid).

## Data Collection and Analysis

Ethnographic fieldwork, including extensive observations, conversational interviews, and semi-structured interviews (Emerson et al., 2011), was led by the first author and occurred in each of the field sites described above from 2013 to 2016. We conducted 44 semi-structured interviews, 60 ethnographic interviews, 106 observations lasting between four and 10 hours (for a total of 350 hours of observation across field sites), and engaged in countless informal conversations.

In the three hospitals, we studied birth certificate clerks, medical coders, medical records administrators, educators, and quality improvement personnel. Specifically, we conducted observations and interviews with five birth certificate clerks from Small Hospital, five from Medium Hospital, four from Large Hospital, and the birth certificate manager at each hospital. We conducted observations and interviews with five medical coders from Medium Hospital and two from Large Hospital, along with the coding managers at each hospital. We conducted observations and interviews with six clinical documentation integrity specialists who serve the entire system, along with their manager.

At Hospital System’s Shared Services, we interviewed 14 midlevel hospital personnel doing data-driven quality improvement for obstetrics. To better understand how Hospital System managed data, we conducted 130 hours of observations with the Information Services teams, the Obstetrical Datamart Design and Build group, and meetings related to data initiatives. Finally, we conducted six interviews with the chief information officer, one with the chief quality officer, two with the director of information services, and five with clinician-led committees that spanned the entire system, including the Women’s Best Practice Committee.

At Obstetric QI, we conducted 78 observations, which encompassed Data Center team meetings, Obstetric QI steering committee meetings, several ad-hoc meetings, and observations of the software development team. In addition, we conducted observations of birth certificate training webinars and birth certificate data quality improvement workshops, and conducted unstructured interviews with stakeholders involved in national obstetric data quality initiatives, such as the initiative to standardize obstetrical data definitions.

Lastly, we drew on archival data such as quality reports from Hospital System, internal memos, and meeting minutes related to data-driven decision-making activities at Hospital System and Data Center, proposals and slide decks describing Hospital System’s data warehouse, and various manuals and materials specifying coding guidelines and measurement algorithms.

Our data analysis followed a grounded theory approach (Charmaz, 2014), with data collection and analysis activities overlapping and informing each other. As new pieces of data were collected, we rigorously compared new data to existing data, noting similarities, differences, and possible directions to explore. In the early phases of data analysis, we systematically reviewed our data (field notes, interview transcripts, materials developed at our research sites) with the assistance of Atlas.ti qualitative data analysis software. We first took an open coding approach, applying descriptive codes to capture when, where, and with what language hospital personnel talked about data. One striking pattern we noticed during open coding was revealed by several situations in which data that was experienced as “good” (i.e., having sufficient integrity for a current use scenario) was suddenly understood by hospital staff as “bad.” Such situations were experienced as stressful (even traumatic) to hospital staff, and became a touchpoint for internal investigations of what happened—did the data itself change? Did data collection efforts change? Did techniques for analyzing the data change? While all these issues arose in our observations, the most common reason that data seemed to suddenly lose integrity was when new expectations and use scenarios were placed on existing datasets and current data collection practices. These situations resulted in intense efforts to reestablish the integrity of the data, often by retraining the staff to collect or record data differently.

In response to this insight, we created structured codes and recoded our data, looking specifically at situations where local hospitals experienced data integrity as suddenly undermined. Examples of structured codes during this phase were: data accuracy, transducing records, analyzing data, data breakdowns, detecting breakdowns, new technology, and so forth. At this point, we used existing literature to hone our analysis of how various properties that were described in the literature as characteristic of high-quality data were undermined when shifts in the data ecosystem affected the integrity of data in practice. At this stage of analysis, we created memos on emergent themes such as ongoing data work, impetuses for data breakdowns, responses to data breakdowns, and the relationship between Hospital System and other governing bodies at the state and federal levels.

We then took a step back and traced each of the specific cases in which data resources lost their integrity, writing comprehensive memos and illustrative flow charts for each case. In so doing, we paid attention to the conditions that led to a situated loss of integrity, how field sites became aware of this loss, and what practices they used to respond to such breakdowns. We ended up with four key cases: the implementation of The Joint Commission’s perinatal core measure set, which resulted in statewide and local efforts to improve birth certificate data; Hospital System’s creation of a “bold goal” to reduce the rate of low risk C-sections performed by clinicians working across the system; specific efforts by Big Hospital to lower its C-section rate; and a multi-sited effort to use data-driven decision-making to address “early elective deliveries,” the term for deliveries induced after a fetus reaches 37 weeks but before 39 weeks of completed gestation without a medical reason or without the birth process beginning on its own. These comprehensive memos and illustrative flow charts became the basis of our core findings.

## Findings

Throughout our fieldwork, we witnessed Hospital System investing heavily in producing quality data. However, we also found that the integrity of data resources in our field sites was constantly undermined in practice, meaning that data that were considered to have sufficient completeness, validity, consistency, and accuracy by the people relying on it to take action or assess a situation were suddenly (and often unexpectedly) seen as no longer having these properties when the expectations, projections, or desired use of the data shifted.

In what follows, we trace four types of data integrity breakdown: (1) negotiated category breakdown, (2) category expansion breakdown, (3) data mismatch breakdown, and (4) data disaggregation breakdown that resulted from: reactions to global mandates, anticipation of what was possible within an existing technological infrastructure, and perceived opportunities for leveraging existing data in new ways. We outline the triggers that led to the undermining of local data integrity, conditions across the ecosystem that exposed data fragility, and how the organization responded to breakdowns.

The loss of data integrity is no small issue for organizations. In efforts that went far beyond traditional ideas of cleaning existing data, Hospital System allocated an enormous amount of time, effort, and resources toward detecting data breakdowns, investigating the source of breakdowns, and rehabilitating the data by building a new IT tool, or (more commonly) retraining personnel to gather and categorize data differently. This suggests that when the stakes for rehabilitating data are clear, high, and pressing, organizations will allocate massive resources toward rehabilitation.

## Negotiated Category Breakdown

## Negotiated Category: Triggering Situated Data Integrity Breakdown.

Data reporting practices changed frequently at Hospital System due to ongoing attempts to employ data-driven decisionmaking (known as “evidence-based medicine” in healthcare) at the national level. In institutionalized environments like healthcare, public education, governmental services, or accounting, it is not unusual for expectations around reporting practices to be updated regularly. Local hospitals are subject to mandates coming from The Joint Commission. And while they are often a direct result of evidence-based medicine, federal mandates can easily ripple in unexpected directions, undermining data integrity elsewhere in the ecosystem. In some cases, evidence-based medicine suggests that new categories should be created (see category expansion breakdown below). In others, categories may remain constant but increasing pressure on local data surfaces differences in how parties interpret categories and assign value to unstructured data.

Starting in the early 2000s, a mounting body of scientific evidence showed that full-term infants (born 37 weeks or later) were not performing equally—infants born in the early part of the full-term period were more likely to go to the neonatal intensive care unit or have other issues than those born 39 weeks or later (Clark & Fleischman, 2011; Fleischman et al., 2010). Based on these findings, the March of Dimes (a nationwide organization that raises money for research and advocacy about pre-term birth) and other organizations began advocating for later deliveries. These pressures led The Joint Commission to begin requiring hospitals to report a set of performance measurements called “perinatal care core measures” annually. Prior to 2014, hospitals that provided obstetrical services were not required to submit these core measures. By 2016, hospitals that had a volume of 300 or more births per year were required to begin reporting the perinatal care core measures annually.

## Negotiated Category: Exposing Data Fragility

One of the measures in the core measure set was the occurrence of “early elective delivery.” This measure, designed to improve the health of infants by avoiding preventable harm, attempts to calculate the percentage of babies that are born “electively” (meaning, for no legitimate medical reason) prior to 39 weeks of completed gestation at a given hospital. The logic was that hospitals providing higher quality care would not induce labor before 39 weeks unless there was a valid medical reason to do so, because evidence shows babies born before 39 weeks have poorer outcomes than those born after 39 weeks. Here, a Hospital System data manager described the early elective delivery measure:

The Joint Commission releases about eight pages of definition. And basically, for my reporting purposes, I try to condense this down as small as possible. So, we are looking at patients who are electively delivered at 37 to 39 weeks, gestation completed. So, the denominator includes patients delivering newborns via induction or Csection between 37 and 0/7 weeks, and 38 and 6/7 week gestation. (Data manager, Hospital System)

The definition released by The Joint Commission outlined numerous details about how to sample cases and which data elements could be used to calculate the measure. After the measure was included in the perinatal measure set, the Centers for Medicaid and Medicare began including early elective delivery in the Medicare Inpatient Quality Reporting Program. Under this program, if a hospital does not report measures, it will be subject to a 0.25% annual decrease in Medicare payments. The early elective delivery measure was also included in public quality reports. Thus, the stakes were high to accurately assess and report early elective delivery rates.

## Negotiated Category: Detecting the Breakdown

Data on gestational age at delivery and the other elements required by the measure were already being collected at Hospital System. Hospital personnel thus initially assumed they could report the measure by gathering data elements from pre-existing datasets. However, they anticipated that it might be difficult to render data in the required format laid out in the measure. In anticipation of this challenge, Hospital System hospitals engaged a core measure vendor to report the measure along with all the other current quality measures required by The Joint Commission. Once it had uploaded its initial data to its chosen core measure vendor and received the results via a report from the vendor, Hospital System was faced with an unfortunate realization: Its performance on the early elective delivery measure was shockingly poor.

The chief quality officer and chief of obstetrics of Hospital System immediately developed a strategy to investigate the source of the issue. Given that the early elective delivery measure is an aggregate metric, it was initially unclear which data element was driving the poor performance of Hospital System and whether the issue was the result of inaccurate data or poor clinical practice.

## Negotiated Category: Investigating the Breakdown

The chief quality officer enrolled medical record managers and obstetrical educators to talk to clinicians and work with Data Center’s quality interface and the internal informatics department to assess the issue. These personnel used Data

Center’s interface to examine the medical record of each case in which an early elective delivery occurred over a period of time.

Investigation revealed a key data element that was negatively affecting Hospital System’s outcomes: induction of labor. An induction of labor occurs when a pregnant person is given certain drugs or procedures to cause the labor process to begin when it has not already done so. In contrast, augmentation of labor is defined as giving a pregnant person certain drugs or procedures to speed up delivery after the labor process has already begun. Induction of labor became a key data element required to calculate early elective delivery (induced labor signals elective delivery, while augmented labor does not).

From a medical perspective, the difference between “induction of labor” and “augmentation of labor” is not typically important. Clinicians are concerned with how labor is progressing, which drugs or procedures are administered during labor, and the timeline between intervention and delivery. Thus, they document these pieces of information carefully. However, the point at which active labor began (“onset of labor”) must be established in order to assess whether drugs or procedures were ordered before or after the onset of active labor. Pinpointing the beginning of labor can be difficult because people often experience symptoms of labor (called “early labor”) for hours or days before meeting criteria for “active labor.” Starting labor is not a simple binary but a progression that unfolds over time. Thus, determining the beginning of labor can feel arbitrary to clinicians and is not generally relevant to the immediate goal of a smooth delivery.

However, the early elective delivery measure algorithm requires that hospitals create a numerator that includes all patients who had an elective delivery, defined as patients who had a medical induction of labor or a cesarean birth meeting specific conditions. To create the population of patients included in this numerator, hospitals use administrative data— the datasets used as part of medical billing and other administrative functions that are created when medical coders code patient charts after a patient’s stay in the hospital is complete. When Hospital System investigated its poor results on the early elective delivery perinatal core measure, it became clear that clinicians were not precisely documenting when the active onset of labor began; thus, medical record coders were not able to accurately indicate whether labor had been induced or augmented, a distinction that was impossible because clinicians used these terms interchangeably on patient medical records. Only after the distinction between induction and augmentation became important did the data become functionally invalid—as it became clear that the data had not been collected in accordance with standardized rules or specifications regarding these categories.

As the director of a nationwide initiative to standardize obstetrical data definitions explained to us, Hospital System was not unique in this reaction to The Joint Commission reporting requirements:

If I say that this is an augmentation of labor, but the onset of labor has not been documented yet, then it’s clearly not an augmentation, because if labor has not started, then it is an induction, because you induced labor. But … right now, if I ask a room full of physicians, a woman shows up with intermittent contractions, and you give her oxytocin, is that induction or augmentation? It’s 50/50 … you can’t stick that into the medical record and then do a performance measure for The Joint Commission like the elective delivery before 39 weeks. If you do the performance measure and … they are miscoding or misdocumenting, or they are not correctly using nomenclature and they are calling everything “augmentation” because that is the way they were trained, which is not necessarily their fault, then all of those “augmentations” are inductions … [and] you can’t do quality improvement because the data doesn’t show you the inductions. … You know what I am saying? (Director, Nationwide Initiative to Standardize Obstetrical Data Definitions)

In sum, as a result of its internal investigation Hospital System learned that its poor performance on the early elective delivery measure was not due to the quality of its services but to its data recording practices. Because clinicians were not distinguishing between induced and augmented deliveries, medical coders could not properly categorize the data. Hospital System’s perinatal data (considered to have integrity before the early elective delivery measure) no longer served to calculate the new measure, and the category of induced vs. augmented had to be renegotiated at the local level.

## Negotiated Category: Rehabilitating Data Integrity

Hospital System leadership began a full-scale effort to rehabilitate the data in response to the change in global reporting expectations. After determining that doctors documentation of induction was negatively impacting Hospital System’s performance on quality measures, quality improvement personnel and obstetrical educators determined that the best way to solve the issue was to educate doctors on the differences in the two terms and teach them how to document their actions differently (no easy task given power dynamics and intensive schedules). Hospital personnel charged medical coders with the task of educating clinicians about the need to distinguish between augmentation and induction in their documentation practices. This group held a series of training sessions for clinicians in which coders reviewed the definitions of induction and augmentation, the correct use of each term in the medical record, and the ramifications of incorrect documentation. Clinicians had to be encouraged, cajoled, and even forced to attend training by peers and hospital officials, but, in the end, training effectively fixed the problem, and Hospital System’s early elective delivery rates improved dramatically between the first and second quarters, according to results reviewed by the Women’s Best Practice Committee meeting. As a member of Data Center’s steering committee described:

Whether we love The Joint Commission’s specifications or not, the purpose is to put everybody on an even playing field, whether you like it or not. And … [for that to happen] there is a lot of data correction that needs to happen.

## Category Expansion Breakdown

## Category Expansion: Triggering Situated Data Integrity Breakdown

The next example resulted from a similar situation to that described above. Just prior to the creation of required prenatal core measures (described above), The Joint Commission created a new category and, in so doing, changed the phenomenon that was being measured. Such changes occur more frequently than one might expect. Categories shape reality, and shifts in scientific knowledge can lead to changes in the understanding of a phenomenon and thus redefinitions of categories. Changes in the definition of a phenomenon ripple, undermining the situated integrity of data at different points in an ecosystem. Not only must organizations learn to produce data that aligns with the new definition, they also have to find a way to manage the inconsistencies between old data collected under a different understanding of the world and new data reflecting the category expansion.

## Category Expansion: Exposing Data Fragility

In 2013, professional standard-bearing organizations, including professional associations for obstetricians, nursemidwives, and obstetrical nurses, carried out a broad initiative to standardize the definitions of key language used in obstetrical care. This initiative, called ReVitalize, changed the definition of the category full-term pregnancy as completed gestation between 39 and 40 weeks, and created a new category, “early term” pregnancy, to capture completed gestation from the beginning of the 37th week to the end of the 38th week. These changes were in response to evidence that babies born in the 37th and 38th weeks had less desirable outcomes. However, by redefining what a full-term pregnancy was, the category expansion immediately undermined the integrity of any dataset that had categorized “full-term” pregnancy as 37+ weeks of gestation and rendered the current data inaccurate. As an informatics nurse working with the national ReVitalize initiative described:

One of the definitions … that ReVitalize took on was the definition of term pregnancy. … ReVitalize chose to divide [it] up into four categories: early term, full term, late term, and post term. Because the literature suggests the complications are different and varied depending on those different categories. And getting more specific allows us then, hopefully, to pull data related to those categories and really study what are the differences in outcomes. (Informatics nurse, Nationwide Initiative to Standardize Obstetrical Data)

This change in the definition of full-term pregnancy immediately preceded The Joint Commission’s adoption of early elective delivery as a perinatal core measure (as described above, early elective delivery is considered the percentage of deliveries performed electively between 37 and 39 weeks of completed gestation). Hospital System, along with hospitals across the country, began calculating the early elective delivery measure and reporting its data to The Joint Commission via its core measure vendor. In so doing, Hospital System started using the new definition of full-term pregnancy.

## Category Expansion: Detecting the Breakdown

Once again, Hospital System was surprised to discover how poor its results were on the early elective delivery measure. As soon as Hospital System was informed by the external vendor chosen that it needed to calculate its results on measures required by The Joint Commission, Hospital System knew the data were problematic.

## Category Expansion: Investigating the Breakdown in Data Integrity

One reason for Hospital System’s poor performance on the early elective delivery measure was the negotiated category breakdown described above. However, Hospital System personnel wondered if category expansion was also an issue because the definition of early elective delivery was anchored on a definition of “early” as any delivery that occurred prior to the beginning of the 39th week of pregnancy, whereas their data categorized “early” as any delivery that occurred prior to the beginning of the 37th week of pregnancy (per the earlier definition).

Upon investigation, Hospital System’s quality improvement team discovered that one of the deficiencies in the data provided to the external core vendor was the gestational age data element being pulled from birth certificates. Further, the team discovered that the data field for gestational age was particularly problematic in birth certificates.

The underlying reason for the problem with the gestational age data entry is that the standardized information system that birth certificate clerks were using to enter data (managed by the state Vital Records Department) had two fields for gestational age. One field was auto-generated by the software based on the date of the patient’s last menstrual period. Prior to the electronic health record tool developed in response to the data mismatch breakdown described below, a birth certificate clerk would have to personally ask a new mother (often distracted by the newborn in her arms) the date of her last menstrual period.

The second field for gestational age provided by the Vital Records information system allowed the birth certificate clerk to manually enter gestational age. Prior to category expansion, clerks tended to enter the gestational age that was auto-populated by the software in both fields—even though estimations based on date of last menstrual cycle are known to be imprecise. A birth certificate training coordinator described why it can be so difficult for birth certificate clerks to enter the “right” gestational age on the birth certificate:

One of the items on the birth certificate is the date of the last menstrual period. Women [who have just given birth] don’t remember that. You can’t find it in prenatal records or anything … you look at prenatal records and the dates are all different. There are sometimes three or four different dates. No wonder it’s wrong.

In addition to the fuzziness of estimating gestational age based on menstrual cycle, clerks at Hospital System were instructed to round gestational age to the nearest week, but without clear instructions as to how to round—i.e., a baby born at 37 weeks and 4 days might be rounded to 38 weeks or down to 37 weeks. Clerks would round up or down depending on their preference. After the definition of full term changed, the birth certificate manager at Medium Hospital described to us: “Now, we are instructed to always round down. I have to remind the clerks to round down; it is hard to remember.”

## Category Expansion: Rehabilitating Data Integrity

With expanding categories and increased pressure on gestational age, Hospital System needed to make sure the birth certificate data was made accurate again by aligning data collection practices with the new categories. Realizing that many hospitals in the region (including those in Hospital System) were struggling with the gestational age data element, Data Center developed a “chart review” component in the Data Center interface that allowed hospital users to more easily monitor the birth certificate data for individual patients. Here, a Data Center developer describes the chart review tool during a webinar attended by Hospital System:

This is how the chart review works, and you are able to correct the data. … This hospital did some data correction in March, I believe ... the yellow highlight shows where they corrected the data. You can hover over it and you can see the original value that was in the birth certificate. And the way you get to the editing screen is you click on the green pencil and this is gestational age. Originally, it shows 38, hospitals wiped out the 38 that it was, turned it to 39, and then pressed save at the bottom.

After the change in definition of a full-term baby, quality improvement personnel at Hospital System used Data Center’s chart review tool to monitor every birth that was logged through their hospitals as “not full term” to ensure that the gestational age had been accurately recorded. Further, birth certificate clerks at Hospital System were taught how to check and calculate gestational age using clinical tools such as a due date “wheel” that allowed clerks to calculate due date based on a patient’s last menstrual period and a tip sheet that explained how to select the best source of gestational age from the medical record.

These improvement efforts were notably successful, and gestational age data recorded on the birth certificate were rehabilitated vis-à-vis the new definition of a full-term pregnancy. As a result, Hospital System’s performance on the early elective delivery metric quickly improved, and soon all hospitals in Hospital System met The Joint Commission’s benchmark of minimal induced deliveries prior to the new definition of full term (39 weeks). Once again, Hospital System had to devote time, energy, and resources into developing new tools and retraining personnel. These efforts to rehabilitate data integrity were not the result of any specific issues with Hospital System’s current tools and practices. Rather, they were due to shifts in how data are analyzed in other areas of the ecosystem. Even if labor and delivery personnel morally agreed with the redefinition of full term to 39+ weeks of gestation, aligning current data practices with expanding data categories was experienced as a massive monster head that had to be removed and neutralized, with the reputation of Hospital System at stake.

## Data Mismatch Breakdown

## Data Mismatch: Triggering Situated Data Integrity Breakdown

Changes in the technological configuration occur frequently in organizational ecosystems because much of the promise of data-driven decision-making lies in using technological tools to link different (often already existing) datasets to learn new things about the organization, enhance decision-making, and/or increase productivity. Hospital System is no exception. Thus, new data tools and capabilities are constantly being developed and introduced into the institutional environment. Hospital System felt compelled to adopt new technological innovations because doing so would help it keep up with its peers, collect new data elements, anticipate future data needs, and demonstrate the high quality of the organization. However, a change in the IT infrastructure can, in essence, “create” a breakdown in data integrity when the act of linking different kinds of data reveals mismatches or previously unknown deficiencies in data completeness.

When we started our fieldwork, the state-level Data Center was in the process of developing an algorithm that linked two previously disconnected datasets: discharge data (classification codes, which are applied to patient medical records by medical records coders and used for insurance billing and other purposes) and birth certificate data (extensive data recorded in the hospital by birth certificate clerks and stored in each state’s office of vital records). Linking the two was seen as an opportunity by Data Center to triangulate and enhance current data assets:

One of the things that makes Obstetrics unique is that … there are two standard administrative data files with clinical information in them. So, you’ve got the patient discharge data that is true for everything. And then Obstetrics is unique because you have this birth certificate record that’s … it’s a whole separate way of tracking that’s standardized and has … has all these additional clinical elements. (Developer, Data Center)

Due to the rollout of The Joint Commission’s core measure set, which required data elements from both datasets to calculate the perinatal core measures, the discharge and birth certificate datasets became more visible and important to Hospital System. Increased attention to these datasets was part of an overall escalation in the stakes of administrative data because of the high degree of importance placed on the core measure set. The regional Data Center was well aware of the stakes, as revealed by this quote from a steering committee meeting:

One of our goals is to improve the quality of the administrative data before it gets sent in. And I think we want to continue that line, both with birth certificate data and the ICD-9 [classification] data. You know, I think one of the mantras has been, “Data doesn’t get improved until you use it … use it for something meaningful like public release or pay for performance or those sorts of things.” And I think this is an opportunity to give people a heads up that this data is now important. (Steering committee member, Data Center)

## Data Mismatch: Exposing Data Fragility

Data Center was particularly motivated to develop tools to link billing and birth certificate data because certain data elements of The Joint Commission’s core measure set were only available from birth certificate data. At the time, little was known about the specific properties of birth certificate data because this data had never been used to assess organizational practice. While birth certificates contained a survey of clinical data that was stored at the state’s vital records office and available for public health research, it was not used to assess labor and delivery outcomes. In practice, birth certificate data were mainly used by parents to establish the basic facts of birth to apply for a Social Security card or future school records. Birth certificate data were also occasionally used for large-scale public health studies, and the integrity of these data was rarely questioned. However, in anticipation of the rollout of core measures, and given the opportunity to link datasets, Data Center wanted to know if birth certificate data was “good” under the new metrics— i.e., sufficiently complete, valid, consistent, and accurate to be used as the basis of The Joint Commission’s core measures.

Data Center also had ideas about how they could use a combination of discharge data and birth certificate data to develop new insights unrelated to the core measure set. Specifically, they wanted to develop tools to conduct complex data analytics that could identify individual patients, organizational providers, and individual providers. The ability to garner insights from publicly available data without the direct participation of hospitals (these datasets were both available to Data Center through access to datasets held at the state level) was appealing. Hospital System, in turn, felt compelled to use the new tools developed by Data Center so that it could keep up with peer hospitals in its network. Hospital personnel felt they could not ignore the broader push for data-driven insights. Doing so would risk falling behind peer institutions and damage their institutional standing.

Led by their internal developer, Data Center developed a new tool to test the quality of data available to hospitals in their network—an algorithm that linked together discharge and birth certificate datasets. Data Center’s developer described how the linkage algorithm works:

There are several different linkages that need to happen, and you do them in a different order than most. The easiest one and most fundamental one is linking birth certificates to mothers. … We do … a relatively standard probabilistic record linkage approach here where we … match on zip code between the mothers PDD and the birth certificate, how well they match on method of delivery and these sort of things … we grab them up and look at all the moms and all the babies and then say, “Okay, how likely are these to have been paired with each other?” and develop the whole table with every combination. And then weigh those and assign them on the basis of which is the best match. Then the second stage is linking the newborn patient discharge data to the pair of mom + birth certificate. And this is a lot harder because there isn’t that much in a newborn’s chart to differentiate them from each other… But it’s the same sort of approach, probabilistic record linkage where we weigh the different factors and link them. (Developer, Data Center)

The ability to link two separate standardized obstetrical datasets together was heralded by Data Center as an important move toward creating infrastructure to support data-driven quality improvement in local hospitals and hospital systems. Data Center allocated considerable resources to develop the linkage algorithm with the hope that it would provide new capabilities to hospitals. However, these efforts exposed the incompleteness and incomparability of data and necessitated substantial rehabilitation.

## Data Mismatch: Detecting the Breakdown

When the linkage algorithm was implemented, Data Center’s interface added new reports that automatically bubbled up on Data Center’s homepage. These reports allowed Hospital System personnel to see a measure of consistency between birth certificate data and discharge data on key data elements. Data Center’s dashboard, which provided a window on performance via different quality measures, also allowed hospital personnel to manually review cases used to calculate the quality measure results. This feature allowed hospital administrators to see where data inconsistencies might be occurring up close and was a key function that Data Center provided to local users of the data.

When Data Center began monitoring discharge data and birth certificate data using the linkage algorithm, they realized that the two datasets were wildly inconsistent in most hospitals across the state. Wide variations in data entry existed from hospital to hospital and clerk to clerk for certain data elements (e.g., gestational age of fetus at birth, diabetic status, and mother’s health). As the developer from Data Center explained:

At the end of the day, there is not a whole lot we can do. Because we are not the source of the data. So, what we try to do is just make it clear when there are data problems, to try to make it clear to both us and the hospital when there are data quality issues.

Through using Data Center’s interface, Hospital System came to the realization that its own birth certificate data were much worse than they realized. As the perinatal outreach director at Big Hospital stated: “You know what, it’s really embarrassing to work at a place that has some of the crappiest data in the state. Very embarrassing, on my end!” Aside from simply being embarrassed about the state of its birth certificate data, Hospital System was concerned because high-stakes quality measures, such as the perinatal core measure set, required data elements that were only tracked in birth certificate data.

It is important to remember that before the linkage algorithm was introduced, Data Center and Hospital System were unconcerned with the properties of birth certificate data—the birth certificate data was seen as perfectly adequate. Indeed, several of the perinatal core measures included in The Joint Commission’s core measure set were designed using birth certificate data elements (based on the assumption that these data were of sufficient integrity to use for high-stakes quality measures). As noted above, while public health researchers had long used birth certificate data for a variety of research purposes, no one had called into question the integrity of birth certificate data on a large scale. At the local level, Hospital System had assumed that its own birth certificate data were sufficient because it was using this dataset to calculate The Joint Commission’s core measures without major problems.

## Investigating the Breakdown in Data Integrity

At a regional level, the linkage algorithm revealed to developers at Data Center that birth certificate data across the state was poor compared to discharge data. Thus, they did not feel the need to investigate the source of the breakdown and moved quickly to the development of repair strategies. As a Data Center developer explained: “The problem is that birth certificate data is collected by birth certificate clerks who are a little bit less well trained and sort of less sophisticated than a typical coder is.”

At the local level, the perinatal outreach director at Hospital System felt the need to investigate Data Center’s finding that Hospital System’s birth certificate data was incomplete and inconsistent. Confusion emerged because Hospital System hospitals were currently performing well on The Joint Commission’s core measures, which led many to believe that they did not have a problem with data quality. However, by investigating what was going on with Data Center’s data reports, the outreach director discovered that it was only through sheer luck that the data being reported to The Joint Commission used a sample of cases with data that coincidentally resulted in high marks vis-à-vis The Joint Commission measures:

I was doing the [birth certificate] data, and I was seeing the difference there. And everyone was saying, ‘Oh, well, yeah. The Joint Commission [measures], we look wonderful.’ Okay, but it’s not true. … So, you are just lucky that they are just doing sample data, and every time we get the right ones in the sample. Because one of these days, they ain’t gonna get the right ones in the sample. (Perinatal outreach director, Big Hospital)

Once the perinatal outreach director became convinced of the issue, she took it upon herself to better understand what was going on. After shadowing birth certificate clerks in Big Hospital for several weeks, she learned that there were no standard processes for gathering data for many fields in the birth certificate record. More specifically, she found that while birth certificate clerks filled out the data fields that they considered important (date of birth, weight, and name) accurately and consistently, other data fields, such as gestational age, mother’s diabetes status, and birth presentation, were often left incomplete, entered inconsistently, or entered inaccurately. She discovered that such issues were not generally due to the laziness of the clerks. Rather, clerks did not have access to the electronic health record and thus could not use the medical record as a source of data for the birth certificate. Instead, clerks were expected to survey patients directly—asking a mother immediately after delivery complex questions about her health, weight, and delivery experience. Not surprisingly, given that these data elements were not seen as important, clerks chose not to bother a mother if she either did not recall the details or felt uncomfortable answering. One clerk described how collecting data from patients directly was difficult because patients were often tired, did not remember information accurately, or were hesitant to report certain information. For example, she explained:

Moms were all very iffy about telling us their prepregnancy weight. I used to leave that field blank because I didn’t want to ask the moms. The LEAN project is making it so that our module just pulls all of that information from what the nurse has entered into [the EHR], so I don’t have to ask the patient.

## Rehabilitating Data Integrity

To improve birth certificate data across the region, Obstetric QI launched a statewide birth certificate data quality improvement program that included an intense pilot in one county, multiple free webinars, and in-person trainings in every county at least once a year. Such trainings were time and resource intensive. However, as birth certificate data had become critically important for carrying out strategic goals at both the national and local levels, the investment was deemed necessary to reestablish the completeness, accuracy, and validity of birth certificate data as the shifting value and demands of these data increased.

At the local level, multiple stakeholders worked to increase the integrity of birth certificate data at Hospital System. Every birth certificate clerk was sent to an Obstetric QI’s training program. In addition, the perinatal outreach director developed the LEAN project to improve birth certificate data. As a result of the LEAN project, the information services team at Hospital System granted birth certificate clerks access to the electronic health record and developed a new tool that pulled data that clerks needed to complete the birth certificate directly from the electronic health record. This tool made it possible to fill out birth certificates based on data that clinicians had already collected and entered in the medical record, rather than collecting the data from patients themselves.

## Data Disaggregation Breakdown

## Data Disaggregation: Triggering Situated Data Integrity Breakdown

While prior examples revealed how global attempts to leverage data within an ecosystem can ripple to the local level in unexpected and problematic ways, local goals and attempts to engage in data-driven decision-making can also create breakdowns in data integrity.

In the early 2000s, childbirth activists in the U.S. began to draw a large amount of media attention to the climbing rate of C-section births occurring in the United States. Birth activists across the country pointed to the rising C-section rate as evidence of subpar women’s healthcare. Because of the widespread media and public attention, reducing the Csection rate became a key priority for Hospital System, which (like other hospitals across the country) was facing intense public scrutiny.

During our fieldwork, Hospital System responded to this scrutiny by spearheading a local initiative to reduce the overall “NTSV C-section rate” in each hospital. NTSV Csections are a category that is supposed to capture the number of surgical births among first-time mothers (nulliparous), carrying a single baby (singleton), considered full term (term), with the baby’s head situated down (vertex) at the time of labor (i.e., NSTV). Aggregate-level hospital data on NSTV C-sections suggested that Hospital System was performing far more of these elective procedures than desirable. However, when data that was accurate at one level of analysis was disaggregated to a more granular level of analysis, it became functionally inaccurate.

## Data Disaggregation: Exposing Data Fragility

Hospital System decided to step up its efforts to decrease its NTSV C-section rate for both regulatory and symbolic reasons. While hospitals in the system were already required to report NTSV C-section rates to the new perinatal core measure set, Hospital System decided to go a step further to demonstrate its commitment to providing high-quality obstetrical care. Specifically, Hospital System selected a benchmark for NTSV C-section rates (15%) that was more specific and more stringent than the metric reported to The Joint Commission. To further demonstrate commitment to this goal, Hospital System declared that reducing NTSV Csections to 12% or below was a “bold goal,” a status reserved for quality improvement goals deemed to be the most important across Hospital System each year.

Once Hospital System adopted the bold goal, leadership had to figure out how to “move the dial” to reduce the NTSV Csection rate. The Women’s Best Practice Committee decided to hire an obstetrician who had successfully lowered the NTSV C-section rate at another hospital and teamed this new hire with an obstetrician who had worked for several years at Hospital System to spearhead the effort. These obstetricians worked collaboratively with an obstetrical educator and an ad hoc nursing committee at Big Hospital. Tasked with meeting Hospital System’s bold goal, the group made a decision: They would calculate the NTSV C-section rates of individual clinicians in order to motivate doctors to reduce their individual rates. One of the physician champions explained how making individual C-section rates visible would help Hospital System make progress:

We [each individual clinician] need to know what our C-section [rate] is, so we know what direction we need to head. That’s really basic. If we are doing a really good job, we don’t have to do anything, and we can just congratulate ourselves and move on to other things. And if we need to improve, we need to know how much (Obstetrician and C-Section Task Force lead, Hospital System)

He goes on to describe how calculating individual C-section rates seemed possible because of Data Center’s interface, which was designed so that users could drill down and look at individual practitioners’ rates.

So, we know at this point that we need to improve. We know that. And so, we try and break down why we have that number [aggregate rate of NSTV C-sections]. And one of the things anyone would tell you that works anywhere around a Labor and Delivery floor is it [the number] varies tremendously by practitioner. So you want to break it down by practitioner. And [Data Center] allows us to do that. (Obstetrician and C-Section Task Force lead, Hospital System)

Hospital System’s information services team assumed they could draw the patient ID number from the birth certificate and, from this number, deduce whether the patient had an NTSV Csection and which doctor had performed the operation. According to the logic of the information services team the patient ID number was also an important data element because it allowed hospital personnel to tie multiple data elements (i.e., method of delivery, complications, gestational age, etc.) to a specific doctor identified in the system as the doctor who performed the surgery (and thus could be held accountable to their specific percentage of NTSV C-sections).

The C-Section Task Force decided to first educate doctors about the need to reduce NSTV C-section rates. If this didn’t work, they planned to publicize the NTSV C-section rate for each doctor in order to encourage (i.e., shame) individual clinicians to perform fewer elective C-sections.

After four months, if [C-section rates don't come down], then we are going to go to Phase 2, which is the legislate part. That involves publishing the data of the C-section rates of the individual physicians. Within our department. So, they kind of get the idea, “I am just not pulling my share.” (Obstetrician and C-Section Task Force lead, Hospital System)

## Data Disaggregation: Detecting the Breakdown

As Big Hospital began calculating the C-section rates for individual doctors, the task force was closely monitoring individual C-section data. In so doing, they discovered a fundamental flaw in their plan: C-section data had sufficient integrity to measure C-section rates at a hospital level, but was rife with missing and inaccurate data at the level of individual doctors. The lack of integrity in the data at the level of individual clinicians was immediately obvious, as the data made it appear as if some clinicians performed zero NSTV C-sections and some performed an enormous number.

## Investigating the Breakdown in Data Integrity

A multidisciplinary team from Hospital System led by the perinatal nurse educator at Big Hospital investigated the breakdown in individuals’ C-section data and identified a number of issues in the current data. One of the most obvious problems was that these were teaching hospitals, and resident doctors did not get their own provider ID number until they completed their residency. Thus, all the C-sections performed by residents were lumped together in a large batch and assigned to a generic resident or attending doctor. An even more surprising issue was that several non-resident physicians were simply missing from the dataset for no apparent reason. Because of this issue, the activity of specific doctors could not be directly linked to the patient ID number without glaring gaps in the data.

The C-Section Reduction Team investigated why certain physicians were missing from the dataset. To do so, they tapped managers overseeing birth certificate clerks and medical coders, who further investigated the work of their supervisees. The C-Section Reduction Team also contacted the state vital records office and met with vital records office personnel and software engineers in charge of the state vital records database in an attempt to pinpoint the reason that individual physicians’ provider numbers were missing from the vital records database. In the end, they simply found that the breakdowns were complex and occurred at multiple locations in the data infrastructure; several analog and automated processes linking the state’s database for medical license numbers, birth certificate data, and the internal data center’s data were broken. The inaccuracy of the data at the individual level thus prevented the C-Section Reduction Team from sharing individual-level data:

We are not publishing [individual NTSV C-section rates] publicly because we are still struggling with getting accurate data. And it’s an interface between our medical credentials and has to do with the state licensing number that is given and how that interfaces with the birth certificate database, because birth certificates are automatically generated based on [that]. … There are several automated processes. And then, ultimately, the birth certificate data is where the [Data Center] extracts the C-section indication … we’ve identified four different steps. The medical staff office here, the physician state licensing board, the state birth certificate … and that gets pushed into the [Data Center]. And there are errors at all four levels.” (Nurse educator, Big Hospital and C-Section Task Force member)

## Rehabilitating Data Integrity

The C-Section Reduction Team attempted to fix the data breakdown by working with Hospital System’s Information Services Team to create an automated tool in the EHR to capture better data about individual C-section rates. The team also tried working with the state’s birth certificate database to improve the data related to individual providers. These improvement efforts lasted a year and cost Hospital System a substantial amount of time, money, and effort.

However, even after months of trying to get complete Csection data at the level of the individual, about 6% of doctors were missing entirely, and multiple other doctors seemed to have inaccurately high or low rates. The C-Section Task Force was pushing for the individual NTSV C-section rates to be released internally (a form of public shaming and peer pressure that they thought would motivate individual clinicians to avoid NSTV C-sections). However, the perinatal nurse educator felt that the data needed to reach a certain level of integrity at the individual level and, because C-section rates have the potential to be inflammatory and potentially could be leaked to the media, the bar for “good enough” data needed to be high. She counseled the task force to hold off on releasing the individual rates to peer doctors until they could ensure that the individual-level data were consistent and accurate.

Ultimately, the team was unable to improve provider-level NTSV C-section data sufficiently to carry out their plan and decided to abandon efforts to tie C-section decisions to individual physicians. Instead, the team focused on a different strategy to make progress on Hospital System’s bold goal— they created a checklist (for both nurses and doctors) that had to be filled out before deciding to do any C-section. The checklist strategy attempted to shift behavior but did not use data provided by Data Center’s interface in the granular fashion that the team had hoped. The existence of multiple compounding errors stymied the task force’s plan to use the patient’s ID number, as recorded in birth certificate data, as a data point to identify the NTSV C-section rate of each individual physician. Data that were sufficiently complete at the hospital level simply broke down and could not be practically rehabilitated when applied to a different level of analysis. The goal of leveraging existing data to change current work practices was abandoned.

## Links Between Triggers and Responses to Data Integrity Breakdowns

While the insights presented here emerged from specific empirical examples and are thus not directly generalizable, we found that it would be worthwhile to trace the links between what led to situated data integrity breakdowns at Hospital System and local and regional responses. This exercise revealed that the nature of the trigger leading to a situated data integrity breakdown affected how the issue was discovered and organizational responses.

## Obvious vs. Subtle Changes to Existing Data Infrastructure

When there is an obvious or anticipated change to an existing data infrastructure, local breakdowns of data integrity are either immediately apparent (i.e., category expansion breakdown) or easily anticipated (i.e., data mismatch breakdown). Obvious changes to the infrastructure can come from any location in the ecosystem—an external mandate might fundamentally alter how data are categorized, or a local desire to link existing datasets might reshape data pathways. Either way, when a trigger is obvious, local personnel are primed that the change might cause an issue with data integrity and are ready to explore its effects. In these cases, organizations may or may not need to deploy resources to investigate the ramifications of the changes to data integrity and infrastructure.

However, some triggers are less visible and take the form of intensifying pressures on existing data. In cases where existing data is expected to answer new questions (i.e., negotiated category breakdown) or pinpoint actions with a new level of specificity (i.e., data disaggregation breakdown), local personnel may not realize that existing data sources are ill-equipped for new uses. In these cases, data integrity breakdowns may not be immediately apparent and may only emerge when the new pressures put on existing data lead to outcomes that feel “off” or problematic to those tasked with understanding and shepherding the data. We can thus imagine multiple situations where situated data integrity breakdowns may go completely unnoticed. That said, in cases where problematic outcomes are noticed and acted upon, substantial organizational resources are required to explore whether a data integrity breakdown has occurred and why.

## Stakes Attached to Situated Data Integrity

In addition to the degree to which data integrity breakdowns can be anticipated, we found that the stakes attached to data integrity matter a great deal. Specifically, when the stakes of a data integrity breakdown were clear, high, and pressing, we saw intense local efforts to rehabilitate data, even when these efforts required a substantial investment of resources.

Not surprisingly, data integrity breakdowns that emerged as a reaction to global mandates carried substantial stakes and were taken extremely seriously at Hospital System. Thus, in the cases of negotiated category and category expansion breakdowns, no efforts were spared in rehabilitating data to align with the shifting measures established by The Joint Commission. Not only were the stakes high for Hospital System (a poor mark by The Joint Commission could affect reputation, accreditation, and reimbursement insurance), but the measures came with a clear timeline that Hospital System was beholden to. In response, Hospital System not only invested resources in investigating the ramifications of the mandates on the existing data infrastructure, but it also invested substantial time, money, and attention toward efforts to develop new protocols, retrain personnel, and align local data collection practices.

Aside from immediate and pressing stakes, data integrity breakdowns were also taken seriously when the stakes were seen as potentially high and imminent. In the case of data mismatch breakdowns, Hospital System personnel expressed great relief that the randomly selected cases given to The Joint Commission from existing birth certificate data had not revealed a serious integrity issue. In surfacing the data mismatch between birth certificate and discharge data, they were able to get ahead of a future problem that they were convinced would inevitably surface, and thus were also willing to invest substantially in efforts toward rehabilitation. In this case, rehabilitation went beyond developing new protocols, retraining personnel, and aligning local data collection practices to the modification of current IT systems and working with the state on a comprehensive retraining initiative for all birth certificate clerks.

Much of the promise of data-driven decision-making lies in the assumption that data that are already being collected can be leveraged to influence action. However, we found that in a situation where Hospital System personnel believed there was an opportunity to achieve a desired change by leveraging existing data, the naivete of this assumption came into relief. In the case of data disaggregation breakdowns, Hospital System dedicated intensive resources to understanding the source of data integrity issues, only to discover they were extremely complex and multi-pronged. Given that the stakes of reducing the C-section rate were less pressing and more uncertain, it made sense that Hospital System decided that the costs of rehabilitating data to make it accurate at the level of the individual were unfeasible, leading to the initiative being abandoned. In sum, the stakes attached to the trigger directly affected the degree to which the organization was willing to commit resources to rehabilitating the integrity of the data.

## Efforts Around Data Rehabilitation

It is worth noting that in each of the successful examples of data rehabilitation, efforts did not follow traditional ideas about what it means to clean existing data. Our findings suggest that data rehabilitation is a complicated process that touches on multiple aspects of work practice. Specifically, we found that in each case, data rehabilitation required developing new organizational protocols and retraining personnel to measure, label, log, and process data in new ways. This is because humans remain the site of data collection. Occasionally, we witnessed system designers or IT workers rethinking the existing technological infrastructure and building new tools. However, even these efforts required retraining of personnel around the new systems.

From this perspective, the metaphor of data rehabilitation is particularly apt; rehabilitation is not simply about fixing something at the site of the breakdown, it requires attention and care in multiple areas of a complex system. Taking the body as an example, healing a muscle strain requires not just strengthening an injured muscle but also strengthening the surrounding and corresponding muscles and retraining the body to move differently. Rehabilitation after neurological and cognitive damage is even more complicated, requiring multipronged efforts to rewire the nervous system and train the brain to take over lost functions (i.e., neuroplasticity).

Similarly, data rehabilitation requires not just pinpointing the cause and site of the data integrity breakdown but also diagnosing the ripple effects of that breakdown and figuring out how to rehabilitate the data through broader organizational interventions. ## Discussion and Conclusion

Data are fragile, particularly when they are subject to the whims of a massive ecosystem, and attempts to leverage the power of data at one point in an ecosystem ripple out in unexpected ways. At the local level, Hospital System had no choice but to be responsive to its broader institutional environment in a manner that repeatedly necessitated substantial changes to current data practices and ushered in breakdowns in data resources. At the regional level, Obstetric QI similarly exerted pressure downward to the hospitals in its network (such as Hospital System) to engage in regional datadriven initiatives, and responded to the shifting pressures applied by other external stakeholders, such as The Joint Commission and public advocacy groups.

At the national level, regulatory bodies were being pushed to take findings from evidence-based medicine into account (Timmermans & Berg, 2003). Taking an ecosystem perspective thus allowed us to see with more clarity the potential effects of data-driven decision-making. Here, we see a wholesale institutional effort to collect the best evidence on clinical practice and outcomes and integrate this evidence into decisions about healthcare services via data-driven quality improvement, in which quality metrics based on medical evidence are used to actively guide organizational practices and policies in response to feedback provided by their data (i.e., the results of quality metrics).

Our findings reveal not only the fragility of data within a shifting ecosystem but also the complexity and costs of data rehabilitation efforts. Thus, we argue that the need for organizations to recognize that becoming data-driven will necessitate repeated allocations of resources to engage in data rehabilitation is a critical but underappreciated aspect of datadriven decision making.

## Data Integrity: A Data-in-Practice View

If data are biased, skewed, mismatched, or don’t reflect empirical reality, they are not helpful in understanding and assessing phenomena or making strategic decisions. Thus, in accord with the extant data quality literature, we find that data need to be relatively complete, valid, consistent, and accurate to be useful for data-driven decision-making (e.g., Cai & Zhu, 2015; Lee et al., 1999). However, by taking a practice lens, our research reveals the situatedness of data integrity and challenges the idea that the core properties of data are static or stable.

Previous IS scholarship on contextual data quality (Wang & Strong, 1996) has examined the impact of context on data quality. In this literature, the dimensions of data that contribute to their quality—i.e., completeness and consistency—are seen as fixed elements (intrinsic or context-independent data quality) that can be assessed using specific measurement criteria. In this literature, context enters the picture in terms of the predetermined needs of dataset consumers (Wang & Strong, 1996), who have specific, context-dependent needs for the data. In this view, data quality must be assessed in terms of both its inherent characteristics and how these inherent characteristics align with the specific needs of potential data consumers. However, in contrast to our findings, characteristics of data quality are seen as inherent and independent of the situated context within which data resources are analyzed and mobilized as data-driven insights, not as inherently relational within an ecosystem (as our findings show).

Aaltonen et al. (2021) showed how data production within organizations can be impacted by the other organizations in a field. We extend this work by illustrating how external pressures can trigger breakdowns in an organization’s data resources, hindering its ability to use its data for critical data-driven initiatives, essential to producing data “commodities” (in the language of Aaltonen et al.) that are critical to maintaining legitimacy within a field (Meyer & Rowan, 1977). Data-inpractice scholars (Bossen et al., 2019; Jones, 2019) have also called attention to the contingent nature of data production. However, scholarship on data quality still lacks empirical accounts and conceptual tools for understanding data quality as rooted in a practice perspective. Our findings show that what it means for data to be considered complete, valid, consistent, and accurate is contingent upon the expectations, uses, and goals at a particular point in time. Expectations on data shift when data are deployed and leveraged in new ways. Further, attempts to use or leverage data to enhance decision-making or resource allocation in one area of an ecosystem can ripple out in unexpected ways, undermining data integrity in other areas of the ecosystem.

By taking the perspective of data integrity in practice, our work bridges data-in-practice scholarship (i.e., Aaltonen et al., 2021; Bossen et al., 2019; Bowker, 2005; Jones, 2019) with data quality scholarship (i.e., Ballou & Pazer, 1985; Batini et al., 2007; Cai & Zhu, 2015; Ge & Helfert, 2007; Kahn et al., 2016; Martens & Provost, 2014; Nelson et al., 2005; Shankaranarayanan & Cai, 2006; Wang & Strong, 1996; Wang, 1998; Wixom & Watson, 2001). This work brings into relief the fragility of data in a data ecosystem, as well as the costs and investments required to rehabilitate data when integrity inevitably breaks down.

## Implications for Theory

The first contribution of our work is exposing the fragility of data within a shifting ecosystem and organizational (and occasionally system-wide) efforts required to rehabilitate data integrity. We also show how attempts to leverage the power of data at one point in an ecosystem can, unexpectedly, undermine the integrity of data throughout the ecosystem. We encourage scholars studying the use and effects of data on organizations to zoom out beyond the specific locations of data-driven decisionmaking to anticipate and explore such ripples.

The second theoretical contribution of this study emerges from our ability to trace the context and lifecycle of four distinct data integrity breakdowns: a negotiated category breakdown that occurred when The Joint Commission placed new pressure on augmented vs. induced labor, a category expansion breakdown that occurred when obstetrical evidence added a new category delineating the length of gestation for a full-term baby, a data mismatch breakdown in which a statewide initiative to match existing datasets to potential future uses revealed vast inconsistencies between them, and a data disaggregation that emerged when Hospital System tried to change local practice by granularizing aggregate data in order to shame individual clinicians into performing fewer elective C-sections. These situated data integrity breakdowns surfaced at Hospital System as either a reaction to obvious or anticipated changes to the data infrastructure or as the result of strange or troubling results that emerged in analysis after pressure was put on existing data to perform in new ways.

Our third theoretical contribution lies in surfacing the effort and ongoing work that organizational personnel engaged in to detect, investigate, and repair data integrity that was undermined as a result of one of the breakdowns outlined above. We trace the intensive efforts needed to rehabilitate data integrity once it had been undermined. After a breakdown was detected, personnel often needed to investigate the cause of the breakdown, and only after the causes of the breakdown were established, could the organization begin to develop organizational strategies of repair—from retraining staff to developing new IT tools. Our empirical insights are striking in that we found that data rehabilitation efforts relied far more on changes in organizational practice than on the cleaning of preexisting data or the refinement of technological data systems. Specifically, rehabilitation generally involved retraining personnel on how to collect and log empirical phenomena in order to reestablish the integrity of data resources.

Finally, we argue that data rehabilitation is inevitable and ongoing. Detecting, investigating, and repairing data have become standard practices in organizations seeking to be datadriven. These efforts are the inevitable result of the promise of data-drivenness and should be considered when establishing the costs of data initiatives. In other words, using data to enhance decision-making at one point in a data ecosystem can create conditions that will undermine data integrity elsewhere in the ecosystem, requiring substantial time, labor, and resources for rehabilitation. Further, given the resource-intensive nature of data rehabilitation, it may occasionally be deemed impossible.

## Implications for Practice

A practical implication of this insight that is worthy of deeper analysis is that the intensive work of data rehabilitation often burdens workers across organizational hierarchies. Our findings suggest that personnel at the bottom of the hierarchy (i.e., birth certificate clerks and medical coders) are doing work that is increasingly consequential in how the organization is measured and assessed. This insight aligns with previous literature on how increased attention to data production burdens frontline workers (e.g., Noesgaard et al., 2022; Pine et al., 2016; Wolf, 2020) and shapes work practices through increased self-monitoring (Aaltonen & Stelmaszak, 2024). At Hospital System, clerks and coders were tasked with attending hours of training and were expected to translate complex clinical knowledge. External measures and internal mandates made the outputs of their efforts more and more visible. Although the weight of organizational success is increasingly held by these frontline workers (Kellogg, 2022), they are typically not compensated for their increased efforts with higher salaries or organizational recognition, despite the additional stress added to their work.

The costs of data rehabilitation may similarly exert larger effects on hospitals that are underresourced. Hospital System is a large, financially successful system with access to Data Center. Although Data Center also caused breakdowns in data integrity, such as when the data linking tool was introduced, it provides crucial infrastructure and support to Hospital System in carrying out data rehabilitation work. At present, many rural hospitals across the U.S. have closed or are in serious danger of closing because they lack financial resources (American Hospital Association, 2022). Such hospitals may be less likely to engage in the data rehabilitation needed to carry out highstakes data-driven initiatives. Further research on data integrity breakdowns and data rehabilitation could help to characterize the true costs of these activities and improve the design and evaluation of interventions to support data rehabilitation practices in organizations.

We also found that depending on the perceived criticality of data rehabilitation efforts, personnel at the top of the hierarchy are expected to shift work practices to align with the needs of a shifting data ecosystem. Hospital System not only invested in retraining birth certificate clerks, it also forced clinicians to become more precise in data categorization when distinguishing between the augmentation vs. induction of labor. One might expect resistance or the undermining of new work practices by those with local power (Beane & Anthony, 2024).

## Future Research and Potential Limitations

A deeper examination of the conditions that threaten current work practices at different levels of an organizational hierarchy is worthy of further investigation. As part of such an investigation, it would be interesting to explore the unexpected benefits of data rehabilitation efforts for the organization. While the costs of rehabilitation are clear, organizations that understand and embrace ongoing efforts to enhance data integrity (in relation to the shifting ecosystem) may develop new “muscles” and become stronger, more responsive, and agile over time.

Another avenue for future work would be to take a more birdseye view of the data ecosystem. One limitation of our methods is that we were deeply embedded in Hospital System. From the perspective of the personnel at Hospital System, data integrity breakdowns emerged and had to be dealt with. In the interest of presenting our data in a comprehensive and engaging manner, the triggers and responses to data integrity breakdowns appear to be more linear than they were in practice. Thus, we encourage future scholarship to trace data integrity across an ecosystem, tracking the ripples in feedback/feedforward loops.

## Implications Beyond Healthcare

Despite being touted as a key to improved organizational functioning, many organizations across sectors struggle to reap value from investments in data-driven decision-making. Our findings suggest that data breakdowns and rehabilitation may play a role in the troubling lack of success of data-driven initiatives, which often fail to yield the expected benefits and results (Božič & Dimovski, 2019; Ranjbarfard & Hatami, 2020; Villamarín-García, 2020). Substantial cost, effort, and resources are required to rehabilitate data integrity when it inevitably breaks down. Such costs are rarely taken into account when organizations herald the benefits of becoming data-driven and invest enormous resources into data infrastructures. The human and economic costs of data rehabilitation are often not factored into the estimated costs of implementing data-driven initiatives or making changes to existing data ecologies—yet even small changes can trigger a need for extensive data rehabilitation to restore integrity.

Medicine is in no way the only site at which data fragility surfaces within a shifting ecosystem. For example, danah boyd’s investigation into the U.S. census outlines in minute detail the efforts that go into collecting census data with sufficient integrity to be used as the basis of democratic representation (through redistricting) (boyd, in press). What data are collected on census forms, and how, is extraordinarily important. For example, longstanding attempts to ask about citizenship status on census forms have been directly tied to attempts to systematically exclude non-citizens from the census count, despite the dubious constitutionality of doing so. It remains to be seen how the citizenship question (which was added in the 2020 census) will ripple throughout politics and affect democratic representation, especially in terms of the ongoing efforts to gerrymander legislative districts (as noncitizens are not allowed to vote).

We can also see numerous instances in education, business, and social services, where attempts to optimize a data-informed system reverberate across an ecosystem, creating new pressures and destabilizing data elsewhere in the system. As an extreme example, take the 2015 sentencing of seven Atlanta educators, who were convicted of racketeering to prison terms after taking part in a expansive data scandal (employees from 35 public schools were named in a 2013 grand jury indictment) in which school employees were found to be systematically correcting students’ answers on standardized tests (Fausset & Blinder, 2015; Aviv, 2014). When, in 2002, the Atlanta superintendent linked accountability measures and performance objectives to standardized test scores, she placed a clear mandate and extremely high stakes on the data (schools whose students did not make appropriate progress toward the standardized test goals received escalating sanctions culminating in replacement of the faculty and staff and the restructuring or closing of the school). These changes in how a “good” school was defined placed immense pressure on educators in lower-income neighborhoods to present data that showed continuous improvement on test scores. These expectations put educators in the untenable position of “fixing” data, however possible. While these actions served to further undermine data integrity, educators felt that there was simply no way to reach standardized benchmarks that, if not achieved, would lead to the dismantling of schools. Given similar scandals in Ohio, Wisconsin, and Texas, it is clear that the consequences of becoming data-driven in these situations can be dire for both teachers and students.

In sum, our findings suggest that any organization or institution should attempt to anticipate the effects of implementing datadriven insights across a data ecosystem. Such initiatives will inevitably ripple, putting pressure on local data resources at multiple points throughout the ecosystem, leading to situated data integrity breakdowns. In the most successful scenarios, such breakdowns are noticed and investigated, and resources are allocated to rehabilitate the data, reestablishing integrity in relation to the shifting context of use. However, if data integrity breakdowns are an inevitable aspect of data-driven decisionmaking, and data rehabilitation efforts require multi-pronged and resource-intensive investments, the invisible costs of leveraging data need be taken into account when considering the financial and human costs of new data-driven initiatives and changes in any data ecosystem.
