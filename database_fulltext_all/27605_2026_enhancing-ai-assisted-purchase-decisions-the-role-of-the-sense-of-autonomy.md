# ENHANCING AI-ASSISTED PURCHASE DECISIONS: THE ROLE OF THE SENSE OF AUTONOMY<sup>1</sup>

Jinghui (Jove) Hou Decision and Information Sciences, C. T. Bauer College of Business, University of Houston Houston, TX U.S.A. {jhou@bauer.uh.edu}

Shuai Yang Glorious Sun School of Business and Management, Donghua University Shanghai, CHINA {shuai.yang@dhu.edu.cn}

Guiyang Xiong Whitman School of Management, Syracuse University Syracuse, NY U.S.A. {gxiong@syr.edu}

Paul A. Pavlou Miami Herbert Business School, University of Miami Coral Gables, FL U.S.A. {pavlou@miami.edu}

Empowered by large-scale consumer data, artificial intelligence (AI) systems act as shopping gurus, serving up highly personalized and expertly curated product recommendations for consumers. Despite their proficiency at inferring what could be the commonly “optimal” choices for the average consumer, AI systems have a limited ability to account for the idiosyncratic factors that uniquely shape each consumer’s purchase decisions, such as one’s hidden motives or complex situations. This inherent limitation, termed AI’s uniqueness neglect, poses a key challenge to the value of AI-advised decisions. Our research identifies a heightened sense of autonomy as a crucial means to improve the quality of AIadvised decisions through compensating for AI’s uniqueness neglect. Across five laboratory experiments and one field experiment in the context of apparel purchases, we show that fostering the sense of autonomy enhances purchase intention and the quality of purchase decisions. We also delineate the underlying mechanism and demonstrate a managerially actionable solution to effectively promote the sense of autonomy. Our work contributes to the literature by uncovering a distinct mechanism by which the sense of autonomy improves the quality of AI-advised decisions and by offering a feasible design strategy that leverages personal smartphones to address the uniqueness neglect of AI recommendation systems. Field data on actual product purchases and returns confirm the efficacy of our AI design in a real-world setting. Our findings offer both theoretical and managerial implications for a wide range of AI-aided decisionmaking contexts where idiosyncratic factors are important to individual human decision makers yet are commonly overlooked by today’s AI systems.

Keywords: Artificial intelligence, uniqueness neglect of AI, sense of autonomy, AI-assisted decisionmaking, decision quality, purchase decision, product return

## Introduction

Making decisions using artificial intelligence (AI) systems differs from conventional information systems (IS). In the classic understanding of how humans use IS to make decisions, agency remains with the user: Users direct the systems, and the systems respond to their will (Berente et al., 2021). In contrast, AI systems can perform cognitive functions previously wielded by humans (Rai et al., 2019), and often more effectively than humans typically do (Davenport et al., 2020). AI learns from data (known as machine learning) and can independently set its own decision rules, which may not be precisely directed (Baird & Maruping, 2021) or even necessarily endorsed by human users. Fueled by large-scale consumer data, a booming AI application is personalized AI recommendation systems that can independently predict consumer preferences and provide expert guidance on the “right” or “best-suited” product choices for each user (e.g., AIdriven apparel shopping systems). On the one hand, consumers may hand over some decision-making (e.g., finding the right size) to the “expert” AI for better choices (Puntoni et al., 2021). On the other hand, consumers may feel that AI constrains or interferes with their own will and priorities, limiting their sense of autonomy in their choice process (Deci & Ryan, 2000). This creates a paradox: As consumers defer to AI to improve their choices, their sense of autonomy may be compromised, which might impair their choices and decision quality. This paradox highlights the need to revisit the role of the consumer’s sense of autonomy in AI-advised decision-making. In this study, we address two research questions: (1) What role does the sense of autonomy play in AI-advised decision-making, and how does it affect decision outcomes? (2) if the sense of autonomy proves to be beneficial, what can be a solution to foster the sense of autonomy, and why might such a solution work?

We posit the crux of the paradox as follows: Despite AI’s proficiency at inferring decision rules that are commonly applicable to consumers, its ability to capture the decision factors unique to an individual is limited. Thus, while AI-recommended choices might be the commonly “optimal” choices for the average consumer, they are not necessarily “optimal” for each individual. Idiosyncratic factors, such as unexpressed motives and complex personal circumstances, are important to consumer decisions, but they are difficult for AI to capture or infer.<sup>2</sup> We refer to this key limitation of AI—its inability to fully account for the idiosyncratic factors that uniquely shape a person’s decisions— as the uniqueness neglect of AI recommendation systems.

We theorize that the sense of autonomy serves as a crucial means to mitigate AI’s uniqueness neglect and improve the quality of AI-guided decisions. The sense of autonomy refers to the experience of self-determination in one’s decisions (Deci & Ryan, 2000). We propose that fostering the sense of autonomy prompts consumers to integrate their unique personal factors into AI-advised decision-making, compensating for what the AI may neglect, and that this, in turn, leads to improved decision quality that is likely to better align with a person’s own needs and preferences. We test our theory in the context of consumer purchase decisions of apparel products using an AI-driven recommendation system. Choosing clothing represents a “complex” decision in that the choice process entails decision drivers that are deeply personal and often internal to each consumer, such as one’s unique taste, values, and personal circumstances (Sierra & Hyman, 2011). Purchasing apparel products thus provides a pertinent context to examine our research questions.

Our theorized mechanism for the role of the sense of autonomy is novel. Recognizing the sense of autonomy as a fundamental human need (Proudfoot & Kay, 2023), some conceptual work argues that AI, as an external influence on consumer decisionmaking, may threaten consumers’ sense of autonomy (Puntoni et al., 2021; André et al., 2018; Carmon et al., 2019) and that it, therefore, “can backfire and generate consumer reactance,” thus leading to suboptimal decisions, despite AI’s potential benefits (André et al., 2018, p. 2). This reactance-based mechanism highlights the need to affirm consumers’ sense of autonomy to reduce resistance to AI. Our argument, by contrast, underscores a self-determined way to utilize AI in accordance with one’s own interests and priorities. Distinct from previous autonomy literature, we propose the sense of autonomy as a remedy for AI’s uniqueness neglect, and we thus delineate a new mechanistic path underlying how a heightened sense of autonomy helps improve AI-guided decision-making.

Intriguingly, guided by our theory, we introduce an innovative design that enables consumers to use AI via their personal smartphones. We propose that using AI on a personal smartphone can foster a greater sense of autonomy for users. Specifically, the private and personal context provided by smartphones activates self-reflection, directing users to focus on their own thoughts and feelings. This introspective state nurtures a strong sense of autonomy, as users are more attuned to their unique needs and preferences and enact decisions accordingly.

Through a set of laboratory and field experiments, we found support for our propositions. First, enhancing the sense of autonomy in using AI improves decision quality, leading to increased purchase intention, confidence, and satisfaction with AI-assisted purchase decisions. Second, we found process evidence that the heightened sense of autonomy prompts consumers to incorporate more of their personal preferences and needs into AI-assisted decision-making, which reduces the concern that their uniqueness might be neglected by the AI. Also, the positive effects of the sense of autonomy diminish if the consumer’s decision needs little unique consideration. Third, our theory-driven design—the personal smartphone-based AI—enhances the sense of autonomy and escalates its observed positive effects on the purchase decisions. Using field data, we observed the realworld significance of the positive impact on actual purchases, showing marked increases in product sales and reductions in product returns in a live retail setting.

The sense of autonomy (or the loss thereof) in relation to the use of AI is a pressing concern that calls for systematic academic work (Puntoni et al., 2021; Botti et al., 2023; Berente et al., 2021). Leading guidelines also highlight “respect for autonomy” as a key principle in designing future AI systems (United Nations, 2022; OECD, 2019). Despite these calls, there remains a lack of theorizing and an absence of empirical investigation into the causal influence of the sense of autonomy on AI-assisted decision-making.

Our research makes three main contributions. First, as decision-making with the aid of AI is a key topic in IS research, we identify a new mechanism that serves to improve AI-advised decision-making—namely, the role of the sense of autonomy in overcoming AI’s uniqueness neglect. This mechanism is distinct from the one derived from reactance theory in prior autonomy literature, adding a new theoretical account to understanding AI-aided decisions. Second, we offer a novel finding that the use of personal smartphones boosts the sense of autonomy, thereby contributing a pioneering theoretical angle to IS research on personal smartphones. Our theory-driven design provides evidence that integrating unique personal factors into AI can be achieved by embedding AI on smartphones. This is an original contribution toward the development of more human-centered AI systems (Jain et al., 2018). Third, our work provides timely and actionable insights for retailers, who are increasingly adopting AI but may be uncertain about best practices and may need practical guidance to optimize its effectiveness (Drenik, 2023). We not only highlight the importance of the sense of autonomy in AI utilization but also show managers and AI developers how to effectively enhance the sense of autonomy to improve business performance and deliver value to consumers.

## Relevant Literature and Gaps

Task delegation to AI: Our work adds to the literature on how humans work and make decisions with the aid of AI, much of which builds on the theoretical framework of task delegation (Baird & Maruping, 2021; Fügener et al., 2022). This framework surmises that while AI excels at certain tasks, humans do better on others; thus, human-AI collaboration should optimally allocate work based on their relative strengths. The joint performance of humans and AI would exceed the performance of either entity working alone. For instance, Fügener et al. (2022) explored how to split work between humans and AI in a task to decide whether an image contains a cat or a dog, finding that one can optimize classification accuracy by delegating images to AI first and then to humans if AI is uncertain. Similar research has examined human-AI task delegation in other contexts, such as equity investment (Marriage, 2017) and talent recruitment (Hook, 2017). Conceptualizations of delegation strategies include substitution, complementarity (Choudhury et al., 2020), augmentation (Krakowski et al., 2022), and human-inthe-loop (De-Arteaga et al., 2020). The core thesis is that, to improve overall decision-making capabilities and performance, one needs to understand when and how to assign tasks to AI versus humans (Lyytinen et al., 2021). While we also examine how to leverage AI to improve decision-making, our theory is grounded on the premise that even the most finely trained AI algorithms may still fall short of capturing human “caprice.” In this logical setting, how to best leverage AI requires accounting for the uniqueness of consumers. Thus, departing from task delegation, we contribute a new theoretical account through the lens of the sense of autonomy to enhance human decision-making with the aid of AI.

AI-advised decisions: Drawing from established advicetaking and judgment literature, the literature on AI-advised decisions posits that while good advice ideally helps improve decisions, people tend to (mistakenly) discount advice from others (Bonaccio & Dalal, 2006). Dietvorst et al. (2015) found an “algorithm aversion” effect, showing that when seeing AI make mistakes in forecasting tasks, people quickly lose confidence in AI and opt for the less accurate advice of humans. Logg et al. (2019) showed an “algorithm appreciation” effect, i.e., people place more weight on advice when they think it comes from an AI algorithm than from a human. They found that the algorithm appreciation effect wanes when people distrust that AI can do well on a given task, such as tasks perceived as subjective (Castelo et al., 2019) or concerning the estimation of hedonic value (Longoni & Cian, 2022). People also perceive AI-generated estimates as less accurate and become less willing to rely on them when AI is slow (Efendić et al., 2020). Even if AI performs well, people may distrust AI because they may not understand how it works (Yeomans et al., 2019); thus, transparent AI (which can explain its estimations) might enhance human adoption of AI advice (Lehmann et al., 2022; Cadario et al., 2021). Unlike these studies focusing on professional tasks, such as demand forecasting and inventory estimation, our research examines personal decisions involving factors that come from the end users themselves, such as one’s own needs and unique preferences. This distinction is important since the two decision domains can gravitate toward divergent judgment and decision processes. While the integration of users’ own preferences appears key to achieving favorable outcomes in our domain of interest, it could be more desirable to minimize human involvement when using AI in non-personal decision domains (Lehmann et al., 2022).

Consumer receptivity to AI: Another stream of related research is the consumer psychology literature on receptivity to AI (Longoni et al., 2019; Yalcin et al., 2022; Schrift et al., 2019; Kim & Duhachek, 2020), which focuses on the psychological mechanisms at play when consumers face AI systems that are intended to serve them. Kim and Duhachek (2020) found that consumers evaluate messages made by AI (vs. by a human) as more appropriate and persuasive when the messages focus on “how” AI implements actions rather than “why” it does so, due to the belief that AI lacks intentionality. Consumers also show different attitudes toward companies that employ AI-driven decisions. According to Yalcin et al. (2022), consumers react more positively when they believe a human (vs. AI) made the decision to accept their membership applications, because it is easy to attribute a positive decision to internal factors (e.g., applicants’ capability) when the decision is made by a human rather than AI (Yalcin et al., 2022). Longoni et al. (2019) showed that patients prefer receiving medical care (e.g., diagnosis and treatment) from human providers versus an equally competent medical AI system. This preference is due to patients’ belief that AI is less able to understand and cater to their unique medical conditions, compared to human providers.<sup>3</sup>

Schrift et al. (2019) found that AI systems learning and predicting consumer preferences can trigger adverse reactions as they threaten consumers’ sense of autonomy. Specifically, when consumers knew that their choices can be predicted by AI, they tend to choose less-preferred options. The authors explained that consumers violate their own preferences to restore their sense of autonomy. Schrift et al.’s study is the cornerstone of the popular discourse about AI as an autonomy threat that can generate consumer reactance (e.g ., André et al., 2018; Davenport et al., 2020). However, despite the recognized importance of the sense of autonomy, the precise impact mechanism and empirical validations remain unexamined.

Addressing this gap is crucial, both theoretically and practically: The two mechanisms of the sense of autonomy—reducing consumer reactance versus addressing AI’s uniqueness neglect—yield discrete predictions and inform divergent interventions. Our direct contributions here are to (1) elucidate the two mechanisms through which the sense of autonomy affects decision quality, and (2) propose a theory-driven design for fostering the sense of autonomy through personal smartphones. Notably, unlike studies based exclusively on hypothetical scenarios of AI use (e.g., Schrift et al., 2019; Longoni et al., 2019), we use a real-life AI system. This allows us to make another contribution by offering a readily implementable, real-life solution to enhance consumers’ sense of autonomy in AI-advised decision-making.

## Empirical Setting and Overview

The AI system: We used an AI-based apparel shopping system that provides personalized recommendations of clothing products (hereafter referred to as store AI or AI). The AI machine appears like a floor mirror, with a 55-inch digital touchscreen and vision processors. To start, the store AI takes a head-to-toe scan of a consumer standing in front of it, capturing data such as gender, age, height, weight, and hairstyle. It then analyzes the consumer’s fashion preferences and characteristics (e.g., body shape, skin tone) and recommends some products. The consumer can view the recommended items displayed on the screen. The consumer can also browse other items not recommended by the AI if they wish. The store AI is designed to offer many benefits. For instance, the AI can estimate fit for various types of clothing and fabrics across different body shapes and choose the right size for a consumer precisely and efficiently, even better than humans can. It can accurately estimate a consumer’s age and select age-appropriate clothing, and can recommend complementary clothing colors for a consumer’s skin tone. By inferring patterns from market data, the AI can also expertly forecast and recommend seasonal trendy picks. Accordingly, the core function of store AI is akin to that of a fashion specialist who can recommend highly personalized, well-suited, expertly curated choices to improve consumer purchase decisions. This aligns with the typical portrayal of store AI in popular press and industry reports: a smart “fashion specialist” who can inform a consumer about “which outfit looks better” (Morgan, 2019) or a “personal stylist” who can help a consumer find the right products (McQuarrie, 2024). ## Studies 1A & 1B: Effect of the Sense of Autonomy on the Purchase Decision

When making purchase decisions, consumers often relinquish control over the decisions to external experts in pursuit of better choices (Solomon, 1986). Given that the store AI can provide highly personalized, expertly curated product recommendations, consumers may opt to let the AI make some decisions for them. In this process, while consumers can improve their decisions by conceding some choice-making to the AI, they may perceive their choices as being constrained or compelled by the store AI. We argue that AI recommendations can feel constraining: personalized expert advice may impose an external force that prescribes what the “right” or “most appropriate” choices are or ought to be (Botti et al., 2023), which potentially “limits or compels people to behave in a manner opposed to meeting essential needs and desires” (Martin & Hill, 2012, p. 1158), thus interfering with an individual’s own interests and priorities. As such, while consumers relinquish control to AI for better decisions, AIdriven recommendations may paradoxically constrain the consumers’ choices and impair their decision quality. We contend that the root of this paradox lies in an innate limitation of AI: although AI excels at inferring decision patterns common among consumers, its ability to fully capture the unique, idiosyncratic factors that shape an individual's decisions is limited. These idiosyncratic factors, such as unexpressed motives and unobservable needs, are important to consumers, but they may not be adequately represented in the training data of AI. Therefore, whereas AI recommendations could be the commonly optimal choices for the average consumer, they are not necessarily optimal for each individual consumer. We term this inherent limitation in capturing idiosyncratic decision factors the uniqueness neglect of AI.

We theorize that enhancing the sense of autonomy in AIassisted decision-making is a pivotal means to mitigate AI’s uniqueness neglect and thus improves decision quality. The sense of autonomy refers to the extent to which consumers experience their decision-making as being self-determined (Deci & Ryan, 2000). It is the feeling that choices align with one’s authentic interests and needs (Chirkov et al., 2003). Enhancing the sense of autonomy directs consumers to selfdetermine how they utilize AI recommendations in accordance with their own priorities. Thus, a heightened sense of autonomy promotes the integration of more personal factors into decision-making, compensating for what the AI might neglect. This mechanism does not emerge in traditional agent-engaged contexts, where typical salespeople are trained as “knowledge brokers” (Homburg et al., 2009; Rapp et al., 2014) who use methods like “dialogue with customers to discover their expressed and unexpressed needs” (Rapp et al., 2014, p. 245). Furthermore, research suggests that adding private information that only humans have (but AI does not) to AI algorithms can produce more precise predictions (Ibrahim et al., 2021). In complex consumer decisions, much of this private information constitutes deeply personal, idiosyncratic factors that cannot be fully captured by AI. For apparel purchases, decision drivers like personal values, tastes, and situations are often internal and unique to each individual (Sierra & Hyman, 2011; Yang et al., 2023). When the store AI fosters a sense of autonomy in its users, consumers are more likely to incorporate such factors, which may be overlooked by the AI. This results in purchase decisions that are likely to more closely match the consumers’ preferences, which is indicative of improved decision quality (Spiller & Belogolova, 2017). Therefore, we predict that enhancing the sense of autonomy in using AI will improve the quality of purchase decisions.

To assess purchase decisions, we examine the outcomes of the final purchase decision itself, i.e., purchase intention (Study 1A, B), as well as decision satisfaction (Study 1A, B) and decision confidence (Study 1B), which are established indicators of decision quality in consumer behavior literature (Pereira, 2000; Fasolo et al., 2005; Thomson, 2006). While purchase intention (and actual purchases in Study 3B) directly shapes retailer performance (Dodds et al., 1991), decision quality measures reflect benefits to consumers. Thus, our focal dependent variables are both theoretically and managerially relevant for assessing both the retailer’s performance and consumer welfare.

## Study 1A: Test of the Sense of Autonomy on Decision Quality

Study 1A tested our prediction: a high (vs. low) sense of autonomy in using AI to make purchase decisions leads to a higher purchase intention and higher decision satisfaction. We experimentally manipulated the sense of autonomy in using AI in this controlled lab experiment.

## Study 1A: Method

124 undergraduates (70.16% female; $M _ { \mathrm { a g e } } \left( S D \right) = 2 0 . 5 2 \left( 1 . 5 6 \right) )$ from a major university in Shanghai participated in this study, in return for 30 RMB. A pre-experiment screening ensured that all participants had apparel shopping experience in retail stores. We used a one-way between-subjects design with three conditions (AI description: high-autonomy vs. low-autonomy vs. control/no description). Participants were randomly assigned to one of the three conditions, with gender approximately balanced across conditions.

Procedure: Upon arrival, participants were told that they were participating in an apparel shopping task for a fictitious retail store. The cover story for this task was that they would be testing an AI system that the retail store planned to implement. Following this general introduction, each participant was given written instructions about the shopping task and a description of the store AI. The AI description varied as a function of the experimental conditions (See Manipulation subsection below).

Participants individually entered a mock retail store, which was a physical store designed to appear like a real clothing store. Clothing was displayed on four bars and two racks, and a mannequin and signs and graphics were also displayed. The store AI was placed on one side of the mock retail store. Participants were directed to the AI by a same-gender confederate posing as a store staff member. The confederates were trained to act in a standard manner to minimize potential confounding. Then, each participant was instructed to use the AI for up to 10 minutes and choose a clothing item to purchase at the end. After completion, participants filled out a questionnaire and were debriefed. A probe showed that none of the participants had used this AI or a similar kind of AI before, and none of the participants guessed the study’s purpose. A randomization check indicated that the participants’ age, gender, or experience of apparel shopping did not affect the interpretation of the results.

Manipulation: We used two versions of the store AI description to induce either a high or a low sense of autonomy in using the store AI. Prior to the manipulation, all participants read the same written instructions:

Imagine that you want to buy an apparel item for yourself. You entered a retail clothing store, in which an artificial intelligence (AI) based machine is available. The sales associate in the store will introduce the machine to you. Then you will use the machine to shop for an apparel item.

Then, our manipulation occurred: participants in the low- and high-autonomy conditions read the following written descriptions of the store AI, respectively.

Store AI Description 1 (low-autonomy): This AI based machine is our store’s sales guide. It will predict your preferences and suggest what clothing items are good choices for you. You do not need to know how the AI works. You just need to follow the sales guide’s instructions to get recommendations. The entire process will be accomplished for you by our store’s sales guide.

Store AI Description 2 (high-autonomy): This AIbased machine is your personal tool. Just like using your own device, you can use it and explore as much as you’d like to. You will get to know more about the AI when using it. Take your time to explore, and you will find some ideas. The entire process is accomplished by you, using your personal tool.

In the low-autonomy condition, we described the store AI following how it is typically portrayed by businesses and mass media, namely as an intelligent guide or “fashion specialist” capable of making the “right” choices for consumers (Morgan, 2019; Puntoni et al., 2021). Such a practice can undermine consumers’ sense of autonomy by implying that the decisionmaking process should be directed by an external authority, potentially deterring consumers from initiating selfdetermined decision-making. Thus, this description of AI is expected to reduce the consumer’s sense of autonomy in using the store AI. In contrast, consumers experience autonomy when they perceive that they can effectively determine their choices in accordance with their authentic selves (Chirkov et al., 2003). Hence, in the high-autonomy condition, the description of the store AI features the AI as a personal tool, emphasizing that consumers can determine their own ways of using the AI to make choices. By promoting the sense of a self-determined decision process, this description of AI is expected to enhance the consumer’s sense of autonomy in using the AI.

Across all conditions, the store AI system itself (i.e., algorithm, functions, displays, and ways of operation) was kept constant. The only difference among treatment conditions was a high vs. a low sense of autonomy of participants in using the store AI (as induced by the manipulation).

Pilot study: We ran a pilot study using a separate sample of 60 adults (63.33% female; $M _ { \mathrm { a g e } } \ ( S D ) = 2 7 . 6 2 \ ( 5 . 5 2 ) )$ to test whether our AI description manipulation induced users’ sense of autonomy (high vs. low) as intended. Participants responded to six items measuring the sense of autonomy adapted from prior research to suit the AI context (Dahl & Moreau, 2007; Thomson, 2006; see Appendix B). The preamble for each item read: “When I was using the AI to make purchase decisions…” Participants rated their agreement with statements such as “I felt that I have freedom in making the choices” on a 7-point scale (1 = strongly disagree; 7 = strongly agree). The ratings were averaged to obtain the overall score $( a = 0 . 8 8 )$ . Potential confounders: We examined whether our manipulation might lead to divergent perceptions of the AI system itself, thereby inducing confounding. To test the potential effects related to perceptions of the technology, we measured perceived newness, understandability, ease-of-use, and trust of the AI using 7-point bipolar scales. These variables did not differ due to our manipulation (see the transparency materials for details). Taken together, the results from the pilot study indicate that the manipulation was successful.

Measures: Participants’ sense of autonomy was measured using the same six items as in the pilot Study $( \alpha = 0 . 8 5 )$ Decision satisfaction was defined as the extent to which participants were satisfied with the purchase decision; it was measured on a three-item scale (Thomson, 2006; 1 = not at all, 7 = very much, $\mathfrak { a } = 0 . 9 1 )$ . Following Chang and Wildt (1994), we measured purchase intention by asking participants to report how likely they would purchase the chosen clothing on a 7-point scale (1 = not at all likely, 7 = extremely likely). For control variables, in a pre-experiment questionnaire, we measured participants’ gender, age, monthly spending on clothing, frequency of buying clothing, and knowledge about fashion (see the transparency materials). We also recorded the selling price of the clothing item each participant had chosen. ## Study 1A: Results

## Study 1B: Test an Alternative Explanation and Explore Process Evidence

We posit that the sense of autonomy explains the positive effects of our treatment on the decision outcomes. It is plausible that our AI description treatment leads consumers to process more information about the products; thus, information processing could be a potential mechanism driving the observed effects. Study 1B tested this alternative account by manipulating the participants’ information processing capacity. Reducing cognitive capacity (via cognitive load) impedes information processing (Etkin & Ghosh, 2018; Gilbert et al., 1995). If the positive effects of the treatment are due to a greater degree of information processing, then imposing a cognitive load (thus curbing information processing) should attenuate the positive main effect (i.e., showing significant moderation). However, if the positive effect is due to an enhanced sense of autonomy, as we theorize, then imposing a cognitive load should have no impact on the main effect.

## Studies 1A & 1B: Discussion

The results from Studies 1A and 1B provide support for our prediction that enhancing the sense of autonomy in using the store AI improves the quality of the purchase decisions, as indicated by higher decision satisfaction, decision confidence, and purchase intention. Study 1B ruled out an alternative explanation of information processing. The post hoc analysis of the interview data offers insights into the process: highautonomy participants thought more about their own needs when making the purchase decision, lending support to our theorized mechanism that the sense of autonomy helps overcome AI’s uniqueness neglect. We also examined and ruled out privacy concerns as an alternative explanation; details are reported in the transparency materials.

As a supplemental study to Study 1A, we ran a randomized field experiment in a real-life fashion store to test the effect of our AI description treatment on actual purchase behavior. This field study replicated the key finding of Study 1A, showing that the high-autonomy condition significantly increased the probability of actual purchase compared to that of the low-autonomy and the control conditions (see details in the transparency materials). together, these studies provided robust evidence for the causal effect, with a high degree of internal and external validity, that enhancing the sense of autonomy in using AI improves purchase decisions. Next, we explicate the underlying impact mechanism of the sense of autonomy on decision quality.

## Studies 2A & 2B: Two Distinct Mechanisms of the Sense Of Autonomy

We theorize that a heightened sense of autonomy in using AI leads to improved purchase decisions because the sense of autonomy can serve as a means to address AI’s uniqueness neglect by encouraging consumers to integrate their own unique personal factors. If this theory holds true, the heightened sense of autonomy should compensate for AI’s uniqueness neglect, which would manifest as a reduction in consumers’ concern about this. Following this logic, we predict that a high (vs. low) sense of autonomy will reduce consumers’ concern about AI’s uniqueness neglect, leading to more positive decision outcomes, as previously discussed.

An alternative mechanism is that the sense of autonomy is an end itself. People have a basic psychological need to remain uncontrolled by external forces (Iyengar & Lepper, 1999; Deci & Ryan, 2000). According to Schrift et al. (2019), knowing that AI can predict consumers’ preferences may lead consumers to deviate from their preferences and choose lesspreferred options, thus implying a general reactance to AI as a threat to consumers’ sense of autonomy. The fundamental driver for this reactance-based mechanism is that individuals resist a deterministic view of their choices and lives (André et al., 2018; Brehm, 1966). The AI can be seen by people as a threat to their sense of autonomy, as it implies that their choices can be predetermined and are not really under their control, thereby running counter to people’s fundamental need for freedom of choice and free will (André et al., 2018). Reactance theory (Brehm, 1966) explains such a phenomenon where people exhibit reactance to attempts viewed as a threat to their sense of autonomy and free will. Reactance is an aversive state people experience when their freedom is threatened. It is typically manifested as aggression or hostility toward the threat (Clee & Wicklund, 1980), measured as anger and negative cognitions (Dillard & Shen, 2005). If this mechanism is true, a high (vs. low) sense of autonomy will reduce consumer reactance toward the AI, which, in turn, will lead to more positive decision outcomes.

The above two mechanisms of the sense of autonomy derive from distinct theories and offer divergent explanations for the observed effects. Distinguishing the two mechanisms is a critical step towards a deeper understanding of AI use and can provide useful guidance on best practices to improve consumer decision-making and welfare. The goal of Studies 2A and 2B is to test these two mechanisms of the sense of autonomy.

## Study 2A: Testing the Two Distinct Mechanisms via Mediation

## Study 2A: Method

83 undergraduates (81.93% female; $M _ { \mathrm { a g e } } \left( S D \right) = 1 9 . 7 7 \left( 1 . 8 8 \right) )$ from a university in Shanghai participated in the experiment on an individual basis, in return for 30 RMB. The experiment used a one-way between-subjects design in which participants were randomly assigned to one of the two AI description conditions (high-autonomy vs. low-autonomy; same as in Study 1A). Attention, manipulation, and randomization checks are reported in the transparency materials.

Participants were allowed up to 10 minutes to complete the same apparel shopping task as in Study 1A. After the task, participants reported the sense of autonomy (α = 0.89), decision satisfaction (α = 0.89), decision confidence (α = 0.84), and purchase intention. We measured the uniqueness neglect concern by asking participants to rate the extent to which they would be concerned that the store AI “would not recognize the uniqueness of your clothing choice,” “would not consider your unique circumstances,” and “would not tailor the recommendation to your unique case” (1 = not at all, 7 = very much, α = 0.80; adapted from Longoni et al., 2019).

To examine the reactance-based mechanism, we assessed reactance by measuring anger and negative cognitions following reactance research (Dillard & Shen, 2005; Gardner & Leshner, 2016). Anger was measured with four items: “to what extent did this store AI make you feel irritated / angry / annoyed / aggravated” (1 = not at all, 7 = very much; Dillard & Shen, 2005). A three-item measure was adapted to assess negative cognitions (e.g., “Did you think of points that went against the store AI while you were using it?” 1 = not at all, 7 = very much; Gardner & Leshner, 2016). The measures of anger and negative cognitions were averaged to obtain the score on the reactance scale (α = 0.88). Table 4 reports the descriptive statistics of these two mediators.

## Study 2A: Results

The sense of autonomy attenuates the uniqueness neglect concern. Participants reported greater decision satisfaction (M = 5.33, SD = 1.10), decision confidence (M = 4.59, SD = 1.08), and purchase intention (M = 4.98, SD = 1.15) in the highautonomy condition than in the low-autonomy condition (decision satisfaction: M = 4.67, SD = 1.31; F(1, 75) = 7.84, p = 0.007, partial η<sup>2</sup> = 0.10; decision confidence: M = 4.17, SD = 0.97; F(1, 75) = 4.14, p = 0.05, partial η<sup>2</sup> = 0.05; purchase intention: M = 4.10, SD = 1.28; F(1, 75) = 12.26, p < 0.001, partial η<sup>2</sup> = 0.14).

To test our proposed mechanism of the sense of autonomy, we ran a bootstrapping mediation analysis (Model 4, 5,000 resamples, Hayes, 2018) on each of the three dependent variables with the uniqueness neglect concern as the mediator. The data revealed that the uniqueness neglect concern mediated the positive effects of high-autonomy (vs. lowautonomy) on decision satisfaction (indirect effect = 0.52, SE = 0.20, confidence interval (CI) [0.12, 0.90]), decision confidence (indirect effect = 0.24, SE = 0.12, CI [0.04, 0.50]), and purchase intention (indirect effect = 0.39, SE = 0.16, CI [0.08, 0.72]). Thus, the results provide process evidence for our theorized mechanism of the sense of autonomy.

Reactance-based mechanism: To test whether reactance explains the observed effects, we ran a mediation analysis (Model 4, 5,000 resamples, Hayes, 2018) following prior reactance research (Dillard & Shen, 2005). The results did not show significant mediation effect of reactance on any of the dependent variables (decision satisfaction: indirect effect = $0 . { \dot { 2 } } 3 , S E = 0 . 1 6 , \mathrm { C I } [ - 0 . 0 8 , 0 . 5 4 ] ;$ decision confidence: indirect $\mathsf { e f f e c t } = 0 . 1 2 , S E = 0 . 0 9 , \mathrm { C I } \ [ - 0 . 0 5 , 0 . 3 3 ] .$ ; purchase intention: indirect effect = 0.18, SE = 0.14, CI [-0.06, 0.48]). Therefore, the results cast doubt on the alternative mechanism that reactance drives the observed effects.

In sum, the results of Study 2A provide mediational evidence for our theorized mechanism, but not for the reactance-based mechanism. We also tested and ruled out additional alternative mediators, such as perceived ai support; we report the details in the transparency materials.

## Study 2B: Test the Two Distinct Mechanisms by Manipulating Unique Consideration

Study 2B further tested the mechanisms of the sense of autonomy by manipulating whether a decision requires considering unique personal factors. If enhancing the sense of autonomy compensates for AI’s uniqueness neglect and leads to improved decisions, then making a decision that requires little unique consideration would attenuate the positive main effects (i.e., showing significant moderation). Otherwise, if the main effects on decision outcomes do not change when a decision needs little unique consideration, it would suggest evidence that the sense of autonomy is an end in itself. Adapting Longoni et al.’s (2019) paradigm, we tested this unique consideration prediction by having participants make a purchase decision, either for themselves (high unique consideration) or for an average person (low unique consideration).

## Study 2B: Method

164 undergraduates (76.83% female; $M _ { \mathrm { a g e } } \left( S D \right) = 2 0 . 5 2 \left( 2 . 1 9 \right) )$ from a university in Shanghai participated on an individual basis, in return for course credit. $\mathbf { A } 2 \times 2$ full-factorial design was used with two factors manipulated between-subjects: (1) AI description (high-autonomy vs. low-autonomy; same as in Study 1A), and (2) receiver of the product (self vs. average other).

For product receivers, we manipulated whether participants made a purchase decision for themselves or for an average other person. Specifically, participants read:

[Receiver of the product: Self] “Imagine that you want to buy an apparel item for yourself. You entered a retail clothing store, in which an artificial intelligence (AI) based machine is available. The sales associate in the store will introduce the machine to you. Then you will use the machine to shop for an apparel item for yourself.”

[Receiver of the product: Average Other Person] “Imagine that you want to buy an apparel item for a typical customer with a similar body size as yours. You entered a retail clothing store, in which an artificial intelligence (AI) based machine is available. The sales associate in the store will introduce the machine to you. Then you will use the machine to shop for an apparel item for that typical customer.”

The decision scenarios were adapted from Longoni et al. (2019). When deciding for an average other person, people are less likely to consider the unique preferences or needs of that person. In other words, compared to deciding for oneself, deciding for an average other person requires little unique consideration. After reading the decision scenario, participants were allowed up to 10 minutes to complete the apparel shopping task, followed by a questionnaire. Our manipulation was successful: 100% (95.24%) of the participants reported that the purchase decision was made for themselves (an average other person) in the self (average other person) condition. further details of attention and randomization checks are reported in the transparency materials.

## Studies 2A & 2B: Discussion

We obtained consistent evidence for our theorized mechanism via mediation (Study 2A) and moderation studies (Study 2B). Our results indicate that the observed effects are unlikely to be explained by the reactance-based mechanism, challenging the notion that AI provokes general consumer reactance (André et al., 2018). Instead, the findings provide process evidence that enhancing the sense of autonomy in using AI invites consumers to integrate their own personal preferences and needs into the AI-assisted decision-making, thereby remediating their unique personal factors that AI might otherwise overlook and thus improving purchase decisions. Next, we propose a practical solution to enhance the sense of autonomy in using AI.

## Studies 3A & 3B: An Intervention of Smartphone-Based Store AI

If enhancing the sense of autonomy in using AI leads to improved purchase decisions, can we achieve it through the design of the AI system? Studies 3A and 3B propose and test a novel design to enhance the sense of autonomy. Specifically, we built a smartphone-based app that enables consumers to use the store AI on their personal smartphones (see Appendix A for more details). IS scholars have been calling for more attention to human-centered AI design, as evidenced by an Information Systems Research special issue emphasizing that “designers of information systems have to increase their focus on interactions, control, and interface points such that the resulting system is efficient, effective, and addresses the issues of appropriate human control” (Jain et al., 2018, p. 250). We respond to this important call. To the best of our knowledge, this research is among the first to explore how the touch point of user interaction with AI (i.e., personal smartphone in this context) may influence AI-assisted decision-making.

We predict that using the store AI on a personal smartphone will enhance the users’ sense of autonomy, which, in turn, will improve the quality of the purchase decisions previously discussed. Theories of self-focus (Gibbons, 1990; Hull et al., 1988) differentiate two states associated with people focusing on themselves: private self-focus and public self-focus. Private self-focus involves an increased focus on private, internal aspects of the self, such as personal thoughts, feelings, and desires. This contrasts with public self-focus, which involves being aware of how one is viewed by others. According to Song and Sela (2023), using personal smartphones activates one’s private self-focus, shifting users’ attention toward one’s personal goals, values, and feelings. They found that making choices on one’s personal smartphone, but not on a generic smartphone, increased choices that expressed one’s personal preferences. We propose that private self-focus fosters the sense of autonomy. As private self-focus turns individuals toward self-reflection and introspection (and away from external influences; Gibbons, 1990), it makes one’s own priorities salient, and thus the decision is likely to be made in accordance with those salient factors. This nurtures the sense of autonomy, i.e., the experience of decisions as determined by one’s own needs and interests (Chirkov et al., 2003). Thus, using AI on personal smartphones is expected to enhance the sense of autonomy in AI-assisted decision-making.

Our prediction is also in line with the literature on ownership and appropriation. Research has shown that owning an object endows its owner with a sense of control and authority over how the object is used (Jussila et al., 2015). An experimental study (Brasel & Gips, 2014; Study 2) showed that consumers perceive greater control when using their own tablets (vs. the laboratory’s tablets) for customer service. Similarly, a qualitative study found that when using customer-owned (vs. firm-owned) selfservice devices, customers largely control and direct how they are being served (Gummerus et al., 2019). These studies suggest that ownership makes the perception specifically salient, such that the consumers themselves, rather than external forces such as a firm, have control over their own actions. This logic aligns with the notion of appropriation, or how individuals actively control the use of technology to suit their needs (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994). Appropriation occurs as users actively select among the elements of a technology, adopting certain ones while omitting others, to control their technology usage and outcomes (Kirk et al., 2015). Research has shown that individuals engage in greater appropriation of personal technologies than organizational ones because individuals perceive that the use of a personal technology is under their volitional control and that what constitutes suitable use is determined by them rather than by a top-down directive (Zamani et al., 2022; Schmitz et al., 2016). Hence, when using a store AI on their smartphones, consumers are more likely to determine how to utilize the AI to suit their own preferences and needs. Altogether, we predict that using a store AI on a personal (vs. the retailer’s) smartphone will enhance consumers’ sense of autonomy, which, in turn, will lead to improved purchase decisions, as previously theorized.

## Study 3A: Effect of a Personal Smartphone-Based AI on the Sense of Autonomy

Study 3A tested our prediction that using the store AI via a personal (vs. the retailer’s) smartphone will enhance the sense of autonomy in using the AI, which, in turn, will lead to increased purchase intention and decision satisfaction. We used a one-way between-subjects design with three conditions (use AI via: personal phone vs. retailer’s phone vs. control). 184 graduate students (77.17% female; $M _ { \mathrm { a g e } } = 2 2 . 3 9 , S D = 2 . 7 9 )$ from a university in Shanghai participated, in return for 30 RMB. Prescreening ensured that all participants used personal smartphones daily and that they all had apparel shopping experience at retail stores.

## Study 3A: Method

The experimental procedure was similar to that of Study 1A. Participants were given up to 15 minutes to complete the same shopping task. In contrast to Study 1A, no AI description was used. All participants first stood in front of the store AI machine for 3-5 seconds when the AI took a body scan, processed data, and recommended products to them (same as in Study 1A). Next, participants in the control condition continued the task using the AI machine. Participants in the personal-phone condition used the AI app on their own smartphones. Participants in the retailer-phone condition used the AI app on a smartphone (iPhone 7) provided by the mock store. Differences among smartphones were minimal; most of the participants phone brands were either iPhone or Huawei. No participant reported any technical difficulties with the AI app. After the shopping task, participants completed a questionnaire. We again checked AI perceptions and time spent on the store AI. None of these variables or the control variables significantly differed across experimental conditions (see the transparency materials).

## Study 3A: Results

A one-way ANCOVA on decision satisfaction revealed a significant main effect, F(2, 175) = 19.51, p < 0.001, partial η<sup>2</sup> = 0.18 (Table 5). Planned contrasts showed greater decision satisfaction among the personal-phone participants $( M = 5 . 8 3$ $S D = 0 . 6 6 )$ compared to the retailer-phone participants (M = 5.01, SD = 0.98), F(1, 116) = 31.18, p < 0.001, partial $\eta ^ { 2 } = 0 . 2 1$ A significant main effect was also revealed on purchase intention, F(2, 175) = 10.34, p < 0.001, partial $\mathfrak { n } ^ { 2 } = 0 . 1 1$ , with personal-phone participants reporting a higher purchase intention $( M = 5 . 4 6 , \ S D = 1 . 0 6 )$ than their retailer-phone counterparts $( M = 4 . 7 0 , S D = 1 . 1 6 ) , F ( 1 , 1 1 6 ) = 1 5 . 0 0 , p <$ 0.001, partial $\mathfrak { n } ^ { 2 } = 0 . 1 1$

We then ran mediation analyses on the sense of autonomy. Participants’ sense of autonomy was significantly higher in the personal-phone condition $( M \bar { = } 5 . 5 7 , \ \bar { \cal S } D = 0 . \bar { 7 } 8 )$ than in the retailer-phone condition (M = 4.71, SD = 1.06), F (1, 116) = 28.97, p < 0.001, partial $\eta ^ { 2 } = 0 . 2 0$ , and the control condition (M = 4.81, SD = 0.86), F (1, 113) = 25.28, p < 0.001, partial $\boldsymbol { \eta } ^ { 2 } =$ 0.18. Mediation analyses (Model 4, Hayes, 2018) revealed that the sense of autonomy mediated the positive effects of our manipulation on decision satisfaction (indirect effect = 0.52, SE = 0.13, CI [.28, 0.80]) and purchase intention (indirect effect = 0.56, SE = 0.13, CI [.33, 0.85]), thus supporting our prediction. In sum, Study 3A showed that our design—the personal smartphone-based store AI—led to a higher sense of autonomy, which in turn increased purchase intention and decision satisfaction. In Study 3B, we tested the applicability of this proposed design in the field.

## Study 3B: Test the Smartphone-based AI Design in a Field Experiment

Hence, this study focused on the effects of two treatments—(1) the highautonomy AI description treatment (for simplicity, hereafter referred to as the “description” condition), and (2) the personal smartphone-based store AI treatment (hereafter referred to as the “personal-phone” condition)—on two important product performance outcomes: product sales and product return rate.

## Study 3B: Method

We conducted Study 3B at a retail fashion store in Shanghai for 6 consecutive weeks during November-December 2019. The first 2 weeks served as the pre-treatment period (i.e., no store AI), the description condition was in the third and fifth weeks, and the personal-phone condition in the fourth and sixth weeks. In the description condition, the high-autonomy AI description was displayed on the screen of the AI machine, but the smartphone-based AI design was not implemented. In the personal-phone condition, instead of using the description, the smartphone-based store AI was implemented. Consumers could use the AI app by scanning a QR code displayed on the store AI with their personal smartphones.<sup>8</sup>

Since we were examining the effects of the two proposed treatments on product sales and product return rate, the unit of analysis was the individual product. Our analyses focused on 221 unique products in three categories (tops, bottoms, and coats/jackets) that could be purchased in the fashion store during the experimental period. We randomly selected

100 products (out of 221) and made them available in the store AI—they were the AI-treated products. The remaining 121 products were not available or could not be seen in the AI and served as the baseline products. Randomization checks confirmed no significant differences in product price, discount, pre-treatment sales volume, or pre-treatment product return rate between the treated products and the baseline products (see Table 6).

## Study 3B: Results

We ran an ANCOVA regression model to test the effects of the two treatments (“personal-phone” and “description”) on weekly product sales and product return rate, with the control variables (i.e., price, discount, product category, holiday,<sup>9</sup> time trend, pre-treatment product sales volume, and pre-treatment product return rate) used as covariates (Table 7). To compare the impacts of the two treatments, we examined the interaction effect of Product type × Treatment condition. Descriptive statistics are reported in the transparency materials. the results revealed a marginally significant interaction effect for product sales (0.32, p = 0.06), indicating that the increase in product sales was greater in the personal-phone condition than in the description condition. A similar result pattern was found for the product return rate (-0.07, p = 0.04); the reduction in return rate was greater in the personal-phone condition than in the description condition.

## Studies 3A & 3B: Discussion

Together, Studies 3A and 3B demonstrate that our novel design of the personal smartphone-based AI boosted the sense of autonomy in using the store AI exceptionally well; notably, this design was field-verified as practically applicable and effective in practice. From an intervention standpoint, this smartphone-based AI treatment has an advantage over the description or the information-based treatment because it requires little reliance on consumers’ attention or comprehension (of a description).

## General Discussion

Across our lab and field studies, we found consistent support for our proposition that promoting consumers’ sense of autonomy in using AI recommendation systems compensates for AI’s uniqueness neglect, thus improving the quality of AIadvised purchase decisions. First, we offer causal evidence that enhancing the sense of autonomy using our AI description treatment leads to better purchase decisions—i.e., consumers are more likely to make purchases and feel more satisfied and more confident in the AI-assisted decisions (Studies 1A, 1B). Second, we provide process evidence that a heightened sense of autonomy in using AI prompts consumers to bring their own personal preferences and needs into the decision-making process (Study 1B), alleviating the concern that their uniqueness might be neglected by the AI (Study 2A), whereas the positive effects of the sense of autonomy diminish for decisions that require little unique consideration (Study 2B). Third, our novel design, which enabled consumers to use store AI on personal smartphones, fosters consumers’ sense of autonomy and produces consistent positive effects on the purchase decision (Study 3A). Finally, a real-world field experiment (Study 3B) confirms our observed positive effects on actual product purchases, confirming the external validity and managerial relevance of our findings. We also ruled out several alternative mechanisms, including consumer reactance (Studies 2A, 2B), information processing (Study 1B), privacy concern, and perceived ai support (reported in the transparency materials).

We also tested the effect of the sense of autonomy on consumer purchase decisions with a range of outcome measures, including individuals’ purchase intention, decision satisfaction (Studies 1A, 1B, 2A, 2B, 3A), decision confidence (Study 1B, 2A, 2B), actual purchase behavior (Supplemental Study 1A), productlevel sales and return rate (Study 3B), and qualitative data on the decision process (Study 1B). The triangulation of multiple sources of data not only provides a comprehensive picture of the effects of the sense of autonomy but also demonstrates the robustness of our results. Overall, our findings show that enhancing the sense of autonomy in using AI improves the quality of purchase decisions that better align with the consumers’ unique personal needs and preferences. Interestingly, using AI on a personal smartphone is an effective and practical way to enhance the consumer’s sense of autonomy.

## Theoretical Contributions

Our research contributes to the AI literature on four main fronts. First, we identify a critical factor that influences AIadvised decision-making, the sense of autonomy, and delineate the mechanism underlying this effect. We theorize that enhancing the sense of autonomy can compensate for the uniqueness neglect of AI recommendation systems—a limitation of AI to fully capture individuals’ idiosyncratic decision factors—by prompting consumers to incorporate their distinct personal factors. This neglect arises from the inherent challenge for AI to accurately represent the deeply personal and often internal individual decision factors. While our research is in the context of apparel purchases, the role of the sense of autonomy can generalize to other AI-advised decision contexts, where personal, idiosyncratic factors are important to human decision makers; thus, AI’s uniqueness neglect may significantly reduce the value of AI-driven advice. Such contexts are common and include, for example, complex purchases (e.g., skincare and beauty products, artwork and gifts, vacation packages, book and music subscriptions), personal choices (e.g., dating partners, fitness plans, and home décor), and other consumer decision contexts (e.g., dining and food, event and party themes). We thus extend the AI-advised decision-making literature, which has mainly focused on decisions involving few human preferences (Lehmann et al., 2022), to the domain of consumer decisions. This is a meaningful contribution because consumer decisions are intrinsically more “complex,” as the factors driving decisions can be deeply individualized. Our work also extends the delegation literature that focuses on splitting work between humans and AI to harness the strengths of each (Fügener et al., 2022). To both lines of inquiry, we offer a new theoretical lens on enhancing decision-making using AI: the integration of AI and humans, with AI-guided decisions being reconciled into human decisions through the sense of autonomy.

Second, we distinguish and explicitly test our mechanism of the sense of autonomy against that based on reactance theory (Brehm, 1966). Our findings challenge the prevailing discourse suggesting that AI poses a threat to consumers’ sense of autonomy and thus generates consumer reactance (André et al., 2018; Davenport et al., 2020; Puntoni et al., 2021). Rooted in reactance theory, this mechanism of the sense of autonomy denotes an orientation in which humans are motivated to resist external influences so as to ensure their freedom of behavior, even if doing so is to their detriment (Brehm, 1966). Within this theoretical tradition, AI systems are viewed as yet another form of “threats to human autonomy” (Davenport et al., 2020), joining the list of other autonomy threats in the reactance literature, such as manipulative promotions, aggressive sales tactics, and unsolicited advice (e.g., Brehm, 1966; Clee & Wicklund, 1980; Dillard & Shen, 2005). In contrast, our proposed theoretical mechanism is rooted in the distinct context of AI-advised decision-making, where the sense of autonomy remediates AI’s uniqueness neglect. We posit that the real issue regarding consumer autonomy is not concerns about fully delegating decisions to AI per se; rather, it relates to the experience that decisions are being compelled by the AI in a way that ignores an individual’s own needs and unique preferences. Hence, our work not only addresses recent calls for empirical research on enhancing consumers’ autonomy (Puntoni et al., 2021; Berente et al., 2021), but it also delineates a novel theoretical mechanism that is distinct from reactance theory.

Third, our notion of the AI’s uniqueness neglect complements Longoni et al. (2019). In the medical decision domain, Longoni et al. use the term uniqueness neglect to refer to consumers belief that AI, compared to a human doctor, may not be able to account for their unique symptoms and circumstances. They show that this belief is a strong psychological driver behind patients’ reluctance to rely on AI for medical decisions, even if the AI decisions are as good as those of a human doctor. In contrast, our notion of AI’s uniqueness neglect refers to a technological limitation inherent to the current state of AI: namely, its insufficient capability to capture the idiosyncratic factors unique to individual consumers. The two notions are connected, and we demonstrate that enhancing the sense of autonomy can compensate for AI’s uniqueness neglect and can reduce consumer concerns about their uniqueness being neglected (Study 2A). Thus, our work extends the literature on AI resistance (e.g., Longoni et al., 2019; Longoni & Cian, 2022; Yeomans et al., 2019; Castelo et al., 2019). The two notions also have divergent implications: Alleviating consumer concerns— which may or may not reflect AI’s actual capabilities—requires effective communication and transparency to build trust in the AI. By contrast, overcoming AI’s limitation of capturing consumers’ unique personal factors would likely necessitate the integration of human control. We demonstrate a practical way to address uniqueness neglect by enhancing the consumer’s sense of autonomy in using AI.

Finally, this is the first study to theorize and empirically test how using AI on personal smartphones can enhance users’ sense of autonomy and their decision-making. We offer an original proposition that using AI on personal smartphones can enhance users’ sense of autonomy because the private and personal nature of smartphone use evokes self-focus, fostering a decisionmaking process in accordance with one’s own preferences. We provide empirical evidence for the effectiveness of this AI design in both lab and field studies. Our findings respond to calls for research on the development of human-centered AI to study “issues of appropriate human control” over AI tools (Jain et al., 2018, p. 250). Our work also contributes to the growing IS research on smartphone use. While past research has focused on the market-level aggregate effects of smartphone use (Lee et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2024; Kwon et al., 2023), we posit a new role of smartphone use in fostering users’ sense of autonomy, which has broad implications for user behaviors, including its potential role in user interactions with smartphone apps and the choices or purchases made on smartphones. Moreover, our work represents an early exploration in advancing the understanding of personal technologies, not only as a technological product that provides the utility attributed to its use but also as a meaningful touch point for user-app interaction. This perspective offers timely insights, given the proliferation of personal devices, such as personal smartphones, smart watches, and smart glasses, in people’s everyday lives.

## Managerial Implications

Our findings provide ready-to-implement implications for managers. First, we caution against the mainstream practices of retailers implementing or portraying a store AI as a professional advisor, fashion specialist, or a similar expert that can take over the entire decision-making process. Common narratives about AI from the mass media also often reinforce this view. Our results imply that this practice in fact diminishes, rather than enhances, the product purchasing experience by inhibiting consumers sense of autonomy. This raises a timely alert for the retail industry, which is under a major expansion in adopting AI to enhance the consumer experience. The importance of “significant management issues undergoing a technology-enabled transformation” is underscored by its presence as an intellectual core of IS (Agarwal & Lucas, 2005, p. 395). Our work points to the importance of the sense of autonomy when consumers use store AI, and highlights the significance of carefully planning and testing the way store AI is deployed in practice.

Second, the findings of the field experiment suggest actionable strategies that can help to enhance consumers’ sense of autonomy in using AI and improve their decision-making: for example, emphasizing users as the central authority in the decision-making process, reminding users that they have free input and should not feel pressured, and making salient the user’s ultimate control over the AI-assisted decision outcomes. Most intriguingly, our field-verified design, which enabled consumers to use the store AI on their personal smartphones via an app, was shown to boost consumers’ sense of autonomy exceptionally well. This design is readily implementable. Beyond the retail context, designers of similar AI recommendation systems can consider creating interface points for consumers to use AI on their personal devices. We hope the proposed lens of the sense of autonomy offers an extendable framework to inform the future design of AI tools: A system (or the way it is used) should deploy measures to enhance the capacity for human user determination so that users are able to retain control over and fully stand behind the AI-advised decisions (i.e., by creating a sense of autonomy in AI tool users).

Third, while understanding how to effectively leverage information technologies to reduce product returns is of great managerial interest, limited IS research has examined the product return rate as a dependent variable (Yang & Xiong, 2019; Hong & Pavlou, 2014). Our field experiment contributes to this effort and, to our knowledge, is the first real-world analysis of store AI in a real-life retail setting. Product returns pose a significant issue for fashion retailers, as returns lead to substantial operational costs and undermine retailer profitability (McKinsey & Company, 2021). Returns can also be inconvenient and burdensome for consumers (Shulman et al., 2011). Our field results suggest that enabling consumers to make AI-assisted purchase decisions on their personal smartphones considerably decreases product return rates (and increases product sales). Together with the findings from our lab studies, we show that this reduction in returns is driven by an enhanced sense of autonomy. As such, effective support for consumers’ sense of autonomy in making purchases might be a cost-effective strategy to achieve a low product return rate.

## Limitations and Suggestions for Future Research

Several limitations and promising areas for future research warrant discussion. First, due to our use of a real store AI system, the AI recommendations to each participant could not be held constant. While this introduced the noise of real-life system use, such variation was necessary in the present study context to produce realistic and credible product recommendations, and it should not affect the interpretation of our results, given our randomized study design. Second, our research context was confined to fashion purchases; thus, the findings could be limited in their generalizability to the broader phenomenon of interest. As personalized AI recommendation systems continue to expand across various decision domains, studying the sense of autonomy is a fruitful and valuable avenue for future research. Future work could examine other domains in which AI is involved in consumer decision-making. For instance, the impact of the sense of autonomy may diminish when users are already familiar with the decision (e.g., shopping for clothing vs. the routine purchasing of household supplies), or when users are less sure about their own preferences (e.g., financial products) or when they are open to experimentation (e.g., food). In certain contexts, the sense of autonomy may even be less advantageous—for example, in contexts where reducing human involvement could allow more rational and efficient decision-making. Our findings thus warrant further investigation to establish their validity and boundaries.

Future research could explore other design elements that may nurture the sense of autonomy when using AI systems. For example, research has shown that haptic interactions with a device (e.g., touchscreen) facilitate a personal bonding between users and the device, but voice interaction (e.g., commands with Siri) leads users to see a device as a separate agent (Brasel, 2016). In this vein, new AI technologies that use innovative human-machine interfaces and interactions, such as gestures (e.g., smart watches), eye movements (e.g., smart glasses), and thought (e.g., brain-computer interface) offer an exciting potential to examine novel interface points with AI tools for IS researchers. Furthermore, as AI systems continue to permeate our daily lives and as people develop habits after repetitive use of these systems in the future, it would be valuable to examine how habitual use of AI over time, especially increasingly more sophisticated AI, might affect the mechanism of the sense of autonomy. For instance, future work could test whether habitual use diminishes the significance of the sense of autonomy in AIadvised decision-making.

## Concluding Remarks

The advent of AI-powered systems capable of taking on expansive decision-making roles brings the issue of human autonomy to the fore. Our work identifies a distinct role of consumers’ sense of autonomy in making decisions with the aid of personalized AI recommendation systems.

We show that the sense of autonomy can compensate for AI’s potential neglect of individual uniqueness, serving as a crucial means for consumers to maximize the benefits of AI-advised decision-making. It is our genuine hope that this work will stimulate a broader conversation on how human decisionmaking could be improved through the effective use of AI systems and that it will lead to future research on developing novel AI tools that enhance decision-making and create value.

## Additional Details and Discussion of the Store AI System

The store AI system: The store AI collects data from users and recommends products. Users can tap the touchscreen to view AI recommendations. In our experiments, the store AI recommended 24 options, and participants were also informed that they could view options not recommended by the AI by tapping a “Cloud Rack” icon on the screen.

Algorithm: The store AI uses machine learning to automatically infer decision rules by learning from data over time, as visualized in the flowchart Figure A1. In brief, based on (a) input data on each user (via sensors that collect the user’s face and body features) and (b) the database (including features of each product and models who are suitable to wear the product), the store AI conducts automatic feature matching and recommends products that are best suited for the user. Each user’s information is later integrated into the database, enabling the AI to learn and improve its recommendations over time.

![](/api/attachments/MPDXSZQJ/fulltext/images/1f72e2105d28ba945ea82fdca14f5890aa7399822a2d9cbdf9f67fdabe207f31.jpg)  
Figure A1. A Visualization of the Store AI’s Algorithm Flowchart

Mainstream perception of the store AI: Several brands, including Vero Moda and Jack & Jones, have begun implementing a focal store AI in their outlets. This AI system is commonly described or branded as a professional or intelligent shopping guide in the marketplace.

As a supplemental study, we performed a content analysis of newspaper articles relevant to the store AI published in 2018 to 2021 in English from the Nexis Uni database (N = 173), and in Chinese from the CND database (N = 123). A high proportion of the articles (87%) described this AI system as smart retail assistants, and/or mentioned its benefit of giving consumers “professional” or “smart” advice in making purchase decisions. Simplifying the shopping process was also a frequently mentioned benefit (68%). Thus, mass media communication seems to reinforce the view of the store AI as an intelligent shopping specialist. This mainstream perception of the AI may explain why our data failed to detect significant differences between the low-autonomy and control conditions in Study 1A. Even in the absence of a specific description of the AI (as in the control condition), participants seem to hold a common perception of the store AI as a professional shopping guide, similar to the description in the low-autonomy condition.

Our experiment setup: In our laboratory experiments, we only focused on the core AI functionality and disabled other add-on features of the machine, such as changing the virtual background. The AI description information was displayed on the screen of the AI machine.
