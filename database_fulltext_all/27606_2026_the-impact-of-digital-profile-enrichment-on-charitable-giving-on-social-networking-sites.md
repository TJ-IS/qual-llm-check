# THE IMPACT OF DIGITAL PROFILE ENRICHMENT ON CHARITABLE GIVING ON SOCIAL NETWORKING SITES<sup>1</sup>

Xue (Jane) Tan Information Technology and Operations Management Department, Cox School of Business Southern Methodist University, Dallas, TX, U.S.A. {janetan@smu.edu}

Yingda Lu Department of Information and Decision Sciences, College of Business Administration University of Illinois Chicago, Chicago, IL, U.S.A. {yingdalu@uic.edu}

Junjie Wu School of Economics and Management, MIIT Key Laboratory of Data Intelligence and Managemen Beihang University, Beijing, China {wujj@buaa.edu.cn}

Yong Tan Department of Information Systems and Operations Management, Foster School of Business University of Washington, Seattle, WA, U.S.A. {ytan@u.washington.edu}

In the last decade, social networking platform designers have made notable efforts to harness the power of networks for social good by elevating the prominence of individual donation information. This study investigates how digital profile enrichment that exhibits users’ charitable giving activities could influence users’ decisions about whether to give, how much to give, and whether to disclose contribution amounts. Our analyses are based on a profile enrichment intervention on Weibo, China’s largest social networking site. We found that the profile enrichment to exhibit users’ historical donation counts on social profiles decreased an average user’s odds of donating by 15.5% but increased the contribution amount by 2.69%. Strikingly, it increased the odds of revealing contribution amounts by 162%. Clustering analyses further revealed three patterns in response to the profile enrichment: “presenters” (9.7%), who reduced donation frequency but increased contribution amounts and the disclosure of contribution amounts; “restrainers” (47.3%), who reduced their giving; and “conformers” (43%), who increased giving after the profile enrichment. We discuss potential mechanisms by comparing the characteristics of different user clusters to underscore donor heterogeneity and uncover the nuanced impact of such digital profile enrichment.

Keywords: Digital profile, interrupted time series design, charitable giving, social networking sites

## Introduction

Social networking sites are increasingly involved in charitable fundraising to harness the power of networks for social good. Facebook has developed fundraising products for people and nonprofits to collect donations for organizational and personal causes through Birthday

Fundraisers, Personal Fundraisers, and Stories.<sup>2</sup> Instagram allows nonprofits to collect donations using the Donation sticker on a story or live video; it also enables donation buttons on nonprofits’ profile pages.<sup>3</sup> X (formerly Twitter) has experimented with several nonprofits to accept donations directly from tweets. <sup>4</sup> The largest social networking site in China, Weibo, launched its affiliated charitable crowdfunding service, Weibo Philanthropy, to raise funds for personal and organizational causes. Despite these efforts, it remains an open question how social networking sites—originally designed for sharing personal updates and opinions—should publicize users’ participation in social fundraising, and how such publicity influences charitable giving.

This paper examines the integration of social fundraising functions in social networking sites by focusing on digital profile enrichment that exhibits users’ historical charitable giving activities. Digital profiles are designed by social networking sites to document users’ subjective biographies and objective digital traces both within and beyond the scope of the social network (e.g., movies watched, places visited, and Yelp reviews generated) (Huang et al., 2016). These digital profiles are the primary channel for users to disclose their preferences, tastes, and opinions, which collectively define who they are. With a profile enrichment to exhibit donation history, users can no longer conceal their giving behavior, allowing others to observe, evaluate, and judge them (Prentice-Dunn & Rogers, 1982). <sup>5</sup> As such, profile enrichment plays a crucial role in influencing donation behavior on social networking platforms. It serves as a key accountability cue to increase people’s public self-awareness that their charitable giving activities are the object of other observers (Orive, 1984).<sup>6</sup> We therefore ask: How does profile enrichment impact people’s donation-related behaviors?

This research question is practically important for the design of social fundraising functions and theoretically intriguing for understanding people’s motivation to give. On the one hand, profile enrichment to exhibit donation history could accentuate charitable giving as a device to bolster moral reputation, which we term a reputation effect (Bénabou & Tirole, 2006; Inesi & Rios, 2023). On the other hand, it may create a saturation effect by making people, especially frequent donors, feel that they have given enough. These donors might, therefore, reduce donations to avoid standing out as extreme altruists (Frey & Meier, 2004). Further, donation-related behavior involves not only whether and how much to give but also whether to disclose contribution amounts. Past studies have shown that people conceal contribution amounts when they are either too large or too small because donors may face social judgment for either undercontribution or overcontribution (Burtch et al., 2015; Raihani, 2014). Heightened public self-awareness may reduce the disclosure of contribution amounts to avoid such judgment. It could also increase disclosure of contribution amounts if doing so brings reputational benefits (e.g., when the contribution amount is sufficiently large to signal generosity). Thus, the impact of profile enrichment on donation-related behavior may be nuanced.

We leveraged a quasi-experiment from the largest microblogging site in China, Sina Weibo, to explore the impact of digital profile enrichment to exhibit charitable giving activities on users’ subsequent decisions about whether to donate, how much to donate, and whether to reveal contribution amounts. Between 1/12/2014 and 1/18/2014,<sup>7</sup> Sina Weibo enriched user digital profiles by exhibiting past donation counts from its affiliated charitable crowdfunding platform, Weibo Philanthropy, on users’ digital profiles. We obtained proprietary data that included both donation and social connection details and analyzed 5,058 users’ 125,915 donations during a long observation window between 11/1/2013 and 2/28/2014, and 3,103 users’ 11,905 donations during a short observation window between 1/1/2014 and 1/29/2014. We performed a population-level analysis over the long observation window by leveraging both an interrupted time-series (ITS) design and a regression discontinuity in time (RDiT) design (Bernal et al., 2017; Wagner et al., 2002) and a choice- and transaction-level analysis over the short observation window using a pre/post analysis. We found that, following the introduction of profile enrichment, the odds of donating decreased by 15.5%, but contribution amounts increased by 2.69%. Strikingly, the odds of disclosing contribution amounts increased by 162%.

To understand the impact of profile enrichment on the collection of donation-related behaviors in an integrated way, we conducted a clustering analysis. For each user, we constructed three data dimensions to represent the changes in their donation frequency, average contribution amounts, and the disclosure rates of contribution amounts before and after the profile enrichment. We used the k-means approach to identify three segments of users: restrainers (47.3%), presenters (9.7%), and conformers (43%). Presenters increased contribution amounts and disclosed their contributions more often to project a moral image, yet reduced donation frequency to avoid appearing excessively altruistic. Their extensive donation history and large follower bases support a reputation effect. In contrast, restrainers decreased, and conformers increased their giving. Comparative statistics show that restrainers had significantly more past donations, which may have led them to reduce contribution frequency to avoid appearing as extreme altruists—a finding that supports the saturation effect. Conversely, conformers had substantially more donor followees (i.e., followees who are also donors), which may have made them feel that they had not contributed enough and thus needed to give more.

This study makes three key contributions. First, we evaluated digital profile enrichment that exhibits users’ historical donations. The focus of a digital profile intervention sets our study apart from the charitable giving literature that has examined donation matching, rebate, and other fundraising appeal designs (Karlan & List, 2007; List, 2007). Second, while past work has discussed the positive link between public selfawareness and citizen behavior (Hwang & Lee, 2019), our empirical analysis reveals both the positive (reputation effect) and negative (saturation effect) mechanisms through which public self-awareness could impact charitable giving. Past works have also discussed the mixed effects of donation visibility. By showing that the positive and negative reactions can coexist in response to higher donation visibility, we help reconcile the mixed findings while adding new insights into donor heterogeneity. Third, prior studies on information control have primarily focused on the consequences of information concealment or revealment (Burtch et al., 2015, 2016; Jiang et al., 2021), whereas few studies have investigated the antecedents of information concealment or revealment (Sisco & Weber, 2019). We contribute to this literature by identifying the antecedents of donation disclosure and its relationship with other donation-related decisions in an integrated way.

## Research Context

## Sina Weibo and Weibo Philanthropy

Our research context is the largest Chinese microblogging platform, Sina Weibo (http://weibo.com), which offers an affiliated charitable crowdfunding application, Weibo Philanthropy (http://gongyi.weibo.com/). Sina Weibo launched in 2009; by 2012, when it developed Weibo Philanthropy, it had 504 million registered users, amounting to 40.9% of the Chinese online population (as of 2012). Due to its vast penetration, Sina Weibo represents a microcosm of Chinese society. Societal issues cover 25.6% of all topics discussed on Sina Weibo, and charity-related topics cover 1.7% (Weibo, 2015). Weibo Philanthropy is one of the largest crowdfunding marketplaces in China. By 2015, 20 million individuals had made charitable contributions on Weibo Philanthropy.

Similar to other crowdfunding marketplaces, a nonprofit organization or a qualified individual can register a project by setting up a web page for a specific beneficiary (Figure 1a). The web page lists descriptions of the charitable cause, the fundraising duration, the pledging goal, and other beneficiary information. The web page is updated with the status of the recipient and the transfer of funds to the recipient. The transaction-level donation history for each charitable project is publicly accessible, and the remaining amount needed to reach the pledging goal is automatically updated. Weibo Philanthropy applies the “keep-it-all” model, such that the amount raised is delivered to the beneficiaries regardless of whether or not the goal is reached. Before users can donate on Weibo Philanthropy, they are required to log in to their Sina Weibo account. This allowed us to identify donors by their usernames and access their social network information on the microblogging platform.

The user makes a sequence of three donation-related decisions on Weibo Philanthropy. First, the user decides whether to donate to a specific project. Second, the user decides how much to contribute to the project (if deciding to donate). Third, the user chooses whether to disclose the contribution amount. If the user decides to disclose, the contribution amount is disclosed in the donor list of the project page. It is also disclosed in the automatically generated microblog posted on the donor’s timeline if the donor chooses to post this microblog (Figures 1B and 1C/1D).

## Digital Profile Enrichment

Between 1/12/2014 and 1/18/2014, Sina Weibo enriched users’ digital profiles by adding a donation history widget to each user’s profile (Figure 2), which publicly exhibited the total number of charitable projects supported by the user through Weibo Philanthropy. We validated this update using the Internet Archive’s Wayback Machine, examining the accounts of celebrities (since the Archive only retains historical data for popular profiles) who were also donors before 01/12/2014. For all these accounts, the widget was not available on 1/11/2014, but it became available on 1/18/2014 (Appendix B). We also received confirmation from Weibo that this was a platform-level intervention that applied to every user. Because the widget was on users’ Sina Weibo profiles, it was publicly accessible for all users on the microblogging platform. While individual users were not able to find out whether other users had visited their profile page, following the profile enrichment, users would have understood that their donation information had become more visible. This profile enrichment is an example of an intervention that heightens users’ public self-awareness (White & Peloza, 2009). This is similar to how public selfawareness increases when subjects are informed that they will be videotaped, although the video may not be watched by anyone (Hass, 1984).

This profile update was not announced prior to its implementation, and there were no other updates that affected the user experience in Weibo Philanthropy within our observation window. As shown in Figure 2, the total number of past donations was highlighted at the top of the widget, and the thumbnails of the supported projects were shown underneath. The contribution amount was not shown in this donation history widget. This widget exhibited all projects supported by a user, even if a user chose not to share donations or concealed the donation amounts.

## Data Collection

We used proprietary donation transaction data obtained from Weibo. Our data covered 11/1/2013 to 2/28/2014. We considered the period between 1/12/2014 and 1/18/2014 to be the profile enrichment implementation period and excluded this period from our analyses. We focused on donors who made at least one donation before our observation window began, ensuring that they were all affected by the profile enrichment. We removed users who participated in charitable crowdfunding projects that included monetary incentives such as lotteries. We also excluded one user who contributed to over 500 projects within our observation and users who made single contributions higher than ￥ 10,000 (\$1623 in 2014). We removed organizational users because they are likely to have distinctive motives for giving.

We used a longer observation window for the population-level analysis, spanning 115 days (11/1/2013 to 1/12/2014 as pretreatment and 1/18/2014 to 2/28/2014 as post-treatment). During this period, we recorded 125,915 donations from 5,058 donors to 1,344 projects. For a more granular choice- and transaction-level analysis, we used a shorter 24-day window (1/1/2014 to 1/12/2014 as pre-treatment and 1/18/2014 to 1/29/2014 as post-treatment), yielding 11,905 donations from 3,103 donors to 420 projects. Our proprietary data allowed us to observe donation amounts regardless of whether donors publicly disclosed them.

## Variables

We constructed three outcome variables at the daily population level. The first outcome of interest is the logarithm of donation counts plus one, log(DonationCnt<sub>t</sub>+1), where t represents the day. The second outcome is the average log-transformed donation amount on day t, AvgDonationAmt<sub>t</sub>. The third outcome is the rate of donation amount disclosure on day t, AvgReveal<sub>t</sub>. The key independent variables are Treatment<sub>t</sub> and TimeSinceTreatment<sub>t</sub>, where Treatment<sub>t</sub> takes the value of 1 in the post-intervention period and 0 otherwise. TimeSinceTreatment is the number of days elapsed since the intervention; it takes the value of 0 in the pre-intervention period. We further used Time<sub>t</sub> to denote the number of days elapsed since the start of the observation window. It is a common practice to include other control variables in population-level quasi-experimental analyses to mitigate time confoundedness and guarantee the smooth impact of unobservable confounders during the intervention. We included two population-level controls: the day of the week (DayofWeek<sub>t</sub>) and the logarithm of the number of active projects plus one, log(ActiveProjects<sub>t</sub>+1). Summary statistics of population-level variables are reported in Appendix Table A1.

## Models

As discussed in Hausman and Rapson (2018), three quasiexperimental designs are widely adopted for policy evaluation using a single time series: RDiT, ITS, and a pre/post analysis. All approaches estimate the statistical difference for the outcomes of interest in pre-intervention and post-intervention time series. ITS differs from RDiT in that it not only estimates the level change but also the slope change of the outcome. Another difference is that ITS accounts for the time effect using linear trends, while RDiT accounts for time effects with polynomial functions. In contrast to RDiT and ITS, which are often estimated over a long time horizon, a pre/post analysis is often estimated over a short time horizon. All approaches are suitable for interventions with clear cutoffs and outcomes with rapid onset, which is the case in our context.

In Equation (1), we present the model for ITS, a tool to measure statistical changes in the level and slope changes of the outcome (Bernal et al., 2017). $\beta _ { 1 }$ measures the level change of the outcome, and $\beta _ { 2 }$ measures the slope change in response to the treatment.

$$
\begin{array}{r} O u t c o m e _ {t} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} T r e a t m e n t _ {t} + \beta_ {2} T i m e S i n c e T r e a t m e n t _ {t} + \\ \beta_ {3} T i m e _ {t} + \beta_ {4} L o g (A c t i v e P r o j e c t s _ {t} + 1) + \beta_ {5} D a y o f W e e k _ {t} + \varepsilon_ {t}. \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

The model of RDiT is presented in Equation (2). Following the framework of RDiT, we included higher-order polynomial control of time. Specifically, we used the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) to choose the order of the polynomials (Hausman & Rapson, 2018).

$$

$$

## Choice and Transaction Analyses

In contrast to stable outcomes such as public health or air quality, online donation behaviors are influenced by many time-varying factors at the project (e.g., funding status) or individual $( \mathrm { e . g . } ,$ number of friends who donated) levels. To better account for these factors and mitigate the corresponding confounding concerns, we performed choice-level analyses at the individual-project-day level and transaction-level analyses for every donation transaction over the short observation window of 24 days. The reasons for the choice- and transaction-level analyses over a short time horizon are fourfold. First, we found from the population-level analyses presented in Table 1 that the short-term impact of the profile enrichment intervention (Models 3, 6, 9 of Table 1) was consistent with its long-term impact at the macro level. Specifically, the results from Models 1 and 2 are consistent with those from Model 3, the results from Models 4 and 5 are consistent with those from Model 6, and the results from Models 7 and 8 are consistent with those from Model 9. Second, a 24-day observation window offered both sufficient cross-sectional variation (i.e., we observed donation behavior from 3,103 donors) and compact bandwidth to minimize time confoundedness. Third, panel data with short time horizons has been used for the evaluation of social features on digital platforms (Cao et al., 2024). Lastly, the number of observations for our dataset increased drastically with the individual-project-day data, and using the long observation window would have resulted in a dataset with more than 781 million observations $( 1 , 3 4 4 \times 1 1 5 { \times } 5 , 0 5 8 )$ , which would have been computationally prohibitive and unnecessary, given the first three reasons. We had a 24-day observation window for the choice- and transaction-level analysis, satisfying the best-practice requirement proposed by Baicker and Svoronos (2019), suggesting that at least 12 time points are needed before and after the intervention, and at least 100 observations should be available at each point in time. Below, we introduce the choiceand transaction-level variables before presenting our models and results.

## Variables

The first dependent variable is the dummy variable Donate<sub>ijt</sub>, constructed at the individual-project-day level to represent choices: Each individual i chooses to make donations to each project j available on day t unless the user has already contributed to the project. We had a balanced dataset for the outcome of whether to donate $( D o n a t e _ { i j t } )$ with 10,907,873 observations. Conditional on the decision to donate, we observed transaction-level data for the donation amount and the amount disclosure decision, with 11,905 observations for the outcomes of log(Amount<sub>ijt</sub>+1) and $R e v e a l _ { i j t } ,$ where $A m o u n t _ { i j t }$ is the amount donated by user i to project j on day t, and Reveal<sub>ijt</sub> is a dummy variable that takes the value of 1 if the donation amount is disclosed and 0 otherwise. Note that we observed the contribution amounts even when the donor did not choose to disclose the donation amount. Similar to the population-level analysis, we used Treatment<sub>it</sub> to indicate whether the profile enrichment was implemented.

To account for the influence from users’ social networks, we used $O n e W F P _ { i j t }$ and $T w o W F P _ { i j t }$ to denote the logarithm of the number of one-way and two-way followees who contributed to project j before the focal user i on day t. To construct these variables, we leveraged network data about when a following relationship was established and reciprocated if it was. We further used $O n e W F I _ { i j t }$ and $T w o W F I _ { i j t }$ to denote the logarithm of the number of one-way and two-way followees who contributed to any project other than j before the focal user i on day t. We used $O t h e r D o n o r s _ { i j t }$ to represent the logarithm of the number of non-followee donors of project j before user i on day t. In addition, we accounted for individual donation histories using the logarithm of the total amount donated in the past, $P a s t A m t _ { i t } ,$ and the logarithm of the donation frequency in the past, PastCnt . We controlled for the logarithm of users’ number of followers and the number of microblogs using $F o l l o w e r _ { i t }$ and $P o s t _ { i t } .$ . We used RemainGoal<sub>jt</sub> to represent the logarithm of the remaining amount to reach the goal for project j on day $t ;$ this variable is negative if the pledged amount has surpassed the goal. We also accounted for the category of project j to differentiate projects that support the recovery of natural disasters, educational purposes, medical assistance, social causes, and veterans (Disaster<sub>j</sub>, Education<sub>j</sub>, $M e d i c a l _ { j } ,$ Social<sub>j</sub>, and Veteran<sub>j</sub>). ## Cluster Analysis

## Clusters Identification

While it is important to examine the impact of profile enrichment on each outcome of our interest separately, it is more intriguing to explore the response patterns in a collective way. For example, did people who increased their contribution amounts also increase contribution amount disclosure? To answer such questions, we identified response patterns using a clustering technique based on the choice- and transaction-level analyses.

First, we constructed longitudinal data for each user with only two periods, before and after the introduction of the widget. We aggregated the three outcomes into donation count, average contribution amount, and contribution amount disclosure rate. We performed log transformation to reduce skewness both for the donation counts and contribution amounts. Next, we collapsed the data to quantify the behavior change by calculating the difference between the posttreatment and pre-treatment values. We took a conservative approach for imputation. When users did not donate in either the pre-treatment or post-treatment period, we considered this user’s contribution amount and disclosure behavior to be unchanged. Finally, we scaled the changes in contribution amounts and donation counts to range from -1 to 1, with the change in average disclosure rate already ranging between -1 and 1. This is a common data pre-processing technique before clustering to guarantee equal impact from each dimension (Mohamad & Usman, 2013). Then, we performed a k-means analysis on the three dimensions to identify user clusters. The k-means approach is an unsupervised learning algorithm that offers a stable way to identify centroids (i.e., a set of points that minimize the mean squared distance from each data point to its nearest centroid to which each observation belongs) in the k-dimensional space. Each centroid represents a behavioral change pattern based on whether to donate, how much to donate, and whether to reveal the contribution amount. We chose $k = 3$ in the following analysis, as it allows easy interpretation and enables the greatest increase in BSS/TSS, as the number of clusters is increased.<sup>9</sup> We report the segment statistics and scatterplot in Table 3. Our main findings remained robust as we increased k.

We labeled the first segment (47.3%) as “restrainers,” as this segment of users reduced donation counts significantly after profile enrichment. The second segment (9.7%) is labeled as “presenters,” as their most significant change was the increase in disclosure rate—their disclosure rate increased by 86.6% after the intervention. The last segment (43%) was labeled as “conformers,” as their most significant change was the increase in donation counts, a conforming behavior following the heightened importance of charitable giving in the social network.

To better understand the differences in these segments, we highlight several observations in comparative statistics (Figure 4). First, the difference between restrainers and conformers, the majority of the population, could stem from their different past donation counts, the key information highlighted by the profile enrichment. Individuals labeled as restrainers exhibited a higher median number of past donations (median = 10) compared to conformers (median = 6) (log-scale shown at Panel A of Figure 4), and the difference in the mean is statistically significant (t-stat = 5.39). This implies that donors with low past donation counts were more susceptible to the reputation effect, which drove them to donate more frequently in higher amounts. However, donors with high past donation counts were more prone to the saturation effect and would have reduced their donation frequency to avoid standing out as extremists. This is consistent with past studies (Frey & Meier, 2004; Jones & Linardi, 2014; Shang & Croson, 2006).

Past donation counts were not the only factor that differentiated restrainers and conformers. As can be seen from Panels C and D of Figure 4, conformers had significantly more one-way (t-stat = 1.875) and two-way followee (t-stat = 2.598) donors. That is, conformers were better integrated into the donor network, and the profile enrichment could have played a more salient role in driving them to believe that they did not contribute enough, as they were surrounded by donors (Bénabou & Tirole, 2006). To support this network perspective, we underscore that conformers are the only group whose donation amounts were positively impacted by the number of two-way followees who supported the same project $( T w o W F P _ { i j t } )$ (Model 6 of Table 4).

Presenters are the only segment that increased both the contribution amounts and disclosure to signal generosity. They only accounted for 9.7% of the population, but they had a median number of 13 past donations and an average of 7,752 followers, significantly higher than the average of 4,117 followers for restrainers (t-stat = 2.546) and 4,631 followers for conformers (t-stat = 1.749) (log-scale in Panels A and B of Figure 4). Since the number of followers is directly linked to social media users’ reputations (Bakshy et al., 2011), presenters’ self-presentational behavior was likely driven by a strong motive to portray a socially desirable image. They were also the only segment that did not follow the choice of their one-way followees when it came to whether to donate, as we observed a marginally negative coefficient for OneWFP<sub>ijt</sub> in Model 2 of Table 4. Since one-way followees were likely users of high status (e.g., opinion leaders), the decision to defy the choice of oneway followees is also likely self-presentational.

## Robustness Checks

To corroborate our findings, we performed a battery of robustness checks both at the population level with a long observation window (Appendix C) and at the choice and transaction level with a short observation window (Appendix D). We first present a falsification check where we used data either before the profile enrichment or after the profile enrichment with a false intervention. The treatment effects were insignificant for both the pre-treatment and the post-treatment data (See Robustness Checks 1-2 in Appendix C for population-level analysis, and Robustness Checks 1-2 in Appendix D for choice- and transaction-level analyses). We then performed a time sensitivity analysis to shorten the observation window for the short observation window (see Robustness Check 3-4 in Appendix D). This analysis is key to the validation of causality because the concerns of confounding issues would have been reduced as we shortened the observation windows. Our findings remained unchanged after we shrank the observation window. Further, we report the results of ITS model specifications that allow the intervention to change both the levels and the slopes of the outcomes while accounting for time trends in the choice- and transaction-level analyses. All results stayed the same except for the additional insights from the transaction-level analysis with the short observation window in which the increase in contribution amount manifested over time (see Robustness Check 5 in Appendix D). This is slightly different from our populationlevel analysis, but the main conclusion stayed the same. Also, we removed users who opted out of posting the automatic message to disclose their donations, with our results remaining the same both for population-level and choiceand transaction-level analyses (see Robustness Check 3 in Appendix C and Robustness Check 6 in Appendix D).

## Discussion

## Contributions to Literature

Despite the persistent research interest in charitable giving, past studies have mostly focused on fundraising strategies such as donation matching, rebates, and the design of appeals (Basil et al., 2009; Harbaugh, 1998; Karlan & List, 2007; Kristofferson et al., 2014). Little effort has been invested in understanding social fundraising functions on digital platforms that could motivate people’s prosocial acts of giving without explicit solicitation. This study contributes to the literature by offering a unique focus on profile enrichment in a social networking environment and by taking an integrated decision-making perspective to incorporate three donation-related decisions.

Our findings reconcile the opposing discoveries about the impact of increased donation visibility. Past studies have discovered both positive (e.g., Bénabou & Tirole, 2006; Andreoni, 1993) and negative responses (e.g., Bracha et al., 2017; Simpson et al., 2018) to heightened donation visibility. Our cluster analysis reveals that the opposing findings can coexist, given people’s heterogeneous susceptibility to the reputation and saturation effects. While increased donation visibility drives presenters and conformers to donate more per transaction due to the reputation effect, it decreases donation frequency for restrainers due to the saturation effect. The novel finding that presenters reduce donation frequency while increasing contribution amount and disclosure further suggests strategic behavior, highlighting donation visibility as a double-edged sword.

Finally, we incorporated the decision of contribution amount disclosure, deepening the understanding of voluntary information disclosure in the nascent literature on information control. Past studies regarding voluntary information disclosure on digital platforms have mostly focused on the consequences of information revealment or concealment but not the antecedents (Burtch et al., 2015, 2016; Jiang et al., 2021). Our study makes novel contributions to this literature by summarizing characteristics of users who will likely increase their contribution amount disclosure rates following a profile enrichment that exhibits donation histories.

## Implications for Practice

Practically, our research offers rich implications for platform designers who seek to transform social networking value into social good. First, our study shows that a digital profile design is critical to people’s social and economic behavior online (Kane et al., 2014). Any changes in digital profiles that highlight a specific facet of users’ online behavior could affect the user’s subsequent behavior. Profile enrichment to incorporate prosocial behaviors should be implemented with great caution because it may unintentionally reduce people’s willingness to perform prosocial behaviors, especially among those who have a good record of prosocial acts. Second, the findings of this study shed light on donor heterogeneity. While it is discouraging to find that the average impact of profile enrichment on donation probability is negative, it is encouraging that profile enrichment can entice charitable contributions for some users without the use of explicit solicitations. To avoid the negative consequences of profile enrichment, platform designers should make such profile enrichment optional. Third, our clustering results suggest some reasonable lines of future platform design and inquiry. We learned that frequent donors who also have many followers are driven by reputational motives. As such, platforms can recognize their contributions and further reinforce their donor identity by adjusting the algorithm to increase the exposure of microblogs that announce their donations. We also found that users who are deeply embedded in the donor network are susceptible to the influence of the donations made by their twoway connections. Making such donations more visible to this group of users through information cues could effectively stimulate giving. Lastly, encouraging voluntary information disclosure continues to be a challenge for online platforms. Our findings suggest that frequent donors with more followers who are likely to be reputation-seeking will increase their disclosure of contribution amounts when they increase their donation amounts. More systematic analyses (e.g., randomized controlled trials) could be designed to identify incentive designs that would encourage contribution amount disclosure, potentially improving fundraising performance.

## Limitations

Our study is not without limitations. First, our study did not have a control group of users who were not affected by the profile enrichment. Despite our efforts to enhance identification by carefully controlling confounders, a better strategy would be to run randomized controlled trials or leverage a difference-in-differences research design. Second, platform intervention offers a unique opportunity to explore people’s different responses to profile enrichment through observational data. However, a firsthand survey analysis would allow a deeper dive into the mechanisms underlying people’s responses.
