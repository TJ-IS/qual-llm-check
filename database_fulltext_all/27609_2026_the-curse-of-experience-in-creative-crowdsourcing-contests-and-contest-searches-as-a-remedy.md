# THE CURSE OF EXPERIENCE IN CREATIVE CROWDSOURCING CONTESTS AND CONTEST SEARCHES AS A REMEDY<sup>1</sup>

Yuan Jin Rawls College of Business, Texas Tech University Lubbock, TX, U.S.A. {yuan.jin@ttu.edu}

Ho Cheung Brian Lee Smeal College of Business, Pennsylvania State University University Park, PA, U.S.A. {brian.lee@psu.edu}

Xinxin Li School of Business, University of Connecticut Storrs, CT, U.S.A. {xinxin.li@uconn.edu}

In creative crowdsourcing contests that seek novel ideas and solutions, experience can help solvers use relevant knowledge to generate solutions, but may also cause solvers to fixate on familiar solution paths, which we call the “curse of experience.” This study examines how experience affects solvers’ performance in such contests and how contest searches (searching descriptive information about other contests), as a type of external information-seeking activity, moderate the effects of experience. Our analyses show that both the benefit and the curse of experience coexist. Solvers’ experience contributes to a higher likelihood of generating acceptable submissions (solutions meeting basic quality standards) but reduces the chance of creating winning solutions (extreme-value solutions selected as contest winners). Contest searches prior to participation in a contest provide solvers with contest choices, strengthening the beneficial effects of experience on creating both acceptable and winning solutions. Contest searches that occur during participation in a contest, although potentially triggering an overload effect that decreases the beneficial effect of experience on solution acceptance, also bring new ideas and inspiration to solvers’ solution search processes, reducing the impedimental effect of experience on creating winning solutions. Searches of contests that differ in skill or context from the focal contest are beneficial for both prior and parallel contest search processes but are not always fully utilized by solvers. These findings delineate a comprehensive picture of how contest search activities can remedy the curse of experience and inform the effective design and use of search features for crowdsourcing platforms and solution seekers to help improve solvers’ performance

Keywords: Crowdsourcing contest, creative innovation, solver performance, solver experience, information search

## Introduction

With advances in digital platform technology, crowdsourcing has become a quickly rising new model for open innovation. While some crowdsourcing platforms enable collaborative activities for value cocreation (e.g., Bayus, 2013), others hold contests (e.g., Boudreau et al., 2011; Terwiesch & Xu, 2008). In a contest, crowdsourcing workers, also called solvers, participate as contestants to generate creative solutions. The clients, also called seekers, provide monetary awards to the solvers who submit the best solutions. A crowdsourcing contest thus organizes a crowd of solvers to contribute to a project and gives the seeker abundant options to choose from. Solvers’ different paths to approach the solution lead to a diversity of solutions and the potential of extreme-value solutions (Boudreau et al., 2011).

Among different types of crowdsourcing contests, the ones that focus on creative and novel ideas and solutions are called creative crowdsourcing contests (Estellés-Arolas & González-Ladrón-de-Guevara, 2012; Schenk & Guittard, 2009). These contests are widely used for accomplishing creative tasks, such as advertisement design and new product ideation (Schenk & Guittard, 2009). Creative crowdsourcing contest platforms such as Tongal, 99Designs, and DesignCrowd are among the most popular crowdsourcing platforms in the industry, each hosting millions of crowdsourcing workers collaborating with a wide range of corporate clients. In these contests, solvers’ experience can be a critical factor influencing their performance. The knowledge formed by such experience acts as a vital internal information source for solvers to generate solutions that meet seekers’ requirements and expectations. However, excessive experience could lead to a converging effect in which one fixates on familiar solution paths, especially given the stress and time constraints of competition, hence limiting the variability and novelty of generated solutions and thus the potential of extreme-value solutions (Audia & Goncalo, 2007). We call this phenomenon the curse of experience.

There might be remedies to alleviate such an undesirable “side effect” of experience. The process of innovation can be viewed as a recombinant search (Fleming, 2001), in which different information components are recombined to produce something new. In this process, it is vital that new, “unfamiliar” components are introduced and used. Knowledge formed by experience can be a source of old, “familiar” components, and thus a possible remedy is getting access to new components that help solvers “think outside the box.” Searches from external information sources can serve this role. In this study, we focus on one type of external information search on the contest platform: searching for information from other contests. Typically, on these platforms, multiple contests are held simultaneously. Solvers can search other contests either to evaluate and choose contests to participate in or to gather new information, particularly from the contests’ homepages, including project descriptions, guidelines, and relevant resources.<sup>2</sup> We refer to this activity as the contest search. When it happens before a solver participates in a contest, a contest search is called a prior search for the focal contest, and when it happens during participation in a contest, it is called a parallel search for the focal contest. Both prior and parallel searches can provide information components, moderating the effects of experience on solvers’ performance. However, the salient roles of prior and parallel searches for a specific contest may differ with the search’s timing relative to the contest: prior searches mainly offer choices from which this focal contest is selected, while parallel searches provide timely access to new information components during the solution search process for the focal contest, although they may overload solvers with redundant information, thus diverting their efforts from the focal task. Coexisting with one’s internal experience-formed knowledge as an external information source, contest searches may complement the role of experience or lead to an overload effect, performing moderating roles in solution generation processes. Furthermore, different types of searches—analogous searches (providing information similar to the focal contest) and distant searches (providing information different from the focal contest)—may have different moderating roles.

In this study, we look at how past contest participation experience influences solver performance in a creative crowdsourcing contest and further examine the potential of contest searches in alleviating the curse of experience by investigating how different types of contest searches moderate the effects of experience on solver performance. Specifically, we address the following research questions: (1) How does the experience of past contest participation affect solver performance in a new crowdsourcing contest seeking creative solutions? (2) How do contest searches moderate the effects of experience? Can contest searches alleviate the curse of experience if it exists? If yes, what types of contest searches are more effective in alleviating it?

We answer the questions using a proprietary dataset from a large creative crowdsourcing contest platform. Based on our analyses, the benefit and curse of experience coexist: Experience benefits solvers with a learning effect, especially in generating acceptable solutions that meet basic quality standards, but it can also lead to undesirable crowdsourcing outcomes through a converging effect that reduces the variability of solutions and hence the chance of producing an extreme-value solution. Contest searches can remedy this curse of experience: Prior searches provide contest choices for solvers to identify contests with better fit, and parallel searches can bring in new information components, stimulating a diversity of solutions. Additionally, compared to analogous searches (e.g., contest searches that look at contests that are similar in required skills or business contexts to the focal contest), distant searches (e.g., contest searches that share little skill or context similarity with the focal contest) are more beneficial to experienced solvers in both prior and parallel contest search processes. Since solvers generally underutilize distant searches, we also provide empirical insights into how platforms can encourage such searches and improve solvers effectiveness in utilizing distant information via the designs of contest availability and monetary prizes.

Our study adds to the crowdsourcing literature by providing a theoretical framework for how external information sources (such as contest searches) moderate the effects of internal information sources (such as experience-formed knowledge) on solvers’ performance in creative crowdsourcing contests. We decompose the effect of experience into learning and converging effects and provide empirical evidence of their coexistence, which may help to reconcile the conflicting findings in prior literature regarding the effects of experience (e.g., Bayus, 2013; Huang et al., 2014; Menon et al., 2020). Our study further reveals the potential of introducing new components into an innovation process via external information searches to alleviate the curse of experience caused by a dominant converging effect, thus also deepening the understanding of information search processes in innovation research. In addition to the theoretical contributions, our findings regarding the moderating effects of contest searches on the effects of experience also provide important managerial implications for contest platforms and solution seekers to more effectively facilitate and leverage information seeking activities.

## Theoretical Background, Literature Review, and Hypotheses

## Innovation and Competition in Creative Crowdsourcing Contests

In a crowdsourcing contest, individuals participate as contestants, working independently to create and submit solutions to a solution seeker in competition for awards. This enables the seeker to obtain a wide range of solutions from an online crowd with diverse knowledge and skills. We define creative crowdsourcing contests as those aimed at generating creative and novel ideas or products, such as ideation or design challenges (Estellés-Arolas & González-Ladrón-de-Guevara, 2012; Schenk & Guittard, 2009). Success in these contests requires solutions that not only meet basic quality standards but also demonstrate a high degree of novelty and divergence.

Innovation, as a key element in creative crowdsourcing contests, can be considered to be a process of recombinant search (Fleming, 2001), in which information and knowledge components are recombined to produce a novel solution that can serve a new function or purpose. In contrast to collaborative crowdsourcing, in which solvers collaborate to work on tasks (e.g., Han et al., 2020; Ye & Jensen, 2022), innovation processes in creative crowdsourcing contests feature the mechanism of competition. Such contests drive innovation by utilizing diverse contributions from competitors with heterogeneous knowledge and award incentives that motivate competitors to deliver novel solutions. Prior research suggests that competition crowd size and reward size influence the effectiveness of innovation in contests by affecting the competition mechanism (e.g., Boudreau et al., 2011; Boudreau et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2014; Terwiesch & Xu, 2008), as do competition designs, such as competition feedback and recommendation (Lee et al., 2018; Mihm & Schlapp, 2019; Mo et al., 2018). Situational factors associated with competition, such as stress and time constraints, can also influence how solvers search and use information in the innovation process, which we further discuss in the next section.

Given the competitive nature of creative crowdsourcing contests, solvers’ performance is reflected, first, in whether their submitted solutions are accepted and, second, in their chances of winning. When working on a contest, a solver typically produces a pool of solutions (consisting of, for example, initial ideas, work-in-progress, and complete solutions) and selects a few complete solutions to submit for evaluation (Bockstedt et al., 2016; Lee et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2014). Not all submissions provide basic functionality and meet the acceptable quality standards. Among those that do, the seeker chooses the ones that are highly creative or original (i.e., providing an extreme value) as the winning solutions. Thus, both acceptance and winning hinge on the value distribution of the solver’s solution pool, ideally characterized by a high average solution value and a high variability. A higher average increases the likelihood that submitted solutions will meet the quality standards and serve the purpose of the product. Greater variability reflects broader exploration of the solution space, increasing the chance of generating extreme-value solutions likely to win. In summary, we conceptualize solver performance in creative crowdsourcing contests along two dimensions: generating acceptable solutions (associated with the average value of the solution pool) and generating winning solutions (associated with the variability of the solution pool).

## Information Search Processes in Creative Crowdsourcing Contests

Individuals’ information seeking behavior has been mainly studied in the consumer behavior literature (e.g., Fodness & Murray, 1998; Moorthy et al., 1997; Punj & Staelin, 1983; Schmidt & Spreng, 1996), which focuses on consumers’ search for information during purchase decision processes to evaluate products at different stages, both internally (e.g., from memories of past consumption experiences) and using external sources. Similarly, when working on creative crowdsourcing tasks, solvers conduct information seeking both internally and externally to assist their innovation processes.

The literature has identified three main dimensions of information seeking strategies: spatial, operational, and temporal (Engel et al., 1991; Fodness & Murray, 1998). Here, we focus on the first two dimensions, leaving the third for discussion in the Moderating Effects of Contest Searches section. The spatial dimension concerns the locus of the information source, i.e., whether the information is obtained internally or externally. The operational dimension reflects the effectiveness and role of the source, i.e., whether the source plays a decisive or auxiliary role in decision-making or problem solving. In innovation processes, knowledge serves as the internal information source. When searching internally, individuals conduct cognitive scans for relevant knowledge stored in their memories and retrieve needed information for the current task. As the source of mental structures and heuristics, one’s internal knowledge serves as the foundation for the decision-making and problem-solving processes and shapes schemas for interpreting and processing external information (Axelrod, 1973; Maitland & Sammartino, 2015), thus functioning as the decisive information source. If the internal knowledge proves inadequate, one will seek information from external sources to compensate for insufficient knowledge. External sources serve an auxiliary role in two possible directions: On the one hand, external sources can provide complementary information or broaden perspectives, functioning as contributory sources that assist the decisive internal source; on the other hand, searches from external sources may be ineffective if they provide limited information while incurring unnecessary costs or distractions. By coexisting with the internal information source and serving auxiliary roles, external information sources interact with the internal source in individuals’ decision-making and problem-solving processes, where the internal search serves as the cognitive foundation and the external search moderates its effects.

In creative crowdsourcing contests, solvers build knowledge from experience (Cropley, 2006) as an internal information source while searching external sources for additional information, such as information about the focal contest and sometimes other contests, to assist in their sensemaking and decision-making (Kuhlthau, 1991). In this study, we focus on solvers’ knowledge formed by experience as the focal internal information source and their activity of searching for information from other contests as an instance of external information searches to study how the interaction between internal and external sources impacts solver performance.

## Effects of Experience-Formed Knowledge

Prior crowdsourcing research has not reached the same conclusion regarding the impact of experience in crowdsourcing tasks that stimulate innovation or generate creative solutions. Some studies have shown that experience benefits performance and the benefits vary with the experience type (Menon et al., 2020; Riedl & Seidel, 2018; Wang & Hahn, 2015), whereas others have presented different results. Huang et al. (2014) found that experience does not directly affect performance, although it positively influences solvers’ learning process. Liu et al. (2020) showed that successful experience increases the likelihood of implementing a solver’s new ideas until it exceeds a certain amount and starts to have a negative impact. Bayus (2013) found that experience likely leads to repetition in ideation and has a negative impact on solvers’ future success. By decomposing the impact of experience into a positive learning effect and a negative converging effect below, we hope to bridge the gap in the extant literature regarding the conflicting findings on how experience affects crowdsourcing and innovation performance.

Learning effect of experience: In creative crowdsourcing contests, learning by doing—a process rooted in individuals sensemaking of their past actions and attempts (Dewey, 1916)—plays a crucial role. By participating in multiple contests, a solver can try different tasks, learn from their experience, and gain valuable insights. This accumulated experience builds a knowledge foundation that facilitates new recombinant searches for future innovation tasks, giving rise to a learning effect that enhances solvers’ performance over time.

The learning effect is associated with both the breadth and depth of a solver’s experience-formed knowledge. To innovate effectively, solvers typically explore the solution space by experimenting with different solution paths and integrating various information components. A greater breadth of past experience forms a more diverse knowledge base across different domains and components, enabling wider exploration and increasing the variability of the solution pool (Hwang et al., 2019; Taylor & Greve, 2006; Xiao et al., 2022), which enhances the likelihood of generating extreme-value solutions and winning the contest. Meanwhile, the depth of experience-formed knowledge contributes to a higher average value of solutions, increasing the likelihood that submissions will meet the basic quality standards and be accepted. Experience-formed knowledge of sufficient depth plays a vital role in converging exploration into relevant and feasible solutions (Audia & Goncalo, 2007; Cropley, 2006; Guilford, 1956; March, 1991). Without sufficient experience to guide convergence, even a diverse solution pool may lack quality, resulting in submissions that do not meet the basic quality standards.

In summary, the learning effect of experience-formed knowledge can improve solvers’ skills in producing solutions with a higher average value and a greater variability, although the extent of improvement can depend on the breadth and depth of their experience. Accordingly, we hypothesize that solvers with more experience with crowdsourcing contests can have a higher chance of generating acceptable submissions and winning solutions.

H1a: Experienced solvers are more likely to generate acceptable solutions.

H1b: Experienced solvers are more likely to generate winning solutions.

Converging effect of experience: Although solvers’ experience leads to knowledge accumulation and generates a learning effect, the information provided by the experience-formed knowledge may not fit the new task properly or may lead to solvers’ fixation on certain solution paths, negatively affecting the diversity of solutions. We refer to this effect of experience as the converging effect.

To effectively perform innovation activities in creative crowdsourcing contests, solvers need sufficient creative ideation, divergent thinking (Amabile, 1996; Cropley, 2006; Runco & Chand, 1995), and enough exploration of the solution space (March, 1991). Even when solvers have extensive experience with innovation tasks, their experience-formed knowledge will not necessarily provide sufficiently varied components to effectively explore the solution space and produce novel solutions. Experienceformed knowledge, especially the in-depth knowledge focusing on limited domains, can be a source of familiar information components that lead to cognitive fixation (Bayus, 2013; Marsh et al., 1996; Smith et al., 1993; Ward, 1994), i.e., a tendency to rely on familiar components from experience and repetitively use similar solution paths. This tendency can be more salient in contests due to the heightened stress and time constraints inherent in competition settings compared to collaborative environments. This effect is particularly pronounced when a solver’s experience primarily consists of similar tasks and solution paths, which is, unfortunately, a common tendency that can be attributed to the status quo bias (Dean et al., 2017; Kahneman et al., 1991), wherein people tend to choose and work on familiar tasks. That is, with extensive experience with similar tasks, solvers tend to follow their old solution paths, limiting the use of new or unfamiliar components that may fit the current task better, which can lead to narrow distributions of solutions that reduce solution variability and the likelihood of creating extreme-value solutions.<sup>3</sup>

In summary, experience-formed knowledge can lead to the overall convergence of solutions. This converging effect from fixation with narrow or unfitting knowledge is where the “curse of experience” is rooted. The divergence of solutions largely determines the solver’s chance of creating an extreme-value solution in a creative project, thus winning the contest (Boudreau et al., 2016). Hence, we propose a competing hypothesis to H1b:

H1c: Experienced solvers are less likely to generate winning solutions.

H1b and H1c depict two countervailing forces (the learning and converging effects) in determining how experience affects solvers’ chances of winning. It is possible that both effects are at work, with their relative strength varying based on the breath, depth, and relevance of the experience-formed knowledge, and the dominant effect determining the direction of the experience’s overall effect. Due to time constraints, pressure, and increased opportunity costs in crowdsourcing contests, solvers often simplify the information search process by relying on familiar information sources rather than engaging in extensive search efforts (Kuhlthau, 1991; Rieskamp & Hoffrage, 2008). This can lead to a more salient converging effect in such competitions compared to collaborative settings. ## Moderating Effects of Contest Searches

Solvers can visit contests available on the platform to choose contests to participate in or to gather useful information. These contests provide descriptive information about their projects, including the project’s objectives, guidelines, and examples or tips. Such information provided by a contest is not only useful for its own project but also can supply information components to a solver’s innovation process in another contest.<sup>4</sup> We refer to the activity of searching for information from other contests as the contest search.

As discussed previously, experience-formed knowledge often serves as the decisive source, and external information sources, such as contest searches, typically serve the auxiliary role in the information search process (Engel et al., 1991; Fodness & Murray, 1998), moderating the effects of the internal source on performance. Depending on the relative benefit and cost of conducting the search (Kuhlthau, 1991), external information searches can influence the effectiveness of individuals’ internal search and the decision-making or problem-solving process shaped by experience-formed knowledge in two opposing ways.

On the one hand, merely relying on the internal source may be inadequate, especially in innovation tasks where fresh perspectives and innovative approaches are crucial to the generation of creative solutions. Additional information is needed to supplement internal knowledge and expand existing mental models. Therefore, external information sources provide contributory information that complements solvers’ knowledge and inspires new ideas, assisting the decisive role of the experience-formed knowledge and presenting a beneficial complementary effect.

On the other hand, it is also possible that the cost of external searches outweighs the benefit under certain conditions (Kuhlthau, 1991). When knowledge already provides sufficient information, external searches can become ineffective (Fodness & Murray, 1998). External searches could further bring possible cognitive “overload,” especially when the new information cannot be incorporated into the schemas formed by the existing knowledge (Sweller, 2011), due to the capacity limits of information processing (Marois & Ivanoff, 2005). This phenomenon has a mechanism similar to information overload, which can waste solvers’ cognitive resources and undermine the effectiveness of experience. We refer to this possible detrimental moderating effect of external searches as the overload effect, which makes it uncertain whether conducting external searches can effectively alleviate the more salient converging effect of experience in the competition context. Hence, identifying which types of external searches are most effective in mitigating the converging effect also becomes particularly important.

To examine the moderating effects of contest searches and the effective search types, we further categorized contest searches along the temporal dimension of information seeking strategies (i.e., the timing of acquiring new information) (Fodness & Murray, 1998). One type of information search occurs right before the focal decision is made or the focal action is taken, directly influencing the decision to take the action, such as participating in a contest. The other type of information search is conducted independently from the focal action and serves the purpose of ongoing general information collection. Following this concept, we categorized a solver’s contest searches into two types based on their timing relative to the focal contest: (1) pre-contest searches (referred to as prior searches in this study), which occur right before the solver participates in the focal contest,<sup>5</sup> and (2) during-contest searches (referred to as parallel searches), which occur during the solver’s participation in the focal contest.

While both prior and parallel searches can collect information that serves as components used in solvers’ solution generation processes, the disparity in timing with respect to the focal contest gives rise to different effects of searches, which are manifested at different contest stages to affect a solver’s performance via different mechanisms.<sup>6</sup> Prior searches happen before a solver’s active participation in the focal contest and mainly serve to provide contest choices for the focal participation decision. Parallel searches happen during the solver’s participation in the focal contest, making parallel searches more salient for the solver to extract useful information components while working on the focal contest, compared to prior searches for the same contest. Based on the information search process theory (Kuhlthau, 1991), during participation in the contest, as the solver has a clear understanding of the focal task and awareness of how new information can be integrated into the task, immediate information components (such as those from parallel searches) can be better processed and embedded into the solver’s knowledge schemas than information encountered in the past (such as those from prior searches). Therefore, we expect that for the same focal contest, the effects of parallel searches will mainly come from providing inspiration and new perspectives during participation, whereas prior searches mainly will affect the choices from which the initial participation decision is made.

Moderating effects of prior searches: Prior searches form the choice set from which the focal contest is chosen. Having more choices increases the likelihood of finding one that best fits the need (Oppewal & Koelemeijer, 2005). When solvers search and compare more contests before participation, they are more likely to find one that fits their expertise. A better contest fit can amplify the learning effect of experience (that is, steepening the upward slope of the solution value with experience that is shown in Figure 1), as the knowledge formed by experience becomes more relevant and useful for the new solution search process, leading to improved average value and variability of solutions. In this way, prior searches can complement the beneficial effects of experience on both the acceptance of solutions and the chance of winning, as summarized in the following hypotheses:

H2a: By amplifying the learning effect of experience, prior searches strengthen the role of experience in increasing the acceptance of solutions.

H2b: By amplifying the learning effect of experience, prior searches strengthen the role of experience in increasing the chance of winning.

A large number of contest choices can also introduce the overload effect. Excessive information, especially in highload situations such as when facing the pressure of competition, can cause stress and reduce individuals’ ability to carefully evaluate options (Eppler & Mengis, 2004; Sweller, 1988), often leading to reliance on familiar strategies such as choosing contests similar to past projects, which could be suboptimal decisions and amplify the converging effect of experience. Also, given more available contest choices, solvers have a higher chance of finding a familiar contest. This better alignment between experience and the focal contest can further lead to overuse of familiar components from experience, making the negative converging effect more salient, narrowing the distributions of the solution value shown in Figure 1 to a greater degree, and thus reducing the chance of generating extreme-value solutions. We summarize this possible detrimental moderating effect of prior searches in a competing hypothesis to H2b.

H2c: By amplifying the converging effect of experience, prior searches strengthen the role of experience in decreasing the chance of winning.

Moderating effects of parallel searches: Parallel searches can happen for reasons like checking out other possible contests to participate in later, looking for useful information for the focal contest, or searching other contests simply for fun. Parallel searches could bring an overload effect during a solver’s contest participation, reducing the learning effect of experience. The learning effect works when the learned knowledge is well incorporated and utilized for the new task. Working with new components and switching to new solution paths inspired by parallel searches could make the accumulated knowledge from past experience less useful for the current project. When the new components cannot be incorporated into the experience-formed knowledge schemas, the informational benefits of parallel searches are reduced while burdens on cognitive load are increased, especially in a competition setting, to process the new information (Sweller, 2011). During a solver’s active participation in a contest, such increased cognitive cost can outweigh the benefit (Engel et al., 1991; Kuhlthau, 1991) since the cognitive resources spent on the searches could be otherwise used to better integrate experience-formed knowledge into the focal contest, hence impeding the knowledge incorporation process. As such, distractive parallel searches can detrimentally reduce the learning effect of experience.

On the other hand, new information distant from the focal task may also benefit the innovation process (Baer, 2010; Ruef, 2002) by mitigating the converging effect of experience. The converging effect is rooted in solvers’ fixation with past experience, which lowers the variability of generated solutions. By exposing solvers to new projects during participation in the focal contest, parallel searches can provide new, unfamiliar information to solvers and hence increase the breadth of information components in solvers’ innovation process, leading to better innovation outcomes (Hwang et al., 2019; Xiao et al., 2022). Parallel searches can thus complement experience-formed knowledge and help reduce the negative converging effect of experience, benefiting the solution variability and the chance of winning.

In summary, the overload effect of parallel searches can reduce the learning effect of experience during solvers’ participation in a contest (that is, a flattened curve of the solution value increasing with experience that is shown in Figure 1), negatively affecting both the solution acceptance and the chance of winning. But at the same time, by adding new information components to the solution generation process, parallel searches can also moderate the effect of experience in a beneficial way by reducing the converging effect (that is, by decelerating the narrowing of the solution value distribution shown in Figure 1), leading to a better chance of winning.

H3a: By reducing the learning effect of experience, parallel searches weaken the role of experience in increasing the acceptance of solutions.

H3b: By reducing the learning effect of experience, parallel searches weaken the role of experience in increasing the chance of winning.

H3c: By reducing the converging effect of experience, parallel searches weaken the role of experience in decreasing the chance of winning.

Collectively, this section hypothesizes the effects of solvers experience on solution acceptance and the chance of winning, and how their contest search activities moderate the effects of experience, as summarized in Figure 2.

## Research Context and Data

## Platform Background

We obtained a proprietary dataset from a crowdsourcing contest platform for creative advertising ideation. By 2019, there had been more than 400,000 registered members on this platform, and the platform had collaborated with a wide range of corporate clients. To seek solutions, clients post open calls for solvers’ participation and provide descriptions of their projects to find new advertising ideas and designs. Solvers can use the information provided on the contest listing page to get basic contest information, such as contest seeker information, task objectives, total prize pool, and contest timetable. Further information can be found on the contest homepage, a single page that displays additional details such as the project description, the seeker’s profile, expectations for the product, and evaluation criteria. Solvers could not observe the number of solvers participating in the contest or other solvers’ submissions to the ongoing and previous contests. Our dataset contained detailed information about solvers’ contest searches and submission activities with timestamps; contest characteristics, including prize size and contest type; and solvers’ contest participation experience and outcomes that showed their performance in a contest. In total, our data sample contained 969 crowdsourcing contests dated from January 2013 to February 2021. Contests received 151 submissions on average, and one or more winners were chosen after the contest ended. The average duration of a contest was approximately 3 weeks.

## Solution Performance

When participating in a contest, solvers could make multiple submissions to the seeker. Our partner platform conducted a two-step procedure to evaluate solutions. First, the platform assigned community managers to screen out unacceptable solutions. Qualified solution criteria were typically listed on the contest homepage. The screening step ensured that the submitted solutions met the basic expectations of the project. It took place immediately after each solution was submitted, and solvers were provided with the screening results once the screening was completed. If a solution was rejected at the screening step, we considered it low quality and unacceptable. On average, about 48% of solution submissions were rejected at this step. Second, seekers evaluated the remaining solutions and chose the winners at the end of the contest. Approximately 3% of the solutions were chosen as winning solutions, which represented extreme-value solutions based on our theoretical discussions. We thus captured a solution’s performance by its acceptance (i.e., whether the solution met the basic quality standards) and its chance of winning (i.e., whether the solution was of extreme value).

## Solvers’ Experience and Searches

We used the number of contests the solver had completed before the first visit to the focal contest to measure a solver’s experience, as a proxy of the solver’s past learning in contests and, hence, the accumulation of experience-formed knowledge.

We defined a contest search as a visit to a contest’s homepage for the first time. For prior searches, we identified the contest searches conducted right before a solver participated in the focal contest. These searches reflected the solver’s contest selection process and formed the prior search session (i.e., the choice set) from which the solver chose the (focal) contest to participate in. To avoid overestimating the size of the choice set and effectively operationalize the prior searches, we defined the contests that were searched by the solver and active when the solver first visited the focal contest as the solver’s choice set, which included the focal contest. Our rationale is that only active contests were available for the solver to participate in.<sup>7</sup> Figure 3 shows an example of how we measured prior searches. In this example, before visiting the focal contest j, solver i visited six contests A to F, but only contests E and F were active when solver i visited contest j. By our definition, only contests E and F were in the choice set when contest ?? was selected. Hence, in this case, the number of prior searches by solver i for contest j is 2.

Unlike prior searches, parallel searches were time variant during a solver’s participation in the focal contest if the solver conducted contest searches at different times while participating in the contest. To measure the number of parallel searches for a focal contest, we used the number of contests that were visited for the first time by the solver after the first submission to the focal contest and before the focal submission day. Figure 4 shows an example of how we measured parallel searches for the focal contest j. ## Identification Strategy

Our main models were estimated based on ordinary least squares (OLS) estimation.<sup>10</sup> Because contest search variables may be influenced by time-varying unobserved factors that may also affect solvers’ contest performance, we also adopted an instrumental variable (IV) approach to alleviate the possible endogeneity concern.

For each contest search variable, we used two instrumental variables. The first instrument is the number of available contests, which directly affects a solver’s search activities but is exogenous to the solver’s performance in the focal contest. For prior searches, we used the number of available contests at the time of the solver’s first visit to the focal contest. For parallel searches, we used the number of available contests on the previous submission day.<sup>11</sup> The second instrument is the number of new contests, which is also exogenous to a solver’s performance in the focal contest, but may have affected the solver’s search likelihood. Solvers often have a low motivation to switch to other activities (such as contest searches) when working on a contest due to the status quo bias (Dean et al., 2017; Kahneman et al., 1991). The posting of new contests, prioritized by the platform through highlighted displays and notifications, can act like a “nudge” and provide an external trigger for solvers to search other contests. For prior searches, we used the number of new contests posted in the solver’s prior search session. For parallel searches, we used the number of new contests posted between the previous and focal submission days. The statistical test results support the relevance and exogeneity of the instruments.<sup>12</sup>

## Robustness Analyses

We ran additional analyses to check the robustness of our results. First, we considered four alternative measures for prior searches, in which prior searches were defined, respectively, as (1) searches that happened in a fixed time span before the first visit to the focal contest (we tested two possible time spans, 1 week and 1 month); (2) searches in a cluster identified by a clustering approach, which considered two contests to be in the same session if the former contest did not end before the time of the latter contest being searched; (3) searches that happened before the first submission instead of before the first visit to the focal contest; and (4) searches that followed the main definition, excluding those that were parallel searches of past contests. Second, we tested two adjusted parallel search measures: (1) using the number of contests visited after the first visit (instead of the first submission) to the focal contest and before the submission day; and (2) following the main definition but also including the contests in prior searches in which the solver actively participated while working on the focal contest.<sup>16</sup> Third, we used the total number of available contests between the previous and focal submission days as an alternative instrument for parallel searches. Fourth, in the main analysis, we considered all the past contests a solver participated in to be the solver’s experience, regardless of the solver’s performance in these contests, because people can learn from both successful and failed experiences (Ellis & Davidi, 2005; Weiner, 1985). In this robustness analysis, we focused only on successful experiences and used the number of previous contests to which the solver submitted at least one accepted solution as the experience variable. Fifth, we tested alternative estimations, including a two-way fixedeffect Poisson model for Equation (1) and a two-way fixedeffect logistic model for Equation (2). To alleviate the potential estimation bias due to the small number of winning submissions, we also applied a bias-adjusted logistic regression for rare events (ReLogit) and a Firth’s logistic regression for Equation (2) (Firth, 1993; King & Zeng, 2001). Sixth, as the chance of winning may be increased indirectly by experience through its effect on solution acceptance, we conducted a mediation analysis to estimate the equations jointly. Finally, while our IV analysis was able to address the possible self-selection bias driven by the experience effect on contest searches that might have affected performance, we further conducted a propensity score matching (PSM) based analysis. The results of these analyses are provided in the Appendix and show that our findings are robust.

## The Role of Search Distance: Analogous and Distant Searches

In previous sections, we focused on the temporal dimension of searches: whether the search happened before (prior search) or during (parallel search) a solver’s participation in the focal contest. Besides the timing of searches, other dimensions of searches may also be relevant. In this section, we further investigate the role of the distance between searches and the focal task.

As discussed and empirically demonstrated in previous sections, contest searches can complement experience as an external information source, affecting solvers’ performance. Experience-formed knowledge likely serves as the decisive internal source, while contest searches function as an auxiliary source. The direction of this auxiliary role— contributory or ineffective—is affected by the benefit and cost of using the searched information, which is influenced by the distance between the searched information and the focal task. As different skills reflect different types of tasks, which further relate to how unfamiliar the components provided by a search are, we used the skill similarity between searches and the focal contest to measure the distance.<sup>17</sup>

Based on skill similarity, we divided searches into two categories: searches targeting information that is associated with skills similar to the focal task, and searches targeting information that shares little similarity in skills with the focal task. We refer to the two categories as analogous searches and distant searches, respectively. There is an interesting trade-off between the low cost of analogous searches and the high benefit of distant searches: analogous searches incur less cost, given the readily applicable information, but information from distant searches can be more beneficial to solvers seeking to resist the converging effect, as it provides new components that were not used or explored in the focal problem domain (Jeppesen & Lakhani, 2010).

Our partner platform held contests that explicitly labeled different required skill sets (e.g., animation creation, creative writing, graphic and illustration design, package design, and video filming). Contests were not limited to requiring only one skill. For example, a contest could require both design and writing skills simultaneously. We first calculated the analogous and distant fractions of the focal contest and a searched contest based on their overlapping skills.<sup>18</sup> We then constructed the new analogous (distant) search variables using the sum of the analogous (distant) fractions for prior and parallel searches, respectively. This shows an interesting phenomenon of how potential monetary incentives affect solvers’ contest searches: a higher likelihood, indicated by more available prizes, will attract solvers’ attention, but a larger prize size, likely implying a more difficult or complex contest, can drive people away.

## Discussion and Conclusion

Our analyses present a comprehensive picture of how experience and experience-formed knowledge affect solver performance and how contest searches moderate the effect of experience in creative crowdsourcing contests. Our findings reflect two dimensions of solver performance, with the acceptance dimension reflecting the performance in generating meeting-standard solutions and the winning dimension reflecting the performance in developing creative, extreme-value solutions. Solvers’ experience participating in previous contests contributes to a higher acceptance of submissions but reduces the chance of winning, possibly due to a reduction in the divergence of solutions, demonstrating the existence of both the learning and converging effects of experience. Prior searches moderate the effects of experience in a beneficial way, as the external search facilitates the efficiency of the internal search during the choice-making process. Parallel searches, while adding new perspectives and providing inspiration to solvers’ solution search processes during participation, can reduce the converging effect of experience that hinders the creation of winning solutions, but they can also reduce the beneficial learning effect of experience on the acceptance of solutions. For both prior and parallel searches, distant searches involving information that does not overlap much with the focal task benefit experienced solvers more than analogous searches.

Our findings are valuable for crowdsourcing contests and activities that look for novel ideas and creative solutions (e.g., design projects on 99Designs, content creation projects on Tongal, problem-solving challenges on InnoCentive, to name just a few), where the curse of experience can be a significant hindrance in the process of solvers producing desirable outcomes. By decomposing the overall effect of experience into the learning and converging effects and examining how contest searches interact with those effects, our theoretical framework and empirical findings shed light on the nuanced effects of experience and the interplay between external information seeking activities and internal information from experience-formed knowledge. Specifically, our findings underscore the need to consider both the beneficial and detrimental effects brought by experience and leverage the interplay between different information sources in designing and facilitating innovation contests and processes, providing useful practical implications.

First, platforms and solution seekers should be aware that experience does not always mean success. Seekers may tend to have a preference toward experienced solvers, but rich experience does not necessarily translate into better performance in innovation contests. Especially for those contests seeking novel, “unexpected” solutions that require a high variability, the converging effect stemming from fixation on familiar, repeated solution paths in one’s experience can lead to reduced novelty. The average value of solutions, on the other hand, benefits from the learning effect rooted in the accumulation of relevant knowledge. Therefore, a balanced combination of experienced and inexperienced solvers might yield better outcomes for solution seekers. A contest can be promoted to solvers with different experience levels based on the contest’s current solver pool to maintain a desirable composition. In this process, the structure of solvers’ experience, such as its depth, breadth, and relevance, can also be considered, as these factors might influence the strengths of the learning and converging effects. This insight can also inform team composition strategies within contests, where assembling teams with diverse experiences could improve overall performance in crowdsourcing efforts.

Second, our findings on the moderating effects of contest searches provide useful implications for designing information search features on contest platforms. Prior searches complement experience in solvers’ informationseeking processes when making contest choices. Platforms could conduct targeted posting and promotion of contests according to users’ experience to make use of the complementary effect while avoiding excessive cognitive loads. Parallel searches stimulate new inspiration that can help improve experienced solvers’ ability to generate extremevalue solutions. Platforms could encourage experienced solvers participating in a contest to seek fresh ideas and perspectives from other contests. For example, they could display snips of other contests on the homepage of the focal contest, similar to the recommendation systems on ecommerce platforms, except that, in this case, the remotely related contests might be most beneficial. They could also extend the practice to include other information sources that can stimulate inspiration and new ideas, such as information on past contests and access to forums and discussion boards, on the contest page to serve as helpful “distractions.”

The search distance analysis provides additional insights into possible directions for platforms to improve the design of contest search features. Distant searches benefit experienced solvers in both prior and parallel contest search processes, but are underutilized in practice. Solvers conduct more prior distant searches as their experience grows, but do not conduct parallel searches as effectively as they should. To assist solvers in conducting searches more effectively, platforms could improve the availability and visibility of distant contests. Based on the search motives provided by monetary prizes, platforms could also conduct targeted contest promotions and highlight distant contests featuring high prize availability (rather than large prize amounts) to attract solvers’ interest.

Besides providing useful practical implications for platforms and solution seekers, this study also contributes to academic research in three ways. First, building on information search process theories (Fodness & Murray, 1998; Kuhlthau, 1991), we discuss how experience-formed knowledge, as an internal information source, affects individual performance and how contest search activities, as an external information source, moderate the effects of experience in the setting of creative crowdsourcing contests. Previous research on consumer purchase decision processes has studied how information searches affect consumer decision-making (e.g., Fodness & Murray, 1998; Moorthy et al., 1997; Punj & Staelin, 1983; Schmidt & Spreng, 1996). However, there is insufficient research on workers’ information-seeking activities and how these activities influence their creative solution generation processes. To the best of our knowledge, our study is the first to examine the moderating roles of online workers’ (solvers’) external information searches in influencing how their internal information sources affect performance in crowdsourcing and innovation processes. We empirically demonstrate that information search indeed moderates the effects of experience, mainly by complementing the influence of experience in solvers’ decision-making and extreme-value solution generation, together with a negative overload effect in influencing the generation of solutions that meet basic quality standards. Our findings hence add to the information search process literature by providing empirical evidence of the moderating effects of external searches on the effectiveness of internal searches in affecting individual performance.

Second, this study adds to the crowdsourcing literature by providing a comprehensive investigation of the impacts of solver experience on crowdsourcing outcomes, together with a possible remedy (contest searches) for the potential negative impact. Extant crowdsourcing studies have discussed how experience influences solver performance (e.g., Bayus, 2013; Liu et al., 2020; Menon et al., 2020), but different conclusions have been reached. In our study, we decomposed the effects of experience into a learning effect and a converging effect and determined that both effects exist. The learning effect enhances solvers’ ability to generate solutions that meet basic quality standards, whereas the converging effect limits the solution divergence and lowers the chance of winning. Our findings provide a possible explanation for the conflicting results in previous studies: Since the positive learning effect and the negative converging effect coexist, how experience affects performance may vary with the nature of the project and the needs of the seeker. To understand the moderating effects of contest searches, we present a theoretical framework on how external information searches can moderate the effects of experience by influencing the learning and converging effects, and our empirical findings demonstrate that external searches can complement experience to amplify the learning effect or reduce the converging effect if such search activities and features are properly designed to avoid potential overload effects. In this way, our study provides valuable insights into the effects of information-seeking activities in creative crowdsourcing contests and also shows how these activities can be effectively facilitated.

Third, adding to the stream of research focusing on innovation processes, this study examines one specific form of introducing new components into an innovation recombinant process: introducing components via external information searches, such as contest searches. The literature suggests that a recombinant search with new components would have a higher chance of producing innovative outcomes (Fleming, 2001). The negative effect of experience mainly comes from the “over-reuse” of old components (ideas, methods, solution paths, etc.) in the solution generation process, and we find that contest searches can bring new components into this process and alleviate the fixation brought by experience. This study complements prior research on the effects of information and experience in knowledge recombination (e.g., Hwang et al., 2019; Taylor & Greve, 2006; Xiao et al., 2022). In contrast to prior literature, which has primarily focused on organizational or collaborative environments, we examined the roles of solver experience as the internal information source in the context of creative crowdsourcing contests. The competitive nature inherent to such contests can potentially limit the breadth of experience and affect solvers’ use of experience-formed knowledge during the innovation and information search processes, influenced by situational factors such as stress and time constraints and cognitive factors such as the status quo bias. We present a theoretical framework supported by empirical evidence highlighting the dominant role of the converging effect of experience in such contexts and further examine how external information searches moderate this effect, providing important insights into the mechanisms underlying the experience effects and potential ways of mitigating the negative impact in the competitive setting of crowdsourcing for innovation.

Finally, we would like to highlight several limitations in our data that call for future research. First, we did not observe the exact process of a solver making the contest participation decision, which limited our ability to precisely construct the solver’s choice set. In addition to the main measure of prior searches, we also considered four alternative measures to ensure the robustness of our results. Nevertheless, if such decision-making processes can be directly observed, it could be interesting to look at the mechanisms guiding solvers’ contest searching and selection processes and how these mechanisms are related to their performance.

Second, due to data limitations, we only examined contest searches as a type of external information search. Other external information search activities, such as information searches from other platforms or external websites, would be an interesting direction for future research. Also, different patterns, sequences, motives, or behavioral strategies for conducting searches may be associated with different outcomes of search processes, which could be another possible extension to investigate with granular data or designed experiments.

Third, due to the structure of contests in our data, submitted solutions did not have ranking information or public ratings. Therefore, we could not examine solvers’ performance in aspects such as ranking improvement. In the Mechanisms for the Effects of Experience section, we provide an additional rating analysis based on a subset of our data in which internal ratings were available, and the results from this analysis support our main findings. Future research could further explore other nuanced aspects of solver performance.

Lastly, our study focuses on the effects of experience and the moderating effects of contest searches in innovative crowdsourcing contests. The theoretical framework and hypotheses are grounded in the competitive nature of this context and its emphasis on creative ideation and design. The core concepts, such as the learning and converging effects, can extend to other innovation contexts, although their relative strengths and the nature of moderating effects might differ across settings. Future research could adapt this framework to other contexts, such as other types of innovation contests or collaborative crowdsourcing settings, and explore more nuanced perspectives, such as knowledge structures and the dynamics of experience accumulation, to further enrich the understanding of experience and knowledge in crowdsourcing and innovation processes.

## Survey on Search Motive and Effect

We conducted an online survey through the Prolific platform in December 2023 to gather views from crowdsourcing solvers regarding contest searches in practice. We first conducted a screening survey to identify individuals who had previously participated in crowdsourcing contests. Out of 950 respondents, 284 had such experience. We then extended invitations for the main survey to these 284 respondents and collected 238 responses to the main survey.

In the main survey, we first asked questions about how many crowdsourcing contests a respondent had participated in, what type of projects these contests focused on, whether the respondent had been selected as a winner for the contests, and whether and why the respondent searched other contests while actively participating in a contest. We then asked two questions to understand how the respondent perceived the usefulness of the information obtained from the project description of a searched contest. Specifically, the first question was a scenario question in which we asked the respondent to imagine that they were working on a creative advertisement project for a restaurant and saw a project description from another advertisement project for a kids’ toy, which was taken from the description of an actual toy project on a widely-used advertisement crowdsourcing platform. The respondent was then prompted to rate, on a 7-point Likert scale, whether reading another project’s description could affect their focal restaurant project in three aspects: provision of inspiration, provision of reference information, and distraction. The second question further asked the respondent, based on their experience of working on crowdsourcing contests, whether they found the descriptions of other projects useful and, if so, in what ways.

Based on the responses, our respondents, on average, had participated in about nine contests. Among the respondents, 91% had participated in crowdsourcing contests focusing on creative solutions, and 29% had participated in other types of crowdsourcing contests. 28% of the respondents were selected as contest winners for at least one of the contests they had participated in. 71% of the respondents had searched for other contests while working on a focal contest, and searching for information for the focal contest was a common purpose of the search (chosen by 58% of participants). Regarding the usefulness of the information from another contest’s project description, in response to the scenario question, 78% of the respondents agreed that the information from another contest’s description could provide inspiration, and 70% agreed that the information could serve as a usefu reference for the focal contest. In the question asking about the respondents’ own experience, 80% of them claimed that, based on their experience of working on crowdsourcing contests, the information from other contests’ descriptions could be useful for the focal contest that they were working on by providing new perspectives or ideas or being used as a reference.

Collectively, these survey results show that crowdsourcing solvers in practice prevalently conduct contest searches to obtain additional information for the focal contest they are working on, and the information from project descriptions of other contests can provide informational benefits for working on the focal contest.
