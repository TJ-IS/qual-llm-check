# EXTENDING THE IT RISK CONTROL FRAMEWORK: INCORPORATING THE ROLE OF TEAM PERSONALITY<sup>1</sup>

Qin Weng Hankamer School of Business, Baylor University Waco, TX, U.S.A. {qin\_weng@baylor.edu}

Carolina Reis

Terry College of Business, University of Georgia Athens, GA, U.S.A. {carolina.reis@uga.edu}

Viswanath Venkatesh Pamplin College of Business, Virginia Tech Blacksburg, VA, U.S.A. {vvenkatesh@vvenkatesh.us}

Many information technology (IT) projects fail to deliver the promised value within the initial budget and the estimated schedule. These shortfalls materialize in part due to unaddressed project risks. The IT project risk and control literature has demonstrated that process controls can alleviate the adverse effects of technical IT project risks on project outcomes. However, the literature has yet to investigate why certain IT project teams respond better than others to process controls. To fill this gap, we extend the IT risk control framework of Venkatesh et al. (2018) by integrating the contingent role of team personality, which includes two meta-traits based on the Big Five personality traits: team stability (conscientiousness, agreeableness, emotional stability) and team plasticity (extroversion, openness to experience). We conducted a field study of 424 offshore unique IT projects, comprising 4,516 unique project team members, to test our model. We found that, in general, teams with a higher level of stability respond better to internal process control, and teams with a higher level of plasticity respond better to external process control. We discuss further nuances of the three-way interactions of technology (technical risks), process (process controls), and people (team personality), and their effects on product and process performance. Our work thus contributes to the IT project management literature by extending the nomological network of IT project risk and control and incorporating the people aspect into this framework. In addition, it outlines how the consideration of team personality can assist managers in better deploying process controls to achieve IT project success.

Keywords: IT projects, IT implementations, project management, technical risks, process control, team personality, IT project performance, IT project success

## Introduction

With information technology (IT) playing a critical role in the economy, organizational change has become heavily driven by IT project implementations (Nieto-Rodriguez, 2021). Indeed, global IT spending, which has been increasing for years, is projected to grow 8% in 2024, reaching a total of \$5.1 trillion (DeLisi & Howley, 2023). However, two out of three IT implementations continue to fall short of the initially agreed-upon terms (Defossez et al., 2020). On average, 14% of IT projects are outright failures, 43% run over budget, 49% run over time, and 31% deliver below the promised value (Project Management Institute, 2017). IT projects are “complex, nonroutine, and dynamic temporary organizations, which entail considerable ambiguity and uncertainty” (Wiener et al., 2016, p. 742). In such settings, multiple stakeholders (e.g., software development organization and client company) work together to achieve common goals that usually entail technological (e.g., new software development) and operational (e.g., time and budget) components (Chua et al., 2012). These ventures promise substantial benefits when successfully deployed and damaging consequences that threaten an organization’s existence when carried out poorly (Moeini & Rivard, 2019). To enhance the likelihood of success, a multifaceted strategy that considers an IT project’s most important elements—i.e., technology, process, and people (Maruping et al., 2019; Nelson, 2007)—is necessary.

The technical aspects of an IT project encompass the technical complexities and interdependencies of a project’s technical architecture (Martin, 2003). Among these technical aspects, prior literature highlights the pivotal role of considering technical IT project risks to enable IT project success (see Wallace et al., 2004a; Windeler et al., 2017). Technical IT project risks reflect the extent to which the IT requirements are unclear and volatile, and the IT artifact is complex (Venkatesh et al., 2018). These risks have increased in recent years with the rapid advancement of big data capabilities (Duan et al., 2019), such as cloud computing and data analytics (Maruping et al., 2019). To overcome technical IT project risks, companies can deploy managerial interventions, such as process controls (Maruping et al., 2019; Tiwana & Keil, 2010). Indeed, researchers advocate for organizations to consider the fit between technical risks and process control mechanisms to achieve greater performance in IT projects (e.g., Keil et al., 2013; Venkatesh et al., 2018). However, the efficacy of process controls in mitigating technical project risks is not yet adequately understood, given that it is missing an important element, i.e., people (Maruping et al., 2019). Specifically, the IT project management literature has predominantly focused on the consequences of control portfolio configurations (what), underemphasizing the role of control enactment (how) (Wiener et al., 2016). In other words, how and why some IT project teams respond better to certain process controls has been largely overlooked. As previously mentioned, despite the three pillars—i.e., technology, process, and people—being critical for a successful IT deployment, the interplay among all three is rarely examined (Maruping et al., 2019). Without the consideration of all three factors (and their interactions), the extent to which managers can make informed decisions and carry out managerial interventions is somewhat limited.

Team composition is an important aspect related to the people facet of risk mitigation and project outcomes. Prior work has examined team composition from different perspectives, such as team culture (e.g., Maruping et al., 2019), team functional and tenure diversity (e.g., Ancona & Caldwell, 1992b), and team personality (e.g., Bradley et al., 2013). Among these perspectives, team personality is particularly relevant to project risk mitigation because it encompasses team characteristics that inform how a team works in complex tasks and risky environments (Beersma et al., 2003), and it has been tied to individuals’ behaviors in team settings (Bell, 2007). In practice, team personality has been portrayed as a key factor that determines a team project’s success (e.g., Winsborough & Chamorro-Premuzic, 2017). For IT projects in particular, research and anecdotal evidence suggest that team members’ personalities influence how individuals act in teams and, in turn, the team’s overall dynamics (e.g., Cruz et al., 2015; Lee et al., 2017; Waller, 2021), making team personality a suitable predictor of teams’ actions in IT project settings. Traditionally, certain personality traits (e.g., conscientiousness) have been believed to be linked to positive outcomes, and some traits (e.g., neuroticism) to negative outcomes (see Bell, 2007). However, a recent multidomain review on personality research challenges the notion that bright traits are beneficial and dark traits are detrimental, and suggests that “effects of personality in organizations are far more complex than previously observed” (Smith et al., 2018, p. 192). It is thus important to investigate the nuanced role of team personality in the IT project context. In particular, how team personality meta-traits, team stability, and team plasticity affect IT project outcomes through their interactions with IT project risks and IT project processes. Here, team stability reflects a team’s general predisposition to maintain the status quo, and team plasticity reflects a team’s tendency to proactively engage in novel courses of action.

To address this gap, we seek to answer the following research question: How does team personality strengthen (or weaken) the mitigating effects of process controls on the effects of technical IT project risks on project outcomes? We extend the projectlevel IT risk control framework, adapted from Venkatesh et al. (2018), that considers the role of technology (i.e., technical project risks) and processes (i.e., process controls), by incorporating the role of people (i.e., IT project team personality). Our work is expected to extend IT risk control research and morto generate IT project-specific understanding that promotes better managerial decision-making.

From a scientific standpoint, our work advances research on IT project management and success by incorporating the role of people and clarifying its interplay with technology and process factors. By incorporating team personality and team processes into the study of IT project risk control, we can extend both personality research and IT project management research and identify the boundary conditions when certain team personality traits are helpful/detrimental to specific team processes. Specifically, since research has questioned whether bright traits are always beneficial and dark traits are always detrimental, understanding the boundary conditions of how they function is needed. Such an understanding is important and pertinent for IT projects considering the complex process controls enacted for risk management. Further, our research advances the literature on personality meta-traits, i.e., stability and plasticity, at the team level, by putting forward a grouplevel concept that plays a critical role in IT projects and could be investigated in other contexts.

From a practical standpoint, uncovering the fit between team personality and team process contributes actionable insights for IT project managers. By considering technical project risks and team personality, managers can make more informed decisions related to resource distribution and process controls, potentially producing better project outcomes. Specifically, we highlight the importance of considering the project team’s personality for better managerial intervention decisions based on some of our key results. First, by knowing that different team personalities respond differently to process controls, managers can proactively adjust their control deployment decisions. For instance, when a project manager knows that a team is largely composed of members high on stability, she can expect that the team will be able to handle high technical project risks, regardless of the nature/level of internal project control. Second, the consideration of team personality can drive more effective team staffing decisions. For instance, in IT projects in which requirements are clear and low external process control is in place, managers should staff the team with individuals with low(er) plasticity levels. Lastly, team personality-related interventions may also be considered to assist with already staffed teams. For example, when there are constraints in team staffing and only technical expertise is considered in the staffing process, teams may benefit from interventions that help them become more cognizant of their traits, so the team’s strengths and weaknesses are determined and considered by managers and team members to improve the team’s overall dynamics. Thus, our research also underscores the importance of adopting specific technologies such as personality profiling tools to support staffing and management decisions throughout an IT project lifecycle.

## Model Development

## IT Risk Control Framework

IT project risks are a “set of factors or conditions that can pose a serious threat to the successful completion” (Wallace et al., 2004b, p. 116) of an IT project. Prior research notes that IT projects must be distinguished as internal or outsourced projects because the latter evolves over a finite timespan where nonrepetitive tasks are more frequent (Wiener et al., 2016). Consequently, IT research has recognized that such differences may affect the success of process controls (Tiwana & Keil, 2010; Wiener et al., 2016). Even though many insights may cut across both types of IT projects, we recognize that some considerations, e.g., multiple stakeholders’ scenarios, may not be applicable to all IT projects. In this research, the main focus is on offshore IT projects. Building on extant prior literature, recent research has proposed IT risk frameworks to explain and predict project success (e.g., Maruping et al., 2019; Venkatesh et al., 2018). Specifically, technical IT project risks have emerged as critical predictors of IT project success, as they represent “the extent to which [IT] projects have volatile or unclear requirements or must be deployed on multiple, unfamiliar or new technology platforms” (Venkatesh et al., 2018, p. 129). These risks have been widely examined in prior literature (see Nidumolu, 1995; Rai et al., 2009; Venkatesh et al., 2018), particularly requirement risk and project complexity risk. Requirement risk is the extent to which the requirements surrounding the technology development and deployment are unclear and volatile (Keil et al., 2013; Venkatesh et al., 2018), and project complexity risk emerges when the project entails using unfamiliar platforms, novel technologies, or integrating multiple systems (Venkatesh et al., 2018). These risks commonly lead to a variety of problems, including code defects, reliability issues, schedule delays, cost overruns, higher maintenance and unanticipated costs, and dissatisfied clients (Keil et al., 2013; Lyytinen et al., 1998; Stewart et al., 2005b; Wiener et al., 2016).

IT project outcomes are typically evaluated based on product and process performance. Product performance pertains to the quality of the IT product by considering the reliability, maintainability, and response time evaluation from the client’s perspective (Nidumolu, 1995; Wiener et al., 2016), and process performance appraises how the project has progressed related to time and budget constraints (Wallace et al., 2004a; Wiener et al., 2016).

To mitigate the adverse effects of technical project risks on project outcomes, researchers advocate using process controls (Tiwana & Keil, 2010; Venkatesh et al., 2018). IT project process controls are defined as activities implemented to regulate or adjust the project stakeholders behaviors toward advancing the project’s objectives (Wiener et al., 2016) and comprise internal and external process controls. Generally, these efforts are outlined by the project manager (Venkatesh et al., 2018) and experienced by the team members. Internal process control directly relates to internal mechanisms employed to align the team’s effort to transform inputs into the requested client’s outputs (Venkatesh et al., 2018). Three broad internal processes and associated controls have been discussed in prior literature:

transition, action, and interpersonal processes (Marks et al., 2001). External process control relates to the project manager’s efforts to stimulate the team’s interaction with the external environment to manage, coordinate, and negotiate external dependencies and resources (Ancona, 1990; Ancona & Caldwell, 1992a). Three main external process control activities have been discussed in prior literature: ambassador, task coordinator, and scout (Ancona & Caldwell, 1992a).

In general, enacting internal and external process controls is expected to ameliorate the adverse effects generated by technical project risks on project outcomes (Venkatesh et al., 2018). Specifically, the deployment of internal process control alleviates the detrimental effects of project risks by promoting a positive attitude toward knowledge sharing (Venkatesh et al., 2018) and overall task synchronization (Mathieu et al., 2006). Likewise, implementing external process control helps clarify the client’s needs and any misrepresentation of their tasks (Venkatesh et al., 2018). Together, process controls help establish IT project milestones, schedules, reprioritization lists, and course-correcting alternatives that are necessary to control the detrimental effects of technical risks on operational goals. Conversely, failure to select and implement proper process controls leads to outdated, incomplete, or unusable systems delivery (Maruping et al., 2019). Venkatesh et al. (2018) present an IT risk control framework that recognizes multilevel relationships related to how process controls moderate the effects of technical risks on individual outcomes. Prior literature supports the detrimental effects of technical project risks at both individual and IT project levels (see Venkatesh et al., 2018; Wallace et al., 2004a, 2004b). Their framework provides the baseline model (Figure 1, thin lines) on which we build, with the previously established role of technology and process noted. We focus solely on the IT project level, as it best relates to our research goals.

## Toward a Sociotechnical IT Risk Control Framework

We extend the IT risk control framework (Venkatesh et al., 2018) and propose a sociotechnical IT risk control framework, where we incorporate the role of people, conceptualized here as project team personality. We postulate that the effectiveness of process controls in alleviating technical risks is contingent on project team personality. In Venkatesh et al., they briefly convey the importance of personality as a predictor of IT project performance. Nevertheless, three main differences exist with respect to how personality is investigated in their study and ours. First, Venkatesh et al. used personality traits, including extraversion and conscientiousness, as control variables, with no explicit theorization on the role of personality. In this work, we theorize and empirically investigate the nuanced role of team personality in IT projects, with the explicit goal of advancing how team personality impacts the mitigating effects of process controls on the effects of technical risks on project outcomes. Second, Venkatesh et al. controlled for a selected set of personality traits at the lower level (Big Five), with no mention of personality meta-traits. In this study, we discuss the role of personality meta-traits, which are higher-order factors that broadly reflect the two fundamental motives underlying human behavior. Third, Venkatesh et al. examined personality traits at the individual level, recognizing their role in influencing individual performance. In this work, we examine these traits at the team level, focusing on their role in IT project outcomes.

## Team Personality Meta-Traits: Stability and Plasticity

In terms of staffing project teams, there is an overreliance on the technical expertise individuals bring to a project team (Grijalva et al., 2020). Rather, their personality traits dictate their patterns of thoughts, feelings, and behaviors (Maier et al., 2019). Personality traits are defined as “probabilistic descriptions of relatively stable patterns of emotion, motivation, cognition, and behavior, in response to classes of stimuli that have been present in human cultures over evolutionary time” (DeYoung et al., 2002, p. 35).

Indeed, prior research has demonstrated that personality traits are critical predictors of how individuals act in team settings (Bell, 2007; Christian et al., 2017). These traits have been widely studied using Costa and McCrae’s (1992) Big Five personality traits. In this study, we focus on the two higherorder factors of the Big Five, known as meta-traits. Although there is some debate on how these personality meta-traits should be organized and labeled, personality theorists broadly agree that there are two fundamental dimensions that underlie human personality (e.g., Abele & Wojciszke, 2014, 2018; Digman, 1997; Entringer et al., 2022; Fiske et al., 2007; Paulhus & John, 1998; Trapnell & Paulhus, 2012; Wiggins, 2003). One of these two broad conceptual dimensions relates to people’s basic need to maintain relationships and social functioning (Abele & Wojciszke, 2014; Digman, 1997). This abstract higher-level trait manifests itself in a constellation of more specific traits related to communion, union, warmth, peer population, and cohesion. The other broad conceptual dimension relates to people’s basic need to pursue growth and enrichment (Digman, 1997). This higher-level trait manifests itself in a constellation of more specific traits related to agency, individuation, competence, status, and openmindedness. These two higher-level factors have been termed stability and plasticity, α and β, or communion and agency, depending on the theoretical perspective adopted to describe the general structure of human personality. We adopt the labels stability and plasticity (in line with DeYoung et al., 2002) for the two personality meta-traits because these terms convey the underlying notions while simultaneously hinting at their complementarity.

Like personality traits themselves, these meta-traits have been mainly studied at the individual level (e.g., DeYoung, 2015; Wu et al., 2020), with little or no work examining the role of these concepts at the team level (an exception is Kollmann et al., 2021). However, in team settings, where members need to work collectively toward common goals (Bell, 2007), the general predisposition of the team to sustain stability and avoid disruptive impulsivity (team stability) and to proactively respond to unexpected changes and cope with uncertain situations (team plasticity) characterizes two critical notions that are worth investigating.

Team stability is defined as a personality meta-trait reflecting a group of individuals’ tendency to maintain the status quo and avoid disruption. This meta-trait relates to socially desirable personality traits—namely, conscientiousness, agreeableness, and emotional stability (opposite of neuroticism) (Digman, 1997). Overall, stability relates to the socialization process itself (in line with prior work, such as Digman, 1997) in the motivational, social, and emotional domains (DeYoung et al., 2002). Delving into each stability trait, team conscientiousness reflects the team’s motivation to strive and persevere toward goal achievement, team agreeableness expresses the team’s ability to maintain a positive and cooperative interpersonal environment, and the team’s emotional stability manifests as the team’s emotional arousal intensity in response to negative events and stressors (Wu et al., 2020). The second personality meta-trait, team plasticity, relates to the team’s tendency to engage in flexible and novel courses of action (DeYoung et al., 2002). As people have a need to actualize and expand the self (Digman, 1997), plasticity relates to the behavioral and cognitive domains that are captured by team extraversion and team openness to experience. Specifically, team extraversion relates to the team’s behavioral exploration and engagement, and team openness reflects the team’s cognitive quest for more information and knowledge (Wu et al., 2020).

## Team Personality and Process Controls

We expect team personality to further moderate the mitigating effects of IT project process controls on the effects of technical project risks on project outcomes. In general, high levels of stability and plasticity are associated with greater task performance (Hui-Hua & Schutte, 2015). Nonetheless, these two traits represent distinct facets of an individual’s and group’s personality. Therefore, these traits regulate different dimensions of task performance.

Digman (1997) summarized different personality frameworks and suggested that the various conceptual systems can be organized in terms of communion (focused on peer popularity), which maps to stability, and agency (focused on status), which maps to plasticity. Specifically, stability is regarded as a socialization factor, promoting internal cohesion that facilitates task-related activities (DeYoung et al., 2002) such as adherence to goals and cooperation (Alessandri & Vecchione, 2012; Hui-Hua & Schutte, 2015). In IT projects, compliance with the team’s normative prescriptions (Ramasubbu et al., 2015) and the regular deployment of feedback loops (Maruping et al., 2021) are key mechanisms to ensure that desired project outcomes are attained. These task-related activities are the focus of internal process control. Plasticity is considered a personal growth factor, featuring open-mindedness (DeYoung et al., 2002), an important trait that facilitates interactions with external constituents, as such individuals embrace/explore new ideas in teams. The predisposition to the unknown is essential to facilitate external interaction activities such as exploring clients’ requests and seeking their feedback. These external interaction activities are the focus of external process control. Overall, team stability and team plasticity are expected to interact with internal and external process controls, respectively (see Table 1).

Our proposed research model is presented in Figure 1, and the construct definitions are shown in Table 2. This model examines how team personality characteristics, together with process controls, promote better mitigation of the effect of risks. In line with prior research (Moeini & Rivard, 2019; Venkatesh et al., 2018), we view IT project process controls as processes delineated by project managers. Therefore, team personality is conceptualized as an exogenous variable.

## Moderating Role of Team Stability

A team with a high level of stability is usually composed of individuals who enjoy structure and a controlled environment. These individuals hold a greater appreciation for orderliness and conformity. When dealing with complex and uncertain IT developments, a team needs to maintain a sense of structure and orientation (Maruping et al., 2021), not losing sight of the existing IT project targets. A team composed of such members is expected to engage well in prioritization lists, formalized procedures, and prearranged communication mechanisms (e.g., frequent alignment meetings). Those activities are directly linked to internal process control.

As previously outlined, stability is the meta-trait for conscientiousness, agreeableness, and neuroticism, which are related to the motivational, social, and emotional domains, respectively. A team with high conscientiousness, which is a trait indicative of motivational stability, is generally self-disciplined, diligent, and hardworking (Devaraj et al., 2008; Wilson et al., 2016). Such teams tend to carefully perform their perceived responsibilities (Tepper et al., 2001) and dedicate more time to work-related matters (Barnett, 2006). On the contrary, a team with low conscientiousness is expected to engage in counterproductive work behaviors (Kluemper et al., 2015; Mount et al., 2006), hold limiting self-regulatory capacities, and react more aggressively to interactions (Witt et al., 2002), hampering the mitigating effects of internal process control.

A team with high agreeableness, which is a trait indicative of social stability, mainly focuses on maintaining stable social relationships (Wu et al., 2020), cooperation, trust, and compliance norms (Gonzalez-Mulé et al., 2014; van Vianen & De Dreu, 2001). Such teams tend to respect each other’s points of view (Kluemper et al., 2015) and rarely disagree in public (LePine & Van Dyne, 2001). They work well together in a collaborative environment (Devaraj et al., 2008; Venkatesh & Windeler, 2012). Their tendency to foster social cohesion, less team conflict, and more communication (Barrick et al., 1998) makes it more likely that they will follow project guidelines (DeYoung et al., 2002), enhancing the mitigating effects of the implemented internal process control. Conversely, a team that is low on agreeableness is expected to be more argumentative (Fisher et al., 2012; Witt et al., 2002) and inflexible (Fisher et al., 2012) and to more actively engage in counterproductive work behaviors (Kluemper et al., 2015; Mount et al., 2006).

A team with low neuroticism, which is a trait indicative of emotional stability, can adjust and regulate their behavioral responses to negative emotions and remain confident in a stressful work environment (Morgeson et al., 2005). Such teams can better manage anxiety associated with environmental (Christian et al., 2017) and structural changes (DeRue et al., 2008) common in complex tasks (Stewart et al., 2005a) and promote group cohesion (Moynihan & Peterson, 2004; van Vianen & De Dreu, 2001). This type of team is expected to respond well to internal process control, as these mechanisms assist the team in structuring how and when to conduct specific tasks. In contrast, teams composed of highly neurotic individuals have more difficulty managing stressful situations (Venkatesh & Windeler, 2012) due to their sensitivity to punishments that may be imposed for not fulfilling required duties (Duffy et al., 2006). Overall, we thus expect teams with a high level of stability to respond more effectively to internal process control than teams with low(er) stability.

H1: A team with a high level of stability will respond more positively to internal process control than a team with a low level of stability, increasing the mitigating effects of internal process control on the negative effects of technical project risks on project outcomes.

## Moderating Role of Team Plasticity

A high level of plasticity in a team stimulates novelty and activities beyond the prescribed courses of action (DeYoung et al., 2002). A team that is high in plasticity will have a greater predisposition to reconceptualize and to think outside the box and will be able to effectively respond to anomalies and deal with unfamiliar situations (DeYoung, 2015). In IT projects, developers frequently deal with technologically complex developments, as almost no off-the-shelf solutions match the client’s requirements (Maruping et al., 2021). A team that is high in plasticity voluntarily seeks ambiguous tasks and is enthusiastic in the face of unanticipated challenges. Therefore, we expect such a team to be highly engaged in frequent interactions with the external environment when a higher level of external process control is in place. A higher level of external process control requires a team to actively manage the team’s external image, handle external dependencies, and secure resources from the environment that are critical for the team’s success (Ancona & Caldwell, 1992a).

As previously mentioned, plasticity is the meta-trait for extraversion and openness, which are related to the behavioral and cognitive domains, respectively. Teams with high extraversion, which is a trait indicative of behavioral exploration, are predisposed to actively explore the environment (Barry & Stewart, 1997). Extraverted individuals are sociable, energetic, and assertive (Gonzalez-Mulé et al., 2014; van Vianen & De Dreu, 2001) and tend to quickly and freely communicate their thoughts in team settings (Barrick et al., 1998; Barry & Stewart, 1997). When external process control is in motion, outspoken team members will more easily make constructive suggestions that facilitate the project’s advancement (LePine & Van Dyne, 2001). These characteristics facilitate information sharing (Fisher et al., 2012) and more easily transmit change-oriented ideas (LePine & Van Dyne, 2001). This will enable better responses to external process control. In IT projects, we expect that a team with high plasticity (especially due to high extraversion) will be more capable of clarifying and communicating with clients and other external constituents (Eckhardt et al., 2016).

A team with high openness, a trait indicative of cognitive exploration, is likely to consider new and original ideas (Bell, 2007). Individuals high in openness tend to be autonomous (van Vianen & De Dreu, 2001), broadminded (Bell, 2007), and intellectually curious (Wilson et al., 2016). In IT projects, engaging with the external environment means there is a need to consider different alternatives and brainstorm different ideas and potential courses of action. Therefore, a team high in openness will invest more effort in considering unconventional paths (Judge & Ilies, 2002; LePine & Van Dyne, 2001) and trying new approaches (Devaraj et al., 2008).

In contrast, a team low in openness will frequently feel frustrated with redesigning technical architectures and will naturally carry a negative attitude when it needs to rethink, brainstorm, and rework new designs (Devaraj et al., 2008). Overall, we thus expect teams with a high level of plasticity to respond more effectively to external process control than teams low(er) on plasticity.

H2: A team with a high level of plasticity will respond more positively to external process control than a team with a low level of plasticity, increasing the mitigating effects of external process control on the negative effects of technical project risks on project outcomes.

## Method

## Sample and Participants

We conducted a field study on offshore information systems (IS) projects implemented by a leading software vendor from India for multiple US-based clients. The sampling frame comprised approximately 1,200 projects; we sought to collect data from approximately 600 project teams comprising nonoverlapping members (to avoid issues of nesting and correlated responses/errors). Of these, we received completed and usable responses from 424 IT project teams, with 4,516 unique and non-overlapping team members, completed over a two-year period, beginning in 2018. In these IS projects, teams from the software vendor firm work on custom developments to match the specific requirements outlined by the clients. In these projects, the teams are typically staffed by considering the needs of the project in terms of experience, skills, and abilities of team members, as well as the availability of team members at different times. These considerations are often deemed more critical and thus supersede the consideration of other factors, such as team personality, during staffing decisions.<sup>2</sup> The projects spanned across various manufacturing and service industries.

## Procedure

Data were collected over the lifecycle of each IT project that typically spanned between 3 and 4 months. Our procedure and timing of data collection were similar to prior IT and general project management research (Maruping et al., 2015;

Venkatesh et al., 2018). At the initiation of the project, team members filled out a baseline survey that included personality. After the initial phase of each project, around the midpoint of the project, once the process controls were established and understood, we collected data from team members about internal and external process controls. We also gathered internal and external process controls data from project managers. Our analyses using these alternate measures showed high correlations with team member perceptions (\~0.70) and a similar pattern of results in structural model results—we report the results from the team member data for the reasons we discuss next in the measurement section. The collection of the self-reported data at different points in time is considered an ex ante method that helps reduce common method bias (CMB) (Podsakoff et al., 2003). At the end of each IT project, data related to project performance, technical risks, IT project characteristics, and team characteristics were directly extracted or calculated from project documentation. Four months after each IT project was delivered and implemented, its client provided their satisfaction with the delivered system.

## Measurement

We used measures from prior literature. We provide details related to our measures here (see Appendix A for more details).

Project outcomes were measured based on product performance and process performance. Product performance was measured as a subjective evaluation of the product by the client of the project, including items of “ease of use of software” and “ability to customize outputs to various user needs” (Rai et al., 2009), using a 7-point Likerttype scale (from “very poor” to “very good”). Process performance was assessed as an objective calculation of cost overruns, based on the percent difference between the actual project costs and the budgeted costs (Rai et al., 2009).

Technical IT project risks were measured by requirement risk and complexity risk from project documentation, thus representing key objective metrics about the projects. Requirement risk was measured as a count of the total number of formal written changes on the project requirements (Rai et al., 2009; Venkatesh et al., 2018). Project complexity risk was calculated using the number of adjusted function points, which employs a list of 14 complexity characteristics that assess the distinctiveness in requirements and technological platforms. This adjusted function points metric, initially developed by Albrecht and Gaffney (1983), considers “a weighted sum of the number of inputs, inquiries, and master files that must be accessed from other systems or platforms and the number of outputs and queries generated for other systems” (Venkatesh et al. 2018, p. 136).

IT project process controls are represented as internal and external process controls. To measure internal process control, we used the 9-item scale from Mathieu et al. (2006), which includes measures of transition, action, and interpersonal processes (Marks et al., 2001). External process control was measured using the 18-item scale from Ancona and Caldwell (1992a), which includes items related to ambassador, task coordination, and scouting activities. Process controls were measured at the individual level to include the team members’ perceptions of the team process deployments. As these processes represent shared team properties, the aggregation at the team level is theoretically justified (James & Williams, 2000). In this case, by collecting and evaluating process controls based on the perceptions of team members, we expected a reflection of experienced process controls to which team members were responding. We used a reference-shift consensus operationalization (Chan, 1998) for both internal and external process controls. Prior research has used a similar approach to operationalize the extent to which teams engage in various activities (e.g., Maruping et al., 2015; Wageman et al., 2005). Both internal and external process controls were modeled consistently with Venkatesh et al. (2018) as first-order reflective and second-order formative constructs. As noted earlier, we also measured process controls from project managers to add robustness to our primary analyses and associated findings.

Team personality was measured using individual self-reports and aggregated to the team level. We used the Big Five scale from Costa and McCrae (1992), which is a widely used measurement. This measurement scale does not make particular reference to the context of investigation, which is common and justified on the basis that these are dispositional, context-general traits (Larson et al., 2020; Minbashian et al., 2010). Team stability comprises three traits: conscientiousness, agreeableness, and emotional stability (opposite of neuroticism), and team plasticity comprises the two other traits: extraversion and openness to experience (DeYoung et al., 2002). We aggregated the personality responses to the team level by combining all responses of the team members and using the additive index (mean) approach (Chan, 1998). This operationalization is theoretically appropriate because personality traits are dispositional characteristics deeply ingrained in individuals and, by association, in teams (Larson et al., 2020). This operationalization is also considered the most appropriate for contexts in which team members execute compensatory tasks (Bell, 2007) and work together to accomplish shared goals (Courtright et al., 2017), which is the case in IT projects. In such contexts, the low-performing team members’ poorer performance can be compensated for by high-performing team members (in line with existing IT project management literature: Ghobadi & Mathiassen, 2017; Wiener et al., 2016). Team personality was modeled as first-order reflective (i.e., items for each lower-level personality trait) and second-order formative (i.e., the lowerlevel traits formed the overall meta-trait). The lower-level traits were modeled as reflective because: (1) the measurement items are manifestations of the lower-level traits, (2) the items for each lower-level trait are interchangeable, (3) each set of items shares a similar content and theme, and (4) dropping an item would not alter the conceptual domain of the lower-level traits (Jarvis et al., 2003). The meta-traits were modeled as formative because: (1) the lower-level traits (composed of a set of measurement items) define each meta-trait, (2) the lower-level traits of each meta-trait are not interchangeable, (3) the lower-level traits do not share the same content, and (4) dropping a lower-level trait would alter the conceptual domain of each meta-trait (Jarvis et al., 2003).

We followed a “less is more” approach (Berneth & Aguinis, 2016), which makes a strong case against the use of too many control variables because of how they distract from the theoretical focus. Thus, despite the availability of several possible control variables in our dataset, we included only those that were theoretically relevant to our focus on project outcomes—the control variables included were project size (number of lines of code), project duration (number of months to complete the project), team size (number of team members), and team experience (average number of years of team members’ project experience) (Maruping et al., 2009, 2019; Rai et al., 2009). In order to add robustness, we conducted additional analyses using other control variables typically used in this domain of research—we note our observations based on those results in the next section.

## Results

We used partial least squares (PLS), a variance-based technique, to test our measurement and structural models. We considered PLS appropriate because it (1) does not make assumptions about the distribution of the data and (2) can handle both formative and reflective measurements (Hair et al., 2011). Smart-PLS 3.3.3 was the software used for the analysis (Ringle et al., 2021).

## Measurement Validity

Descriptive statistics and correlations for the study’s variables are shown in Table B1 (in Appendix B). The results of the measurement model testing suggested that the scales displayed good psychometric properties, including composite reliability, convergent validity, discriminant validity, and construct portability. All scale reliabilities for constructs with reflective indicators were greater than 0.70, thus supporting reliability. All factor loadings for multi-item reflective scales were greater than 0.70, and cross-loadings were lower than the loadings, thus supporting convergent and discriminant validity. The average variance extracted (AVE) was greater than 0.50, and the correlations between variables were all below the square root of AVE, also supporting convergent and discriminant validity (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). This assessment was only applicable to the product performance construct (0.74). With team personality meta-traits and process controls, the weights of the second-order factors on the latent variables ranged from 0.23 to 0.57<sup>3</sup> and were all significant: stability—conscientiousness (0.34\*\*\*), agreeableness (0.38\*\*\*), and emotional stability (0.23\*\*\*); plasticity—extroversion (0.33\*\*\*) and openness (0.41\*\*\*); internal process control—transition process (0.57\*\*\*), action process (0.35\*\*\*), and interpersonal process (0.31\*\*\*); external process control—ambassador (0.28\*\*\*), task coordination $( 0 . 2 9 ^ { * * * } ) ,$ and scouting $( 0 . 4 4 ^ { * * * } )$ . The formative constructs displayed good construct portability. Construct portability represents the relative invariance of a construct’s item weights as the construct is used in multiple nomological networks (Cenfetelli & Bassellier, 2009). We performed a redundancy analysis of the project team personality meta-traits and a comparison weight analysis of the IT project process controls. A redundancy analysis is more appropriate when the formative constructs are new, and a comparison weight analysis is adequate when the constructs have been used in prior studies (Cenfetelli & Bassellier, 2009). The redundancy analysis was performed by creating two latent constructs for each team personality meta-trait, where one construct was measured with formative items and that construct was causal to the second construct, which was conceptually equivalent but measured with reflective items (Cenfetelli & Bassellier, 2009). The magnitudes of the structural paths were above 0.90, thus supporting the construct validity of the personality meta-traits: stability (0.92, p < 0.001) and plasticity (0.91, p < 0.001) (Chin, 1998). The comparison of item weights of our process controls constructs to process controls constructs in prior studies (e.g., Venkatesh et al., 2018) showed similar patterns, thus corroborating construct portability of the process controls.

Together, these patterns of results supported reliability, validity, and portability.

To justify the aggregation of the individual responses about internal and external process controls, we examined withinaggregate-unit agreement and between-unit disagreement levels. The average index of agreement, $\mathbf { r } _ { \mathrm { w g ( j ) , } }$ for the internal process control items was 0.73, and for the external process control items, it was 0.74, which demonstrated strong agreement (LeBreton & Senter, 2008). The intraclass correlation coefficient of interrater reliability, ICC(2), which represents the reliability of team-level means, for internal process control was 0.73, and for external process control, it was 0.71, which demonstrated strong agreement (LeBreton & Senter, 2008). The intraclass correlation coefficient of between-groups variance, ICC(1), which represents the proportion of variance explained by between-team differences, for internal process control was 0.28, and for external process control, it was 0.29, which are considered large effects (LeBreton & Senter, 2008). Hence, these results suggested that it was appropriate to aggregate individual responses about internal and external process controls within each project and use the team-level scores. This pattern of results was generally consistent with Venkatesh et al. (2018). Unlike process controls that are experienced by team members, where their level of shared understanding is important, in the case of team personality, the scores of team members cannot be expected to be correlated, and agreement is thus not expected. Nevertheless, $\mathbf { r } _ { \mathrm { w g ( j ) } }$ for team stability items was 0.71, and for team plasticity items, it was 0.71, which demonstrated strong agreement (LeBreton & Senter, 2008). The ICC(2) for team stability was 0.73, and for team plasticity, it was 0.77, which demonstrated strong agreement<sup>4</sup> (LeBreton & Senter, 2008). The ICC(1) for team stability was 0.28, and for team plasticity, it was 0.36, which are considered large effects (LeBreton & Senter, 2008). In this case, we also averaged individuals’ responses within each project team to compute the team-level score for team stability and team plasticity.

We deployed two ex post methods to diagnose the presence of common method bias (CMB). First, using Harman’s onefactor test (Podsakoff et al., 2003), we found that no single factor emerged as the dominant one that accounted for most of the variance, thus suggesting that CMB was not a problem in our dataset. Second, using full collinearity tests (recommended by Kock, 2015), we found that all variance inflation factors (VIF) were below the threshold of 3.3.

## Hypotheses Testing

Table 3 shows the structural model results, with Models 1a and 1b showing control variables only, Models 2a and 2b showing main effects, Models 3a and 3b showing main effects and moderators, and Models 4a and 4b showing interactions also. We used a bootstrap resampling method (10,000 resamples) to determine the path significance of the relationships in the structural model. Consistent with Sykes (2015) and other exemplars, given the nested nature of the models and two dependent variables, we estimated the complete model with both dependent variables (shown as Models 4a and 4b in Table 3) and used the estimated latent variable scores to estimate the subset models (Models 1a, 1b, 2a, 2b, 3a, and 3b). Estimating the latent variable scores was also necessary for our endogeneity testing, summarized later.

Before proceeding to test the hypotheses, it was necessary to determine how much of the variance in project outcomes was attributable to (1) the project and team characteristics, (2) the main effects of technical IT project risks, and (3) the main effects of process controls and team personality. Therefore, we ran a null model (Models 1a and 1b), one model with only direct effects of technical IT project risks (Models 2a and 2b), and one model with the direct effects of all variables (Models 3a and 3b). Results from the null model showed that 1% and 2% of the variance in product and process performance between IT projects, respectively, was explained by project and team characteristics. Results from the main direct effects model predicting product and process performance showed that 7% and 8% of the variance in product and process performance between IT projects, respectively, was explained by project characteristics, team characteristics, and technical IT project risks. Results from the full direct effects model predicting product and process performance showed that 11% and 12% of the variance in product and process performance between IT projects, respectively, was explained by project characteristics, team characteristics, technical IT project risks, process controls,<sup>5</sup> and team personality.

Both our hypotheses were mostly supported. The three-way interactions among technical IT project risks, internal process control, and team stability, and among technical IT project risks, external process control, and team plasticity were significant (see Table 3 and Figure 2), explaining 31% and 29% of the variance in product and process performance, respectively. Specifically, we can see that the three-way interaction of requirement risk with internal (external) process control and team stability (team plasticity) was significant as a determinant in driving both product and process performance. In the case of project complexity risk, the three-way interaction with internal process control and team stability was a significant driver of both product and process performance; however, the three-way interaction with external process control and team plasticity was nonsignificant for both performance outcomes. To assess the magnitude of the interactions’ impact on the dependent variables (product performance and process performance), we calculated Cohen’s f<sup>2</sup>, which represents the extent to which a phenomenon is present in a given population (Chin et al., 2003). The f<sup>2</sup> effect sizes were above 0.15, suggesting medium effects (Chin, 1998): product performance (0.29) and process performance (0.24). This indicates that the interaction effects played a significant role in explaining the variance in both IT project outcomes.<sup>6</sup>

## Robustness Checks

We conducted multiple tests for endogeneity. Although we controlled for key potential confounds, there might still be endogeneity caused by omitted variable bias. For example, manager experience may potentially affect process controls and project outcomes. To alleviate the omitted variable bias concerns, we included project manager gender identity (biological sex), as an instrumental variable for process controls because it is likely correlated to process controls and weakly correlated to project outcomes, as a manager of a specific gender may be inclined to use specific sets of process controls (e.g., Bobe & Kober, 2020 and Carmona et al., 2018, highlighted the existence of gender differences in how leaders approach and adopt management styles)<sup>7</sup>. For robustness checks, we also considered budgeted upfront cost and estimated final upfront cost as instrumental variables for process controls, as these metrics are likely correlated to IT project processes but weakly correlated to IT project outcomes (in line with prior work, Krishnan et al., 2000; Ramasubbu et al., 2015). Likewise, team members’ cognitive abilities are likely to be correlated to team personality and to affect performance outcomes. We followed Tacheva and Ivanov’s (2021) approach and, using the text analysis software Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC2015: Pennebaker et al., 2015), identified multiple language-related characteristics measures extracted from the project team’s email communication during the first four weeks of the project that were potential instrumental variables for the two team personality meta-traits. Out of a set of 46 characteristics (such as tone, nouns, verbs, and punctuation), we selected two language characteristics measures (clout and emotional tone, respectively) that were strongly correlated to team personality meta-traits (stability and plasticity) and weakly correlated to project outcomes. We ran 3SLS using the estimated latent variable scores from our PLS model. The results of this analysis are provided in Table B2, Appendix B. In these analyses to address issues of endogeneity, although the resulting inverse Mills ratio was significant, there was no substantive change in the pattern of results in terms of the significance of coefficients of key main and interaction terms. Overall, these analyses suggest that our results were robust to endogeneity concerns

## Discussion

Our work adds to the literature on IT project management and augments the understanding of how IT project managers can mitigate IT project risks by considering team personality. Specifically, we incorporated team personality into the IT risk control framework (Venkatesh et al., 2018). Our field study of 424 offshore IT projects revealed the different effects of the two team-level personality meta-traits (i.e., team stability and team plasticity) on the risk mitigation effects of process controls. Our model, which included three-way interactions among risks, process controls, and personality, explained 31% and 29% of the variance in product performance and process performance, respectively. Our model and underlying hypotheses were mostly supported, and we found that the interplay of these three elements (technology manifested as risks, process manifested as controls, and people manifested as personality) was consistent across two types of risk, two types of controls, and two personality meta-traits.

Going beyond the support for the model, the patterns of impacts on performance outcomes were complex (as noted earlier in Tables 5 and 6). Regardless of the level of technical risks, a high-stability team with high internal process control in place achieved the best performance. This finding aligns with our hypothesis (H1) that high-stability teams ease the broader project’s objectives of adhering to institutional policies and deploying the overall project plan. Specifically, we found that in high technical risk environments, the adverse effects of technical risks are strongly mitigated when the project team has high stability or when high internal process control is in place. Indeed, in such settings, team stability can potentially act as an inherent control that helps mitigate the effects of technical risks just as effectively as internal process control can. Whereas in an environment with low technical risks, teams with high stability with low internal process control are likely to engage in a more passive mindset that may lead to groupthink (a critical cause of poor group decisions: Miranda, 1994), thus resulting in poor(er) performance. In these environments, teams with low stability achieve better performance, most likely due to the simpler and/or clearer requirements that can be effectively accomplished even if team members adopt more unstructured coordination.

Regardless of the level of technical risks, a high-plasticity team with high external process control in place will achieve the best performance. This finding aligns with our hypothesis (H2) that high-plasticity teams engage more effectively in exchanging ideas, which increases the effectiveness of external process control. Notably, in environments with high technical risks, a team with high plasticity will have a greater predisposition to think outside the box, which in turn could help mitigate technical risks. In contrast, in environments with low technical risks, when low external process control is in place, teams with high plasticity are less defined by challenging tasks and by the communication with external constituents, which leads to their demotivation and consequently poor(er) performance.

## Theoretical Contributions

The theoretical contributions of our work are multifold. First, we contribute to the IT project management literature in general and IT risk control literature in particular by extending the nomological network of IT project management to more completely integrate technology, process, and people. By incorporating team personality into the existing IT risk control framework (Venkatesh et al., 2018), we focus on the critical people aspect of IT projects and provide a more nuanced understanding of how and why some teams respond better to certain process controls. Considering the role of people in this domain is crucial because conducting an IT project inherently entails a collective effort in which multiple individuals work together to accomplish common goals (Maruping et al., 2019). By expanding the IT risk framework to incorporate aspects of the sociotechnical framework, we recognize and advance how to account for the collective nature of IT projects and the interdependence of the team’s actions to mitigate technical risks and achieve IT project performance.

Second, our study contributes to the IT risk control literature by identifying the types of team personality for which different types of controls may be more effective. Recognizing the harmful effects of technical project risks (see Wallace et al., 2004b), researchers advocated and demonstrated how process controls can mitigate these adverse effects (e.g., Tiwana & Keil, 2010; Venkatesh et al., 2018). We extend the IT risk control framework of Venkatesh et al. (2018) by incorporating the three-way interactions among technical risks, process controls, and team personality to predict IT project outcomes. Specifically, our findings show that (1) the interaction of team stability and internal process control with technical risks and (2) the interaction of team plasticity and external process control with technical risks predict IT project outcomes. Importantly, we underscore that overlooking team personality may exacerbate the harmful effects of technical risks. Relatedly, our work also responds to broader calls in IS research to integrate the role of personality into IS models (Eckhardt et al., 2016).

Third, understanding the interdependence of these factors also helps to pinpoint possible points of intervention worth investigating. Our research shows that project team personality, when interacting with process controls, can play an active role in reducing the negative effects of technical risks on project team success. Prior research has made limited efforts to examine project management interventions when considering technology, process, and people factors. Thus, our work provides evidence of the importance of considering the theoretical mechanisms by which technology, process, and people act together to delineate success in IT project contexts.

Finally, there is limited research examining the role of personality meta-traits at the team level. By theorizing about the two personality meta-traits at the team level, we contribute by providing an exemplar on how these constructs could play a useful role in understanding phenomena at the team level. This examination of personality at the team level advances our understanding of how the lower-level elements function at a higher level, where their interactions with other higher-level constructs also play a role (the importance of constructs at different levels is discussed in Kozlowski & Klein, 2000, and more recently, in Venkatesh et al., 2023). We hope that future research and researchers with similar data that they have already collected can reflect more deeply on team personality to understand how it may play a role in IT projects and in other phenomena.

## Practical Contributions

An important implication for project managers is that team personality matters when it comes to managing IT project risk control. Organizations devote significant resources to IT projects (Karhade & Dong, 2021), yet the returns remain highly variable (Project Management Institute, 2017). Practice has recognized that this problem endures because managers do not consider the type of teams they have and/or do not use them to their full potential (Grijalva et al., 2020; Vickberg & Christfort, 2017). We demonstrate how the consideration of team personality can assist managers in better deploying IT project process controls. Specifically, IT project managers can benefit from our finding that higher stability teams respond better to internal process control and higher plasticity teams respond better to external process control. Thus, this investigation highlights the importance of adopting personality profiling tools that help managers identify the strengths and shortcomings of the personality composition of the team in order to deploy the most effective managerial interventions.

When deciding on IT project process controls, project managers can preemptively consider how teams will perform differently based on their project team personality, the technical IT project risks, and the implemented IT project process controls. For instance, when a project team exhibits a high level of stability, a project manager can be reasonably confident that the team will be able to handle projects with high technical risks and will not necessarily require a high level of internal process control to achieve project success. Interestingly, a team that exhibits a high level of stability will require high internal process control to succeed when the project involves low technical risks. In contrast, when a project team exhibits a low level of stability, the project manager can be reasonably confident that the team will be able to handle projects with low technical risks and will not necessarily require a high level of internal process control to succeed. However, a team that exhibits a low level of stability will require high internal process control to succeed when the project involves high technical risks. Similarly, knowing that a team exhibits a high level of plasticity, a project manager can be reasonably assured that the team will be able to handle projects with high technical risks and will be able to achieve project success, regardless of the level of external process control. Interestingly, a team that exhibits a high level of plasticity will require high external process control to succeed when the project involves low technical risks. In contrast, when a project team exhibits a low level of plasticity, the project manager can be reasonably confident that the team will be able to handle projects with high technical risks and will not necessarily require a high level of external process control to succeed. However, a team that exhibits a low level of plasticity will require high external process control to succeed when the project involves high technical risks. The general recommendation is for managers to consider the information already available about the IT project, particularly about the technical IT project risks, the IT project process controls, and/or the IT project team personality, and determine the optimal sociotechnical approach for managing IT risks and achieving IT project success.

Importantly, the consideration of the IT project team personality can help managers improve IT project success through their selection and staffing decisions. Even though our sample was composed of teams that remained stable throughout the duration of the projects, it is common for managers to face the need to restaff their teams (e.g., when clients’ requirements change, affecting the need for staff members with different skill sets). Therefore, the consideration of the IT project team personality and its relationship with technical IT project risks and IT project process controls can be valuable not only for initial staffing decisions but also for ongoing adjustments in the IT project team, such as team member replacement and team expansion.

Other forms of team personality-related interventions can also be considered to improve IT project outcomes. Management research has highlighted the importance of training exercises that help teams develop an awareness of the team’s needs, strengths, and areas of improvement (Mathieu et al., 2014). This type of training helps managers and team members more consciously discuss and strategize about how they can leverage team personality composition strengths and compensate for their potential weaknesses. Anecdotal evidence shows that such technologies are already helping internal teams in the IT industry by educating team members and managers about each member’s personality, giving individuals the confidence to address any issues before they become serious obstacles that can hinder team performance (The Predictive Index, 2022). Our findings can help refine such technologies and should also be studied for their efficacy vis-à-vis current approaches for the design of these technologies.

## Limitations and Future Research

A few limitations and possible future research directions should be highlighted. First, it should be noted that the generalizability of our findings may be constrained to the setting of our study. We considered offshore IT projects— outsourced from the U.S. and conducted offshore in India. This concern is, to some extent, alleviated given that the IT projects in our study encompassed multiple client companies from the U.S. that operate in different industries. Further, offshore IT projects often deal with cultural differences at multiple levels and thus as a cross-level effect (particularly in India; Rai et al., 2009), supporting the notion that these projects are likely to encompass people who espouse a diverse set of values. In offshore projects, research has shown that differences in vendor and client work practices may negatively affect project performance (Levina & Vaast, 2008; Rai et al., 2009). Yet it is possible to manage a client-vendor relationship through aligning norms and open communication to achieve IT project success (Rai et al., 2009). Another inherent limitation of field studies is that they do not allow for control over the setting (e.g., how teams are formed) or the variables of interest (e.g., project team personality). These constraints are typical of most field studies, especially those in the domain of IT projects. This concern is somewhat lessened because the pattern of results was unchanged with the inclusion of control variables such as team familiarity, which accounted for the consequences of team assignment. Having said that, our sample is relatively diverse in that it includes a series of projects with different project-related and teamrelated characteristics. Future work could investigate whether our results can be generalized to other settings, such as onshore IT projects in different cultural settings.

Second, our study focused on one of the several potential team personality compositions—the mean value of team members’ personalities based on Costa and McCrae’s NEO personality inventory (1992). Other composition and diversity models to include variance, maximum, and minimum scores of personality variables could be used to further examine the role of personality. For scope reasons and as a first step in our understanding of the role of personality in the context of the IT project risk control framework, we focus on the mean value of team members’ personalities because the team mean is considered the most appropriate team-level operationalization of team composition for teams performing compensatory tasks (Bell, 2007) and working together toward common goals (Courtright et al., 2017). Future research could expand our understanding of personality in this context to include other personality conceptualizations, such as dark personality traits, also known as the dark triad, which may prevent teams from capitalizing on process controls (e.g., Grijalva et al., 2020, showed how team-level narcissism prevents teams from capitalizing on normative coordination).

Third, we acknowledge that teams may frequently experience changes in their member composition. For instance, project members leaving in the middle of the project or project members joining after the project has been initiated may affect a team’s overall personality. Prior research has examined how such team member changes directly affect team performance (e.g., DeRue et al., 2008). A potential extension of our work would involve incorporating the temporal aspect of team composition by investigating how changes in team personality over time (see Zaheer et al., 1999) would affect the relationships in our expanded project risk control framework.

Fourth, the interplay across the three elements of our predictor variables is an important next step. Although we did test for endogeneity, further research is necessary to disentangle whether managers actively deploy specific controls for certain personality types. Likewise, certain teams may be constituted based on the expected technical risks. These types of correlations and influence among the predictor variables require further study, which would, in turn, enhance our understanding of project outcomes. Related to this, the role of leadership can be studied. Nonetheless, if team processes are deployed in keeping with team personality, finding significant interaction effects presents a conservative test. Also, other technology-related, process-related, and people-related factors could emerge as important predictors of IT project performance. Further work is needed along these lines.

Finally, our research focuses on team-level outcomes and could be extended to include individual-level outcomes. For example, the work of Venkatesh et al. (2018) on IT project risk control examined individual-level outcomes, including job performance and psychological stress, in a multilevel model. Future research could investigate the role of team personality in reinforcing or eroding the effect of process controls on individual outcomes. Through multilevel modeling, future work could also incorporate individual-level variables, including personality, as predictors into the model. It can be readily seen that personality variables, which are based on individual-level reports, can be incorporated into a multilevel understanding. Such work will help us gain a holistic understanding of IT risk controls at both the individual and team levels.

## Conclusion

There have been few studies of the role of team personality in IT projects. However, “the ability to achieve high IT project performance is contingent on the influence of technology, people and process” (Maruping et al., 2019, p. 152). The complex interactions among technical IT project risks, IT project process controls, and team personality examined here highlight the critical role of team personality in contributing to IT project success, as team personality may not only reinforce (or weaken) the effectiveness of process controls but may also serve as a substitute for these same controls—and to a different extent depending on the technical risks.
