# PERFORMANCE OF ALGORITHMIC MODELS AND SENSITIVITY TO CROWD CHARACTERISTICS: EVIDENCE FROM CROSS-PLATFORM POSTING<sup>1</sup>

Qianzhou Du Faculty of Business for Science and Technology, School of Management University of Science and Technology of China, Hefei, CHINA {qianzhoudu@ustc.edu.cn}

Xiaohui Zhang Sheldon B. Lubar College of Business, University of Wisconsin–Milwaukee Milwaukee, WI, U.S.A. {xhzhang@uwm.edu}

Zhongju Zhang

W. P. Carey School of Business, Arizona State University Tempe, AZ, U.S.A. {zhongju.zhang@asu.edu}

We examine the effects of crowd characteristics on crowd value, which is measured by the improvement in the power to predict stock volatility using crowd-generated content. Leveraging a natural platformwide event that changes the crowd compositions of S&P 500 stock discussions, we found empirical evidence that content from a larger crowd size is associated with a higher crowd value. Moreover, the magnitude of the effect of crowd size on crowd value decreases with increased diversity of the crowd’s background and the independence of the crowd’s opinions. Additionally, we found that crowd values derived using various machine learning algorithms exhibit different sensitivities to these crowd characteristics. Algorithms that can handle interrelated observations (i.e., non-independent) and potential nonlinear relationships among crowd-generated content are more robust in performance than algorithms that cannot. We discuss the mechanisms that drive these findings and highlight the implications of crowd diversity and crowd independence on model performances when analyzing crowdgenerated content.

Keywords: Crowd-generated content, crowd characteristics, crowd value, algorithmic model, platform decoupling

## Introduction

Social media platforms and crowd-sourced content on such platforms have received significant attention from academia and industry. Surowiecki (2005) argues that the data/information from a diverse collection of independently deciding individuals, once aggregated, can lead to better decisions (i.e., the wisdom of crowds). In recent years, with the rapid development of algorithmic models, firms have increasingly turned to crowdgenerated data (often bounded by a shared interest or purpose)

on social media platforms for insights. Such data help firms in decision-making and offer firms a competitive edge (Liu et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2020). According to a recent survey from McKinsey, 58% of firms have adopted algorithmic models for at least one of their business functions, and 63% of these firms got more than 5% revenue increases from such adoptions (Balakrishnan et al., 2020).

Despite ample evidence of using crowd-generated data to improve decision-making, there are still concerns about the reliability and stability of such data’s value for algorithms. Bensoussan et al. (2009) argued that model drifts due to input (data) change are a critical threat to the value of algorithmic models. A lack of understanding of the input data and the underlying data generation process could even lead to negative values from implementing algorithmic models (Adomavicius & Yang, 2022; Fang et al., 2013). Microsoft’s artificial intelligence chatbot Tay is one example of algorithmic misbehavior, caused by the feeding of inappropriate crowd-generated text/voice inputs (Schuetz & Venkatesh, 2020). From the human-algorithm interaction perspective, research has shown that algorithm performance is susceptible to human biases embedded in data inputs (Ahsen et al., 2019). Human bias in individual rating behavior tends to be influenced by the herding behavior of the crowd the individual belongs to (Muchnik et al., 2013).

While the wisdom of the crowd literature (Surowiecki, 2005) has shown that an efficient market aggregation mechanism can smooth out individual biases and differences in ratings, leading to more accurate predictions and better problemsolving, the accuracy and sensitivity of the aggregated opinion value remain unclear when the aggregation mechanism is not explicitly defined. This often occurs when complex machine learning and deep learning models are used instead of simple arithmetic means, geometric means, medians, or marketclearing prices. The challenge is further compounded by the fact that the crowd-generated data is typically unstructured and high-dimensional, necessitating the use of complex algorithmic models. Additionally, it is unclear how the aggregated value produced by algorithmic models from a crowd responds to changes in crowd characteristics.

In this paper, we examine the performance of algorithmic models in aggregating unstructured data generated by various crowds and explore how these models respond to three key crowd characteristics: size, diversity, and independence. Our analysis evaluates the aggregated predictive value of stock discussions from a financial social media platform. In the main analysis, we measured predictive value (crowd value) by the improvement in algorithmic model performance for predicting stock volatility using crowd-generated content.<sup>2</sup> We implemented six algorithmic models, ranging from simpler ones (naive Bayes, linear model, and panel vector auto regression) to more complex ones (XGBoost, random forest, and convolutional neural networks).

To establish empirical evidence, we assembled a unique panel dataset that combines discussions of various stocks on the StockTwits platform, detailed user profiles, users’ following/follower networks on StockTwits, and stock performance information. To test our hypotheses, we leveraged a natural experiment setting. When StockTwits was launched in 2009, it integrated with Twitter’s application programming interface (API) to allow Twitter users to automatically share their posts to the StockTwits platform using a cashtag (a feature similar to Twitter hashtags, designed to organize stock-related content). StockTwits decided to discontinue this feature on 3/1/2013. This policy change was at the platform level and was exogenous at the crowd level. The exogenous shock changed the number of users who participated in stock discussions on the StockTwits platform and allowed us to compare the differences in the predictive value of the content generated by various crowds before and after the exogenous shock.

This study employs a two-stage research design. In the first stage, we performed text mining and implemented various algorithmic models to measure the predictive value of StockTwits discussions in forecasting stock volatility. In the second stage, we analyzed the relationship between crowd characteristics and the predictive value of corresponding crowd-generated content. Leveraging the decoupling between Twitter and StockTwits, we used a difference-indifferences (DID) analysis to establish causal evidence. Our results show that content from larger crowds is associated with higher crowd value, driven by error reduction and enhanced signal coverage. However, the effect of crowd size on crowd value diminishes as crowd diversity and opinion independence increase. While early studies (e.g., Hong et al., 2020; Robert & Romero, 2017) identified positive associations between crowd characteristics and crowd performance, they lacked robust identification strategies and did not investigate the marginal effect of crowd size. Additionally, we examined how algorithmic models respond to changes in crowd size, finding that predictive values extracted by simple algorithmic models are more sensitive to crowd size variations than those from complex models. This difference is largely due to the models’ underlying assumptions about data distributions and linearity.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. The next section summarizes related literature and develops our hypotheses. Following that, we describe our research contexts and outline the procedures for processing data and constructing important variables. We then discuss the research methods, identification strategies, estimation results, and robustness checks. Finally, the concluding section provides the theoretical and practical implications of this study.

Related Literature and Hypotheses Development

## Related Literature

This study builds on the wisdom of the crowd literature, which posits that the collective judgment of many often exceeds that of individual experts (Surowiecki, 2005). Previous studies (e.g., Becker et al., 2022; Da & Huang, 2020) have primarily focused on traditional settings where simple aggregation methods, such as the arithmetic mean or market-clearing prices, are applied to structured and well-defined individual data to reach a consensus estimate. However, a common critique of crowd wisdom is that knowledge of others’ estimates or ratings can narrow the diversity of opinions, potentially undermining the accuracy of the collective judgment (Lorenz et al., 2011). This issue is particularly prevalent online due to social influence and herding behaviors. Therefore, it is crucial to examine how other crowd characteristics, such as diversity and independence, in addition to crowd size, affect the accuracy of the collective wisdom.

Our study also relates to the growing research on algorithmic models that use crowd-generated data in information systems (IS). Incorporating such data into algorithms has proven valuable in various contexts, including predicting product sales (Cui et al., 2018; Song et al., 2019), discovering product defects (Abrahams et al., 2015), diagnosing mental health issues (Chau et al., 2020), detecting misinformation (Wei et al., 2022), and analyzing risk/return in financial markets (Chen et al., 2024; Oh & Sheng, 2011). Additionally, the volume, sentiment, and disagreement index of online content—such as social media posts, blogs, and message boards—have been linked to future market performance, including returns (Chen et al., 2014; Oh & Sheng, 2011; Sul et al., 2017; Xie et al., 2020), volatility, and trading volume (Antweiler & Frank, 2004; Cookson & Niessner, 2020). While these studies stop short of investigating the effects of crowd characteristics, they guide our choice of algorithmic models and methods for measuring crowd value.

Another closely related research area examines how crowd characteristics impact the aggregated content generated by crowds. Most studies, however, focus on the direct effects of a single crowd characteristic on content co-creation platforms like Wikipedia. For instance, they explore the influence of crowd size on contributions (Zhang & Zhu, 2011), diversity on productivity (Ren et al., 2016), and independence on viewership (Ransbotham et al., 2012). Two separate studies, Hong et al. (2020) and Robert and Romero (2017), identified positive associations between crowd size, diversity, and independence with crowd performance. However, both studies lacked robust identification strategies and did not formally investigate how different algorithmic models respond to changes in crowd size.

Additionally, Robert and Romero (2017) attempted to argue that crowd diversity positively moderates the relationship between crowd size and crowd performance, while Hong et al. (2020) attempted to argue that crowd size positively moderates the relationship between crowd diversity and crowd performance. Although both studies identified positive interaction effects in their analyses, a closer examination revealed that the authors primarily focused on the main effects between crowd characteristics and crowd performance. In contrast, we propose that the marginal effect of crowd size on crowd value diminishes as crowd diversity and independence increase. Using a natural experiment, we empirically demonstrated this diminishing effect. Furthermore, we investigated how human factors can influence model performance, revealing that simple algorithmic models are more sensitive to crowd composition.

## Hypotheses Development

## Crowd Characteristics and Predictive Value

A large crowd size is essential for generating informative content with predictive value for algorithmic models (Davis-Stober et al., 2014). A large crowd helps reduce errors by “hedging” the biases of different individuals. In a large crowd, incorrect information from independent individuals can cancel out through randomness, allowing the correct information to emerge due to the “gravity” of truth (Page, 2008). Smaller crowds are more susceptible to the influence of bias, extremity, and inaccuracy, while larger crowds can mitigate these issues (Davis-Stober et al., 2015). Content generated by a crowd is a mix of bias, noise, and valuable information, including original insights, intentional signals, and interpretations of external signals (Xie et al., 2020). Satopää et al. (2021) found that larger crowds produce less biased and noisy content while offering more predictive information. Similarly, Saha et al. (2021) demonstrated that integrating neural decision signals from multiple brains improves visual search performance, with accuracy increasing as crowd size grows from 1 to 17. Thus, due to the law of large numbers, a larger crowd size benefits algorithmic models by filtering out noise and highlighting valuable signals, preventing distortions caused by biased inputs.

From a resource-based perspective, a large crowd can overcome the attention limitations faced by individuals or small groups by capturing a broader range of signals and factors relevant to the problem at hand (Wagner & Suh, 2014). Tausczik et al. (2017) found that in Q&A communities, larger contributor size, topic audience size, and question audience size were all positively associated with solution quality. They attributed this to the greater likelihood of finding well-suited and diverse experts in a larger crowd. A large crowd is also more resilient to potential information loss, which is a significant threat to information quality (Lukyanenko et al., 2014). Additionally, the increased capacity of a large crowd reduces the likelihood of “false negative” errors, as valuable information is more likely to be expressed or interpreted multiple times, minimizing the chances of it being overlooked (Mollick & Nanda, 2016). This comprehensive signal coverage enhances the value of content for predictions. Furthermore, a larger crowd size improves content quality, as contributors are often motivated by reputation or imagerelated factors; the larger the crowd, the stronger these motivations become (Zhang & Zhu, 2011). Members in a larger crowd will have more image motivations, and such incentives drive individuals to put more effort into their contributions, leading to higher prediction accuracy (Qiu & Kumar, 2017). High-quality information is essential for further content utilization, such as serving as inputs for algorithmic models (Lukyanenko et al., 2014) In sum, a large crowd size helps mitigate individual biases, covers a wider range of signals, and motivates high-quality contributions. Therefore, we hypothesize:

## H1: Content from a larger crowd size is associated with a higher predictive value.

We further argue that the strength of the above relationship (H1) hinges on crowd diversity and crowd independence, two essential and distinct characteristics highlighted in the wisdom of the crowd literature (Surowiecki, 2005). Crowd diversity encompasses the varied perspectives and interpretations that crowd members bring to the target of interest (Page, 2008; Davis-Stober et al., 2015), while crowd independence measures the extent to which each crowd member can make decisions and judgments free from the influence of others (Da & Huang, 2020; Surowiecki, 2005). When crowd diversity is high, the effect of increasing crowd size on crowd value may be diminished. In a highly diverse crowd, it is likely that a wide range of unique perspectives and insights are already presented (Ren et al., 2016). As the crowd increases in size, additional members are less likely to contribute genuinely new or unique information. Beyond a certain point, as the crowd expands, additional inputs tend to become repetitive, adding little new value to the predictions. This leads to a saturation effect and diminishing returns on new perspectives, as insights may already be reflected by others in the diverse group. Additionally, when diverse crowd members bring highly varied information, the crowd reaches a level of signal coverage more quickly. With greater diversity, the crowd does not need as many members to filter out noise and achieve error cancellation (Xie et al., 2020); thus, increasing crowd size adds less value to the accuracy of the crowd-generated content. Finally, the crowd’s value lies more in the quality and uniqueness of contributions rather than the quantity of contributors. In a highly diverse crowd, the aggregation of fewer but varied perspectives produces an efficient and balanced view of market trends, so expanding crowd size adds minimal value. In sum, a smaller, diverse crowd provides much of the error reduction, robustness, and richness in perspectives that a larger crowd size might otherwise offer, therefore diminishing the marginal impact of crowd size on crowd value as diversity increases.

## H2: The magnitude of the effect of crowd size on crowd value decreases when crowd diversity increases.

Similarly, when crowd members are highly independent, the effect of crowd size on crowd value is diminished. Independent members make decisions based on their own analysis and mental models rather than following the majority opinion. This independence helps decrease the risk of herd behavior, which can distort predictions and make the crowd less reliable. With minimal herding, each contribution is more likely to be unique and more likely to offer fresh perspectives, as members are less likely to be influenced by dominant or external voices (Da & Huang, 2020; Lorenz et al., 2011). The overall crowd’s insights thus remain robust and reflective of true market conditions even if the crowd size is smaller. Therefore, the need to increase crowd size for higher stability and robustness in prediction is reduced. Moreover, with increased independence, crowd members are less prone to amplify each other’s views or fall into similar thought patterns, reducing the echo chamber effect that can make additional members redundant. As a result, the crowd gains significant value from independent insights, even at smaller sizes, and the marginal benefit of adding more members decreases. In sum, when crowd members are highly independent, they bring unique perspectives that reduce redundancy, resist herd behavior, and lower the risk of the echo chamber effect. This allows a smaller independent crowd to produce valuable and stable predictions without the need for a larger group. Consequently, as crowd independence increases, the incremental value of adding more members diminishes. Hence, we hypothesize:

## H3: The magnitude of the effect of crowd size on crowd value decreases when crowd independence increases.

## Model Sensitivity to Crowd Size Change

In addition to the link between crowd size and the predictive value of content, different algorithmic models may respond with varying sensitivities to changes in crowd size, depending on their data assumptions and processing capabilities. Simple models with stricter assumptions, such as cross-sectional independence and linearity, tend to be more sensitive to crowd size changes compared to more complex models like machine learning and deep learning models (Sigmund & Ferstl, 2021).

For example, panel data models often assume that units (individuals in the crowd) are independent of each other. Violating this independence can lead to inconsistent parameter estimates, causing significant prediction shifts even with slight data changes (Ursu & Duchesne, 2009). Similarly, the linearity assumption in simple models makes them more vulnerable to data changes; a misspecification, such as the absence of interaction terms or higher-order relationships, can disproportionately affect the model’s fit and predictions (Inoue & Kilian, 2005). In contrast, more complex models like machine learning and deep learning, which don’t rely on such assumptions, are better equipped to handle data dependencies and nonlinear relationships. As a result, these models may maintain more stable predictive value despite changes in crowd characteristics. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

H4: Simple algorithmic models are more sensitive to changes in crowd size than complex models.

## Research Context and Data

In this section, we first introduce the research context, followed by the methods used to extract and aggregate variables from multiple sources.

## StockTwits

Our research context is StockTwits, a Twitter-like social media platform launched in 2009. StockTwits is designed for investors to share ideas about financial assets such as stocks, foreign exchange markets, and cryptocurrencies. Content on StockTwits is organized using cashtags (similar to Twitter hashtags), allowing users to tag their opinions on specific financial assets. As a result, all discussions on StockTwits can be linked to the mentioned asset. Figure 1 shows users’ discussions on the platform. StockTwits is one of the most popular finance-focused social media platforms, with over 3 million active members as of 2020.<sup>3</sup>

Several factors make StockTwits an ideal context for exploring the predictive value of crowd-generated content. First, StockTwits is focused on finance, a domain inherently oriented toward the future, making it more suitable for examining the predictive potential of crowd-generated content than more general platforms like Twitter (now X). Research has shown that StockTwits can enhance the predictions of stock performance (Deng et al., 2018; Oh & Sheng, 2011). Second, compared to other finance-focused social media platforms like SeekingAlpha, StockTwits captures the opinions of a broader base of investors. Finally, because discussions are organized by cashtags,<sup>4</sup> we can clearly define the boundaries of crowds, allowing us to study the impact of various crowd-level characteristics.

We supplemented this data with each stock’s financial performance information from Yahoo! Finance and CompStat.

## Data Process and Variables

In the first stage of our analysis, we measured the added value of StockTwits discussions for predicting stock volatility. Stock volatility, or realized volatility, is defined as the standard deviation of a stock’s return over a period. We calculated weekly volatility for each stock. For stock i, the abnormal return on day $t ^ { \prime } ,$ denoted $A R _ { i t ^ { \prime } }$ is the stock’s return minus the market return $R _ { m t ^ { \prime } }$ . The weekly volatility $V _ { i t }$ was then calculated as the standard deviation of $A R _ { i t ^ { \prime } }$ over week t (McAleer & Medeiros, 2008).

We used several common financial indicators to create a baseline prediction for stock volatility. The cumulative abnormal return $C A R _ { i t }$ is the total of stock i’s abnormal return for week t. The trading volume $V o l u m e _ { i t }$ represents the total trading volume for stock i during week t. ## Impacts of Crowd Characteristics on Content’s Predictive Value

## Causal Identification Strategy

We investigated how crowd characteristics affect the predictive value of crowd-generated content. We used the decoupling of Twitter and StockTwits as a natural experiment to gather causal evidence. As illustrated in Figure 4, before the decoupling on March 1, 2013, users could easily participate in StockTwits discussions via Twitter. By January 1, 2013, about 25% of StockTwits posts were made through Twitter, with this percentage varying across different stock discussions. After the decoupling, users had to log in directly through the StockTwits website to post content.

The decoupling increased participation costs for some users, leading to a loss of users. Since the decoupling was a platformwide change, variations in user counts across different crowds are considered exogenous. Before the change, the number of Twitter users varied widely among stock discussions, with weekly averages ranging from 3.49 to 541.11 users and percentages from 1.55% to 45.32%. Crowds with higher preshock Twitter user percentages were more affected and lost more users. We classified crowds into treatment and control groups based on their average percentage of Twitter users, with the top quarter (weekly average Twitter user percentage over 36.28%) as the treatment group. This group lost an average of 42.43 (12.8%) more users than the control group. Details on the impact of the decoupling on crowd characteristics are provided in Appendix C, which shows that the primary effect was a reduction in crowd size.

We used the exogenous change in crowd size to perform a DID analysis with a two-way fixed effects model. We created a dummy variable, Treat , which is 1 for post-shock observations in the treatment groups, and 0 for other observations. We estimated the following DID model; $\alpha _ { i }$ captures the stock crowd fixed effects, absorbing the treatment group’s effect; $\delta _ { t }$ captures the time-fixed effects, absorbing the effect of the shock. Thus $\beta$ measures causal effects resulting from the change in crowd size.

$$
D V _ {i t} = \alpha_ {i} + \delta_ {t} + \beta T r e a t _ {i t} + \varepsilon_ {i t}\tag{1}
$$

We also examined how crowd diversity and independence affected the impact of the decoupling on the predictive value of StockTwits discussions. We included these factors as moderators in our model, which is outlined below:

$$
\begin{array}{c} D V _ {i t} = \alpha_ {i} + \delta_ {t} + \beta T r e a t _ {i t} + \beta_ {2} C r o w d D i v e r s i t y _ {i t} \\ + \beta_ {3} C r o w d I n d e p e n d e n c e _ {i t} + \beta_ {4} T r e a t _ {i t} * C r o w d D i v e r s i t y _ {i t} \\ + \beta_ {5} T r e a t _ {i t} * C r o w d I n d e p e n d e n c e _ {i t} + \varepsilon_ {i t} \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

## Difference-in-Difference Analysis

The estimation results for Equation (1) are reported in Table 3. Each column represents a different predictive model, with Panels A and B showing different feature sets used for the model inputs. The estimated coefficients indicate the effect of the platform decoupling on the value of crowd content for each model. All coefficients are significantly negative, ranging from -0.112 (DV: TextNB) to -0.048 (DV: SentiCNN). This suggests that the predictive value of content from the treatment group crowds decreased more, compared to the control group crowds. For example, with the CNN model (TextCNN), the predictive value of content features (such as sentiment features and text vectors) dropped 0.076 more for treatment group content compared to control group content. This decrease represents 19.638% of the average TextCNN and 3.677% of the average stock volatility. The significant reduction in predictive value for the treatment group confirms H1, showing that a larger crowd size is associated with more valuable predictive content.

We further examined how crowd diversity and independence affected the relationship between reduced crowd size and content value across different models. The results of this analysis are shown in Table 4. All interaction terms are positive and significant, supporting H2 and H3. The positive interactions indicate that a reduction in crowd size leads to a smaller drop in crowd value when the crowd maintains high levels of diversity and independence. Figure 5 depicts the marginal effects of the treatment on crowd value, estimated using TextCNN. Notably, when crowd diversity or independence exceeds certain thresholds (approximately 1.26 and 0.63, respectively), the marginal effect of crowd size may become negligible. This underscores the critical role of crowd composition, with diversity and independence acting as substitutes for crowd size in driving value creation.

## Robustness Checks

## Parallel Trend Assumption: Relative Time Model

The parallel trend assumption is crucial for DID analysis, as it requires that, without the exogenous shock, the treatment and control groups would follow similar trends over time (Angrist & Pischke, 2008). If this assumption is violated, the comparison between the groups for causal identification becomes invalid. A common method to test this assumption is the relative time model, which checks for heterogeneous trends before the treatment (Autor, 2003; Li et al., 2021). The relative time model uses time dummies to capture the temporal distance between each week and the platform decoupling week, as shown below:

$$
D V _ {i t} = \alpha_ {i} + \delta_ {t} + \sum_ {j = - 8} ^ {j = 7} \beta_ {j} T _ {j} + \varepsilon_ {i t}\tag{3}
$$

??<sub>??</sub> represents the relative time dummies, $\alpha _ { \mathrm { I } }$ captures the stock fixed effects, and $\delta _ { t }$ captures the time-fixed effects. We set the week before the platform decoupling (T = −1) as the baseline period. According to the parallel trend assumption, the time dummies before the decoupling should be insignificant. Figure 6 shows the impact on value measurements for different feature inputs across three models. The figure reveals that most leading dummies are insignificant, indicating no significant trend difference between the control and treatment groups.

The relative time model supports the parallel trend assumption and reveals the dynamic effects of the platform decoupling. As shown in Figure 6, the impact on StockTwits discussions’ predictive value was strongest in the first few weeks and gradually diminished over time. The predictive value of the treatment group discussions gradually recovered, with coefficients becoming insignificant about six weeks after the decoupling. This pattern suggests that the main effects in our previous DID analyses were primarily driven by short-term impacts. This dynamic pattern likely stems from the sudden loss of users. As StockTwits was a fast-growing platform in 2013, the reduced crowd size would have naturally recovered over time with the influx of new users, potentially explaining the observed trend. A similar pattern is evident when crowd size was used as the dependent variable (Appendix C). Overall, the relative time model analysis reinforces the conclusion that a larger crowd size enhances the predictive value of the content.

## Alternative Specification: Count-based Treatment

In the main analysis, we defined treatment and control groups based on the proportion of Twitter users in each crowd before the platform decoupling. Larger crowds might have had more Twitter users in absolute numbers but a smaller proportion. To ensure that this didn’t bias our results, we redefined the groups based on the absolute number of Twitter users in each crowd. We sorted the crowds by Twitter user count and selected the top quarter (those with more than 12 weekly average Twitter users) as the treatment group. This count-based group overlapped significantly (73.81%, or 62 out of 84) with the original treatment group. We then reran the DID analysis using this new definition; the results in Table 6 remain consistent with our previous findings.

## Alternative Specification: Continuous Treatment

In the main analysis, we used a binary treatment specification, which aligns with previous IS research (Huang et al., 2020; Kummer et al., 2020). However, different cutoff points could have potentially affected the results. To check the sensitivity of our findings, we estimated a continuous treatment DID model. We used the predecoupling weekly average count of Twitter users as a proxy for user loss and replaced the binary Treat variable in Equation (1) with the log-transformed user loss count. For pre-decoupling observations, the user loss count was zero, while for post-decoupling, it reflected the weekly average count of Twitter users in each stock crowd. This approach captures the varying impact of platform decoupling across different crowds.

The results, presented in Table 7, are consistent with the binary DID analysis. They also quantify the impact of crowd size. For example, in the TextCNN model, a 1% decrease in crowd size led to a 0.00033 reduction in prediction value (absolute error reduction). ## Alternative Sample: Coarsened Exact Match

Our results demonstrate that crowd size significantly influences the predictive value of crowd-generated content and that the composition and structure of the crowd moderate this effect. To further assess whether these results were sensitive to the sample used, we incorporated matching techniques. If certain firms or industries were more frequently discussed and their stock volatilities were easier to predict, our findings could have been biased. Although this concern was mitigated by the inclusion of stock fixed effects, we used matching methods to create a more balanced subsample. Specifically, we matched stocks in the treatment and control groups based on sector, industry, market capitalization, and average weekly post and user counts before the platform decoupling.

Our original sample consisted of 320 stock crowds across 19 main sectors and 130 industries. Given the high-dimensional nature of these variables, coarsened exact matching (CEM) was appropriate. After matching, our sample included 115 stock crowds, with 45 in the treatment group. The differences between the treatment and control groups in terms of log-transformed user count (3.863 vs. 3.681; p = 0.407) and post count (4.865 vs. 4.680; p = 0.522) were not significant. This matched sample excluded some influential stocks like Apple (AAPL) and Google (GOOG). We then reran the DID analysis on this CEM-matched sample. The results, shown in Table 8, are consistent with our earlier findings. We also replicated the moderation analysis on the matched sample; the results align with those from the main analysis (see Appendix E, Table E1).

## Alternative Measurements: Crowd Diversity, Crowd Independence, and Stock Volatility

Previously, we measured crowd diversity using the average Euclidean distance of user attributes. This method captures differences across all user attributes. To test the robustness of our results, we also used Blau’s Index, a common measure of diversity in sociology. The Blau’s Index formula is $1 - \textstyle \sum p _ { k } ^ { 2 }$ where p<sub>k</sub> represents the proportion of each user type in the crowd. We defined each combination of user attributes as a unique type. The Blau’s Index ranged from 0 to 0.9883, and its correlation with our previous Euclidean distance measure was 0.936. We redid the DID analysis using Blau’s Index to measure crowd diversity. The results are presented in Appendix F, Table F1. While the findings are generally consistent, we observe that the interaction coefficient between crowd diversity and treatment became insignificant in the RF model, though it remained in the expected direction.

To verify the robustness of our findings on crowd independence, we used network degree centrality as an alternative metric. The formula for degree centrality is: $\begin{array} { r } { D e g r e e C e n t r a l i t y = \sum _ { v \in G } \frac { | d e g ( V _ { m a x } ) - d e g ( v ) | } { ( | V | - 1 ) ( | V | - 2 ) } } \end{array}$ , where |V| denotes the total number of nodes in the network $G , d e g ( V _ { m a x } )$ denotes the largest in-degree of all nodes, and deg(v) denotes the indegree of node v. This measure reflects the disparity between the highest degree and each node’s degree. A high degree centrality indicates lower crowd independence. ## Additional Checks

Our causal analysis depends on changes in crowd size due to platform decoupling. To ensure that our results were not due to Twitter users predicting better than StockTwits users, we used the observations before the platform decoupling and included the proportion of Twitter users in a panel regression with TextCNN and crowd characteristics. The coefficient for Twitter user proportion is insignificant, suggesting no significant difference between Twitter and StockTwits users in predicting content. Additionally, we checked for the impact of time-varying market factors and found that they did not alter our results. Details are presented in Appendix G.

To test our hypothesis about why different models reacted differently to crowd size changes, we analyzed how well our assumptions held for each model. We checked if data independence and linearity assumptions were met using the Durbin-Watson (DW) test (Blattberg, 1973). We ran linear regressions of stock volatility on sentiment scores for each stock crowd and used the DW test to check for independence. A significant DW statistic would mean that the data failed this assumption. We found that 111 crowds met the independence assumption, while 209 did not. We also tested if sentiment scores affect stock volatility linearly by adding squared and cubic terms to the regressions and performing F-tests. This showed that 73 crowds met the linearity assumption, while 247 did not. We then repeated our main analysis for four subgroups based on these tests. The results, shown in Table 9, indicate that when both assumptions are met, all models exhibit similar responses to changes in crowd size. However, when these assumptions are violated, the simple models (PVAR, linear, and naive Bayes) demonstrate stronger reactions. This finding supports the notion that model sensitivity is influenced by data characteristics.

## Discussion and Conclusion

Advances in big data and machine learning have made mining crowd-generated data valuable for many organizations. However, assuming that all data is inherently beneficial, without considering the composition of its source, can limit its effectiveness (Fang et al., 2013). This paper investigates how crowd characteristics—such as size, diversity, and independence—affect the predictive value of crowdgenerated content. We evaluated various content features, including sentiment and text vectors, across several algorithmic models for predicting stock volatility and returns. Inspired by the wisdom of the crowd literature, we hypothesized that these characteristics impact predictive value and that the performance of algorithmic models is sensitive to them. Using StockTwits, where users discuss various stocks, we first assessed the predictive value of the content and then leveraged the Twitter-StockTwits decoupling as an exogenous shock to empirically test our hypotheses.

Our analysis yields several key insights. First, a decrease in crowd size reduces while an increase enhances the predictive value of the content. However, this reduction is less pronounced for crowds with higher diversity and independence. We also found that knowledge diversity is more important than functional diversity. Additionally, we found that complex models are less sensitive to changes in crowd characteristics compared to simpler models. This difference is due to varying requirements of and assumptions about data distributions and the data generation process. Overall, these findings offer valuable theoretical and practical insights.

## Implications for Research

Our study contributes to the wisdom of the crowd literature by providing theoretical insights into the interplay between crowd characteristics and their collective predictive value.

Prior research has consistently demonstrated that larger crowds are generally more effective at aggregating signals and cancelling out individual errors, leading to improved collective predictions. However, highly diverse crowds can offer a wide range of complementary and nonredundant perspectives, partially substituting for the benefits typically associated with larger crowd sizes. Effective aggregation relies not only on diversity but also on independence. When individuals act independently, their perspectives are less likely to influence one another, resulting in uncorrelated errors that are more effectively cancelled out. This dynamic further reduces the reliance on large crowd size, as the marginal benefits of additional members diminish due to decreasing returns in error reduction. Overall, we emphasize that crowd size, diversity, and independence are interconnected factors that collectively shape crowd value. The diminishing marginal effect of crowd size highlights the importance of designing crowds that prioritize diversity and independence rather than simply maximizing size.

Furthermore, this research enhances our understanding of the value of crowd-generated data in algorithmic models. While prior studies have primarily focused on extracting and quantifying the value of such data to improve model performance (Chau et al., 2020; Song et al., 2019; Wei et al., 2022), our study shifts attention to the human factors underpinning this value. Although some research (e.g., Robert & Romero, 2017; Hong et al., 2020) has explored the connection between crowd characteristics and crowd performance, we formally investigated the interactions among these characteristics and highlighted the diminishing returns of crowd size on crowd value as diversity and/or independence increase. Additionally, we examined how various algorithm models respond to these crowd characteristics. This aligns with recent calls to explore the role of human factors in algorithmic applications (Adomavicius & Yang, 2022).

Finally, while prior research has often focused on scalar features and simple aggregation methods (Cui et al., 2018; Hong et al., 2020; Song et al., 2019), our study assesses the value of crowd-generated content using advanced algorithmic models and various content features, such as sentiment analysis and BERT-generated text vectors. By examining how algorithms respond to variations in human factors, we offer a novel perspective on human-algorithm interactions. In contrast to previous studies exploring human responses to algorithms (Fuegener et al., 2021), we investigated how algorithms’ sensitivity to human factors influences model performance. This perspective is particularly relevant for guiding the selection and curation of training datasets for large language models (Bender et al., 2021), emphasizing the need to prioritize diversity and independence in training data and models rather than merely increasing data volume or model size. Ultimately, our findings highlight the importance of understanding all aspects of algorithmic applications, including the data generation process, crowd characteristics, feature types, and model selection.

## Implications for Practice

This study offers several practical implications in the areas of data source evaluation, algorithmic model selection, strategic crowdsourcing practices, and platform ecosystem strategies. It provides actionable recommendations for firms seeking to optimize data collection and usage in an era of rapidly accelerating information generation. Collecting all available data is neither feasible nor efficient. Instead, data sourced from diverse and independent contributors can significantly improve model performance, even when the overall dataset size is limited. This underscores the importance of balancing dataset volume with quality. For instance, when selecting crowdsourced data for predictive tasks, firms should prioritize crowds with diverse taskrelated expertise and establish mechanisms to preserve independence in individual contributions. This approach maximizes data value while minimizing unnecessary computational costs associated with processing redundant or highly correlated information.

Additionally, this study highlights the importance of selecting appropriate algorithmic models based on the nature of the crowd-generated data. Complex models capable of handling high-dimensional features, nonlinear relationships, and interdependencies—such as deep learning models or ensemble models—are better suited for extracting meaningful insights from imperfect or interrelated data. Firms should consider investing in such advanced models when working with crowds that lack complete independence or exhibit intricate internal relationships. These models ensure that even less-than-ideal data sources can yield actionable insights, thereby enhancing decision-making capabilities.

Our findings also highlight the need for firms to design effective crowdsourcing strategies. Beyond recruiting larger crowds, firms should actively foster diversity in crowd composition and encourage independence among contributors. For example, anonymizing contributors to reduce social influence or implementing incentives that attract individuals with varied backgrounds and perspectives could improve crowd performance. By optimizing these characteristics, firms can achieve higher predictive value from crowd-generated content while maintaining manageable crowd sizes and resource demands.

Finally, our research provides valuable insights for platforms adopting envelope strategies—where one platform allows users from another platform to seamlessly contribute content. For instance, StockTwits previously enabled Twitter users to post content directly on its platform. While this strategy can help smaller platforms grow quickly, it may lead to short-term challenges during the process of decoupling, such as an initial decline in predictive value due to a sudden loss of users. However, the subsequent recovery underscores the importance of cultivating a strong, organic user base. Platforms should focus on retaining unique users and ensuring that the crowd’s composition aligns with long-term strategic goals. Some strategies could include targeted outreach, incentives for participation, or features that encourage diverse content creation.

## Limitations and Future Directions

While our study offers valuable insights, we also acknowledge its limitations and suggest directions for future research. First, predicting the stock market is inherently challenging due to its complexity (D’Ecclesia & Clementi, 2019). Our study focuses on improving performance by incorporating features from crowd discussions, but there’s still room for refinement. Future research could develop new methods to enhance prediction accuracy. For our analysis of model sensitivities, we primarily considered models’ data requirements and assumptions. Future studies could explore other model attributes that may impact sensitivity. Finally, regarding crowd characteristics (size, diversity, and independence), our study leveraged the decoupling of two platforms, which significantly altered the number of participants in some stock discussions, allowing us to test the causal effect of crowd size on predictive value. However, we observed no significant changes in crowd diversity and independence before and after the policy change (see Appendix C), only variations in size across different crowds. Therefore, we limited our analysis to the moderating effects of diversity and independence. We encourage future research to explore the main causal effects and algorithm sensitivities related to these two characteristics. Such work could contribute to a unified theoretical framework on the roles and interactions of crowd size, diversity, and independence in the wisdom of the crowd literature.

## A1: Content Features and Prediction Models

The baseline features include four financial variables $( V _ { i t - 1 } , C A R _ { i t - 1 } , V o l u m e _ { i t - 1 } , V I X _ { t - 1 } )$ described earlier. The first set consists of sentiment features, as previous research has shown that post sentiment and sentiment disagreement can help predict stock market performance (Antweiler & Frank, 2004). We conducted sentiment analysis on each post using the Financial Sentiment Dictionary (LM Dictionary), a lexicon specifically designed for finance (Loughran & McDonald, 2011). To make predictions, we implemented several algorithmic models : naive Bayes (NB), linear models (OLS), panel vector autoregression (PVAR), XGBoost (XGB), random forest (RF), and convolutional neural network (CNN). ## A2: Evaluation of Crowd Content’s Predictive Value

We used walk-forward cross-validation to assess the performance of different models (Hyndman & Athanasopoulos, 2018). The out-of sample predictive value of StockTwits features was evaluated based on the performance of these models across 10 test splits. We measured performance using four traditional metrics: mean absolute error (MAE), mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), mean squared error (MSE), and root mean squared error (RMSE). To compare models with and without StockTwits features, we conducted paired t-tests for each metric against the baseline model.

Table A1 presents the results. Among the models, CNN outperformed all other models. This highlights the superiority of machine and deep learning in financial predictions. Adding sentiment features to the baseline model consistently reduced prediction errors across all models. Further, incorporating BERT-generated text vectors into RF and CNN models led to additional error reductions. Overall, these findings confirm the predictive value of StockTwits discussions in forecasting stock volatility in out-of-sample scenarios.

## Appendix H

## Mediation Analysis on Content Similarity and Topic Coverage

We used BERT-generated text vectors to measure content similarity between posts. For all posts about stock i during week t, we computed the cosine similarity between all pairs of posts and averaged these similarities to get the content similarity for that week (ContentSimilarity<sub>it</sub>). To assess topic coverage, we used the LDA method to identify seven topics: firm news, trading behaviors, financial report analysis, firm strategy, technology firms, market trends, and investor behaviors. We then calculated the number of topics covered in discussions about stock i during week t (TopicNum<sub>it</sub>).

We first examined how a decrease in crowd size affects content similarity and topic coverage. Our estimation of Equation (1) with ContentSimilarity<sub>it</sub> and TopicNum<sub>it</sub> as dependent variables shows that (1) a decrease in crowd size leads to higher content similarity (Column 1, Table H1), even when controlling for diversity and independence, (2) fewer covered topics are associated with a smaller crowd siz (Column 2), and (3) high content similarity is linked to lower predictive value, while more covered topics improve predictive value (Columns 3 and 4).

We further tested whether content similarity mediates the relationship between crowd size reduction and predictive value. Including content similarity and topic number in the model reduces the effect of crowd size reduction (Treat) from 0.076 to 0.048 (Column 6 compared to Column 5), indicating partial mediation. The causal mediation test (Table H2) shows that content similarity mediates about 36.1% of the effect of Treat on predictive value.
