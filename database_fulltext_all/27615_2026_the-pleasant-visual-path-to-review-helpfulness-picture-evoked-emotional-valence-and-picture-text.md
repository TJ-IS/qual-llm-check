# THE PLEASANT VISUAL PATH TO REVIEW HELPFULNESS: PICTURE-EVOKED EMOTIONAL VALENCE AND PICTURE-TEXT ALIGNMENT <sup>1</sup>

Yifan Yu HKU Business School, The University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong, CHINA {yifanyu@hku.hk}

Xinyao Wang and Jinghua Huang Research Center for Contemporary Management, School of Economics and Management Tsinghua University, Beijing, CHINA {wangxinyao13@tsinghua.org.cn} {huangjh@sem.tsinghua.edu.cn}

Yong Tan

Department of Information Systems and Operations Management, Michael G. Foster School of Business University of Washington, Seattle, WA, U.S.A. {ytan@uw.edu}

Viewing pictures evokes pleasant or unpleasant feelings (valence) and influences perceptions. How valence evoked by pictures in online reviews impacts reader perceptions of review helpfulness remains understudied. Based on affect-as-information theory, we propose that both picture-evoked emotional valence (PEvoV) and its alignment with text-expressed emotional valence (TExpV) exhibit a positive effect on perceived review helpfulness. A large-scale field test and a series of laboratory experiments support our hypotheses. The positive effects are partially mediated by conceptual processing fluency. Additionally, PEvoV is associated with various interpretable picture features. Our empirical strategy involves techniques of computer vision, deep learning, and econometrics. From an emotion-focused perspective, our work deepens the understanding of helpful reviews, contributes to the literature on picture-text interaction in reviews, and derives theoretical insight into underlying mechanisms. It offers practical implications for online review platform design and online reputation management.

Keywords: Review helpfulness, picture-evoked emotions, valence, picture-text alignment, conceptual processing fluency

## Introduction

Reading online reviews has become an integral part of customers’ purchase decision-making process. A recent survey showed that a significant proportion of customers (96.5%) tend to consider online reviews before making a purchase such as a movie ticket (Yu et al., 2023). Due to the large number of reviews available online, customers often require assistance in identifying the most helpful reviews. Review platforms have a vested interest in understanding what makes a review helpful, as such insight can enable them to develop mechanisms to identify and promote diagnostic review content or to establish guidelines to encourage the generation of helpful reviews (Yin et al., 2017). It is also essential for firms to estimate whether a review is likely to be deemed helpful. This knowledge can enable them to effectively respond to, repost, and highlight positive and potentially helpful reviews to attract new customers, and can also help them provide responses to negative but potentially helpful reviews in a way that mitigates their adverse effects on sales and online reputation (Song et al., 2019; Yu et al., 2023).

Accordingly, understanding what contributes to review helpfulness is an essential topic in the information systems (IS) literature on online word-of-mouth (see Jabr et al., 2020, for a review). Review helpfulness is defined as the extent to which a review is perceived by consumers as facilitating their purchasedecision process (Yin et al., 2014). A growing body of literature is dedicated to the affective “ingredients” of review helpfulness (Yin et al., 2014, 2017, 2021; Yu et al., 2023). While the importance of cognitive ingredients, including review depth and readability (Yin et al., 2016), is well established, affective ingredients can significantly impact perceived review helpfulness as well. Further, affective ingredients have been found to have similar or even higher effect sizes than cognitive ones, underscoring the economic significance of affect in online reviews (Yu et al., 2023). This supports the argument that affect is an essential element in IS research (Zhang, 2013). Valence, the degree to which an experience is perceived as pleasant or unpleasant, constitutes a fundamental dimension of affect (Russell & Barrett, 1999). Indeed, the literature on review helpfulness has predominantly focused on the writer’s expressed valence in texts (TExpV) (Yin et al., 2016; Yu et al., 2023), which impacts a reader’s cognitive inferences about the writer, thereby shaping review helpfulness (Yin et al., 2014, 2017). In contrast, we focus on a distinct pathway where the reader’s felt emotions influence their perception of review helpfulness. This is motivated by the picture modality. While the rich visual attributes of pictures often shape a reader’s valence by eliciting feelings of pleasantness or unpleasantness (Branco et al., 2023; Hou et al., 2023; Kurdi et al., 2016; You et al., 2016), the effects and mechanisms of picture-evoked emotional valence (PEvoV) and its interplay with text-expressed emotional valence (TExpV) on review helpfulness remain inadequately explored.

Building on literature on affect-as-information and cognitive processing (Ceylan et al., 2024; Yin et al., 2016; Yu et al., 2023; Zadra & Clore, 2011), we propose that PEvoV is positively associated with perceived review helpfulness. Further, the alignment between PEvoV and TExpV (picture-text valence alignment, for short) also exhibits a positive association with perceived review helpfulness. This positive association is partially mediated by conceptual processing fluency, “the ease of mental operations concerned with stimulus meaning and its relation to semantic knowledge structures” (Reber et al., 2004, p. 366). In our context, it is the ease of understanding the meaning or the message contained in the review (Storme et al., 2015; Yu et al., 2023).

To examine our predictions, we conducted a field test in Study 1 and a series of laboratory experiments in Study 2. Following the literature (Xiao et al., 2022; Yu et al., 2023), we focused on the experience goods (restaurant and hotel services) because reviews tend to be more impactful for experience than search goods (Huang et al., 2009). In Study 1, leveraging a large-scale dataset on restaurant reviews from dianping.com, the largest online review platform in China, we manually constructed an annotated set of PEvoV, trained deep learning models to predict it, and built an econometric model to estimate the associations of PEvoV and picture-text valence alignment with review helpfulness votes. To improve the interpretability of our deep learning models, add managerial value, and inform our experimental design, we explored the associations between interpretable picture features and PEvoV. In Study 2, we directly manipulated PEvoV and TExpV to verify the associations in Study 1 in a more controlled setting. We tested the generalizability of conclusions in Study 1 by extending our study to hotel reviews and to U.S./U.K. customers. More importantly, we demonstrated the mediating role of conceptual processing fluency.

Our work makes several theoretical contributions. First, prior studies have mainly focused on the impacts of a writer’s emotional expression on review helpfulness via a reader’s inferences about the writer (Xiao et al., 2018; Yin et al., 2014, 2017, 2021; Yu et al., 2023). In contrast, we shift the investigation from the writer’s expressed emotions (via text) to the reader’s emotions evoked by a different modality, namely pictures. Moreover, this work deepens the understanding of interpretable picture attributes that evoke emotions, enabling future researchers to manipulate picture-evoked emotions and understand their impacts in other contexts. Second, a cognitionbased alignment measure—specifically, picture-text semantic similarity—is typically the focus of the literature on reviews (Ceylan et al., 2024; Li & Xie, 2020; Xiao et al., 2022). Our work offers a perspective that is more nuanced than semantic similarity by providing a new emotion-focused lens, which contributes a novel dimension to the literature on emotions and picture-text interaction (Xiao et al., 2022): namely, the alignment between external affective cues (expressed valence in texts) and internal affective states (evoked valence in pictures). Third, we unveil the underlying mechanism, the mediating role of conceptual processing fluency. Moreover, the literature shows that conceptual processing fluency depends on content-context congruence (Peng et al., 2020), semantic congruence (Ceylan et al., 2024), and discrete emotional expressions (Yu et al., 2023). This work contributes a novel pathway to this literature: the review reader’s felt emotional valence.

Our work offers important managerial implications. First, by identifying new ingredients of helpful reviews, our work can be used by review platforms to advance their helpfulness prediction systems. Better prediction and understanding of review helpfulness, in turn, will lead to more accurate review recommendations and rankings. Second, review platforms can incorporate our findings in their review-generation guidelines and support systems to encourage more helpful reviews. Third, firms can incorporate the new ingredients we identify to better estimate, a priori, the extent to which a review will be deemed helpful, allowing managerial responses to reviews to be offered in a more timely and efficient manner.

## Literature Review

## Picture-Related Valence and Its Impact

Consistent with the literature on pictures and affect (Branco et al., 2023; Hou et al., 2023; You et al., 2016), PEvoV in this work refers to a viewer’s emotional valence evoked by pictures. Although valence expressed in text is well defined and has been amply studied (Malik & Hussain, 2017; Yin et al., 2014, 2017, 2021; Yu et al., 2023, in press), the concept of picture-expressed valence is not as clear when applied to review pictures of food, restaurants, or hotels, likely because writers may convey valence through the pictures they use only implicitly, and it is difficult to claim that readers always perceive the expressed valence. In contrast, at least 69 studies in the literature have focused on emotions evoked (rather than expressed) by pictures (see the review by Branco et al., 2023).

While the IS field has started to explore how picture characteristics influence perception (e.g., Shin et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2022), the impact of picture-related affect, particularly PEvoV, has received much less attention. One exception is the work by Hou et al. (2023), which explores “what image attributes evoke image emotions, and how image emotions are related to the success of charity fundraising projects” (p. 535). Outside the IS field, computer science researchers have mainly focused on building annotation sets for emotions evoked by pictures (Kurdi et al., 2016; You et al., 2016). Additionally, emotions evoked by pictures have been linked to responses to artwork and aesthetic appreciation in the art literature (Barry, 2006; Carroll, 2003). Therefore, it is valuable to understand interpretable picture attributes in reviews as antecedents of PEvoV and to study the association between PEvoV and review helpfulness.

## Understanding the Ingredients of Helpful Reviews

Helpful reviews exhibit a significant impact on purchase intention and product sales (Yin et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2023). IS researchers have been particularly interested in understanding the ingredients of helpful reviews (for a detailed review, see Jabr et al., 2020) because such an understanding can enable review platforms to identify and promote diagnostic reviews, develop guidelines for helpful review generation, and eventually improve user satisfaction (Mudambi & Schuff, 2010; Yin et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2023).

There are two categories of helpful review ingredients, affective and non-affective. Affect, which is a composite of valence and arousal, can significantly impact review helpfulness in review text (Mudambi & Schuff, 2010; Yin et al., 2014, 2017, 2021; Yu et al., 2023). In contrast to valence, arousal is the extent to which an experience is perceived as exciting or calm (Russell & Barrett, 1999). Non-affective ingredients include (1) contextual or content characteristics of reviews, such as review rankings, timing, length, font, language styles, readability, and quality (see Ceylan et al., 2024; Yin et al., 2016; Yu et al., 2023, for literature reviews), and (2) review writers’ characteristics, such as writers engagement (Ngo-Ye & Sinha, 2014) and writers’ impact (Huang et al., 2015). IS studies have argued that affective ingredients have similar or even higher effect sizes than nonaffective ingredients (Zhang, 2013), demonstrating the economic significance of affect in online reviews (Yu et al., 2023).

The current research thus contributes to the literature on affect and review helpfulness, particularly through the study of PEvoV. Early research on review helpfulness in the IS literature focused on numeric/star ratings (as a measure of valence) (Mudambi & Schuff, 2010). More recently, IS researchers have focused on TExpV because (1) review text contains much richer affective expression than ratings (Malik & Hussain, 2017; Yin et al., 2017; Yu et al., 2023), and (2) online review platforms suffer from rating inflation issues (see Aziz et al., 2022, for a review). Numeric ratings and TExpV are both affective cues of reviews (Zhang, 2013), and IS studies have found that affective cues in text/ratings impact review helpfulness via a reader’s cognitive evaluation, including confirmation bias (i.e., whether the affective cues are congruent with their prior belief) (Yin et al., 2016) and cognitive inferences about the writer (i.e., perceived writer’s rationality and cognitive effort) (Yin et al., 2014, 2017; Yu et al., 2023).

However, much less attention has been devoted to the reader’s induced affective state beyond their cognitive evaluation of affective cues. Relatedly, online reviews have begun to shift from being text-centric to being picture-centric (Xiao et al., 2022). Since viewing pictures often evokes pleasant or unpleasant feelings (Hou et al., 2023), from an affect-asinformation perspective, this evoked emotional valence may serve as an informational cue contributing to value judgments (i.e., review helpfulness in our context) (Clore & Huntsinger, 2007; Zadra & Clore, 2011; Zhang, 2013).

Pictures in reviews also have substantial predictive capability regarding review helpfulness, particularly when combined with review text (Xiao et al., 2022), motivating the emerging literature on picture-text interaction and review helpfulness (Ceylan et al., 2024; Xiao et al., 2022). One of the first causal studies on picture-text interaction in reviews showed that the semantic alignment (defined as the alignment between the objects depicted in the picture and those described in the text—e.g., the picture and the text might both describe a cup of coffee) can positively impact review helpfulness (Ceylan et al., 2024). Ceylan et al. (2024) note that picture-text alignment in reviews is a promising research area and call for “future research to investigate other dimensions on which text and photos can align, such as valence” (Ceylan et al., 2024, p. 7). Our emotion-focused perspective responds to this call by introducing a new dimension of valence alignment to the literature. Importantly, this valence alignment is different from simply describing the same object in two different ways (Ceylan et al., 2024); rather, the valence alignment we explore here concerns the interaction between an internal affective state (PEvoV) and external affective cues (TExpV). As Xiao et al. (2022) and Ceylan et al. (2024) argue, we can advance the understanding of review helpfulness if we shift the investigation from the reader’s cognitive evaluation of affective cues (i.e., TExpV) (Jabr et al., 2020; Yin et al., 2016) to their induced affective states (PEvoV) and the picture-text valence alignment. Our research also seeks to advance the emerging picture-text interaction and review helpfulness literature by opening new research avenues on the underlying mechanisms.

## Theoretical Foundation and Hypothesis Development

In this section, we theoretically associate PEvoV and picturetext valence alignment with perceived review helpfulness and discuss the mediating role of conceptual processing fluency. First, we posit that PEvoV can be positively associated with perceived review helpfulness. Based on affect-as-information theory, valence can provide information about value (goodness vs. badness) and influence perception (Clore & Huntsinger, 2007; Zadra & Clore, 2011). For example, PEvoV can influence a viewer’s perception of others’ well-being (Hou et al., 2023). The information cues come from the mechanism of (mis)attribution. People are more sensitive to their emotions than to where their emotions come from (Schwarz, 2012), and their ability to track the complex causes of their emotions is limited (Zhang, 2013). Hence, people usually attribute their emotions to the object they are focusing on, even if their feelings are incidental and irrelevant to it (termed misattribution) (Lerner et al., 2015; Schwarz, 2012; Zhang, 2013). In our context, the focal object is a review containing pictures. When individuals process the review, the emotional valence evoked by the picture may be (mis)attributed to the review as a whole due to the shared field of focus. Hence, PEvoV may provide value information, signaling the goodness (helpfulness) of the review. Based on the above theoretical development, we propose the following:

H1: PEvoV is positively associated with perceived review helpfulness.

Next, we posit that picture-text valence alignment is positively associated with perceived review helpfulness. Regarding picture-text interaction, researchers have argued that pictures grab the viewer’s attention and are processed first (Ceylan et al., 2024; Pieters and Wedel, 2004). Relatedly, dual process theory argues that the cognitive process of visual and emotional cues is typically fast and heuristic (De Neys, 2018). Therefore, PEvoV (as an internal information cue) can help the reader form a heuristic belief about experience goods before they conceptually process the associated textual information. According to cognitive dissonance theory, people value confirmatory information because it helps them avoid psychological discomfort (for a review on confirmation bias and review helpfulness, see Yin et al., 2016); thus, the writer’s congruent valence expression in text (as an external affective cue) will tend to confirm the heuristic belief, causing the review to be perceived as more helpful.

Furthermore, conceptual processing fluency can partially mediate the effect of picture-text valence alignment. Conceptual processing fluency is positively influenced by congruity (Peng et al., 2020; Winkielman et al., 2003). According to affective priming theory (and supported by related experimental research), congruent affective information can be processed more easily (Klauer, 1997; Peng et al., 2020), whereas incongruent information may disrupt fluency (Storme et al., 2015). In our context, the review reader may find it easier to semantically understand why the writer expressed positive or negative emotions when the textual expression is congruent with the reader’s (picture-evoked) feelings. Another possible mechanism is expectancy (Klauer, 1997). A reader with positive (negative) picture-evoked feelings may expect the writer to feel the same, leading them to express similar emotions in text. Such expectancy can allow the reader to cognitively process positive (negative) textual information more quickly and easily (Klauer, 1997; Yu et al., 2023).

Based on the definition of perceived review helpfulness (Yin et al., 2014, 2016, 2017), reviews that are difficult to understand are unlikely to facilitate purchase decisions or to be perceived as helpful. Relatedly, a recent experimental study showed that the “ease of processing” can positively impact review helpfulness (Ceylan et al., 2024). As additional evidence, conceptual processing fluency has been linked to a positive attitude toward marketer-generated content (see Storme et al., 2015 and references cited therein). Hence, it is reasonable to expect that conceptual processing fluency is associated with perceived review helpfulness. Based on the above theoretical development, we propose the following:

H3: The effect of picture-text valence alignment on perceived review helpfulness is mediated by conceptual processing fluency.

## Study 1

## Research Setting and Data Collection

We selected dianping.com as the research context. Dianping.com is the largest online review platform devoted to local leisure businesses (e.g., restaurants, hotels, shopping) in China, with over 600 million active users. In this study, we focused on restaurant reviews; the data generation process can be summarized as follows: (1) A potential reviewer visits a restaurant and consumes products and services. (2) The reviewer writes a review about their experience at the restaurant on dianping.com, which contains numeric ratings, a timestamp, and possibly text and pictures. (3) Another dianping.com user visits the focal restaurant’s page, views the review content, and rates the helpfulness of the review. Figure 1 provides a sample screenshot of a restaurant review.

We focused on the 100 restaurants with the highest sales in the data provided by dianping.com, ensuring a sufficient number of reviews and helpfulness votes. At the time of data collection, however, two restaurants were no longer in operation, resulting in missing data; we thus excluded these, retaining 98 restaurants. We included all of the review timestamps, ratings, text, and pictures posted from March 2015 to March 2017, along with the IDs of the reviewers in the dataset. Then, we preprocessed the data as follows. First, we removed reviews with no more than two words and without pictures due to poor informativeness. Second, we removed reviews that contained corrupted picture URLs (i.e., URLs that were no longer active or with pictures that had been deleted). Third, we identified and removed meaningless reviews that contained only URLs and emojis. After data preprocessing and cleaning, we obtained 10,271 reviews and 42,941 pictures. Next, we analyzed valence and arousal for pictures and text. Since our variable of interest is valence, arousal is an important confounding factor to control for.

## Analysis of Valence and Arousal

## Picture-Evoked Emotional Valence and Arousal

Before we trained the deep learning model to predict PEvoV and PEvoA, we constructed an annotated training set. We selected 1,325 reviews for affect annotation, considering mainly the following factors. First, to reduce the measurement error caused by an imbalanced sample selection, we chose the same number of pictures and texts. In addition, to maximize the consistency between the content of texts and that of pictures, we selected reviews containing only one picture, where the picture and text were in one-to-one correspondence. In our dataset, there were a total of 1,379 reviews containing only one picture. Subsequently, we manually removed reviews that met any of the following conditions: (1) The picture was an advertisement or emojis only. (2) The picture was not related to the dining experience. (3) The text was not related to the dining experience. This left us with 1,325 reviews for affect rating.

Kurdi et al. (2016) developed protocols to rate PEvoV and PEvoA and constructed a labeled set called OASIS. Because OASIS is not specific to restaurant pictures, based on their work, we constructed our labeled set. We specifically followed their protocol of rating PEvoV and PEvoA. We recruited 150 individuals from WJX.com to perform the rating annotation task. These participants were divided into 53 groups, with each group consisting of two or three participants responsible for 25 pictures. Limiting each participant to 25 pictures helped prevent rating inconsistency due to fatigue. Additionally, ensuring that each picture was rated by at least two participants helps to mitigate bias from individual subjectivity (Yu et al., 2023). To avoid interference when participants rated valence and arousal simultaneously, we randomly assigned participants to rate either valence or arousal, ensuring each participant was unaware of the other dimension.

The rating annotation process closely followed the protocol suggested by Kurdi et al. (2016). The participants were invited to complete the rating in two stages. In the first stage, we informed the participants about the goal of the rating and the concepts of variables to be rated (i.e., valence and arousal). On the first screen of the computer, the participants read a short description of the rating, including a privacy statement and a brief introduction to the experiment. The second screen provided information about the concepts of valence (i.e., the degree of positive or negative affective response evoked by the picture) or arousal (i.e., the intensity of the affective response evoked by the picture). On the following screen, we displayed the picture from OASIS with values of 1, 4, and 7 of valence or arousal to help participants establish numerical perceptions for rating purposes. In the second stage, the participants browsed through their assigned pictures in random order and were asked to use a 7-point Likert scale to mark the valence or arousal levels evoked by the pictures. For valence, the extremes of the scale were labeled “very negative” (1) and “very positive” (7). For arousal, the extremes of the scale were labeled “very low” (1) and “very high” (7). To ensure the participants’ attention when rating pictures and to minimize the impact of rated pictures on the participants, we ensured that only one picture appeared on each screen and gave the participants two minutes to rate each picture. After rating each picture, the participants clicked a button to proceed to the next screen. The process was set up to proceed in a forward-only direction; that is, the participants were not allowed to return to a previous screen.

To ensure that the participants were in a state of concentration during the rating process, we set up attention-check questions at the 10th and 20th pictures, respectively. Only those samples that passed the attention check were considered valid and used for subsequent deep-learning model training. For the valence and arousal rating, we received 128 valid questionnaires. Among the respondents with valid questionnaires, there were 86 females and 42 males; 39 were 20 years old or less, 49 were between 21 and 39, and 40 were 40+ years old; 3 had an annual income over 200,000 Chinese yuan, 40 had an annual income of 50,000- 200,000 Chinese yuan, and 85 had an annual income of less than 50,000 Chinese yuan; finally, 24 had an associate’s degree, 67 had a bachelor’s degree, 13 had a master’s degree or above, and 24 had a high school diploma or less.

The soundness of our measures is supported by interrater reliability and the similarity between the valence (val) and arousal (aro) distributions in the current study and OASIS (Kurdi et al., 2016). The interrater reliability was $R _ { v a l } =$ 0.984 $( S D = 0 . 0 0 2 , R _ { m i n } = 0 . 9 7 4$ and $R _ { m a x } = 0 . 9 8 9 )$ for valence and $R _ { a r o } = 0 . 9 2 9 ( S D = 0 . 0 1 5 , R _ { m i n } = 0 . 8 3 3$ and $R _ { m a x } = 0 . 9 5 8 )$ for arousal. The results indicate high interrater reliability. Moreover, the results of the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test results confirm that the valence and arousal scores in our study and those in OASIS were sampled from similar, if not the same, distributions $( D _ { v a l } = 0 . 0 5 0 , p _ { v a l } =$ 0.197; $D _ { a r o } = 0 . 0 7 0 , p _ { a r o } = 0 . 0 8 4 )$ . These results confirm that review pictures tend to evoke relatively consistent levels of affect in readers, despite individual heterogeneity. Given such stability, predicting an average reader’s picture-evoked affect with machine learning techniques is feasible and meaningful.

We employed a commonly used computer vision model, the convolutional neural network (CNN), to predict PEvoV and PEvoA. CNN has variants, which differ mainly in terms of network architecture, parameters, and filter design. We used the VGGNet architecture, also known as VGG-16, which is widely used for its simplicity and interpretability of concepts (Zhang & Pan, 2019). VGGNet uses 16 convolutional layers for feature extraction and three fully connected layers for picture classification. VGGNet was pretrained on a dataset of 1.2 million pictures, which were divided into more than 1,000 classes. We did not use the pretrained VGG model entirely, as most of our pictures were related to food and restaurants, and a considerable portion of them belonged to the Chinese cuisine category. This category was not originally used for recognition and classification by the VGG model. We left the first 12 convolutional layers of VGGNet unchanged as they capture basic visual features (e.g., edges, circles). These foundational features are then used by subsequent layers to learn more complex, task-specific features. The structure of the last four layers remained consistent with the original VGG architecture in terms of filter size and stride.

To ensure optimal performance and accurate predictions in our VGG model, we carefully selected and tuned several hyperparameters. The initial learning rate was set to 0.01 to balance the trade-off between convergence speed and the risk of overshooting the optimal weights. We employed the Adam optimizer, known for its adaptive learning rate and efficient handling of sparse gradients, which helps achieve faster convergence and improved performance. The dropout rate was set at 0.7 to effectively prevent overfitting by randomly deactivating 70% of the neurons during training, thereby promoting the robustness of the model.

We fine-tuned the last four convolutional layers of VGGNet using our annotated dataset. The domain-specific features are high-level features and are likely to be captured in the final convolutional layers (Zhang & Pan, 2019). For our loss function, we used the mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) due to its intuitive interpretation and robustness to outliers, and its particular usefulness for regression tasks (Song et al., 2019; Yu et al., 2023).

Since our task involved predicting continuous variables, specifically valence and arousal, we did not use an activation function in the final fully connected layer. In the final prediction phase, we utilized the XGBoost and random forest models, which assemble several decision trees to predict the outcomes. These models were chosen for their ability to handle complex, nonlinear relationships and provide robust, high-accuracy predictions (Yu et al., 2023). The models were implemented with the Python machine learning tool, Scikit-Learn. We optimized the hyperparameters. The number of decision trees was evaluated within the list [10, 50, 100, 200, 500], and the maximum depth of a tree was evaluated within the list [2, 5, 10, 20, 50, None], where “None” means no restriction of maximum depth. The predicted values for valence and arousal were constrained to a range of 1 to 7, aligning with our rating scales.

Our dataset contained the 1,325 labeled pictures from dianping.com and pictures from Kurdi et al. (2016). We randomly split them into the training (80%), validation (10%), and test (10%) sets. For valence, the optimal model was the random forest with 50 decision trees and a maximum depth of 10. For arousal, the optimal model was XGBoost with 100 decision trees and a maximum depth of 10. The MAPE of valence and arousal in the validation set were 20.07% and 22.36%, respectively. In the test set, the out-of-sample MAPEs of valence and arousal were 27.38% and 28.94%, respectively. Although MAPE has a straightforward interpretation, we enriched the model evaluation by discretizing the outcome variables and constructing classification models. We considered pictures with valence (arousal) greater than 4 as high-valence (high-arousal) and the rest as low-valence (low-arousal). The AUC scores of the models for predicting valence and arousal were 0.72 and 0.71, respectively. Research indicates that an AUC score between 0.7 and 0.8 indicates “acceptable” classification performance (Mandrekar, 2010), and it is comparable to the AUC scores for review helpfulness prediction in Xiao et al. (2022). To further validate our approach, we used GPT-4.0 to establish another benchmark by few-shot learning following Zhang et al. (2023): We first input five examples of pictures that had prelabeled PEvoV and PEvoA values. Subsequently, we asked GPT-4.0 to predict the PEvoV and PEvoA for new pictures. The prediction errors generated by GPT-4.0 (MAPE = 30.49% for valence and MAPE = 29.13% for arousal) were slightly higher than those of our models. In sum, our model achieved satisfactory performance.

## Text-Expressed Valence and Arousal

To predict text-expressed valence and arousal (TExpV and TExpA), we utilized a lexicon from a comprehensive annotation study that provides valence and arousal ratings for 11,310 simplified Chinese words. This lexicon, as detailed in Xu et al. (2022), offers robust and validated affective ratings, ensuring high reliability and accuracy. Lexicon-based methods are commonly used in detecting text-expressed affect (Yu et al., 2023). To our knowledge, Xu et al.’s (2022) study provides the most up-to-date humanannotated lexicon for Chinese texts on valence and arousal. We employed a systematic approach to utilizing the lexicon for detecting text valence and arousal. First, we used Jieba, a well-known Chinese text segmentation tool in Python, to segment the review texts into individual words. This segmentation allowed us to accurately identify and isolate each word in the text. Next, we matched the segmented words with the affective words in the lexicon. For each review, we then calculated the average valence and arousal values of all the identified affective words, using these averaged values as the text valence and arousal. In cases where a review contained no affective words, we assumed that its TExpV and TExpA were neutral.

In addition to using the lexicon, we also employed GPT-4.0 to predict TExpV and TExpA and used the predicted values for robustness check purposes (detailed later). Similar to picture analysis, we used the same few-shot learning approach, following Zhang et al. (2023), i.e., after presenting five examples of review text that had prelabeled valence and arousal, we asked the language model to annotate TExpV and TExpA for new texts.

## Empirical Models

Dianping.com uses the helpfulness votes of reviews as a proxy of perceived helpfulness. Hence, we used the votes as the dependent variable (Ceylan et al., 2024). The dependent variable is the count data with significant overdispersion, which is formally confirmed by a likelihood-ratio test $( p <$ 0.01). To account for this challenge, we chose negative binomial regression to model the outcome. Importantly, the review and picture data were nested within restaurants and review writers, which may share common characteristics. For example, for a certain restaurant, the appeal of the room decoration and the style of dishes in the pictures may be the same. Moreover, for a certain review writer, the styles of the language and photography used in their reviews may be similar. Thus, if we were to use an ordinary least squares regression, the independent assumption across different reviews may be violated, and the model might provide misleading results. For this reason, we leveraged a multilevel model to account for restaurant- and reviewer-specific effects at different levels. Notably, these effects can be modeled by either random or fixed effects. We chose the random effects model over the fixed effects model in consideration of the potential restaurant- and reviewer-level heterogeneity in the data collection. As we explain later, this choice is supported by the Hausman test results. Further, the choice is consistent with the previous research on restaurant reviews (Gan et al., 2016).

In our context, the model can be divided into three levels: the review level (Level 1), restaurant level (Level 2), and reviewer level (Level 3), where Level 1 is nested within Level 2 and Level 3.

Level 1:

$$
H V _ {i j k} \sim N e g a t i v e B i n o m i a l \bigl (\mu_ {i j k} \bigr),\tag{1}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \mu_ {i j k} = & \exp (\alpha_ {j} + \alpha_ {k} + \beta_ {0} \\ & + \beta_ {1} P E v o V _ {i j k} + \beta_ {2} P E v o A _ {i j k} \\ & + \beta_ {3} P E v o A _ {i j k} ^ {2} + \beta_ {4} T E x p V _ {i j k} \\ & + \beta_ {5} T E x p A _ {i j k} + \beta_ {6} T E x p A _ {i j k} ^ {2} \\ & + \beta_ {7} P E v o V _ {i j k} * T E x p V _ {i j k} \\ & + \beta_ {8} V a l A l i g n _ {i j k} \\ & + \lambda_ {1} W o r d C o u n t _ {i j k} \\ & + \lambda_ {2} P i c C o u n t _ {i j k} \\ & + \lambda_ {3} P o s t P e r i o d _ {i j k} \\ & + \lambda_ {4} R e a d a b i l i t y _ {i j k} \\ & + \lambda_ {5} R a t i n g _ {i j k} \\ & + \sum_ {t = 1} ^ {1 1} \delta_ {1 t} M o n t h _ {-} t _ {i j k} + \varepsilon_ {j k}), \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

Level 2:

$$
\alpha_ {j} = \delta_ {1} R e v i e w C o u n t _ {j} + \sum_ {s = 1} ^ {4} \delta_ {2 s} T y p e _ {s j} + \nu_ {j},\tag{3}
$$

Level 3:

$$
\begin{array}{c} \alpha_ {k} = \nu_ {k}, \\ \varepsilon_ {j k} \sim N (0, \sigma^ {2}), \nu_ {j} \sim N (0, \tau_ {1} ^ {2}), \nu_ {k} \sim N (0, \tau_ {2} ^ {2}), i. i. d. \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

We used $k \in \{ 1 , 2 , \ldots , N _ { 1 } \}$ to denote the reviewer (where $N _ { 1 }$ is the number of unique reviewers) and $j \in \{ 1 , 2 , \dots , N _ { 2 } \}$ } to denote the restaurant (where $N _ { 2 }$ is the number of unique restaurants). For all reviews of the $j ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ restaurant generated by the $k ^ { \mathrm { { t h } } }$ reviewer, we labeled them as $R _ { i j k } \ : ( \ : i \in \ : \lbrace 1 , 2 , \ldots , N _ { j k } \rbrace$ where $N _ { j k }$ is the total number of reviews for the $j ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ restaurant generated by the $k ^ { \mathrm { { t h } } }$ reviewer).

At Level 1, we modeled the helpfulness votes of $R _ { i j k } \ ( H V _ { i j k } )$ as the random realization of a negative binomial distribution with mean $\mu _ { i j k }$ . The logarithm of $\mu _ { i j k }$ is linked to the sum of a within-restaurant effect term $( \alpha _ { j } ) _ { \ast }$ , a within-reviewer effect term $( \alpha _ { k } )$ , an intercept term $( \beta _ { 0 } )$ , and a reviewer-restaurant pair-wise random error term $( \varepsilon _ { j k } )$ . For each review, $P E v o V _ { i j k }$ and $P E v o A _ { i j k }$ refer to valence and arousal evoked by the picture(s) in the review, respectively; if a review contained multiple pictures, the two variables were operationalized as the average values of all pictures in the review (Ceylan et al., 2024). In addition, we added the following review-level control variables: the valence and arousal expressed in text (i.e., $T E x p V _ { i j k } ;$ $T E x p A _ { i j k } )$ and the quadratic term of arousal to control its potential nonlinear effects (Yin et al., 2017), the number of words in the review $( W o r d C o u n t _ { i j k } ) .$ , the number of pictures in a review $( P i c C o u n t _ { i j k } )$ , the number of days between the date the review was posted and the date of our data collection $( P o s t P e r i o d _ { i j k } )$ , readability $( R e a d a b i l i t y _ { i j k } )$ , and ratings $( R a t i n g _ { i j k } )$ of the review (Li & Xie, 2020; Yin et al., 2016). In addition, the review content and the writing style of reviewers may differ at certain time points (such as holidays and peak/offpeak seasons). Therefore, we controlled for monthly fixed effects (????????ℎ ${ } _ { t _ { i j k } ) }$ at Level 1.

We incorporated picture-text valence alignment $( V a l A l i g n _ { i j k } )$ We considered two alternative measures, the absolute difference alignment (ADA) and the relative proportion alignment (RPA). ADA is defined $\mathrm { a s } - \lvert P E v o V _ { i j k } - T E x p V _ { i j k } \rvert$ , measuring the alignment regarding the absolute difference between PEvoV and TExpV. While straightforward, ADA might raise concerns because the ranges and distributions of PEvoV and TExpV can differ. As shown in the following descriptive results, PEvoV has a larger mean value and standard deviation than TExpV. To address this discrepancy, it may be more reasonable to normalize the absolute difference by considering the magnitudes of PEvoV and TExpV. We propose normalizing ADA by using the average valence of the picture and the text (which can be viewed as the valence of the review). This normalization process ensures that the comparison is fairer and less skewed by the inherent differences in the distributions of PEvoV and TExpV. RPA is thus defined as $\begin{array} { r } { - \frac { \left| P E v o V _ { i j k } - T E x p V _ { i j k } \right| ^ { - } } { ( P E v o V _ { i j k } + T E x p V _ { i j k } ) / 2 } } \end{array}$ . As detailed later, we show that the two measures yielded qualitatively consistent results. In addition to alignment, another potential interactive relationship between these two types of valences is the complementary or substitute effect. We therefore controlled the interaction term between TExpV and PEvoV to improve the identification of the valence alignment effect.

At Level 2, we modeled $\alpha _ { j }$ as the sum of restaurant-level variables (i.e., ?????????????????????? and $T y p e _ { s j }$ refer to the total number of reviews under the $j ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ restaurant and whether the $j ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ restaurant provides the $s ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ type of food, respectively), along with a random effect term, $\nu _ { j }$ , capturing the potentially unobservable restaurant heterogeneity. At Level 3, we modeled the reviewerspecific effect $\alpha _ { k }$ as a random effect term $\nu _ { k }$ , capturing the unobservable reviewer heterogeneity. The random error terms $\varepsilon _ { j k } , v _ { j }$ , and $v _ { k }$ are assumed to follow normal distributions with a mean equal to zero (as the mean effect is absorbed in the intercept term $\beta _ { 0 } )$ and with unknown standard deviations $\sigma , \tau _ { 1 } ,$ and $\tau _ { 2 }$ that need to be estimated from data. If these parameters are statistically significantly larger than zero, then heterogeneity exists among reviews and restaurants, and the multilevel model is preferred to an ordinary negative binomial model. ## Results

The results of the Hausman test $( p > 0 . 1 )$ confirmed that the selection of the random effects of reviewers and restaurants was suitable for our model. This research developed four models by gradually adding variables, the results of which are presented in Table 2. The models were all estimated following Rönnegård et al.’s (2010) procedure. First, we ran an unconditional model that contained only the intercept term and three variance components (Model 1). All three variance components $( \sigma , \ \tau _ { 1 } ,$ and $\tau _ { 2 } )$ were statistically significantly higher than zero $( p s ~ < ~ 0 . 0 5 )$ indicating significant heterogeneity within restaurants, reviewers, and restaurant-review pairs. Therefore, a multilevel model is preferred over an ordinary negative binomial model. Second, we added the textual and control variables into Model 1 (Model 2). Compared with Model 1, both Akaike’s information criterion (AIC) and the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) in Model 2 decreased, indicating that the textual and control variables increased the model fitness. This outcome demonstrates the importance of controlling these variables. Third, we added PEvoV and controlled for PEvoA (Model 3). The model fitness was improved compared to Model $^ { 2 , }$ demonstrating that the PEvoV and PEvoA were of significant explanatory power to the outcome variable, in addition to the explanatory power of the textual and control variables. Fourth, we added the RPA alignment and the interaction term (Model 4), which further increased the model’s explanatory power.

Next, we modified Model 4 in several ways for robustness checks. First, we replaced the TExpV and TExpA generated using Xu et al.’s (2022) lexicon with ChatGPT-generated variables (Model 4 with GPT). Second, we replaced the RPA alignment with the ADA alignment (Model 4 with ADA). Third, we constructed a subsample that only contained reviews posted within three months of the observational point (Model 4 with 3M). We also constructed a subsample within six months (Model 4 with 6M). This subsample analysis helped mitigate several concerns. To begin, it helped minimize the potential influence of changes in restaurant operations or other unforeseen events that could affect the helpfulness of reviews over an extended period. Further, the relatively short duration of each subsample ensured that the time frame for each review to receive upvotes was more consistent than in our main analysis. Fourth, we evenly divided the restaurants into three subsamples: those with small, median, and large numbers of reviews (Model 4 with SNR, MNR, and HNR, respectively). This helped us check the robustness of our results to restaurants’ unobservable characteristics associated with the number of reviews, including the number of review readers, popularity, and promotion of word-of-mouth generation.

Table 2 shows the model estimation results. Model 3 and all variants of Model 4 consistently showed a significant and positive association between PEvoV and helpfulness votes, supporting H1. Further, all variants of Model 4 consistently showed a significant and positive association between picture-text valence alignment and helpfulness votes, supporting H2. The results indicate that our conclusions are robust to different measures and subsamples. Additionally, the results confirm the positive association between TExpV and helpfulness votes (Yin et al., 2016; Yu et al., 2023). Finally, the interaction term of PEvoV and TExpV is negative (a substitution effect) and significant in three variants of Model 4 but not in others, which implies that the alignment effect is more significant and robust than the substitution effect.

## Interpreting Picture-Evoked Emotional Valence and Arousal

We associated PEvoV and PEvoA with interpretable picture features for the following reasons. First, while our empirical model supported our hypotheses and showed the robustness of the results, a randomized experiment would further help us verify the relationship and explore the underlying mechanisms. Therefore, we sought to identify interpretable picture features that impact valence without significantly changing arousal. Second, interpretability helped deepen our understanding of the sources of PEvoV and PEvoA. Therefore, we realized that interventions of PEvoV and PEvoA may be possible, which would offer methodological implications to future research.

We explored both domain-unrelated and domain-related interpretable features (Table 3). Domain-related features refer to high-level features that are unique to a picture in a certain domain (e.g., dining in our context), and the domainunrelated features are mid- or low-level features that can be extracted and compared for any picture. Domain-unrelated features include the visual aesthetics of the pictures and include mainly composition, color, and figure-ground (F-G) relationships (Zhang et al., 2022). The composition of the picture includes four subfeatures: the extent to which the picture conforms to diagonal dominance and the rule of thirds, the visual balance intensity, and the visual balance color of the picture. The color of the picture includes five subfeatures: warm hue, saturation, brightness, contrast of brightness, and image clarity. The F-G relationship of the picture includes three subfeatures: size difference, color difference, and texture difference.

Based on the literature, we focused on four domain-related features: namely, whether the picture contained (1) a dining environment, (2) dishes, (3) food variety, or (4) people (Cai et al., 2020; Ryu & Jang, 2007; Wadhera & Capaldi-Phillips, 2014). These features have been determined to have an impact on people’s cognitive evaluation. They may be associated with PEvoV and PEvoA because emotions are complex reactions to cognitive evaluations of actual or imaginary experiences (Smith & Lazarus, 1993). Dishes and food variety may affect the judgment of whether the food is rich and nutritionally balanced (Larson et al., 2014; Rolls et al., 1981). Pictures that contain dining environments stimulate individuals’ sense of space (Ryu et al., 2012). Pictures that contain people cause individuals to relate to the dining scene in the restaurant and evaluate the quality of the restaurant’s service (Ryu & Jang, 2007). These cognitive evaluations may induce feelings of enjoyment, comfort, social presence, or friendliness, thereby evoking valence and arousal.

To explore the associations between the above variables and PEvoV/PEvoA, we first constructed linear regression models (Table 4), gradually adding domain-unrelated features and

domain-related features. The results show that variables related to composition and the F-G relationship have a more significant impact on PEvoV than PEvoA. More importantly, the rule of thirds, F-G color difference, and F-G texture difference were found to be significantly associated with PEvoV but not with PEvoA. Our results show that variables related to color have a more significant impact on PEvoA, including saturation, brightness, contrast of brightness, and image clarity. Our domain-unrelated features produced an adjusted $R ^ { 2 }$ of 0.532 and 0.491 for PEvoV and PEvoA, respectively, indicating that these variables effectively explained about half of the variance in picture affect. Among the domain-related features, only the variety of food exhibited a significant effect on picture arousal. After adding domainrelated features, the adjusted $R ^ { 2 }$ of both models increased by only 0.001, indicating that PEvoV and PEvoA were driven mainly by domain-unrelated features rather than domainrelated features.

Although the linear regression models provided valuable results, they are unable to consider the possible nonlinear and interactive effects of interpretable features on valence and arousal. Indeed, using decision trees, we showed that there exist complex interactive and nonlinear effects. For example, we found that the effects of texture difference on valence are not necessarily linear. The detailed results of our decision tree analysis can be found in Appendix A.

## Study 2

## Pretest on Pictures

In this pretest, we aimed to manipulate and select experimental pictures that were significantly different in PEvoV but without significant differences in the confounding factors. We opted to manipulate PEvoV through color and texture differences because the empirical analysis showed that they were significantly associated with valence rather than arousal. While the rule-of-thirds feature also exhibited such an effect, manipulating this feature would have inevitably changed the position of the objects in the picture, impacting the picture content, and potentially inducing other confounding factors.

## Procedure

We selected 10 recently posted review pictures and their associated review texts from Yelp.com as our candidate materials. Five of the pictures were for restaurants, and the rest were for hotels. We explored five different manipulations regarding color and texture differences on each of the 10 pictures. Table 5 presents the manipulations.

We employed ImageJ (https://imagej.net/), software specializing in the processing and analysis of scientific images, to apply the manipulations to the pictures. Throughout the manipulation process, we ensured that the average color and texture measurements of the entire picture remained unchanged, changing only the color and texture differences between the figure and the ground.

A total of 450 U.S./U.K. participants were recruited from Prolific.com, a respected platform for academic research recruitment, ensuring a diverse and representative sample. A total of 439 participants passed the attention checks and submitted responses. The pretest protocol was designed as follows: Participants first viewed screens that explained the pretest’s purpose and were instructed to rate only one of the following: PEvoV, PEvoA, or other attributes. We took this approach in order to prevent participants from considering other variables during the rating process, which may have led to inaccurate results (Kurdi et al., 2016). Participants were then asked to describe the task and provide their Prolific ID as a comprehension check. Next, they were randomly assigned to one of the five manipulation conditions, where they viewed 10 pictures, one at a time, and answered associated questions. The order of the experimental pictures was randomized. A filler review was used between pictures to mitigate any potential interference from the previous picture (Yin et al., 2021).

For each picture, we measured PEvoV and PEvoA (Kurdi et al., 2016) and perceived picture quality (Li & Xie, 2020). We also reviewed the literature and measured important context-related confounding factors. For hotel pictures, we additionally measured facility aesthetics and the layout of the hotel (Ryu & Jang, 2007). For restaurant pictures, we additionally measured perceived healthiness and palatability of food (Hagen, 2020). Appendix B shows the questions used for measuring constructs. For experimental details, including content on each screen, the attention check, and the balance check, please see the transparency materials (https://osf.io/erk4c/overview).

## Results

We conducted pairwise comparisons for each review in different valence conditions, as shown in Table 12. The mean values for each condition are in the expected order. The t-tests (with the very high valence condition as the reference group) show that expressed valence differs significantly across conditions for all three experimental reviews (ps < 0.1), indicating that the manipulations were successful. As we only manipulated valence-related expressions in reviews, we expected that there would be no significant difference in the arousal dimension. To confirm this, we used the Affective Norms for English Words (ANEW) lexicon (Bradley & Lang, 1999). It provides affective ratings for English words, including means and standard deviations of arousal rated by 8 to 25 annotators. For each textual manipulation, we matched words to ANEW and, if unmatched, used synonyms from the Merriam-Webster thesaurus. Synonym ratings in ANEW showed no statistically significant differences. For each review and valence group, we calculated the mean and standard deviation of emotional arousal by aggregating ratings of matched words. Using a sample size of 16 (the mean number of annotators), we conducted ANOVA tests to compare arousal means across valence groups. The p-values (0.94, 0.43, 0.47) indicate no significant differences in arousal across reviews (see Figure 2).

## Main Experiment

Next, we conducted our main experiment to test the effects of PEvoV and picture-text valence alignment on perceived review helpfulness and on the mediating role of conceptual processing fluency.

## Procedure

We first defined different experimental groups. From the first pretest, we obtained three conditions for each experimental picture, i.e., low, median, and high valence. We added another condition of not containing any picture. From the second pretest, we obtained four conditions for each experimental text, i.e., very low, low, median, and high valence. We added another condition of not containing any text. Considering that the presence of either a text or a picture is mandatory, these combinations yielded a total of nineteen groups (five text conditions × four picture conditions minus one scenario where both text and pictures are absent). Participants were randomly assigned to one of the 19 groups. Each participant read the three reviews (with filler reviews in between) and was asked to rate perceived review helpfulness (Sen & Lerman, 2007; Yin et al., 2014) and conceptual processing fluency (Storme et al., 2015) (see Appendix B for details). They were also asked to report their demographic information for randomization check and control purposes, as detailed later. The details of the experiment procedure are reported in the transparency materials. ## Results

To ensure the integrity of randomization, we performed balance checks on the demographic characteristics (including gender, age, education, income, race, and ideology) of the participants who provided valid responses. The objective of this step was to confirm that participants were randomly assigned to the various experimental groups. We employed Pearson’s chi-square test to assess the balance of these characteristics across groups, following Yu et al. (2023). If the resulting p-value from Pearson’s chi-square test exceeded 0.1, we considered this a failure to reject the null hypothesis of balanced assignment, indicating no significant difference in the distribution of the characteristic across groups. The p-values for gender, age, income, education, race, and ideology were 0.60, 0.54, 0.19, 0.48, 0.39, and 0.60, respectively.

To ensure the accuracy and reliability of our measurements, we performed reliability and validity tests on the constructs of our study. The results of these tests are detailed in Table 13. The table reveals that the Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for all constructs exceeded 0.90, which is indicative of strong internal consistency and construct reliability. Additionally, confirmatory factor analysis revealed that the AVE for each construct was above the threshold of 0.60, suggesting robust convergent validity. Furthermore, the AVE for each construct was found to be greater than the square of the correlation coefficient between the two constructs (0.62 vs. 0.14), satisfying the criterion for discriminant validity as suggested by Fornell & Larcker (1981). These findings provide confidence in the quality and integrity of our measurement instruments, enhancing the credibility of our subsequent analyses.

Next, we examined H1, the relationship between PEvoV and perceived review helpfulness, by focusing on the experimental conditions that only involved a picture. As such, we eliminated the influence of review text. As shown in Table 14, we calculated the mean values and standard deviations of perceived review helpfulness for each valence condition and each picture. The first three rows show the results for each picture under different valence conditions, and the last row shows the results of pooling three pictures under the same valence condition together. All rows show the positive effect of PEvoV on perceived review helpfulness. ANOVA test results show that the positive effect is significant (ps < 0.05). Therefore, H1 is supported by both the experimental analysis and the empirical analysis.

We also verified the link between TExpV and perceived review helpfulness, even though it is not the relationship of interest in this work. For conditions that only contained review text, the mean values of perceived review helpfulness were 4.38, 4.94, 5.33, and 5.34 for very low, low, median, and high valence conditions, respectively. The positive association between TExpV and perceived review helpfulness is consistent with the prior literature (Yin et al., 2016; Yu et al., 2023) and our results in Study 1.

Next, we examined H2, the relationship between picture-text valence alignment and perceived review helpfulness. Table 15 defines different valence alignment levels across groups, i.e., aligned (A), moderately aligned (MA), and not aligned (NA). A indicates valence differences smaller than 1, NA indicates valence differences larger than 2, and MA indicates “otherwise.” The mean values for perceived review helpfulness for A, MA, and NA are 4.39, 5.22, and 5.28, respectively. The ANOVA test for the difference across the three groups yielded significant results $( F = 5 3 . 8 1 , p <$ 0.0001). For robustness, we also tested the difference between the A group and the other two groups. The difference is also significant $( F = 1 8 . 6 0 , p < 0 . 0 0 0 1 )$

Next, we added an interaction plot (Figure 3) to better understand the relationship between PEvoV and text-picture valence alignment. We found that the not-aligned group had significantly lower perceived review helpfulness than the aligned group, confirming the effect of alignment. The effect of picture-evoked valence was positive in both groups.

Finally, we tested H3, the mediating effects of conceptual processing fluency, using the PROCESS approach with 5,000 bootstrap samples (Hayes, 2013). The results show that the indirect effect of ValAlign on perceived review helpfulness via conceptual processing fluency was positive and significant (path coefficient = 0.11, SE = 0.02, 95% CI = [0.08, 0.14], p < 0.05). After adding conceptual processing fluency to the model, the direct effect of ValAlign on perceived review helpfulness became 48.4% smaller (β decreased to 0.16 from $0 . 3 1 , \mathrm { S E } = 0 . 0 7 , t ( 1 7 4 0 ) = 2 . 4 3 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , which indicates a partial mediation effect, supporting H3.

Interestingly, we found that the effect of PEvoV on perceived review helpfulness was also partially mediated by conceptual processing fluency, although it was not our main relationship of interest. The indirect effect of PEvoV on perceived review helpfulness via conceptual processing fluency was positive and significant (path coefficient = 0.04, SE = 0.02, 95% CI = [0.00, 0.07], p < 0.05). After adding conceptual processing fluency to the model, the direct effect of PEvoV on perceived review helpfulness became 9.1% smaller (β decreased to 0.10 from 0.11, SE = 0.04, t(1740) = 2.53, p < 0.05), indicating a partial mediation effect.

## Discussion

Our work makes several contributions to the IS literature on review helpfulness and the impact of affect. First, prior research has mainly focused on the impacts of expressed emotions in text and used the theoretical lens of the reader’s cognitive evaluation of the writer. Such evaluation includes the writer’s perceived cognitive effort and rationality (Xiao et al., 2018; Yin et al., 2014; Yu et al., 2023, in press). Relatedly, the theoretical foundations stem more from a cognition-based perspective, such as that of cognitive appraisals (Yin et al., 2014), confirmation bias (Yin et al., 2016), and inferential processes (Yin et al., 2017, 2021) toward others’ emotional expressions. Motivated by the picture modality and its emotion-evoking nature, we advance the literature by studying a reader’s induced affective states (differing from a writer’s emotional expressions and a reader’s cognitive evaluation). The novel construct (PEvoV) then calls for a new theoretical lens, the affect-asinformation perspective (Clore & Huntsinger, 2007; Zadra & Clore, 2011). It implies that since people may not attribute their induced affective states to the right causes, the misattribution may influence perceived review helpfulness.

Moreover, IS researchers are increasingly interested in pixeland object-level picture features, such as color, composition, and object complexity (e.g., Shin et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2022), but the impact of picture-evoked affect remains understudied. This work deepens our understanding of interpretable picture attributes that evoke valence and arousal. It enables future researchers to manipulate picture-evoked emotions and understand their impacts in other related contexts. PEvoV and PEvoA are driven mainly by domain-unrelated perceptual visual features (composition, color, and F-G relationships) rather than domain-related conceptual features. Our results suggest that perceptual and affective processes are closely related, consistent with a dual-process theory perspective (De Neys, 2018).

Second, cognition-based picture-text alignment measure, particularly picture-text semantic similarity, is the focus of the literature on reviews (Ceylan et al., 2024; Li & Xie, 2020; Xiao et al., 2022). Through a new emotion-focused lens, the alignment in our work is more nuanced than semantic similarity. It opens a novel dimension to the literature on emotions and picture-text interaction (Ceylan et al., 2024; Xiao et al., 2022): the alignment of external affective cues (TExpV) and internal affective states (PEvoV). As a secondary contribution, our textual treatments examine a different type of TExpV. We focus on the appraisal of a stimulus’ affective quality (termed affective evaluation, e.g., “It had an unpleasant taste.”) (Zhang, 2013), whereas the current literature mainly focuses on the expression of a specific emotional state (e.g., “I am a little bit angry.”) (Yin et al., 2014, 2021; Yu et al., 2023).

Third, our work deepens our understanding of conceptual processing fluency (Reber et al., 2004; Storme et al., 2015) in the online review literature. Based on the prior literature, content-context congruence (Peng et al., 2020), semantic congruence (Ceylan et al., 2024), and discrete emotional expressions (Yu et al., 2023) affect a reader’s conceptual processing fluency, thereby impacting the perception of helpfulness. Using the affect-as-information perspective, our findings regarding the picture-text valence alignment unveil a new pathway.

This work has several managerial implications. Our research identified important ingredients of helpful reviews, i.e., PEvoV and picture-text valence alignment. For review platforms, as Yin et al. (2021) stated, “understanding the ingredients of a ‘helpful’ review enables review platform to identify and promote diagnostic content, develop guidelines encouraging such content, and ultimately increase user satisfaction” (p. 1059). More specifically, this study can enable review platforms to refine their models of review helpfulness by incorporating new picture-related features and relationships. A better understanding of perceived review helpfulness can enable more accurate review recommendations and more favorable review ranking systems (Yin et al., 2021). Further, review platforms are developing review-generation guidelines and support systems (Yin et al., 2014; Yu et al., 2023). Such systems may focus on predicting PEvoV and, accordingly, provide suggestions or tools to refine user-uploaded pictures and strengthen their synergy with textual content. Identifying potentially helpful reviews is also valuable to firms on the review platforms. In online reputation management, firms are faced with an enormous volume of reviews and may wish to automatically estimate, a priori, the extent to which a review will be deemed helpful (Yin et al., 2017). Our machine learning and econometric approaches can be used to predict the helpfulness of a review right after it is posted. Then, for potentially helpful and negative reviews, firms can provide managerial responses before the reviews spread virally and harm future sales (Yu et al., 2023, in press), whereas for potentially helpful and positive reviews, firms can highlight, reply, or repost the reviews to attract potential customers (Song et al., 2019).

Certain limitations and secondary findings present opportunities for future research. First, valence and arousal are the two dimensions of affect, and we focus on valence and treat arousal as a control variable in this work. We leave it to future research to deepen the causal and theoretical understanding of the link between PEvoA and review helpfulness. Second, we found that conceptual processing fluency partially mediates the effect of PEvoV. We call for future researchers to pay attention to the broaden-and-build theory of emotion and the work by Peng et al. (2020). Based on the broaden-and-build theory, positive emotions broaden people’s thought repertoires and induce thought patterns that are open to new information and efficient in information processing (Estrada et al., 1997; Fredrickson, 2001; Isen & Means, 1983), thereby enhancing conceptual processing fluency. Additionally, consistent with the affective priming theory (Klauer, 1997), if readers are primed by picture-evoked emotions, they may fluently process the emotional information in texts (Peng et al., 2020). Third, the IS literature indicates that contextual differences may moderate the effect of valence on review helpfulness. On microblogging platforms with a “push” mechanism, where potential consumers often lack a strong prior preference for a product and merely passively receive reviews, negative reviews tend to have a greater impact (Yu et al., 2023). Conversely, on platforms with a “pull” mechanism, where consumers are typically interested in the product (e.g., a restaurant) and actively seek reviews to confirm their positive beliefs, positive WOM is often perceived as more helpful in concluding their search process (Yin et al., 2016). Fourth, although this work follows the frameworks of Kim et al. (2018), Ortis et al. (2020), and Zhang et al. (2022) to investigate 16 interpretable picture features, additional picture content features could be studied regarding their relationships with PEvoV. Fifth, certain unobservable restaurant-level features, such as overall rating, were only implicitly addressed due to data limitations. While these factors are controlled in randomized experimental settings, future researchers can improve our empirical model by incorporating more features.

## Interpreting Picture-Evoked Emotional Valence and Arousal

In theory, there are hundreds of non-linear and interactive relationships among the features. To maintain clarity, we need to determine the most important relationships. We thus employ a decision tree model for the following motivations. By construction, this model can explore all possible non-linear and interactive effects of features. Notably, the most predictive features (and rules) are automatically selected. Further, decision trees provide clear prediction rules. The decision tree analysis of PEvoV is shown in Figure A1. In the figure, A2 represents the minimum distance between the visual subject and the third lines, and the larger the value, the less the picture conforms to the rule of thirds. A12 represents the texture difference, and the smaller the value, the less obvious the texture difference between the figure and the ground of the picture. The decision tree results show that, when the picture conforms to the rule of thirds, it will evoke a higher level of valence (the values of the two branches on the left of the tree are higher than those on the right). The secondary factor that affects the PEvoV is the texture difference. The more obvious the texture difference between the figure and the ground, the higher the valence it will evoke.

Figure A2 shows the results of PEvoA. In the figure, A9 represents the clarity of the picture, with larger values indicating that the picture is easier to view. A1 represents the distance from the diagonal, with larger values indicating that the picture is less in line with diagonal dominance. A7 represents the mean brightness of the picture; the results show that pictures can be initially divided into high-brightness and low-brightness pictures. In the former case, the more a picture conforms to diagonal dominance in composition, the higher arousal it evokes; in the latter case, higher-brightness pictures evoke higher arousal.
