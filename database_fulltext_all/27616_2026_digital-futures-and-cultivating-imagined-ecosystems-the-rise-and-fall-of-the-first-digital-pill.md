# DIGITAL FUTURES AND CULTIVATING IMAGINED ECOSYSTEMS: THE RISE AND FALL OF THE FIRST DIGITAL PILL VENTURE<sup>1</sup>

Valerie Handunge Cambridge Digital Innovation, University of Cambridge, Cambridge, U.K. {phd17vh@mail.wbs.ac.uk}

Eivor Oborn Warwick University, Coventry, U.K. {eivor.oborn@wbs.ac.uk}

Michael Barrett University of Cambridge, Cambridge, U.K. {m.barrett@jbs.cam.ac.uk}

Existing literature has primarily focused on how established firms engage in orchestration through deliberate, purposeful actions to promote existing digital innovation ecosystems. However, many such digital innovation ecosystems start as digital ventures aspiring to build ecosystems around their digital product innovations. Less is known about how these ventures initiate and engage in ecosystem dynamics before established ecosystem structures are in place. In this study, we address this shortcoming by examining how a digital venture enacts a digital innovation ecosystem de novo. To do so, we conducted a three-year inductive field study of an ingestible biosensor venture that sought to develop a platformenabled ecosystem around the first digital pill. Our findings suggest that digital venturing unfolds within temporal tensions that create dissonance between digitization and digitalization. We develop the concept of cultivating an imagined ecosystem, which comprises three core mechanisms, namely entrepreneurial trajectory dynamics, digital future-making, and sociotechnical enactment. Our findings highlight three cultivation practices—perceptive response to emergent futures, prefigurative action in relation to desired distant futures, and inattentive inaction to alternative futures that become consequential in enacting an ecosystem de novo. Finally, we present a model of cultivating an imagined ecosystem, which offers a future-oriented perspective of the mutually reinforcing dynamics of the social and the technical in digital venturing.

Keywords: Digital innovation ecosystems, de novo digital categories, digital entrepreneurship, digital venturing, digital futures, cultivation

## Introduction

Digital innovation ecosystems often start out as digital entrepreneurial ventures (hereafter, digital ventures) (Nambisan et al., 2019; Yoo et al., 2012)—young, privately owned firms or start-ups that leverage digital technologies at the core of their value propositions, market offerings, or business models (Davidson & Vaast, 2010; Lehmann et al., 2022). While digital ventures do not exclusively call for ecosystem structures, the quest to imagine and develop ecosystems is perhaps not surprising, considering the inherent ambiguity of digital technologies (Kallinikos et al., 2013). That is, the recombinability, generativity, and largely unbounded and unstable qualities of digital technologies (Faulkner & Runde, 2019; Yoo, 2010) make them conducive to ecosystem structures as loosely coupled constellations of actors, components, and complements (Wang, 2021; Thomas & Autio, 2020). Yet the lack of hierarchical fiat means that to collectively generate a digital product or service, the interactions between independent yet interdependent actors need to be coordinated (Barrett, et al., 2015; Adner, 2017;; Wang, 2021).

Current literature has advanced orchestration as a theoretical mechanism for coordinating digital innovation ecosystems (Shen et al., 2024; Kindermann et al., 2022; Nambisan et al., 2017). Orchestration refers to a set of deliberate, purposeful actions undertaken by one or more focal actors to organize, direct, and influence interorganizational relationships within an ecosystem (Dhanaraj & Parkhe, 2006; Autio, 2022). This involves facilitating upstream component providers and downstream complements (Adner & Kapoor, 2010), as well as managing externalities to mitigate risks and maximize benefits (Parker et al., 2016; Jacobides et al., 2018).

While recent studies on orchestration have explored the context of ecosystem emergence, their focus has largely been on established firms (Ansari et al., 2016; Snihur et al., 2018; Hannah & Eisenhardt, 2018). These studies emphasize adaptability to current conditions and generativity as a continuation of past innovations (Pushpananthan & Elmquist, 2022; Patvardhan & Ramachandran, 2020). This perspective aligns with the view of orchestration as a means of negotiating and maintaining the survival of focal firms (Dattée et al., 2018; Thomas et al., 2022), inadvertently leading to a literature that predominantly examines success cases. Such research offers valuable insights into how actors align and retain ecosystem participants (Lingens et al., 2021; Yoo et al., 2024; Ojala & Lyytinen, 2024), as well as the tensions associated with rules, regulations, ecosystem access, and architecture (Wareham et al., 2014; Yoo et al., 2010). However, few frameworks address the failure of digital innovation ecosystems, particularly those that emerge during their nascent stages (Wang, 2021), where constructing an ecosystem de novo remains a critical challenge.

Moreover, orchestration often presupposes the existence of established firms, roles, and structures characterized by layered modularity, complementarity, and network effects (Yoo et al., 2010; Jacobides et al., 2018; Autio & Thomas, 2014). In contrast, digital ventures aspiring to build new ecosystems face a markedly different reality. For these ventures, ecosystem characteristics may exist only as aspirations, given their future-oriented nature. Digital ventures often explore the application of emerging digital technologies to address anticipated future contexts (Garud et al., 2018; Lehmann et al., 2022), with ecosystems that may never fully materialize (Miranda et al., 2022). Considering that many digital innovation ecosystems originate from digital ventures, we propose that such ventures begin envisioning ecosystem dynamics even before a structured ecosystem exists. These dynamics would thereby inform the construction of digital product innovations, preceding the establishment of ecosystem structures and their orchestration. In this study, we ask: How does a digital venture enact a digital innovation ecosystem de novo?

To explore ecosystem dynamics in digital ventures, we conducted a field study of SensorCorp,<sup>2</sup> a platform-enabled digital health start-up that sought to develop an ecosystem around the first-ever digital pill. Digital pills, or digital medicines, combine ingestible biosensors with pharmaceutical drugs to track medication adherence, with the intention of improving health outcomes. Through interviews, observations at field configuring events (Garud & Rappa, 1994; Lampel & Meyer, 2008), and archival data, we studied SensorCorp’s innovation trajectory. Considering the futureoriented nature of digital ventures (Lehmann et al., 2022), we turned to the literature on imagined futures (Beckert, 2013, 2021; Augustine et al., 2019) and sociotechnical imaginaries (Jasanoff & Kim, 2015; Mager & Katzenbach, 2021), and the digitalization literature (Baiyere et al., 2023; Lyytinen, 2022), in theorizing our findings. This approach opened new ways to understand ecosystem emergence in the absence of established or stabilized structures. It allowed us to include fictionality and imagination as cultural resources in how entrepreneurs identify, create, and exploit opportunities to launch new business ventures enabled by digital technologies (Autio & Thomas, 2018; Yoo et al., 2012). Imagined futures are of particular importance under conditions of uncertainty, as in the case of nascent digital ventures and generative digital technologies, where the incalculability of possibilities can be paralyzing (Beckert, 2021).

We contribute to the literature on digital futures (Schlagwein et al., 2024; Hovorka & Peter, 2021) and, more specifically, the emergence of digital innovation ecosystems (Nambisan et al., 2017; Wang, 2021; Baiyere et al., 2023). First, we found that digital ventures engage in building layered modularity, complementarity, and network effects within temporal tensions. These tensions arise from the dissonance between the generative capabilities of digitization and the capacity and readiness for digitalization to embed these capabilities in an evolving sociotechnical context. Digital ventures, aspiring toward ecosystem structures, rely on sociotechnical fictional expectations that foreground creative, distributed, and decentered agencies that are acutely future oriented. We conceptualize these ecosystems-in-the-making as “imagined ecosystems,” which perform as instruments of imagination (Beckert, 2021) to shape expectations, coordinate action, and bridge the gap between present collective knowledge and future uncertainty. Second, we offer the notion of “cultivating imagined ecosystems” to complement our existing understanding of orchestration. While orchestration foregrounds flexibility in adapting to present environments, firm-level strategies, and centralized agency (Nambisan et al., 2017; Autio, 2022), cultivating reflects a process of creating ecosystems in the absence of preexisting structures. Cultivating imagined ecosystems involves three contiguous, interrelated, and continuously evolving temporal elements: the passive conditioning influence of entrepreneurial trajectory dynamics, the agentic actions of digital futuremaking, and the capacity to shape and be shaped through sociotechnical enactment over time. Our study allows us to extend the definition of digital venturing as the process of creating and scaling ventures that exploit digital futures in their value propositions to cultivate an ecosystem. Further, our study suggests three practices for ecosystems in the making, involving emergent futures, desired distant futures, and alternative futures. Finally, these insights allowed us to develop a model advancing a future-oriented view of the mutually reinforcing effects of the social and the technical (Sarker et al., 2019; Baiyere et al., 2023) in digital venturing.

## Background Literature

We review existing research on orchestrating digital innovation ecosystems and why digital ventures often have ecosystem aspirations. Then, we outline the literature on imagined futures and sociotechnical imaginaries, which provide a foundation for developing our theory of imagined ecosystems in entrepreneurial digital venturing.

## Orchestrating Digital Innovation Ecosystems

Ecosystems have become a popular lens through which to understand innovation in the digital age (Nambisan et al., 2017; Jacobides et al., 2018). Ecosystem participants are hierarchically independent, yet heterogeneous, interdependent, dynamic, and evolving (Autio & Thomas, 2014; Thomas & Autio, 2020). Wang (2021) defines a digital innovation ecosystem as “a loosely coupled set of autonomous actors (people and organizations who interact without hierarchical fiat) involved in development and implementation of innovations enabled by digital technologies” (p. 397). Digital product innovation ecosystems include not only the focal product but the providers of complementary products and services that are bundled into an offering for customers to buy or use (Wang, 2021; Frels et al., 2003).

The current literature emphasizes that ecosystems are managed via orchestration mechanisms that are the responsibility of one or more hub firms (Dhanaraj & Parkhe, 2006; Nambisan et al.,

2017; Autio, 2022). More recent research has framed orchestrators as brokers who influence, mediate, and modify relationships in an ecosystem (Shen et al., 2024; Ritala et al., 2023; Linde et al., 2021). Ultimately, orchestration involves managing the interdependencies and aligning the interests of ecosystem participants and potential members. This includes the upstream suppliers of products and services that are bundled by the hub firm (similar to a value chain) and non-contractually governed downstream complementors, including user communities who bundle their products and services with the focal firm’s digital offering (Adner & Kapoor, 2010; Autio & Thomas, 2020). While downstream complementors do not directly participate in the supply chain of a digital innovation, they are essential to value creation and value appropriation (Autio & Thomas, 2020), which often requires them to apply and embed the innovation into their everyday lives (Baiyere et al., 2023; Lyytinen, 2022; Barrett, et al., 2016).

The challenge with applying the orchestration lens to digital ventures is that orchestration foregrounds existing resources, present capabilities, and current arrangements—today’s realities for which recognizable ecosystem characteristics are a prerequisite (Lingens et al., 2021; Autio, 2022). For example, Wang’s (2021) novel holon approach emphasizes the ongoing dynamism across multiple levels in ecosystems, a conceptualization highlighting (present) flexibility and adaptation to existing environments. Nambisan et al. (2017) have suggested problem-solution matching as a microfoundation of orchestration, which “involves waiting for the right problem to enter the stage to match with an available or new potential solution” (p. 231). This perspective attends to waiting for relevant problems or opportunities, responding to the present. However, digital ventures may entail constructing market offerings to address future needs (Lehmann et al., 2022). Additionally, with first-in-class products, there is little precedence around integrating a digital innovation into contexts of digitalization (Lyytinen, 2022). This study seeks to theorize the future-oriented dynamics involved in how a digital venture works toward a new ecosystem.

## Digital Ventures and Ecosystems

Although the orchestration literature explains why digital products and platforms may benefit from ecosystem organizing visions (Wang, 2021; Bogers et al., 2022) and offers insights into ecosystem structure (Adner, 2017; Autio & Thomas, 2018), governance (Gawer & Cusumano, 2014), and dynamic capabilities (Kindermann et al., 2022) once an ecosystem exists, the literature has less to say about how digital ventures construct an ecosystem de novo. This is problematic because digital innovation ecosystems often start off as digital ventures (Nambisan et al., 2017; Jacobides et al., 2018). While digital innovation does not have to take place in ecosystems, three characteristics of digital innovations explain why digital ventures may have ecosystem aspirations.

First, the (re)combinatorial potential of digital product and service components, both upstream and downstream, has led to unprecedented generativity (Lehmann et al., 2022; Lyytinen et al., 2017) through product and industry agnosticism (Henfridsson et al., 2018; Yoo et al., 2010, 2012), platformization (Gawer, 2021; Gawer & Cusumano, 2014), and value creation through network effects (Adner & Kapoor, 2010; Cennamo & Santaló, 2019). This necessitates open and flexible ways of developing, producing, and using innovations (Bogers et al., 2022). An implication is that the locus of innovation activities is distributed outside organizational boundaries, leading to the convergence of innovation agencies across heterogeneous participants (Lyytinen et al., 2016; Nambisan et al., 2017; Yoo et al., 2012). These dynamics are best understood through ecosystem organizing principles that allow for non-hierarchically controlled relationships between actors (Adner, 2017; Autio, 2022).

Second, the technological structure of digital innovations is inherently layered and modular (Yoo et al., 2010), where physical and analog objects are encoded into digital representations through bitstrings—software and data, which carry the properties of digitization, such as reprogrammability, traceability, and editability, among others (Kallinikos et al., 2013 Faulkner & Runde, 2019). The layers (hardware and software) are loosely coupled with modularity within and across layers, where components and modules can be developed, replaced, evolved, and scaled independently. Changes in one layer (e.g., in the software or service layer) do not require changes in others (e.g., the hardware layer), enabling replicability (Yoo et al., 2010). Such characteristics make platformization and ecosystems not only possible but desirable for orchestrating digital innovation (Jacobides et al., 2018).

Third, and relatedly, the affordance of layered modular architecture allows for complementary applications of the core technology, bringing about ecosystem structures through digitalization—the deployment of digital innovations (Baiyere et al., 2023; Gray & Rumpe, 2015). Digitalization extends the unique material properties of digital objects (digitization) into meaningful semiotic entities enacted through societal use, where the same technology stack can be employed to solve different problems for different actors (Lyytinen, 2022; Yoo et al., 2010). While digitization emphasizes the technological possibilities of digital objects, digitalization focuses on action potential and outcomes in sociotechnical contexts (Baiyere et al., 2023; Gray & Rumpe, 2015). The agential character of digitalization is in its contextual embedding through ongoing waves of innovation in social and organizational settings (Lyytinen, 2022; Boland et al., 2007). Digitalization extends ecosystems from technical actors to beneficiaries, implementation partners, regulators, and others with a willing or inertial stake in maturing an ecosystem.

## Imagined Futures and Sociotechnical Imaginaries

The future is uncertain—it is impossible to know through qualitative or probabilistic means what the future may be a priori (Dequech, 2006). Just as much as the future is open and indeterminate (Beckert, 2013), digital innovations are inherently malleable, and therefore, unstable, unbounded, and ambiguous (Kallinikos et al., 2013; Ekbia, 2009). Further, digital innovations are contingent on the creative reactions of actors in their sociotechnical settings (Baiyere et al., 2023; Lyytinen, 2022), foregrounding the distributed nature of agency (Garud & Karnøe, 2005), adding to the complexity. These conditions are intensified in digital ventures, which are precarious by nature, due to their ecosystems being weakly developed.

We employ the notion of imagined futures as a lens through which to view the way that interested actors overcome the paralyzing power of fundamental innovation uncertainty to envision a digital venture into being (Meyer, 2019; Sismondo, 2020). Beckert (2013) defines “imagined future” as cognitively accessible projections of future states of the world or course of events—trajectories—that motivate action in the present. Relatedly, sociotechnical imaginaries are “collectively held, institutionally stabilized, and publicly performed” visions of the future in support of or achieved through forms of science and technology, often underpinned by sociopolitical influence and wider historical contexts (Jasanoff & Kim, 2015, p. 4). Imagined futures transform the instability, unknowability, and unpredictability of the future into “fictional expectations,” protecting ideas from delegitimacy so that their novelty can be cultivated over time (Augustine et al., 2019; van Lente & Rip, 1998).

Fictional expectations are not merely untamed speculations. They achieve their credibility through their coherence, informed judgements, and by being interwoven with nonfictional elements (Beckert, 2013, 2021). A mutually gratifying experience for multiple actors creates a willingness and trust to conform to a specific imagined future, particularly when sociotechnical imaginaries stem from institutional actors and are evidenced through resource allocation (Mager & Katzenbach, 2021). Imaginaries can function at a collective level anchored in normative values of “goodness” (Augustine et al., 2019) or national interests via public policy (Jasanoff & Kim, 2015). In our case, value-based care and the Accountable Care Act formed such sociotechnical imaginaries around which SensorCorp organized its digital venture.

The self-referential nature of imagined futures can lead to selffulfilling prophecies as decisions cause the fulfillment of those futures (Beckert, 2013). At the same time, sociotechnical imaginaries are multiple (held by not just state actors but commercial actors, technology companies, and grassroots activists, among others), contested (with imaginaries from multiple sources coexisting and struggling for dominance), and dynamic (imaginaries of how trajectories themselves unfold, change over time) (Mager & Katzenbach, 2021; Jasanoff & Kim, 2015). The ability to construct credible or popular imaginaries can open the door for powerful interest groups to assert their influence, suppressing known flaws or inconsistencies of particular depictions of the future (Beckert, 2021; van Lente & Rip, 1998) or for commercial actors to shape and legitimize their products and services in their promises to fix social problems (Mager & Katzenbach, 2021).

Extending these ideas, we suggest that digital venturing is directed towards imagined ecosystems. Further, and distinct from orchestration, we develop an understanding of imagined ecosystems as involving future-making practices around cultivating a future ecosystem or an ecosystem in the making. Through future-making, actors make sense of possible and probable futures, and evaluate, negotiate, and give form to desired futures using practices such as envisioning, predicting, and projecting, which in turn, structure “realizable courses of action” (Comi & Whyte, 2018, p. 1055). Imagined futures and sociotechnical imaginaries become performative in the present through future-making, as they influence what actors actually “do” together—decisions they make and relationships they form—to produce and enact the future through situated, purposeful, and spontaneous actions (Oomen et al., 2022).

## Methods

## Case Context

SensorCorp, a Silicon Valley-based digital health start-up, was founded in 2001 to develop micro-electromechanical systems (MEMS)—miniaturized biosensors that combine electronic functions and mechanical actions, for digital applications within the body. SensorCorp’s first digital product was a multisensor lead that enables cardiac motion sensing in cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) in the treatment of congestive heart failure. In 2005, they pivoted to develop the first ingestible event marker biosensor. This biosensor, in combination with traditional pharmaceuticals (upstream complementor), was designed to support value propositions around medication adherence within a digital health ecosystem consisting of healthcare providers (organizations and individuals—physicians and clinicians), insurers, patients, and caregivers (downstream complementors):

Digital medicines are drugs that, when ingested, communicate to a consumer’s mobile device and report the time of ingestion, along with accurate metrics of activities of daily life collected by a wearable patch and communicated using the patient’s mobile device, delivering accurate feedback that enhances trust, improves collaboration, and delivers much better, personalized outcomes. (SensorCorp executive, Interview)

SensorCorp raised a total of \$487 million in funding over 11 rounds. Over time, they built a panel of more than 20 digital medicines (e.g., for hepatitis C, Type 2 diabetes, and hypertension) and held approximately 400 patents. SensorCorp was exemplified as a trailblazer in digital health as a World Economic Forum (WEF) Pioneer. This success came alongside SensorCorp’s partnership with a midsized pharma firm that we call PharmB. PharmB held the patent for Xenfil, an atypical antipsychotic.<sup>3</sup> With PharmB, SensorCorp developed a first-in-class prescription digital pill, Xenfil-digi. In doing so, they created a de novo regulatory pathway for drug-device combinations before becoming one of the first digital health unicorn start-ups, with an over \$1.5 billion valuation at its peak.

SensorCorp gradually built a reputation as a leader in digital health, with SensorCorp and PharmB executives often headlining industry conferences. PharmB secured a partnership with PBMCorp, a pharmacy benefits manager of prescription drugs on behalf of health insurers. PBMCorp managed to get Xenfil-digi on the formulary for Medicaid patients in Florida, building out the ecosystem to deliver digital medicines to patients through traditional healthcare provider channels. This model served as one ecosystem blueprint that SensorCorp sought to replicate with other pharma partners. Yet they struggled to secure additional commercially viable partners and filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy. ## Data Collection

We conducted a qualitative field study of SensorCorp. In March 2018, the first author attended an annual pharma industry conference, where SensorCorp and PharmB co-presented. Here, access was negotiated. Starting later that year and over a three-year period, we conducted 67 semi-structured interviews averaging 1 to 1.5 hours long and observed 53 hours of industry events across various stakeholders directly and indirectly involved in SensorCorp’s innovation trajectory and in digital health more broadly (see Table 2). Archival data, including industry analyst reports, press releases, and recordings of past industry events, as well as retrospective interviews, allowed us to piece together SensorCorp’s innovation trajectory going back to 2001. We followed SensorCorp activities in real-time between 2018 and early 2021.

## Data Analysis

Audio recordings of interviews and events were run through a transcription tool, Otter.ai, followed by manual correction. Transcripts and fieldnotes were analyzed in the qualitative research software, ATLAS.ti Cloud, within two to four days of data collection when possible. Our process of data analysis was as follows: We analyzed the first set of interviews (\~10) and event transcripts (4). A broad research question on ecosystems gave structure to the initial interview guide and open coding process, which took a manual “shoe box” and highlighter approach. Following reading and rereading of the transcripts and repeated cycles of coding, we transitioned from open codes to secondary thematic codes (Golden-Biddle & Locke, 2006).

After several rounds of analysis, we decided to use turning points as an analytical tool because they allowed us to understand ecosystem emergence. Turning point analyses stem from a sociological tradition (Laub & Sampson, 1993; Abbott, 1997) and are conducive to process ontologies of innovation. Turning points mark a redirection when something in a trajectory begins to change—a shift in the direction, rhythm, intensities, or momentum in an otherwise undifferentiated flow of time (Abbott, 1997; Emirbayer & Mische, 1998; Mead,

1932). SensorCorp serves as an extreme case (Yin, 2018) of a digital venture experiencing dramatic success and failure, which allowed us to identify clear turning points in its innovation trajectory. Such an approach to understanding change in innovations accounts for an innovation’s multiple and dynamic interactions with other trajectories constituting distinct contexts, including their entrepreneurial contexts. Our perspective involves a focal trajectory (the digital innovation trajectory) and the diverse and continually evolving contextual trajectories—the social, cultural, historical, economic, and policy trajectories shaping the entrepreneurial context in which a digital venture operates (Autio et al., 2014). A turning point is empirically situated and requires the choice of a perspective (in our case, SensorCorp) and a frame of reference (developing an ecosystem de novo). For the unit of analysis, we chose to look for key ecosystem signifiers: (1) a problem-solution pairing to support product-market fit, (2) attracting upstream and downstream complementors, and (3) building network effects and scaling.

As with most process theories (Garud et al., 2017; Cloutier & Langley, 2020), the study of turning points involves curating events, mapping relationships between events, and tracing their change over time (Laub & Sampson, 1993; Nguyen & Loughran, 2018). From the secondary thematic codes, we extrapolated 300+ distinctive events into an Excel inventory. Multiple rounds of analysis and diagramming were needed to disentangle trajectories. We categorized events into clusters, such as regulatory, investment, R&D, care delivery, and so on, which were visually mapped in chronological order, allowing us to map the trajectory dynamics, or the relationships between events across event clusters (Oborn, et al., 2019. In analyzing the visual representations of the trajectories, the ebbs, turning points, and flows of SensorCorp’s innovation trajectory became evident. We narrowed it down to three turning points that we believed had the greatest impact on SensorCorp. We conducted a final round of post-bankruptcy interviews to ask any “burning questions,” including a direct question: “What were key turning points in your innovation trajectory/ in building an ecosystem?” in line with the traditional abductive nature in which turning points research is conducted (Laub & Sampson, 1993). We arrived at the same turning point moments inductively through the interviewees’ responses.

## Findings

## The Digital Pill as a Hybrid Digital Object

SensorCorp’s biosensor had the potential to create value through intracorporeal applications by capturing unique datasets of activity within the body. This virtual digital object was designed to be integrated and analyzed in combination with other digital objects. Being industry and product agnostic, decisions had to be made around a problem-solution pairing, complementor model(s), and value proposition(s). In time, SensorCorp settled on embedding its focal hardware innovation to form digital pills (pivoting from CRT devices). SensorCorp also developed a wearable patch, a skin-adhering sensor, serving as an intermediary device working in conjunction with the biosensor to capture an ingestion timestamp. Upon ingestion of the drug-biosensor combination, stomach acids activate the biocompatible material that the sensor is made of, and a unique signal—a digital code—would be sent to the wearable patch that is worn on the user’s body (e.g., abdomen or chest), using near-field technology. The digital pill itself does not perform within the body; it is a material bearer (Faulkner & Runde, 2019) traveling through the body, collecting data as the biosensor reacts to stomach acids, recording its presence as a virtual digital object. The biosensor is excreted through the digestive system—its job is done!

The patch was developed to capture biometric data related to heart and respiratory rates, activity levels (steps, movements), and sleep/wake cycles, in addition to ingestion data. The data captured by the patch was transmitted via Bluetooth low energy (BLE) technology to a connected mobile device equipped with SensorCorp’s corresponding mobile application (patient app). Users had to have a smartphone or tablet nearby. The patient app supported customization, enabling users to document side effects, self-reported outcomes, and other data as negotiated by partner agreements. The app uploaded all the data to a secure cloud platform where the data was stored, aggregated, and visualized through reporting in the patient app and a customizable clinical dashboard with a drill-down view of a patient population.

Biosensors can be embedded in different drugs for various disease applications, making them product-agnostic. Additionally, SensorCorp built functionality to integrate with hospital electronic medical record systems and third-party devices, such as glucose monitors and blood pressure cuffs as required by individual partners. Alongside the passive datasets automatically created by the biosensor and patch, actively created datasets from user interactions with the app can also be integrated, analyzed, and visualized in different combinations for various use cases. The performance of the different digital objects that comprise the digital pill as a hybrid object can be contextualized in diverse settings. This flexibility enabled SensorCorp to explore multiple complementor models with an evolving configuration stack of digital objects, bringing their platform-enabled biosensor to market in an industry-agnostic manner.

The potential for value comes from the contextual embedding of the virtualized data across a range of use cases—patient self-management, population health management, remote monitoring of clinical trials, patent extensions through the digitization of branded drugs, and so on. Consequently, SensorCorp was able to explore partnerships with specialist pharmacy firms on direct-to-consumer models, managed care providers operating under risk-based contracts, and various pharma firms and generics manufacturers. SensorCorp also considered data-as-a-service models, providing aggregated, anonymized patient data to deliver insights on patient adherence, treatment efficacy, and overall health patterns, thus creating value beyond the digital pill itself. Through their product and service stack, SensorCorp envisioned a future where “every drug in the supply chain” would be a platformenabled ecosystem, underpinned by analytics and data-driven service innovation that would transform the social practices of drug delivery and patient care:

We have to build an ecosystem that enables people to get much more value out of their drug. If you look at where we are today, we have built a platform. There are two companies SensorCorp and PharmB, that are creating drugs that hang off that platform. Our ecosystem includes Amazon Web Services in the U.S. because they’re our cloud services provider. It includes Apple because they’re our principal hardware partner. It includes Optum because they are our national policy partner; it includes all those who are and will be either codeveloping or codelivering products and services through an ecosystem model. It’s not a transaction model. (SensorCorp cofounder, field event)

SensorCorp’s funding partners believed in an ecosystem structure as much as SensorCorp. They viewed emerging Accountable Care Organizations (ACO) policies that necessitated payers and providers working together as a potential facilitator for SensorCorp’s ecosystem:

There are novel risk-bearing entities that can use some of [SensorCorp’s] tools to manage populations on behalf of either payers or providers and take real risk around outcomes. So implicit in that is we believe these tools actually can have value. They don’t have demonstrable value on their own. They have to be part of a larger solution to manage populations. (Digital health investor, interview)

## Identifying a Novel Problem-Solution Pairing for Biosensors

The first turning point involved identifying a novel digital application for the original microscale hardware device, which had the potential to interact with biological systems. The biosensor was developed to capture specific data points through the sensing (detecting or measuring a single event) or monitoring (continuous or repeated tracking) of biological or physiological parameters or discrete stimuli. In its first application, the biosensor was embedded into CRT devices (a pacemaker-like implant) to detect vibrations caused by the heart’s motions, such as the pumping action of the four chambers or respiration. The virtualization of cardiac motions would allow physicians to monitor in real time potential abnormalities in the electrical signals sent out by the implanted device to the lower chambers of the heart (ventricles). There were only a handful of device companies, two of which were SensorCorp partners and investors. The biosensor could easily be embedded into existing regulatory approval programs. A simple dashboard could be integrated to remotely monitor patients through the existing clinical pathways of congestive heart failure. There was a clear path towards digitalization.

However, the scaling potential was low. Like pacemakers, CRT devices have low-volume usage, and SensorCorp struggled to attract new investors, bringing the start-up to a standstill. Their present struggles took the cofounders back to a decade-old Time magazine article on the futuristic notion of tiny scissors floating in the bloodstream, chewing up cholesterol molecules, a type of bodily MEMS application that was science fiction. However, it was a story that always fascinated the entrepreneurs, and they came back to it periodically, particularly when they needed a boost of creativity. This time, there was a difference—the progression in MEMS technology, particularly its advanced miniaturization, led the cofounders to say “maybe the time is right” for alternative intracorporeal uses of MEMS.

A desire to align SensorCorp’s MEMS technology to solve more radical problems instigated a process dynamic known in innovation circles as a “skunkworks, backroom unfunded project,” which brought together a small team of initiativetaking senior engineers. Skunkworks creates the space, often unsanctioned, for experimentation by a small group of employees with a shared interest in a different application of an innovation artifact. A catalyst for a turning point arose when SensorCorp pivoted from its original family of products to ingestible biosensors for medication adherence monitoring:

[We] started thinking about interacting with medical products that have millions, if not trillions of uses per year. And that would be the daily pill … [we found some] really smart people with the same technology base and capability set, very small, very low power novel integrated circuits to say, what would you do if you were going to implant one of these into a pill? (SensorCorp cofounder, Interview)

Cardiac motion sensors were protected by being embedded in CRT devices, but ingestible biosensors would need to survive within bodily tissue to be activated by the pH and moisture in stomach acids to create a data point. It was a digitization aspiration whose feasibility was met by industry leaders with a resounding: “Yeah right, when pigs fly!”

The idea was that you could bring active digital sensors out of existing medical devices… into tissue, which usually would be hostile to digital technology…. But if you could protect it and wait for scale, you could bring those sensors out into the body tissue. (SensorCorp cofounder, Interview)

Despite the skepticism, SensorCorp’s pivot led to a turning point in their innovation trajectory. An early prototype of the ingestible biosensor earned SensorCorp national recognition as a “pioneer” in digital health, according to industry analysts, and led to three successful funding rounds within a four-year period (2006 to 2009) at \$32.7M, \$32M, and \$25M as they found a solution that investors could get behind. The biosensor seemed to be from the realm of science fiction. However, the cofounders who met in graduate school in the late 1980s were seasoned entrepreneurs who had sold two medical device startups in the 1990s for more than \$65 million. They had a “track record”—a mature career trajectory grounded in a history of successful ventures, which reassured investors in taking a risk on their digital venture.

While the momentum generated by SensorCorp’s pivot to ingestible biosensors contributed to this turning point, the skunkworks project and the subsequent prototype were facilitated by a number of kairotic, commensurable trajectories and perceptive future-making decisions by SensorCorp’s leaders within the broader sociotechnical context of advancing digital capabilities and industry servitization. First, with the progressive miniaturization of MEMS in the mid-2000s, sensors reached a size threshold that made a once futuristic application of MEMS an emerging possibility. Ideas on miniaturized technologies can be traced back to theoretical physicists Richard Feynman and Albert Hibbs in the 1950s/1960s, who speculated on denser computer circuitry in the form of an ingestible surgical robot. However, it was not until the 1980s that real-world application of MEMS began in academic research labs, and it was not until the mid-1990s that MEMS evolved to industrial uses as its size decreased over time. With the emergence of the Internet of Things (IoT) and cloud computing in the early 2000s, applications of MEMS technology to physical objects in our everyday world became not only feasible but valuable, as the data produced could be used for a multiplicity of value propositions. SensorCorp was one of the first digital health start-ups to make the connection between these emerging technologies and image a solution where value is derived from the virtualization and contextualization of data through an evolving configuration stack of digital objects comprising the digital pill.

Another trajectory that enabled the development of the ingestible biosensor was a newly available skilled labor force, or the “right digital talent,” as SensorCorp’s cofounder explained. During the dot-com era, healthcare software development was not an attractive career option:

Most of those people would have tended to work on things that were kind of very high volume, like telecommunications, applications sort of in the hundreds of millions to billions, versus low hundreds of thousands. So medical products were sort of the poor stepchild of MEMS. (SensorCorp cofounder, interview)

However, in the early 2000s, the dot-com bubble burst. As people lost their lucrative jobs, they became “hirable,” that is, affordable to SensorCorp. This “ultra-talented” digital workforce was able to tackle the advanced applications of MEMS that many did not have the confidence or foresight to do. One such person was SensorCorp’s new chief technology officer, a seasoned MEMS technologist who invented the first microfabricated valve (microvalve) and held several patents. He was about to take an executive role at a fiber-optic switching company when the SensorCorp cofounders convinced him to join their team. He became instrumental in developing the ingestible biosensor and the digital platform around it.

Finally, in the early 2000s, the trajectory of health financing and investment was shifting. In the previous decades, venture capitalists had considered healthcare a “low-growth” market segment. However, around this time, digital health began to emerge as an “underserved hot category” and a more promising target for venture capitalists:

It’s not that we woke up and realized it’s our role to improve health and wellness for everyone in the world. No, it’s because healthcare as an industry segment has changed. It is now tagged as faster growing, emergent, and high capacity. So, structures of funds hadn’t really changed since the 70s. But healthcare [was] starting to look good. (Partner, healthcare technology venture capital firm, interview)

Investor interest in the application of digital technologies in healthcare was partially conditioned by U.S. healthcare policy, including the 21st Century Cures Act, which sought to “accelerate medical product development and bring new innovations and advances to patients who need them faster and more efficiently.” Healthcare costs were rising, the notion of healthcare consumerism was growing, and digital technologies that proposed solutions to address large categories of expenditure offered value to policymakers, and by extension, investors. SensorCorp, as a first mover, stood out. They were able to articulate their value proposition to funders—the virtualization of medication ingestion in the sociotechnical context of a long-standing healthcare problem—patients not taking their medications was costly to society. A 2018 study published in the Annals of Pharmacotherapy quantified the burden of nonoptimized medication therapy at \$528 billion, or 16% of (U.S.) annual healthcare costs.

New types of venture funds emerged to account for the longer evidence generation timelines and regulatory demands of digital health technologies. By this time, SensorCorp already had early versions of an FDA-approved biosensor technology, while other start-ups were just getting started.

In sum, trajectory dynamics at the dawn of the millennium culminated in a sociotechnical context that was favorable to SensorCorp. There were progressive and incremental changes in trajectories, such as MEMS for the virtualization of intracorporeal data and cloud computing for platformization. The digitalization capacities of IoT as a trajectory were also seeking value complementarity in combination with the cloud, Bluetooth connectivity, and data visualizations, creating standards for the then-emerging IoT platform infrastructure. SensorCorp engaged in imaginatively combining MEMS with the still-new cloud computing capabilities to create one of the first IoT digital health platforms. Then, there were unexpected changes in the directionality of certain trajectories that were complementary to digital venturing, such as the recent availability of highly competent digital resources. Trajectories became synchronized over time to create conditions that were conducive to SensorCorp’s ecosystem aspirations:

It seemed like a moment in time where the nature of that technology, the size, the scale, the power consumption, might be ready to start to really do some sophisticated digital things in existing medical products, if you got the right people involved and it all came together. (SensorCorp cofounder, interview)

There was a “moment in time”—a kairotic moment, when certain trajectories became synergistic, while competing trajectories became less germane. The generative potential of the hardware embedding of the early biosensor and its industry agnosticism (CRT, drugs, or some other application) opened numerous choices for digitization, including futuristic options that could have been paralyzing, especially in the face of skepticism. SensorCorp needed to engage creatively and respond adaptively to an evolving sociotechnical context. This involved remaining open to participating in diverse trajectories, comparing them, and forging connections, while recognizing these trajectories as catalysts for innovation and potential value creation. SensorCorp leveraged future-making practices by “seizing the moment” and extrapolating opportunities as they emerged.

At the same time, because of the evolving policy context, there was a readiness and receptivity in the sociotechnical context for novel digital solutions. Policymakers were overtly supporting digital health through new laws. Investors were developing more products to accommodate healthcare’s nuances in order to facilitate financing for digital health solutions. This enabled contextual embedding, radical experimentation through skunkworks, and stitching together trajectories through prompt actions as novelty—once unimaginable—was emerging. It became encapsulated in the company mascot of flying pigs and its motto, “let’s fly.”

Aspirations towards digitalization led SensorCorp to experiment with various complementor models, each incorporating customized platform components that varied based on the service delivery context. Futuremaking involved adopting a perspective on distant future trajectories, which were used to construct future-oriented value propositions. This approach enabled SensorCorp to build different imaginaries of ecosystems to see “what sticks.” Our analysis focuses on three complementor models— specialty pharmacy, healthcare provider organizations, and pharma complementors, where sociotechnical enactment was considered most feasible. In an early instance, SensorCorp partnered with a large pharmacy chain, Village Pharmacy, building on the emerging consumerism movement captured by the notion of the “passive patient” transitioning to the “digitally empowered patient.” As one interviewee explained: “Healthcare is moving from a Blockbuster to a Netflix model because consumers want the freedom of choice.” Village Pharmacy’s specialist compounding services allowed local pharmacists to encapsulate the biosensor with any medication to create direct-to-consumer digital pills. Village Pharmacy targeted the busy adult children of the aging baby boomer population to take advantage of the economic purchasing power of these tech-savvy consumers:

Anyone taking several medications knows how easy it can be to lose track of whether or not you’ve taken the correct tablets that day. Add to that complex health issues and families caring for loved ones who may not live with them, and you can appreciate the benefits of an information service that helps patients get the most from their treatments and for families to help them remain well. The partnership with Village Pharmacy created significant value for SensorCorp in the eyes of investors, who, in the early 2010s, propelled by the introduction of the Accountable Care Act (ACA) and recent regulatory dynamics towards consumerdriven health plans in the U.S., predicted that people would shop for coverage in a marketplace oriented toward a businessto-consumer model. However, the Village Pharmacy program attracted limited interest from customers (as did consumerdriven health plans over time). It became evident that although direct-to-consumer products required a less complex ecosystem structure compared with navigating the pharma or provider reimbursement models, building a sustained and stable user base was a challenge:

Consumer utilization purchases falls off the cliff after 90 days, so you’re constantly having to acquire new members. It becomes elusively expensive to build a meaningful business that’s consumer focused. You’re asking the consumer to do something they’ve never done before. (Venture capitalist, interview)

The relationship with Village Pharmacy dissolved after the oneyear pilot when SensorCorp abandoned direct-to-consumer models and pharmacies as a type of complementor. In a pivot, SensorCorp sought to bring together new provider collaborators, once again, building on the ACA, which mandated that Medicare transition from a fee-for-service model (payment based on the quantity of care provisioned) to valuebased reimbursement (payment for outcomes). The latter would, in time, require remote monitoring capabilities, creating opportunities for start-ups like SensorCorp to offer digital solutions to support providers in gathering outcomes data.

To secure complementors, SensorCorp employed a futuremaking practice that we call temporal propositioning, a narrative instrument that builds on fictional expectations. Temporal propositioning constructs storylines for collaboration that start with problematizing the past through discursive means, such as history-laden stories, move on to legitimizing present collaboration, and close with hypothesizing shared value in a distant future. The potential for digitalization was conditioned by the promise of value-based care/purchasing post-ACA regulations. There was a collective expectation that provider organizations and pharma would eventually be compensated based on clinical outcomes rather than resource consumption. The data generated by the biosensors could support clinical decision-making at the point of care and enable population-level analytics and alerts, fostering remote engagement with noncompliant patients. However, the shift to value-based care was in its early trajectory and later slowed down as new government leadership took power. The value of digital pills lies in the data they provide, but digitalization requires larger organizational process changes, specifically clinicians engaging with patients through novel care delivery models, such as patient-centered medical homes, which would focus on proactive medication adherence support and selfmanagement. These models were just gaining traction in hospital systems, and SensorCorp conducted pilot programs with over 10 such hospitals. However, many of these relationships did not move beyond the pilot phase, a phenomenon that analysts called “pilotitis”—the tendency to launch small-scale pilots without scaling to full implementations. Despite this, pilots offered SensorCorp a low-risk, lower-cost way to gather data, validate efficacy, and build an evidence base through published studies in order to bolster legitimacy. Nonetheless, a scalable commercialization pathway with providers in a fee-for-service context was not financially viable at that time.

Another complementor SensorCorp prioritized was pharma manufacturers, who were experiencing the aggregate challenges of two trajectories: the widespread expiration of patents on brand-name drugs (patent cliffs) and the declining pace of blockbuster drug production.

We’ve had 5 decades of getting slower less successful and more expensive. (SensorCorp cofounder, field event)

You can continue platform development over decades, every single drug you have in your portfolio, to expand label claims, to use these tools for discovery.”(SensorCorp executive, industry conference)</td><td>“10 years ahead, there won’t be a drug in the supply chain that isn’t a digital object. Your grandchildren are going to be incredulous that you put things in your body and have no idea whether they gave you any benefit.”(SensorCorp executive, interview)</td></tr></table>

Eroom’s law captures the cumulative effect of the patent lifecycle and the progressively growing difficulty in sustaining new drug development. When a drug loses patent protection, lower-priced generics can “shave off between 80 to 90 percent of its revenue,” raising concerns about the longterm viability of traditional pharma R&D, which has become slower and more expensive. While this creates public value by lowering drug costs, it conflicts with pharma manufacturers’ financial incentives to maintain brand exclusivity. To attract pharma complementors, SensorCorp projected itself into pharma’s patent cliff trajectory by offering incremental modifications through digitizing branded drugs nearing patent expiration. Biosensors could extend patent life, providing a longer window to generate additional revenue.

Table 4 demonstrates SensorCorp’s temporal proposition to pharma manufacturers. First, the traditional pharma business model is identified as outdated and unstable. Present collaboration in a proposed ecosystem is legitimized as a secure solution to reduce business model risk. A distant future, “where grandchildren are incredulous at our current lack of monitoring,” elucidates the potential value of participating in SensorCorp’s proposed ecosystem. This vision also highlights SensorCorp’s aspirations for network effects, integrating a data-driven value framework into the future of drug delivery systems.

An early pharma partner, a large firm that we call BigPharma, invested \$24M to secure an exclusive license on key conditions, including organ transplantation. Despite a pilot study with 20 heart transplant patients that demonstrated improved outcomes using digitized medicine and an initial understanding for the exploration of additional disease applications, the partnership with BigPharma ultimately ended. A SensorCorp executive explained:

So much of [the partnership] was about locking up the rights. It turns out what they wanted to do was make sure nobody else gets to sell a digital product [drug]. And that is death for an early-stage company that’s just trying to get scale and adoption. (Interview)

After the dissolution of the BigPharma partnership, SensorCorp struggled to secure another pharma partner, despite the buzz surrounding the biosensor. This made evident the difficulty in accurately predicting future trajectory dynamics. While potential pharma partners may have collectively aligned with SensorCorp’s vision of a value-based care future, especially given supporting material and policy directives, the distant nature of this envisioned future made many firms hesitant. The future felt too remote, prompting many risk-averse pharma companies, shaped by their cautious organizational identities, to adopt a “wait-and-see” approach:

Everything in pharma is about the benefit/risk balance. If you’ve put \$2 billion and a huge team to knowing everything about how a product works over a decade of study, if at the last minute, you’re going to put in a sensor that you don’t have years of data on, you don’t have years of how the technology works, it just adds a really unnecessary complication or risk. (Chief digital officer, pharma complementor, interview)

Additionally, while some pharma firms viewed patent extensions through digitization as a potential future strategy, only a few were directly “feeling the crunch” of the impending patent cliffs enough to motivate collaboration with SensorCorp. Eventually, SensorCorp secured a nonexclusive agreement with a midsized pharma firm, PharmB, which not only agreed to partner but invested \$88 million in the start-up, gaining a joint stake in SensorCorp’s ecosystem development. PharmB, a “one-drug-wonder,” was SensorCorp’s predicted candidate, experiencing the patent cliff and Eroom’s law as predicted—but in the present. Facing the imminent loss of exclusivity on its sole blockbuster drug, one of the highest revenue-generating drugs in the industry at the time, PharmB saw SensorCorp’s digitization capabilities as a last resort to compete with generic alternatives and extend the drug’s profit lifeline:

The stakes for [PharmB] were huge. Global sales of [Xenfil] in 2014 were \$4.14 billion, accounting for 40% of the company’s group sales … As a patent cliff beckons, [PharmB] was seeking a lifeline with technology … [PharmB] would be one of the few pharma companies that put their hand up to say, “look going digital should be a key part of our growth strategy.” (SensorCorp executive, interview)

The PharmB partnership marked a critical turning point, evidenced by the momentum it generated through sociotechnical enactment in regulatory and market access contexts. First, the partnership led to the landmark regulatory approval of the first branded digital pill, Xenfil-digi, in the first new drug category approved by the FDA since 1982. In doing so, it established a precedent for future drug-biosensor combinations, setting a model for pharma complementarity. Second, PharmB collaborated with PBMCorp, a health insurance intermediary, to launch Xenfil-digi through two Medicaid community-based mental health programs in

Florida and Virginia. This effectively extended the ecosystem to providers and patients, creating a market access pathway for value-based purchasing, which demonstrated the feasibility of sociotechnical enactment. Third, the PharmB partnership elevated SensorCorp’s standing, propelling the start-up to “unicorn” status, with a valuation exceeding \$1.5 billion—a rare achievement in the venture capital space. Achieving these milestones for digitalization would not have been possible without a pharma complementor. PharmB was the first and only partner with whom SensorCorp successfully developed an end-to-end ecosystem encompassing both upstream and downstream complementarity. With this ecosystem blueprint in place, SensorCorp’s next step was to recruit additional pharma partners and build network effects to realize their aspirations: extending the patent lifecycle of every brand-name drug through digitization and embedding remote monitoring of medication adherence as a standard practice in patient care via digitalization.

## Stifled Digitalization Compromises Network Effects

In mid-2020, a new momentum of change arose, reshaping the slowly emerging digital innovation ecosystem. A dramatic turning point brought SensorCorp to apply for unanticipated bankruptcy with a snowballing of events. Just six months earlier, SensorCorp was poised for an initial public offering, according to multiple investor market reports. However, PharmB declined to invest further, leading other investors and potential pharma partners to “backpedal.” As noted by industry analysts, this turning point was unforeseen: “From a vaunted healthcare unicorn to a petitioner for Chapter 11 bankruptcy (in just 6 months), how did the company falter?” Despite their feat in digitization with a futuristic, first-in-class digital object, SensorCorp’s innovation journey culminated in a cautionary tale of unmet potential in digitalization. In this section, we examine the role of attentionality and related agentic inattentiveness—avoidance and overrationalizations—that contributed to the dissolution of SensorCorp’s emerging ecosystem.

First, SensorCorp had foreseen the challenges posed by Eroom’s Law and patent cliffs “eating into traditional revenue streams.” This anticipation led them to position digital pills as a means for pharma manufacturers to extract new value through patent extensions by digitizing branded drugs and leveraging real-world data in value-based contracting—a future-oriented example of digitalization. While the slowing pace and rising cost of R&D, often likened to an immutable law of nature, indeed materialized, the anticipated collapse of the traditional “blockbuster drug” was less impactful on pharma profits than Eroom’s Law had suggested:

When they built [SensorCorp] 15 years ago, they thought that drug companies were not going to continue to enjoy the margin growth rate of, you know, 15-20% year-over-year profitability increases. So, if you’re a company that is built on the premise of that pressure and the pressure never shows up, it becomes a challenge. (SensorCorp executive, post-bankruptcy interview)

Meanwhile, behind the shadows, pharma had begun shaping alternative futures to mitigate the profitability challenges posed by Eroom’s Law. This included an industry-wide pivot and a shift in clinical focus that SensorCorp failed to recognize or give credence to:

Patent cliffs aren’t a surprise to anybody. You plan for it for 15 years, from the day you start creating the drug, but [Eroom’s law] is changing the way we are doing research. We are shifting focus from the blockbuster model into precision medicine. We already see this in rare diseases with genomics, personalized immunotherapy treatments, and stem cell therapies. (Chief digital officer, pharma complementor, interview)

The shift toward precision medicine offered a promising path for pharma to sustain or even exceed the profit levels achieved through traditional blockbuster drugs. However, SensorCorp either overlooked these emerging strategies or failed to anticipate their significance. Their focus on digitizing branded drugs proved to be an overly incremental solution to pharma’s “existential problem,” yielding profits that were negligible compared to the industry’s accustomed scale.

The second alternative future—a stagnated one—was constrained by existing insurance structures. Insurers were slow to integrate digital health offerings into their drug formularies, leaving patients to bear the costs out-of-pocket. Although separate digital health formularies exist today, they were yet to be developed when SensorCorp was building its ecosystem. At the time, insurers cited multiple challenges with digital health, including evaluating clinical efficacy, costeffectiveness, regulatory uncertainties, reimbursement complexities, data security, and a lack of patient engagement. These factors contributed to skepticism about the value of digital health products. Crucially, insurers had no inherent incentive to cover these innovations, as doing so would either increase premiums for beneficiaries or erode profit margins. While SensorCorp argued that investing in medication adherence would, in the long term, improve patient outcomes and reduce healthcare costs, the “membership churn” in the insurance market discouraged insurers from committing to upstream programs like digital pills. For insurers, the longterm benefits did not outweigh the immediate financial risks.

Folks are on a plan for two and a half years on average, and then they move on to something else. So, if you’re that payer, do you want to pay for something that may be expensive, may have great long-term health benefits, but you anticipate that patient is going to be in somebody else’s plan in the future, and then they’re going to benefit. (Pharmaceutical policy analyst, interview)

Another stagnated future was the transition to value-based reimbursement, which did not gain the momentum the industry had anticipated. This shift was a critical financial prerequisite for SensorCorp’s medication adherence value proposition:

With the passage of Accountable Care Act, I thought, holy cow, this is gonna be an absolute boom for all these technologies. I heard so much about, “well, we’re not in risk contracts now, but we know that this is the kind of care management technology that we’ll need. So, let’s go ahead and spend a million dollars on some clinical cost and quality toolset.” But then when the value-based stuff never came, it was like, “well, wait a minute, why do we actually need all this stuff if it’s gonna remain primarily fee-for-service.” (SensorCorp executive, post-bankruptcy interview)

The ACA’s value-based care programs focused on government contracts with provider organizations, aiming to inspire commercial payers, pharma, and other healthcare actors to proactively adopt similar models. However, despite ongoing policy discussions around value-based drug reimbursement, regulatory reforms remained pending, and pharma manufacturers had little motivation to digitize existing drugs to generate real-world evidence. Similarly, payers showed no urgency to negotiate value-based drug pricing that could incentivize drug digitization. Progress was sluggish, and the envisioned future of value-based care remained stalled:

We will see refiling of generics plus in the future. We refile together with a digital component, instead of trying to only find additional new indications. And they will get some kind of protection, but the whole framework is not ready yet. It’s slow moving. The timing is not right yet. (Chief digital officer, pharma complementor, interview)

The stalled transition to value-based care had a downstream impact on provider complementors and frontline clinicians. While policy administrators promoted digital solutions that track medication adherence as valuable tools for achieving quality metrics and improving patient outcomes, the realities on the ground told a different story. Physician compensation models and clinical workflows, although now changing, were not designed to accommodate the additional effort required to proactively monitor medication adherence, leaving a critical gap between policy ambitions and practical implementation. As one physician executive explained:

If I see my patients for med checks every 15 minutes and that’s about 40% of my income, and if all of a sudden, I don’t need to do med checks anymore, you’ve hit my bottom line … you’re going to be a threat to the current economic model of noncompliance…. There’s not a fee that one could use for this type of remote monitoring or investments needed to infrastructure. (Interview)

Remote monitoring billing codes, which are now widely available, were not in place during SensorCorp’s time as a digital venture. Some might argue that SensorCorp was “ahead of the curve,” operating in a sociotechnical context that was evolving too slowly to support their value propositions of improved longterm health outcomes across a fragmented healthcare system.

There are doctor, patient, organizational, and systemic issues, and fundamentally, the rules have to change. Rules drive ecosystems, ecosystems drive business models, business models facilitate or inhibit innovation. The rules have to do with care coordination, reimbursement, intellectual property, policy, etc. But that’s why it building an ecosystem takes so long. Because it's so hard to change the rules of how sick care is structured. (SensorCorp cofounder, interview)

A final alternative future emerged from the tension between SensorCorp’s innovation trajectory and the rising mental health social justice movement, what can be described as a historically qualified future. An underestimated challenge in the PharmB collaboration was that Xenfil, an antipsychotic drug, inherently linked SensorCorp’s innovation with the stigmatized history of mental illness. Digitizing Xenfil positioned the technology as part of a future that could be viewed as a surveillance tool, driven by intrusive data collection. SensorCorp underestimated the profound impact of the painful history of asylums, with critics framing digital medicine as a “digital asylum.” While the value of the technology could be framed in terms of improved medical outcomes, it triggered fears, as the envisioned ecosystem became associated with a stigmatized, historically troubling system of care:

Police and behavioral control have been involved in the mental health system since the beginning. You go back to Bedlam, to those asylums of old…. They’re missing the patient autonomy and patient rights. Not just the right to volunteer for this treatment but the right to refuse treatment, which is really important in the psychiatric context, where trust is already hanging by a thread— because of history. (Patient advocate, interview)

SensorCorp participated and hosted panels on ethics in digital health as part of its self-regulatory efforts, bringing together industry and academic leaders, which were included in our observational dataset. The first author’s field notes document a prevailing sense of indifference toward the social and moral implications of behavioral monitoring and a noticeable lack of urgency in addressing the concerns of the “end user”:

An audience member asks a question about patient rights. [SensorCorp representative] seems quick to quell this line of questioning: “It’s very important that [the digital pill] is not a surveillance tool.” No one in the audience seems convinced. But their tone of voice is too intimidating, and no one seems eager to push this further. (Author fieldnotes)

At the various SensorCorp presentations attended by the first author, she observed a noticeable lack of meaningful engagement with the social justice movement in mental health, which advocates for autonomy, dignity, equality, and other forms of biographical value. While such concerns conflicted with SensorCorp’s value proposition, other pharma industry executives seemed more attuned to these complexities. One interviewee remarked: “You’re picking a new space with ingestibles, which is like crazy new, but you’re also tackling the giant, messy, historically complex topic.” Despite their intrigue with the SensorCorp/PharmB collaboration, most pharma executives were reluctant to fully embrace the venture, hesitant to navigate an uncertain future shaped by a familiar and complicated historical context:

The risk of lawsuits and the risk of bad press… you could get a huge backlash; again, this is still an unknown entity…. You would not willingly associate all of that unknown with your very well-known product as you’re bringing it to market, you could tank your company, and it’s not worth it. (Chief digital officer, pharma complementor, interview)

The “bad press” surrounding Xenfil-Digi was captured in media headlines such as “Worries about Biomedical Big Brother,” casting the product as an “Ethically Hard Pill to Swallow.”<sup>4</sup> For pharma complements from a self-proclaimed risk-averse sector, who were inclined to “watch and learn” from the Xenfil-Digi experience, the media response was far from reassuring. These Orwellian portrayals of surveilled mental health patients reshaped the perceived value of the platform-enabled ecosystem, one with theoretical generativity and limitless potential, yet constrained by the complex sociotechnical context in which it existed.

In conclusion, the digital pill exemplified digitization by converting medication ingestion into a measurable datapoint, effectively creating a first-of-its-kind digital object. The platform generated flexible, reconfigurable data streams that held the latent potential to revolutionize patient care through adherence monitoring and improved health outcomes, making it a promising fit for a value-based healthcare system. The product stack was designed to accommodate various complementor models, such as partnerships with pharma, payers, or providers, which had potential for value creation through network effects. However, these data-generating capabilities, platform flexibility, and product replicability lacked value without a sociotechnical context that could render the data meaningful. SensorCorp struggled to attract scalable, revenue-generating complementors beyond PharmB, as pharma manufacturers enacted competing futures around precision medicine. Simultaneously, providers experienced stagnant futures with the slow transition to valuebased care reimbursement and the absence of supporting care delivery models. SensorCorp miscalculated the readiness of pharma and healthcare systems to integrate digital pills, failing to explore transitional use cases—a lack of attention to evolving trajectory dynamics. Further complicating the digitalization process were concerns related to privacy, surveillance, and the stigma surrounding mental health, which undermined the technology’s broader acceptance. SensorCorp failed to meaningfully engage with these trajectory dynamics and pressed forward with an incomplete ecosystem design. Ultimately, SensorCorp failed to establish a stable, sustainable product stack that could consistently provide value to pharma, providers, and patients alike.

## Discussion

## Ecosystem Dynamics in Digital Venturing

We began with the premise that digital innovation ecosystems often originate as digital ventures (Nambisan et al., 2019; Yoo et al., 2012). Then, we set out to understand how a digital venture enacts a digital innovation ecosystem de novo. We demonstrated that ecosystem dynamics, the work involved in pursuing layered modularity, complementarity, and network effects, typically associated with orchestrating established ecosystems, begin and persist within digital ventures, even when the ecosystem structure itself remains largely conceptual or aspirational. Building on recent literature on the co-constitution of digitization and digitalization (Baiyere et al., 2023; Lyytinen, 2022), our findings suggest that digital venturing unfolds within tensions arising from the interplay between digitization’s generative capabilities—the technical enablement of digital features—and digitalization’s capacity and readiness to deploy those digital features in everyday practices. These tensions are not merely operational but temporal, complicating digital venturing in that digitization can progress at a different pace, one distinct from the temporal rhythms of digitalization strategies.

To elaborate, digital ventures are often launched with the forward-looking aim to address future needs (Lehmann et al., 2022). However, these ventures simultaneously depend on those envisioned futures—the transformed sociotechnical contexts capable of integrating digital capabilities—to begin materializing in the present, to initiate ecosystems. This creates temporal dissonance: a misalignment between the rapid advancements of digitization and the slower, adaptive pace of digitalization. Paradoxically, this dissonance provides fertile ground for novelty, as the increasingly prolific capabilities of digitization can act as a catalyst for digitalization, enabling institutional actors to reimagine new realities and enact novel future-making practices in the present. For instance, digital technologies may be leveraged to deliver low-cost, high-quality healthcare, encouraging healthcare policymakers to enact programs and policies to this effect. Achieving digitalization often requires a collective temporal orientation: shared expectations of the future among potential ecosystem participants (e.g., through a focus on value-based care). Such alignment can inspire speculative designs, mobilize storytelling, and encourage complementors or potential proponents to pilot specific use cases. These efforts temporarily enact digital innovation ecosystems, fostering collaboration and experimentation around the digital venture.

At the same time, the value of digitization is moderated by the temporal readiness for digitalization, which may vary across ecosystem actors. Regulatory inertia, institutional misalignment, and end users’ sociocultural adaptations may create different priorities and misaligned time horizons that compromise collective expectations and hinder ecosystem formation. Additionally, ecosystem contexts evolve in nonlinear and unpredictable ways. Competing visions of alternate futures, rooted in divergent complementor priorities or externalities, can render collective expectations fractious.

Achieving temporal synchronicity between digitization and digitalization is a delicate balance. If achieved too quickly, novelty may diminish, and market entry to competitors might be simplified. If achieved too late, the venture risks irrelevance in an evolved context. If temporal dissonance persists, the digital venture may be too early (“ahead of its time”), leading to generativity without adoption. Managing temporal dissonance requires digital ventures to not only forge ahead but to continuously revisit and challenge past assumptions.

Traditional orchestration frameworks, which emphasize firmlevel strategies, present-focused flexibility, and centralized agency, fall short in addressing such temporal tensions between digitization and digitalization. This gap is especially pronounced in digital venturing, which is inherently future oriented, particularly when introducing a digital object that is the first of its kind. We build on Nambisan et al. (2017) and others who emphasize that ecosystem orchestration involves distributed agency. While a digital venture may serve as a focal actor, agency is far more dispersed and ephemeral than in established ecosystems. Digital ventures do not rely on predefined agency but instead creatively disrupt the status quo, imagining partnerships, projecting value, and proposing assumptions to support the emergence of a future ecosystem. Indeed, as we illustrate in the following section, agency in digital venturing is not only distributed but decentralized; arising through collective dynamics, situated relationships, and interactions within and across broader sociotechnical contexts, roles, and trajectories (Garud & Karnøe, 2005; Mead, 1932). Whereas much of the ecosystem literature focuses on value capture and appropriation from the orchestrator’s perspective, our findings underscore the importance of value across both willing and inadvertent participants (e.g., mental health advocates). Thus, we highlight the criticality of demand-side externalities in digital venturing. Calling attention to the broader forces constituting ecosystem dynamics does not deny the power of intentionality and action but rather emphasizes that ecosystem emergence transcends orchestrators’ capabilities.

Since ecosystem dynamics in digital venturing are not confined by present realities, fictionality becomes an important source of creativity, motivating decision-making and showing how actors can be incorporated into a potential future ecosystem. Creativity inspires innovation, legitimizes digital ventures under conditions of uncertainty, and motivates complementors and stakeholders to imagine and align with future ecosystems. However, fictionality can also create a false sense of progress, leading ventures to overestimate their readiness for digitalization (or indeed digitization) and the potential for ecosystem emergence. As our case study illustrates, this doubleedged nature of creativity can result in detrimental consequences if speculative practices outpace practical embedding in sociotechnical contexts (or the alternative, where digitalization outpaces digitization with exaggerated capabilities that do not stand up to expectations of use).

In summary, the role of fictionality and the creative, distributed, and decentralized agencies involved in navigating the temporal tensions between the generativity potential of digitization and the capacity and readiness for digitalization necessitate an alternative, complementary lens to orchestration. We propose the concept of cultivating imagined ecosystems—a preorchestration practice involved in enacting an ecosystem that has yet to emerge, one that must first be imagined. We suggest that through cultivating imagined ecosystems, we can further our understanding of the mutual constitution and temporal dissonance in the unfolding of digitization and digitalization in digital venturing. Next, we synthesize and theorize three elements of cultivating imagined ecosystems from our findings.

## Cultivating Imagined Ecosystems

We theorize “cultivating” as consisting of three contiguous, interrelated, continuously evolving temporal elements: the passive conditioning influence of entrepreneurial trajectory dynamics; the diversity of digital futures and consequently sensing, producing, and enacting those futures through digital future-making; and the capacity to shape and be shaped through sociotechnical enactment.<sup>5</sup> We call these temporal elements because each concept is inherently engaged with the past, directed toward the future, and takes place in the present (Emirbayer & Mische, 1998; Mead, 1932). Cultivating reflects an unfolding, a happening, or a becoming that involves the agency of focal actors but goes beyond them. The concept of cultivating is sensitive to the future-making aspects of digital venturing and accommodates the creative, distributed, decentered nature of agency, where fictionality becomes an important resource for building layered modularity, complementarity, and network effects. Next, we elaborate on each element and its interrelationships.

The conditioning influence of entrepreneurial trajectories stems from the technological, social, cultural, historical, economic, regional, and policy contexts of innovation, in which a digital venture operates<sup>6</sup> (Autio et al., 2014). Entrepreneurial trajectory dynamics cultivate potentialities, which are new and evolving conditions of possibilities, such as emerging digital capabilities that can be sensed and acted on through future-making practices. Conditioning exerts a passive influence through the exposure to possibilities for action along continually unfolding and recursively interacting trajectories (Mead, 1932; Abbott, 1997). Correspondingly, sensing involves being attuned to unfolding trajectory dynamics—the changing direction, intensity, and momentum of trajectories and their interaction with each other (Oborn, et al., 2019). It is how evolving conditions of possibility are noticed and acted on by focal actors, complementors, and others with a direct or indirect interest in the digital venture or the broader entrepreneurial environment. For example, by the end of SensorCorp’s innovation trajectory, value-based care was still strong in healthcare discourse; however, the degree of commitment and momentum of change to reimbursement and patient care models supporting value-based care had slowed down. Sensing such changes to the temporal qualities of trajectory dynamics, which SensorCorp failed to do, was critical to the coevolution of digitization and digitalization in the innovation trajectory of the de novo digital category.

In this way, sensing is constitutive of future-making, structuring social (in)actions, decisions made, and relationships formed (Beckert, 2013), with implications for ecosystem emergence. We define digital future-making as what organizational actors do to imagine, enact, and give form to a multiplicity of futures to influence or be influenced by digital technologies within diverse and continuously evolving sociotechnical contexts (Schlagwein et al., 2024; Oomen et al., 2022). Digital futuremaking offers a practice-based understanding of engaging with sociotechnical contexts of digital ventures, where imaginaries become realizable courses of action (Jasanoff & Kim, 2015; Comi & Whyte, 2018). In focusing on what actors actually “do” together, we gain insight into how actors envisage and pursue different product combinations, problem-solution pairings, and complementor models, which, when successful, become enacted in sociotechnical contexts of use, changing wider social practices. Digital future-making can involve tracking the trajectories of multiple sociotechnical imaginaries and their relationships to each other and the digital venture. At the same time, digital future-making is decentered through the influence of externalities, outside the direct control of a digital venture. In this way, imagined futures become reality in the present through sociotechnical enactment.

Sociotechnical enactment in digital venturing is influenced by the capacity and readiness for digitalization, in particular, the action potential within institutional, organizational, social, and localized contexts of deploying and scaling novel digital solutions. Sociotechnical enactment is conditioned by trajectory dynamics where the broader entrepreneurial context influences complementors and their evaluation of a digital venture. A new digital product requires interest and capacity on the part of adopters and the (re)configuration of the sociotechnical environment within which it is expected to be embedded (Baiyere et al., 2023; Lyytinen, 2022). While entrepreneurial trajectory dynamics are also sociotechnical in nature, they exert a more passive influence on future-making practices; sociotechnical enactment is situated and active, both driving and being driven by the shaping effects of future-making.

In sociotechnical enactment, there is a mutual influence between the capacity for future-making and its intended contexts of use, comprised of the social actors (individuals, organizations), social systems (institutions, norms, user practices), and technological systems (tools, infrastructures, interoperability) that are actively shaped and cocreated through diverse interactions. This necessitates interaction at the technical domains of software/hardware and the humans who use the digital innovation, as well as the digital-digital and digital-physical interactions in situated contexts and confined spaces (e.g., frontline clinical care). When sociotechnical enactment is successful, like when a new regulatory pathway was created for Xenfil-Digi, as de novo digital category, it can shape broader trajectory dynamics, such as the availability of the new pathway for other drug-device combinations, creating potential for scaling and network effects.

In SensorCorp’s case, while the digitization (data and analytics) capabilities of facilitating clinician-supported medication adherence and patient engagement had generative potential, the sociotechnical infrastructure underpinning digitalization was not ready to realize value from digital pills. In platform-enabled ecosystems, such as with SensorCorp, value is experienced through the application of data in social practices (in patient care management, elder care, clinical research, and so on). The generative potential of data and platformization relies on changes to the way work is done—routines, competencies, shared meanings—in everyday life (Shove et al., 2012). In other words, digital capabilities are wrapped around service innovations to social practices—for example, how providers are reimbursed, how physicians are compensated, and how workflows are centered on population health management and the routines of new ancillary staff. Network effects are reliant on and intertwined with evolving social practices, exemplifying the mutual constitution of digitization and digitalization (Baiyere et al., 2023).

## Three Cultivation Practices

Next, we outline three cultivation practices involved in enacting an imagined ecosystem: perceptive response, prefigurative action, and inattentive inaction, each working in distinct ways with unique digital futures—emergent, desired distant, and alternative futures. Figure 4 summarizes the elements of cultivating imagined ecosystems as they unfold within the tensions produced by the generative capabilities of digitization and the capacity for digitalization, as well as the three cultivation practices that emerged from the findings of our study.

Perceptive response involves reacting to once distant futures that have progressively transitioned to become nearer and more tangible emergent futures. Emergent futures emphasize continuity between the present and the future; the relative nearness of the future is partly due to decreasing ambiguity (Beckert, 2021). Actors engage with emerging resources made available through trajectory dynamics that are incrementally progressive (e.g., ongoing miniaturization of MEMS), synergistic (Cloud, MEMS, and healthcare regulations), or when competing trajectories stall (e.g., dot.com bust). Perceptive response inspires change of a more immediate nature (e.g., pivoting from CRT). Cultivating is achieved through present moment discernment, making prompt choices, and taking decisive action on which trajectories to stitch together and develop into digital ventures. Future-making may entail technology repurposing, radical experimentation with semi-autonomous trial and error-led prototyping (e.g., SensorCorp’s skunkworks project and regulators’ development of novel pathways for health technologies), or an early recognition of potential (e.g., IoT possibilities in healthcare), even in the face of skepticism (e.g., critics who said, “when pigs fly!”). Cultivating also involves sociotechnical readiness (e.g., emerging specialized venture funds), where there is an economic, cultural, and institutional willingness to experiment with existing paradigms and to create new structures, policies, and standards to accommodate novel digital ventures to the extent that they are contextualized with the direction of broader change and trajectory dynamics. When cultivating is successful, there is an air of serendipity, but it is actors’ discerning reactions to unfolding events that enable creative discovery, rather than merely reflecting established capabilities and relationships.

Prefigurative action involves engaging with desired distant futures, often through rhetorical persuasive devices and anticipatory experimentation that proactively collate trajectories to motivate ecosystem dynamics (e.g., collaborative arrangements) for future value realization. A desired distant future takes the perspective of a particular innovation actor, as it is a focal actor’s preferred version of a distant future. Cultivating focuses explicitly on creating credible predictions to drive present action through “as if” realities (Beckert, 2021)—assuming the world will unfold in ways where a digital innovation will be readily institutionalized and integrated into everyday life. The confidence to act “as if” an imaginary were present reality proactively prefigures a reference point for decision-making, with stories articulating how plans will unfold, how value will be created, and what the future ecosystem will look like, with a (hoped-for) performative effect. For example, temporal propositions offer a storyline for collaboration, constructed by problematizing the past, legitimizing present collaboration, and hypothesizing shared value. Policy instruments (e.g., value-based care, ACA) create fictional expectations that legitimize yet-to-be future states around which actors converge. In this way, shared imagined futures can be performative (e.g., BigPharma’s early exclusive agreement with SensorCorp to “lock up the (future) market”). Other examples of prefigurative action involve the piloting of new digital technologies, allowing a digital venture to demonstrate how an ecosystem might be structured. However, distant futures are abstract, hypothetical, and lack continuity with present arrangements (Augustine et al., 2019). Cultivating must overcome a greater level of ambiguity, a frequent challenge for nascent ecosystems (Dattée et al., 2018). Building credibility in a desired distant future may involve ignoring the multiplicity of distant futures (Beckert, 2021), creating a false sense of certainty around how and when a potential future might be realized. This may lead to unrealistic valuations of a digital venture. Cultivation practices that fail to account for the inherent fallacies of desired distant futures tend to hinge on “happy path” trajectory dynamics, which may ultimately lead to value dissipation.

This takes us to the third element of imagined ecosystems: inattentive inaction. Attentionality is an agentic awareness of the degree of influence of broader contextual conditions and their evolution over time. Inattentive inaction highlights the importance of attention—or, in our case, inattention—in digital venturing and the potential development of multiple possible futures: alternative futures and so-called zemblanity. Alternative futures emerge when innovators do not adequately consider the multiplicity of fictional expectations or countertrajectories stemming from past conditioning of practices and the individual agencies of actors. As noted in our case of the mental health advocates, expectations are impacted by the struggles of actors, as some might counter the future as projected, drawing on their own cultural resources to create historically qualified alternative futures, an important politics of expectations (Beckert, 2021). The inability, or inaction, when integrating tangential actors into a digital venture, influences how futures unfold, leading to undesirable externalities compromising ecosystem emergence. While digital ventures may aim to retain control of imagined futures, inattentiveness to such politics and inactivity in counterproposing expectations can lead to the dissipation of value. Inattentiveness can be shaped by hyperprojectivity (Mische, 2014). In our case, field events, such as symposiums and industry conferences, allowed open debates, locating problems, and scenario setting, with explicit consideration being given to reflecting on SensorCorp’s future imaginaries, thus becoming sites of hyperprojectivity. The consequence of such hyperprojectivity may yield inattentiveness to alternative interpretations of the past and oppositional imaginaries, such as those proposed by mental health advocates. This can increase the escalation of competing futures with dramatic, unexpected consequences for the imagined ecosystem. Inattention is also influenced by contextual cues from institutional sociotechnical imaginaries (e.g., policies on healthcare cost management) that underpin collective expectations, which shape what gets noticed or ignored in the process of cultivation. A failure to notice visible but unexpected events can lead to rigidity in expectations and inflexibility over time, where digital ventures struggle to evolve within changing sociotechnical contexts.

## Conclusion

Our study advances the understanding of digital venturing by introducing the concept of cultivating imagined ecosystems. In doing so, it contributes to the growing body of research on digital futures, which has recently gained attention in information systems, emphasizing imagination and the radical uncertainties that span technical, social, economic, and environmental dimensions (Schlagwein et al., 2024; Hovorka & Peter, 2021). We conceptualize cultivation as comprising three interrelated elements: entrepreneurial trajectory dynamics, digital future-making, and sociotechnical enactment. Additionally, we identify three cultivation practices, namely perceptive response to emergent futures, prefigurative action toward desired distant futures, and inattentive inaction toward alternative futures, each consequential to enacting an ecosystem de novo.

We acknowledge that these insights emerge from a single longitudinal case study of the first digital pill, which carries implications for the nature and scope of generalizability. Specifically, our model offers analytical generalizability, enabling transferability where relevant and appropriate in other contexts. We also propose boundary conditions for our study, which examined the attempted formation of an ecosystem around a new product category. A key boundary condition is the radical novelty of the proposed digital innovation. The applicability of imagined ecosystems in contexts with established arrangements, where firms can imitate others or learn from previous failed first-mover attempts, is less certain. Our study also focuses on healthcare, a highly regulated sector characterized by complex funding structures and the intricate establishment of service arrangements. Additionally, since our research centers on digital entrepreneurial ventures, another boundary condition is the extent to which our findings apply to other forms of digital innovation, particularly those led by wellestablished firms.

That said, we believe that the mechanisms of cultivation and imagined ecosystems can be applied to theorize what is yet to exist in emerging digital phenomena or digital futures. This is particularly important considering the possible combinatorial innovation enabled by artificial intelligence and quantum computing, and the desirable futures (Gümüsay & Reinecke, 2024; Comi et al., 2025) we ought to enact.
