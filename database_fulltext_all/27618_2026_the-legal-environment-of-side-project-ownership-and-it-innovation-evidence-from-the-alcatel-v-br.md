# THE LEGAL ENVIRONMENT OF SIDE PROJECT OWNERSHIP AND IT INNOVATION: EVIDENCE FROM THE ALCATEL V. BROWN CASE<sup>1</sup>

Xi Wu Tilburg School of Economics and Management, Tilburg University Tilburg, THE NETHERLANDS {xi.wu@tilburguniversity.edu}

Charlotte R. Ren School of Arts and Sciences, University of Pennsylvania Philadelphia, PA, U.S.A {rren@upenn.edu}

Min-Seok Pang Wisconsin School of Business, University of Wisconsin–Madison Madison, WI, U.S.A. {mpang9@wisc.edu}

Engaging in side projects outside of regular employment has become a growing trend among knowledge workers, particularly information technology (IT) professionals. Side projects offer valuable opportunities for employees to learn new skills and foster creativity. However, the legal ownership of side projects remains uncertain, raising questions about how this affects employee innovation at their primary jobs, which we refer to as “employee innovation at work.” In this study, we leverage an exogenous change in the lega arrangement of side project ownership—the Alcatel v. Brown case—to investigate how firms’ enhanced control over side projects influences employees’ innovation performance at work. We find that in states where firms gained greater contractual authority to claim ownership of employees’ side projects, the number of IT patents owned by firms decreased. However, paradoxically, the quality of these patents, as measured by the number of forward citations, improved following the legal change. Further analyses of the underlying mechanisms suggest that these contrasting findings likely stem from shifts in both employee innovation behaviors and firms’ innovation strategies, post-Alcatel v. Brown. Our findings contribute to the information systems literature by highlighting the nuanced effects of side project ownership on IT innovation.

Keywords: Employee innovation at work, side project, IT innovation, intellectual property, Alcatel v. Brown

## Introduction

In today’s information age, where knowledge is easily created and accessed, the locus of employee innovation lies not only in employees’ day-to-day work but also beyond their workspace. Initiatives that employees pursue beyond their employment scope and outside of normal work hours are commonly referred to as side projects (Davis et al., 2013; Mehra et al., 2011). Side projects are particularly prevalent among information technology (IT) professionals. For example, GitHub had more than 100 million users and 372 million repositories, as of January 2023, many of which were dedicated to side projects (Lehechka, 2020). According to a 2022 survey by Stack Overflow involving 67,963 software developers, 88% reported working on side projects. <sup>2</sup> Side projects not only provide employees with opportunities to learn new skills (Sonmez, 2017) but also serve as a fertile ground for groundbreaking innovations and new business opportunities (Burgelman, 1983; Davis et al., 2013). Notably, some popular products and companies, such as Slack, Twitter, Craigslist, and Trello, originated as side projects.

Despite the growing prevalence and importance of side projects among IT professionals, a key concern for many, particularly those employed by firms, centers around the ownership of intellectual property rights (IPR) related to these projects. While employees often assume that anything created during their personal time belongs to them, this is not always the case (Spolsky, 2016). A recent survey of open source software (OSS) contributors revealed that fewer than half of the respondents felt they had the freedom to work on OSS projects without their employers asserting IPR over their work (Nagle et al., 2020). In the current U.S. legal system, the ownership of side projects remains ambiguous due to the absence of federal laws governing IPR for such projects (Parker, 1984). Instead, whether an employee’s side project is owned by the employee or their employer hinges on two key factors (Stasa, 2024; Spolsky, 2016). The first is the employment contract, which typically assigns the IPR of side projects to the employer. The second is state-level statutes on side project ownership, which determine the enforceability of employment contracts. For example, states like California, Delaware, and Minnesota have statutes protecting employees’ ownership rights over their side projects, while other states may grant employers more contractual power over such projects. These factors contribute to the complexities and inconsistencies of side project ownership across the U.S.

The impact of IPR legal regimes on innovation has been extensively discussed in prior literature (e.g., Contigiani et al., 2018; Gans et al., 2008; Marx, 2011; Samila & Sorenson, 2011). Notwithstanding the valuable insights from prior work, little research has examined how the legal environment concerning the ownership of employees’ side projects influences their innovation, especially their innovation performance at work. We define “employee innovation at work” as any innovation output produced by employees that is subsequently owned by their employer.<sup>3</sup> The implications of employees’ relinquishing the IPR of side projects to their employers are multifaceted. On the one hand, it is common in the IT industry for employees to start their own ventures, raising concerns about innovation appropriation. When employers perceive such risks to be lower due to greater control over side projects, they may be more inclined to invest in employees and offer additional support (e.g., training and collaborative opportunities) (Hellmann, 2007; Hellmann & Perotti, 2011; Liebeskind, 1997). This support can, in turn, stimulate more innovative activities by employees (Mehra & Mookerjee, 2012). On the other hand, the lack of IPR protection for employees may reduce their incentive to innovate and restrict their ability to learn outside of work, which could subsequently hinder their innovation performance at work (Durcikova et al., 2011; Huang & Zhang, 2016). Overall, the relationship between side project IPR and employees’ innovation performance at work remains theoretically ambiguous.

In this study, we address this gap by investigating how the legal environment governing the IPR of side projects affects employees’ IT innovation at work. Specifically, we focus on U.S. state-level statutes regarding side project IPR and exploit the ruling of Alcatel USA, Inc. v. Brown (2002) (hereafter Alcatel v. Brown) as an exogenous treatment. Alcatel v. Brown is a landmark legal case where a Texas court ruled that the firm Alcatel owned the IPR of all forms of employee innovation, including even an abstract idea its employee Evan Brown had developed for his side projects. This ruling significantly enhanced firms’ contractual control over employees’ creative activities, including side projects at any stage of development. This case has become “a symbol of outrage” among employees required to give up their innovations to their employers (Lobel, 2014a) and serves as one of the most famous disputes on side project ownership (Collins, 2017).

Alcatel v. Brown serves as a precedent for future court decisions nationwide, but its enforceability varies across states. Table 1 lists the eight states that do not adhere to Alcatel v. Brown. These states explicitly protect employees’ rights to claim IPR for side projects, nullifying employment contracts that allow firms to claim ownership of employees’ every innovation. We exploit this contrast in legal regimes across the states and implement a difference-in-differences (DID) method to investigate the effect of Alcatel v. Brown on employee IT innovation at work.

Our results show that in states where employees’ IPR regarding side projects is not explicitly protected by state laws, there was a decrease in innovation quantity, measured by the number of IT patent filings owned by firms, following the Alcatel v. Brown ruling. Surprisingly, despite this decline in quantity, we observed an increase in innovation quality, as evidenced by higher average citations received per patent, during the same period. To elucidate the contrasting effects on innovation quantity and quality, we identified several mechanisms related to employees’ innovation behaviors and firms’ innovation strategies. We found that after Alcatel v. Brown, employees became less likely to explore new technological domains, resulting in a smaller total number of patents. However, they concentrated their filings in fewer patent classes and drew on a broader range of prior knowledge in their backward citations. This suggests a deeper investment in each patent and more diverse knowledge use, resulting in higher-quality patents. Additionally, with reduced concerns about misappropriation following Alcatel v. Brown, firms were more motivated to invest in high-impact (albeit potentially riskier) innovations. In turn, employees were particularly drawn to promising technological domains where firms previously had limited activity, reinforcing strategic alignment and further improving patent quality.

Our research makes several important contributions. First, our work advances the information systems (IS) literature on IT workplaces and professionals (e.g., Levina & Xin, 2007; Pawlowski & Robey, 2004; Tambe & Hitt, 2014; Venkatesh et al., 2017). We show that IT professionals are concerned about side project ownership, which impacts their innovation performance at work. By investigating the relationship between side project ownership and IT innovation at work, our study significantly advances prior work on informal learning in IT industries (Cha et al., 2008; Mehra et al., 2011). Second, our research contributes to the broader literature on innovation management. While extant research provides valuable insights into how different legal regimes (e.g., trade-secret protection and non-compete covenants) affect innovation (Contigiani et al., 2018; Marx, 2011; Samila & Sorenson, 2011), relatively little is known about the impact of limited legal protection for employees’ side project ownership. Our study breaks new ground by investigating the effects of IPR laws governing side projects. Extending prior work, we show that despite a decline in innovation quantity following the legal shift, there was an improvement in innovation quality. In essence, the change triggered by Alcatel v. Brown does not appear to uniformly hinder innovation. Third, we contribute to the literature on side project activities (Burgelman, 1983; Davis et al., 2013; Hellmann, 2007; Hellmann & Thiele, 2011) by examining the IP disputes about side projects between employees and employers. The impact of side project ownership on employees’ innovation at work proves to be less straightforward and more nuanced than originally anticipated.

## Legal Environment of Side Project Ownership

## Before Alcatel v. Brown

In the U.S., common law is a body of unwritten laws derived from legal precedents, which dictates adjudication processes in cases where a decision cannot easily be made based on existing statutes or rules (Pound, 1908). State courts follow federal precedents and common law to address cases concerning employee innovation (Parker, 1984). The common law concerning employee innovation can be summarized as follows: (1) If there is an employment contract expressly stating that an employee’s innovations belong to the employer, the employer retains the ownership of any innovations created during the employment. (2) In the absence of such a contract, the employer retains ownership of the employee’s innovations if the employee is specifically hired to work on those innovations. (3) If the employee is not hired for specific innovation tasks, in the absence of a contract, the employer has an implied “shop right” to claim ownership of the innovations if the employee uses the employer’s materials, facilities, or time to create them. Under common law, employers can assert ownership over innovations created by employees during the employment period, including both normal working hours and spare time.

While common law establishes foundational principles, its implementation varies across states. As noted, the eight states listed in Table 1 have statutes to protect employee innovation from being claimed by employers. For example, California limits the enforceability of pre-innovation IPR assignment agreements. California Labor Code §2870-2872 states that “any provision in an employment agreement that provides that an employee shall assign or offer to assign any rights in an invention to his/her employer shall not apply to an invention that the employee develops entirely on his or her own time without using the employer’s equipment, supplies, facilities, or trade secret information. … Any provision that purports to do so shall be unenforceable and against the state’s public policy.” In contrast, in states without such statutes, employers can use written contracts to claim ownership of employee innovation.

## Alcatel v. Brown: Important Features

Alcatel v. Brown represents an important milestone in the legal environment of side project ownership. Evan Brown, a software developer in Texas, claimed to have conceived a software idea in 1975, long before he joined DSC Communications (subsequently acquired by Alcatel). When Brown mentioned his idea to Alcatel before any tangible solution was developed and attempted to negotiate a deal to share the profits of a potential solution with his employer, Alcatel instead fired him and sued him in April 1997, seeking full ownership of the idea. The 219th Judicial District Court of Texas ruled in July 2002 that Brown’s idea fell under the terms of his employment agreement, which entitled Alcatel to the “full legal right, title and interests” in any innovations (Lai, 2003, p. 320; Lobel, 2014b). The state appeals court upheld the decision in June 2004.

The decision of Alcatel v. Brown was based on a contractual perspective. The court held that “the invention disclosure agreement between Brown and DSC was valid and enforceable.” By signing this IPR assignment agreement covering “all inventions,” which “include but [are] not limited to all matters subject to patent,” (Lai, 2003, pp. 299-301) Brown was obligated to fully disclose the idea to Alcatel. In other words, the court allowed the employer to use the IPR assignment agreement in its employment contract, which required employees to assign their future innovation rights to the employer as a condition of employment and enabled employers to claim IPR ownership of all forms of employee innovation, including even an immature idea for a side project (Lai, 2003; Lobel, 2014b). Lai (2003, pp. 296-297) stated that “[by] recognizing an employer’s ownership of intellectual property that does not exist as defined by the law of copyright, patent, trademark, and trade secret, the court’s decision [of Alcatel v. Brown] provides companies with a way to restrain the intellectual property market with all-encompassing invention disclosure agreements.” Consequently, the ruling substantially expanded firms’ contractual controls over employee innovation (Lobel, 2014b).

The ruling of Alcatel v. Brown introduced two important features that significantly impacted the legal framework of side project ownership. First, it established that an intangible idea of an employee could be considered a form of intellectual property. Prior to the ruling, an abstract idea was not eligible for any of the four forms of intellectual property—copyright, patent, trademark, or trade secret—all of which require something tangible (Lai, 2003; Lobel, 2014b). Thus, employees did not need to worry about IPR ownership unless their side projects became tangible innovations. However, following Alcatel v. Brown, intangible side projects became claimable by employers through employment contracts. As a result, employers gained the legal right to own the innovation at any stage of side project development, and employees who test out earlystage ideas may risk losing ownership of them. Given that software innovations are often considered abstract (see Alice Corp. v. CLS Bank International, 2014), the implications of Alcatel v. Brown are significant for IT professionals.

Second, the ruling of Alcatel v. Brown had a retrospective effect, enabling former employers to claim IPR over any work developed during an employee’s tenure, even years after employment concluded (as seen in subsequent cases such as DDB Technologies v. MLB Advanced Media, 2008). While most side projects do not achieve the prominence of Twitter or Gmail, a legal environment that does not protect employees’ ownership rights can diminish their motivation to pursue such projects. Moreover, the court’s adjudication on Brown’s IPR assignment agreement set a precedent for future legal cases (Lai, 2003) in states governed by common law (i.e., those not listed in Table 1), where employment contracts wield significant influence over the IPR ownership of employee innovations. We next discuss the scope of this legal case’s influence.

## The Influence of Alcatel v. Brown as a Precedent

Alcatel v. Brown is the first case regarding side project ownership and has attracted considerable attention from both the public and academia (Lai, 2003; Lobel, 2014b). From a legal standpoint, however, the impact of Alcatel v. Brown as a precedent is not uniform across all states. A precedent refers to a court decision used to adjudicate subsequent cases involving the same or similar facts or issues (Fon & Parisi, 2006). There are two types of precedents: binding and persuasive. Binding precedents serve as a mandatory authority in which lower courts are obliged to follow the decisions of higher courts in the same jurisdiction. Under this principle of binding precedents, a state district court is required to follow decisions by the supreme or appeals courts in the same state or the federal courts presiding over the state. While court decisions in other states are not binding, they serve as persuasive precedents (Oyen, 2017). When there is no explicit statute or binding precedent to follow, a state court can base its decision on persuasive precedents from other states and utilize legal reasoning and interpretations of laws from these precedents. For instance, in DocRx, Inc. v. EMI Servs. of N.C., LLC (2014) the North Carolina Court of Appeals applied appellate court decisions from Utah (Bankler v. Bankler, 1998), Montana (Carr v. Bett, 1998), and Colorado (Craven v. Southern Farm Bureau Cas. Ins., 2004) as persuasive precedents.

Given that the ruling of Alcatel v. Brown originated from the district court of Texas and was affirmed by the appeals court of Texas, it stands as a binding precedent in Texas. Furthermore, it holds influence as a persuasive precedent in the other 41 states and the District of Columbia, where explicit statutes delineating employee IPR in side projects are lacking. In other words, courts in these states may follow the ruling of Alcatel v. Brown when adjudicating similar employer-employee IPR disputes (Oyen, 2017), heightening employees’ concerns regarding firms’ assertions over side project IPR. The impact of persuasive precedents on judicial decision-making has been extensively discussed in both the legal and economics literatures (e.g., Chen & Eraslan, 2020; Dobbins, 2010; Fon & Parisi, 2006). Legal uncertainties caused by well-known lawsuits have also been examined in IS research to assess how legal environments affect innovation activities. For instance, Wen et al. (2013) utilized the filing of SCO v. IBM (2017) to explore the effect of IPR enforcement on OSS and found that the case had a significant impact even before it was adjudicated. Hence, we argue that Alcatel v. Brown has an impact not only in Texas but also in other states that lack explicit protection statutes governing the IPR ownership of side projects.

## Theoretical Background and Research Questions

## Side Projects and Employee Innovation at Work

One of the main sources of IT innovation at work is creative ideas generated by employees (Gong et al., 2009). Extensive prior research has explored the sources of employee creativity. Studies have examined the relationship between employee creativity and personal characteristics (Oldham & Cummings, 1996), organizational context (Gong et al., 2009), and workplace learning (Hirst et al., 2009). Scholars have also recommended that firms institute proper incentive structures (e.g., compensation, autonomy) to encourage employees to innovate (Hellmann & Thiele, 2011; Samila & Sorenson, 2011; Sauermann & Cohen, 2010). Overall, most of the prior studies focus on employees’ innovation activities within formal work settings.

While employees’ innovation within formal work settings is important to firms, a growing stream of literature has recently investigated side projects, which constitute employee innovation away from the workplace. It is worth noting that some scholars (e.g., Davis et al., 2013) use such terms as “spare time invention” or “leisure time invention,” all of which refer to inventions that employees create outside of formal employment agreements. The motives for employees to work on side projects include learning new skills, developing new products or services, seeking future employment opportunities, or for pure personal enjoyment. For example, IS scholars (e.g., von Krogh et al., 2012; Roberts et al., 2006; Singh & Phelps, 2013) have examined the motivations of OSS contributors and found that they are motivated to pursue OSS projects by a variety of factors, including monetary rewards, career opportunities, reputation, and self-accomplishment.

Side projects require considerable time and effort from employees, which may divert their attention from assigned tasks at work (Hellmann & Thiele, 2011). However, if managed effectively, side projects can yield benefits for both employees and firms. Research indicates that employees’ side project ideas usually solve different problems than those encountered in formal work tasks (Davis et al., 2013; Harhoff & Hoisl, 2007). This provides an opportunity for employees to learn informally, improving their overall innovation capabilities (Hemetsberger & Reinhardt, 2006; Singh et al., 2011). Firms also benefit from the expansion of employees knowledge base. Since the recombination of diverse knowledge and ideas is a source of successful innovation (Gruber et al., 2013), employees can contribute to innovation at work to a greater extent when they work on different problems from assigned tasks and their side projects.

## Side Project IPR and Employees’ Innovation at Work

Although working on side projects could potentially benefit both firms and employees, these benefits depend on who owns the IPR of these projects (Lobel, 2014a). Our research question is thus: How does the legal environment of side project ownership influence employees’ innovation performance at work, in terms of both quantity and quality? More specifically, when firms assert ownership over the IPR of side projects, does it enhance or hinder employee innovation at work?

An increase in firms’ contractual control over side projects can foster employee innovation at work in several ways. First, by securing IPR to side projects, firms not only gain an extra avenue for innovation but also safeguard it from imitation and ensure value appropriation (Hellmann & Perotti, 2011; Liebeskind, 1997). Consequently, firms are more likely to invest in their employees’ skills and human capital development (Leiponen, 2008; Levin et al., 1987), anticipating that their investments would not inadvertently foster side projects that could evolve into competitive startups. With the decrease in risk of value misappropriation, such ownership also allows firms to strategically invest in high-impact technological domains.

Hellmann (2007) provides insights into how firms manage both assigned innovative tasks at work and employee side projects. The findings show that when firms own the IPR of everything employees create, they should implement policies to bolster employees’ human capital development and provide adequate incentives, thereby ensuring that all employees focus on their assigned tasks at work.

Second, compared to individual inventors, firms possess greater financial resources, intellectual capital, networking opportunities, and collaborative infrastructures, all of which are key factors for fostering an innovative climate (Scott & Bruce, 1994). For example, firms are better equipped to evaluate the quality of ideas and identify the most promising ones for further exploration than individuals (Girotra et al., 2010). They can also assign more experienced employees to collaborate with those who are new to innovation tasks to enhance innovation outcomes (Samila & Sorenson, 2011). Research indicates that compared to solo inventors (especially those lacking formal affiliations with organizations), those inventors embedded within a network of collaborators are more likely to achieve breakthroughs (Singh & Fleming, 2010). Furthermore, firms that exert greater control over employee side projects are better positioned to oversee the overall innovation portfolio of their employees and align these efforts with the firms’ innovation goals (Davis et al., 2013). The structured approach to innovation, coupled with abundant financial and intellectual resources, supportive networking environments, and collaborative infrastructure, enhances employees’ ability to integrate knowledge and achieve superior innovation outcomes (Carnabuci & Operti, 2013; Karim & Kaul, 2015).

Despite the arguments indicating a positive relationship between firms’ ownership of side projects and employee innovation at work, there are theoretical reasons suggesting the opposite effect. First, when firms claim the IPR over side projects, it can diminish employees’ incentive to engage in any form of innovation activity. Research underscores that a climate of innovation (i.e., employees’ belief that their creativity is encouraged in the workplace) and a climate of autonomy (i.e., employees’ self-determination with respect to work procedures, goals, and priorities) are both positively related to employee innovation (Durcikova et al., 2011). When side projects are owned by firms, it raises concerns among employees as to whether it is fair for firms to claim rights to their work beyond the scope of employment. Due to a lack of control over their own ideas, employees also feel uncertain about whether their ideas would receive enough attention from collaborators. Moreover, when firms retain the ownership of all employee-created innovation, including side projects, employees may feel that their autonomy is lost in the process of innovation. The concerns about fairness, uncertainty, and loss of autonomy can hinder employees creativity, interest, and enthusiasm in their innovation activities at work (Durcikova et al., 2011).

Second, when firms own the IPR of side projects and appropriate most of their value, they discourage employees from pursuing side projects, potentially undermining their capability to innovate at work. As stated above, engagement in side projects offers valuable opportunities for employees to learn and develop new IT skills. Prior literature has emphasized the role of such learning in employee innovation at work (Susomrith & Coetzer, 2019; Svensson et al., 2004). Learning outside of the workplace can also lead to higher job satisfaction and potential career advancement, which can improve employees’ innovation performance at work (Huang & Zhang, 2016). The extent to which employees’ skill sets gained in their spare time affect their innovation is particularly prominent in the IT field, where knowledge and skills become obsolete very quickly. For example, working on OSS projects can improve software developers’ technical skills (Hemetsberger & Reinhardt, 2006; Singh et al., 2011), enhancing productivity in their day-to-day work (Mehra et al., 2011; Mehra & Mookerjee, 2012). Hence, if employees are no longer interested in side projects, they would be less willing to upgrade their IT skills and explore new ideas, thereby undermining their productivity in innovation at work.

Ultimately, employees’ innovation performance at work is shaped by both their incentives and capabilities. On the one hand, employees would be less incentivized to innovate if they anticipated losing ownership of their innovations. Hence, their ability to innovate would be hindered, as reduced engagement in side projects would limit their opportunities to learn new skills, stay up to date in the field, and explore new avenues—actions that are critical for IT professionals. On the other hand, employees’ innovation capabilities can be bolstered when firms provide more resources and networking opportunities and invest more in the skill development of employees. Due to these contrasting forces, it remains theoretically unclear whether employees’ innovation performance at work would be expected to improve or deteriorate after the legal change triggered by Alcatel v. Brown.

## Data, Measures, and Empirical Methods

We first describe our data sources. We used IT patent filings to measure IT innovation activities in our main models. Patents are one of the most widely used measures of firm innovation in many industries, including IT industries (Chung et al., 2019; Park et al., 2007). We used data from the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO).<sup>4</sup> We also used data from the Fung Institute for Engineering Leadership (FIEL) (Lai et al., 2014) to identify the inventors and assignees of each patent.

Our analysis focuses on IT patents filed by firms, which serve as proxies for firms’ IT innovation output. This is consistent with our goal of investigating how the legal framework surrounding side project ownership influences employees’ IT innovation at work. Firm-filed patents typically list employees as inventors and firms as assignees. While we could not identify whether a patent stems from a firm’s core project, external project, or side project, firm involvement is reasonably inferred when it is designated as an assignee. To ensure methodological consistency, we focused on patents with clearly identified firm assignees, excluding those filed solely by individuals, assigned to self-owned firms (where assignees include inventor names), and those by universities or governmental entities. Our final sample included 1,543,439 utility patent applications in 1992- 2011. We also collected demographic and economic data at the state and county levels from the U.S. Census Bureau and the Bureau of Economic Analysis.

Next, we describe our variables and main models. We constructed panel datasets at both the state-year and countyyear levels, covering patent filings across the 51 states from 1992 to 2011. <sup>5</sup> At the county-year level, we applied a coarsened exact matching (CEM) approach to match treated and control counties based on county-level demographics such as population, income, employment, and wages. After matching, our dataset included 1,745 counties in the treatment group and 447 counties in the control group. Our dependent variables capture two dimensions of employees’ IT innovation at work. Patent Counts<sub>it</sub> measures the quantity of IT innovation and is computed as the natural logarithm of firmowned IT patent counts in unit i (state or county) in year t. Patent Citations<sub>it</sub> denotes IT innovation quality and is measured as the average number of 5-year forward citations<sup>6</sup> received by firm-owned IT patents.

Our models incorporate two independent variables reflecting the two differences in our DID design. Because Alcatel v. Brown set a precedent for future IPR-assignment cases, we treated the ruling as an exogenous shock. Figure 1 shows the timeline of Alcatel v. Brown. The first difference is Ruling , which equals 1 if year t is after 2002 (the year of the Alcatel v. Brown ruling). This variable indicates whether there was a change in the legal environment of side project ownership. The second difference is NoStatutes<sub>i</sub>, which equals 1 if unit i was in a state in the treatment group, comprising the states with no statutes protecting the employees’ IPR of side projects. These eight states, which are shown in Table 1, limit the enforceability of employment contracts in IPR-assignment disputes, thereby excluding Alcatel v. Brown as a precedent. We thus designated these eight states as the control group, while the remaining 42 states and the District of Columbia formed the treatment group.

We incorporated a comprehensive set of controls at multiple levels to account for various factors that may influence innovation performance. At the state and county levels, we included Population, Employment, Income, and Wages to capture time-varying observable heterogeneity across states and counties that may affect innovation outcomes.<sup>7</sup> To control for state-level industry differences, we introduced IT Ratio, which is measured as the proportion of IT patents to all patents at the state-year level. Given that the location decisions of high-tech firms may confound the estimation, we added HighTech Firm Count, which is the number of high-tech firms (i.e., those filing in the top 1% by the number of IT patents) in each state-year. To account for R&D investment incentives, we also included the level of R&D tax credits (Bartik, 2017) at the state-year level. Our models also included unit (i.e., state, county) fixed effects to control for unobserved factors specific to each unit. Year fixed effects were included to capture the effects of nationwide policy changes, such as the Patent Reform Act of 2011 and the Patent Reform Act of 2005. The formal DID specification is as follows.

$$

$$

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text {Patent Citations} _ {i t} = \\ \beta_ {2 0} + \beta_ {2 I} \text {NoStatutes} _ {i} \times \text {Ruling} _ {t} + \text {Two - Way FE} + \text {Controls} + \delta_ {i t} \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

## Results

## Survey Results on Side Project Ownership and Employee Innovation

Prior to the main analyses, we conducted a small-scale survey to gain illustrative insights into how employees perceive side project ownership and how legal environments influence their innovation motivations. We recruited 104 U.S. respondents working in the software and IT services industry through Amazon Mechanical Turk. Although these respondents were not part of our patent dataset, their perspectives shed light on how IT professionals view side project ownership and their innovation motivation. The survey revealed that 92% of the respondents had ongoing personal side projects. The top three reasons for engaging in side projects were learning new skills (25%), career development (23%), and building networks (15%). Importantly, 84% of the respondents agreed that working on side projects benefited their primary work role.

We asked participants about their opinions on the IPR assignment of side projects. Over half (53%) believed employees should own their side project IPR, while 22% favored shared ownership between employers and employees. When asked about their employers’ IPR policies, 47% stated that their employers allowed them to own side projects under specific conditions, aligning with the findings from a survey of OSS contributors (Nagle et al., 2020). We also assessed participants’ knowledge of the legal regime governing side project ownership; 79% of them stated that they were aware of state statutes on the IPR of side projects (e.g., California Labor Code §2870-2872), and 18% said that they were very familiar with the legal details. A significant 65% claimed that they would undertake more side projects if they resided in a state with protective statutes. When asked whether they were aware of Alcatel v. Brown, 58% of respondents answered “Yes,” and 77% said that they had become more concerned about the IPR of side projects after learning about this case.

## Investigation of Possible Mechanisms

How can we make sense of the mixed findings on employee innovation at work in Table 2 (a decrease in innovation quantity but an increase in quality)? Drawing on the searchbased perspective of Ahuja et al. (2008) and Ahuja and Lampert (2001), we view an inventor’s search behavior as how they allocate resources across technological domains and utilize prior knowledge through citations. According to Ahuja and Lampert (2001), innovation outcomes are constrained when inventors focus narrowly on familiar domains, target areas that are already too mature, or rely on prior knowledge concentrated in a few closely related domains. These patterns are shaped by internal firm dynamics, such as strategic priorities and how innovation benefits are shared between firms and employees, and are also influenced by broader institutional conditions. Legal shifts such as the Alcatel v. Brown ruling can reshape these incentives and constraints, thereby influencing inventors’ search patterns. Building on this search-based framework, we explored several mechanisms through inventor-level analyses, which reflect changes in both employees’ innovation behaviors and firms’ strategies. These analyses supplemented our state- and county-level evidence and shed light on the contrasting findings, particularly the observed improvement in innovation quality.

Decrease in innovation quantity: Alcatel v. Brown heightened employees’ sense of unfairness, uncertainty, and loss of autonomy, which, in turn, diminished their incentives to innovate at work (Durcikova et al., 2011). This decrease in motivation would be evident in a decline in exploration of new technological domains. Employees can either exploit existing technological domains or take a more adventurous approach by exploring new ones (March, 1991). However, when firms exercise greater control over side project IPR, employees will likely feel less motivated to pursue new and uncertain ideas (Hellmann & Thiele, 2011). Without the prospect of personal gain from exploring novel domains, they will tend to focus on familiar areas, which are perceived to offer more predictable outcomes and rewards (Ahuja & Lampert, 2001; March, 1991). This decline in exploration will ultimately lead to fewer patents (Garcia-Vega, 2006; Nesta & Saviotti, 2005).

To test whether a decrease in exploration of new technological domains drives the decline in innovation quantity, we conducted a mediation analysis (Lin & Rai, 2023). We introduced a mediator variable, New Class<sub>mt</sub> , defined as the logarithm of the number of new patent classes inventor m enters in year t. We utilized a similar DID framework at the inventor-year level;<sup>10</sup> results are presented in Table 4. Model 9 shows that NoStatutes×Ruling significantly reduced the number of new classes, indicating a decrease in exploration of new domains post-ruling. Next, we tested whether this mediator significantly affected Patent Counts and whether its inclusion significantly reduced the treatment effect (Lin & Rai, 2023). In Model 11, the coefficient of New Class suggests that the exploration of new domains positively affected patent counts. Comparing Models 10 and 11, the coefficient of NoStatutes×Ruling dropped by about 50% and became insignificant when the mediator was included. The total effect of the ruling on patent counts is significantly different from the direct effect, based on 1,000 bootstrap simulations (p < 0.01) (direct effect: -0.0037, indirect effect: -0.0039, total effect: -0.0076). Overall, the mediation analysis shows that the decrease in exploration of new technological domains is a key pathway through which Alcatel v. Brown reduced patent quantity.

We also examined whether the decrease in patent counts was driven by a shift in firms’ patenting propensity following Alcatel v. Brown. Stronger control over side projects lowered firms’ concerns about misappropriation, reducing the need to file patents. While firms’ patenting propensity was not directly observable, we analyzed multi-location firms and compared the quantity of patents filed between the treatment and control groups, both before and after the ruling. Given that patenting strategies are typically a centralized decision aligned with firms’ overall strategic objectives, we assumed firms maintained a consistent patenting propensity across locations.<sup>11</sup> This approach allowed us to difference out firmlevel patenting strategy effects and assess whether the observed decrease in patent counts was primarily driven by firms’ patent filing decisions. Our analysis revealed that the main findings remained consistent, suggesting that the decrease in patent counts was not primarily driven by a shift in firms’ patenting strategies.

Increase in patent quality: The increase in innovation quality after Alcatel v. Brown deserves further attention. Since limited exploration of new technological domains altered the focus and scope of employees’ innovation efforts, we analyzed two search behaviors as predictors for innovation quality (Ahuja et al., 2008; Ahuja & Lampert, 2001; Burgelman, 1983). First, we measured the level of concentration across different patent classes, capturing how employees allocated resources across technological domains. We introduced Specialization<sub>mt</sub>, calculated as a Herfindahl concentration ratio across the patent classes that inventor $m ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s }$ patents covered each year. Mathematically, it is defined as $\Sigma _ { j } ( N _ { m j t } / N _ { m t } ) ^ { 2 }$ , where $N _ { m t }$ denotes the total number of patents inventor m files in year t and $N _ { m j t }$ is the number of patents inventor m files in class j in year t. A higher value of Specialization indicates a more specialized patent portfolio. Second, we examined how employees cited prior patents to reflect their use of previous knowledge. We defined Backward Cite Concentration<sub>mt</sub> as $\Sigma _ { j } ( B C _ { m j t } / \bar { B } C _ { m t } ) ^ { 2 }$ where $B C _ { m t }$ represents the total number of prior patents inventor m cites in year t and $B C _ { m j t }$ is the number of prior patents inventor m cites in class j in year t. A lower value indicates a broader use of prior knowledge. These variables assessed employees’ concentration on technological domains and reliance on diverse prior knowledge.

Another possible explanation is that firms may have adjusted their innovation strategies. While employees may have retained autonomy in how they innovated, the choice of technological domains is often shaped by firm-level priorities. Firms are consistently concerned about knowledge misappropriation stemming from employees’ mobility and spin-out activities (Chen et al., 2020; Kim & Steensma, 2017). After Alcatel v. Brown, firms gained greater control over employees’ innovation outputs, including side projects. This allowed firms to redirect their employees’ efforts towards more impactful technological domains, potentially leading to higher citation rates for patents.<sup>12</sup>

To explore this possibility, we analyzed how employees selected patent classes, which were likely influenced by firms’ strategies. The first aspect evaluated the prominence of technological domains covered by inventors. We defined Domain $I m p a c t _ { m t }$ as the average number of forward citations received within a five-year window (from t-2 to t+2) by all patents within the classes inventor m worked on in year t. This measured whether firms focused on more impactful domains post-ruling. Domain $S h a r e _ { m t }$ quantified the extent to which a firm engaged with inventors’ patent classes. To compute this, for each patent class inventor m worked on in year t, we calculated the firm’s cumulative share of patents within the class (i.e., the total number of patents the firm filed prior to year t in that class, divided by the number of patents filed in the class by all firms) and then averaged these shares across patent classes. A lower value of this measure indicates that the firm had a previously limited presence in the inventor’s selected technological domains.

In Models 12-13 of Table 5, the coefficients of NoStatutes×Ruling indicate that after the ruling, employees concentrated on fewer technological domains but cited patents from a more diverse set of technological domains. Models 14- 15 suggest that firms directed employees to focus on patent classes with higher impact, which had not previously received as much investment from firms. The coefficients in these models confirm that the ruling significantly influenced the mediators in the expected direction. The next step was to confirm the effect of these mediators (Lin & Rai, 2023). In Model 17, the coefficients of Specialization and Backward Cite Concentration suggest that a more specialized patent portfolio and broader citations of prior knowledge contributed to higher innovation quality. The coefficients of Domain Impact and Domain Share indicate that focusing on higherimpact technological domains where firms had limited prior presence enhanced innovation quality. Comparing Models 16 and 17 revealed that once the mediators were included, the coefficient of NoStatutes×Ruling decreased by 0.130. A substantial proportion of the ruling’s effect on citations was mediated by the indirect effects of the four mediators (direct effect: 0.508, indirect effect: 0.122, total effect: 0.631) at $p <$

0.01 based on 1,000 bootstrap simulations.<sup>13</sup> In summary, the mediation analyses in Table 5 suggest that the positive impact of Alcatel v. Brown on innovation quality can be explained by the four mediators that reflected changes in inventors innovation behaviors and firms’ innovation strategies.

## Discussion and Conclusion

In this study, we used the legal precedent set by Alcatel v. Brown to investigate how variations in state-level statutes governing side project IPR influence employee IT innovation at work, yielding intriguing and unexpected findings. Our results show that in states where employees’ ownership of their side project IPR is not protected, Alcatel v. Brown significantly decreased the quantity of firm-owned IT innovations while concurrently enhancing their quality.

We found that the decrease in exploration of new technological domains, driven by limited incentives for innovation following Alcatel v. Brown, led to an overall decline in innovation quantity. With firms gaining greater control over employees’ side project IPR, employees were less motivated to explore new areas, whether through side projects or within their primary job functions, reducing overall innovation output. However, this reduction in quantity was not accompanied by a decline in quality; rather, innovation quality improved following the legal change. We identified several mechanisms underlying this improvement in innovation quality from both the employee and firm perspectives. Specifically, after Alcatel v. Brown, employees directed their innovation efforts to fewer technological domains while citing a broader range of prior knowledge, suggesting that more time, effort, and resources were devoted to each innovation. Additional mechanisms involve the selection of technological domains, which are shaped by firms’ innovation strategies. With decreased concerns about knowledge misappropriation, firms incentivized employees to pivot toward patent classes with higher citation rates and smaller historical firm presence. This suggests that after the ruling, inventors became less inclined to pursue entirely unfamiliar domains and instead focused on areas where they had some prior experience but their firms had limited presence, indicating a strategic alignment of inventors’ efforts with firm priorities. Taken together, these shifts suggest that inventors responded strategically to the legal change by reconfiguring the scope and direction of their efforts. In doing so, they may have been able to avoid the familiarity trap, as identified by Ahuja and Lampert (2001), while leveraging underexplored but promising technological domains. Importantly, these adjustments enhanced the quality of innovation output, even as the overall quantity declined.

Our work makes significant contributions to the IS literature on IT workplaces and IT professionals. It underscores the importance of side project ownership in the employeremployee relationship among IT professionals. Our findings reveal that reallocating the IPR of side projects from employees to employers decreased the quantity of IT patents. However, unexpectedly, the overall quality of IT patents improved, as firms prioritized more impactful knowledge areas. Employees became more concentrated in their technological domains and drew on a broader knowledge base, which also contributed to the production of higherquality patents.

Second, our study makes important contributions to the literature on innovation management. A growing body of research has examined the impact of employer-friendly policies such as trade secret protections (Contigiani et al., 2018) and non-compete covenants (Marx et al., 2009; Samila & Sorenson, 2011) on innovation. However, there is little research exploring how the ownership of employees’ side projects affects innovation at work. Our paper is among the first to examine the effect of IPR laws concerning side projects on employee innovation at work. While our finding of a decrease in innovation quantity aligns with prior research, we introduce a novel and significant insight: Increased employer control over employee innovation can enhance innovation quality. This suggests that more employer-friendly policies regarding side project ownership do not necessarily impede innovation. Instead, they can encourage employees to concentrate on fewer patent classes and invest time and effort in technological domains that firms had previously neglected but later recognized as having a greater impact.

Third, we contribute to the literature on side project activities (Davis et al., 2013; Hellmann, 2007; Hellmann & Thiele, 2011) by examining IPR disputes about side projects between employees and employers and how the legal environment influences innovation at work. While it is evident that the loss of control over side projects reduced employees’ engagement in such activities, the impact on their innovation at work was less straightforward and more nuanced than initially expected. From the employees’ perspective, losing control over side projects diminishes motivation and restricts opportunities to learn and apply new skills (Mehra et al., 2011). Conversely, they may receive increased support and resources from their employers. Our findings underscore the intricate interplay of these factors.

Our study also provides managerial insights into how to foster employee innovation at work. A pertinent question arises: Do our findings imply that the ruling in Alcatel v. Brown enhances a firm’s innovation performance? In the short term, this appears to be the case: By compelling employees to concentrate more on a firm’s core technological domains, the ruling resulted in higher-quality patents. From the firms’ standpoint, they could leverage the opportunity to direct employees toward high-impact technologies that had previously been overlooked. However, the decrease in patent counts indicates a decline in employee motivation to innovate, raising potential concerns. Essentially, while innovation often arises from spontaneous, bottom-up initiatives (Burgelman, 1983), a more employer-friendly legal environment allows firms to structure their innovation efforts in a more organized, top-down manner. Therefore, we recommend seeking solutions that satisfy both employers and employees in a mutually beneficial manner. If both parties can collaborate effectively on side projects through a well-designed IPR allocation strategy, innovation quantity and quality could improve concurrently, given proper incentives and organizational support.

We note several limitations in our analysis. First, while we used patents as a measure of innovation, not all innovations are patented, and other forms of IT innovation (e.g., open innovation) are beyond our scope. Patent data also limited our ability to access granular information about innovation stemming from side project activities, and we did not distinguish between citations made by inventors and those added by patent examiners. In addition, our survey sample may not fully represent all inventors in our patent dataset. This lack of data constrains a deeper exploration of idea generation and value creation within the innovation processes. While our focus was on how changes in the legal environment affect overall employee innovation at work, we were unable to investigate their direct impact on employee commitment to side projects. Future researchers with access to more detailed data could extend our work in this direction. Finally, in addition to Alcatel v. Brown, other legal cases and IPR policies may have influenced innovation and warrant future investigation from a broader policy perspective.

Our work offers ample opportunities for future research. This study examines how employees innovate in the workplace in response to changes in the legal environment surrounding side project ownership. Future research could explore whether employees react to these legal changes by considering alternatives, such as relocating to more employee-friendly states or countries, filing patents in their own names, or even starting their own businesses. Researchers could also conduct more detailed investigations at the firm level, provided that such data are available. Some interesting questions to pursue include: What constitutes an optimal IPR allocation scheme for side projects that benefits both employees and employers? How can the management of employees’ side projects be optimized to enhance overall innovation performance (both quantity and quality)? Further research in these areas promises significant contributions across disciplines such as IS, innovation, law, and economics.

Her primary research interest is to understand knowledge creation, innovation, and entrepreneurship in information technology industries. Her current work focuses on digital platforms, mobile app markets, software development, and intellectual property in the digital age. ORCID: https://orcid.org/ 0000-0001-9160-6523

Charlotte Ren is the Robert A. Fox Leadership International Faculty Director and professor of practice in Strategy and Innovation at the Partnership for Innovation, Cross-Sector Collaboration, Leadership, and Organization (PICCLO) in the University of Pennsylvania’s School of Arts and Sciences. She is also a Senior Fellow at the Wharton School’s Mack Institute for Innovation Management. Charlotte holds a Ph.D. in management and an M.A. in economics from UCLA, as well as a bachelor’s degree in international politics and a minor degree in law from Peking University.
