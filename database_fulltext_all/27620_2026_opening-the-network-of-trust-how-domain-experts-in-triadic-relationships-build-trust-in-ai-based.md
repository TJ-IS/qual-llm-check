# OPENING THE NETWORK OF TRUST: HOW DOMAIN EXPERTS IN TRIADIC RELATIONSHIPS BUILD TRUST IN AI-BASED COUNTERPARTS<sup>1</sup>

André Hanelt Digital Transformation Management, University of Kassel Kassel, GERMANY {hanelt@uni-kassel.de}

Manuel Wiesche Digital Transformation, TU Dortmund University, Dortmund, GERMANY {manuel.wiesche@tu-dortmund.de}

Alexander Benlian Information Systems & E-Services, Technical University of Darmstadt, Darmstadt, GERMANY {benlian@ise.tu-darmstadt.de}

Oliver Hinz Information Systems and Information Management, Goethe University Frankfurt am Main Frankfurt am Main, GERMANY {ohinz@wiwi.uni-frankfurt.de}

The growing integration of AI into professional domains amplifies the epistemic and relational stakes for domain experts—ranging from financial advisors to medical and legal professionals—who must entrust their clients to emerging AI-based counterparts. For these domain experts in triadic relationships, trusting AI is challenging, as AI’s inscrutability and autonomy provoke fundamental trust tensions of opacity vs. performance and replacement vs. complementarity. Drawing on an in-depth longitudinal case study at a large European bank that established a robo-advisor alongside domain experts and their clients, we investigated how domain experts navigate these trust tensions. We found that they interpret the AI-based counterpart with regard to their relational networks (relational interpretation) and subsequently adapt these networks (relational adaptation) to cope with emerging vulnerabilities. Domain experts develop trust through three stages that engender specific sequences of relational interpretation and adaptation, leading to avoided, safeguarded, and, ultimately, accepted vulnerabilities. We contribute to research on trust in AI by overcoming the focus on dyadic perspectives involving AI and users to uncover complex interrelationships between different trustors and specific trust tensions. Going beyond technology-centric perspectives on trust-building processes, our longitudinal analysis reveals relational perspectives on trusting beliefs and behaviors in a multistage process where influences of the social context precede and shape trust in AI.

Keywords: Artificial intelligence, trust, triadic relationships, domain experts, robo-advisory

## Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) can be defined as “the ability of a machine to perform cognitive functions that we associate with human minds, such as perceiving, reasoning, learning, interacting with the environment, problem solving, decisionmaking, and even demonstrating creativity” (Rai et al., 2019, p. iii). The association with human minds indicates ambivalence in the human-AI relationship (Carroll et al., 2024; Huysman, 2020). Although AI-based systems can achieve high performance in cognitive functions (Constantinides et al., 2024), humans frequently encounter difficulties in comprehending how such performance is generated (Asatiani et al., 2021; Berente et al., 2021). Relatedly, delegating cognitive functions to AI can free up human capacities (Constantinides et al., 2024; Grønsund & Aanestad, 2020), yet also creates fears of being replaced (Strich et al., 2021). Accordingly, establishing human trust in AI-based systems remains a critical and complex challenge that has attracted significant research in recent years (Vanneste & Puranam, 2024; Wang et al., 2023; Yang & Wibowo, 2022). This research has almost exclusively focused on the trust exhibited by the direct users of AI (Glikson & Woolley, 2020; Thiebes et al., 2021). However, as AI-based systems spread, trust in them is not just about users anymore—it also involves those who recommend these systems to others without using them directly (Berente et al., 2021; Glikson & Woolley, 2020; Lukyanenko et al., 2022).

Trust, defined as “the willingness of a party to be vulnerable to the actions of another party based on the expectation that the other will perform a particular action important to the trustor, irrespective of the ability to monitor or control that other party” (Mayer et al., 1995, p. 712), may evolve differently, depending on the type of trustor in relation to the AI-based system (Lockey et al., 2021; Vanneste & Puranam, 2024). AI users interact directly with an AI-based system, its design, and interfaces, and are directly affected by the system’s operations (Yang & Wibowo, 2022). Increasingly, however, triadic relationships, including AI-based systems, users, and domain experts, such as doctors, financial advisors, or attorneys, are emerging. In our study, we refer to domain experts in such triadic relationships (simply domain experts hereafter) as individuals drawing on relational expertise— professional knowledge and skills developed, applied, and evaluated in interaction (Pakarinen & Huising, 2023) in a particular professional domain. Their “performances are developed in interaction with those who are subject to the performance of expertise” (Pakarinen & Huising, 2023, p. 14), whom we refer to as clients. For the latter, domain experts diagnose personal situations, scrutinize potential solutions, and then give advice to help them achieve their personal objectives (Cruciani et al., 2021; Schwartz et al., 2011). Examples of triadic relationship contexts include human financial advisors recommending robo-advisors to their customers for particular investments (Zhang et al., 2021), doctors recommending AI-based medical systems that their patients interact with for specific aspects of treatments or diagnoses, such as health monitoring (Bohr & Memarzadeh, 2020), and attorneys recommending robo-lawyers to their clients under certain circumstances involving standard cases (Marchant & Covey, 2018). In these triadic relationships, an AI-based system can be understood as a counterpart—i.e., “an actor within a system of relations that extend outside of its immediate context” (Anthony et al., 2023; p. 1673)—that operates within the professional domain of human experts while interacting with their clients. Domain experts play a pivotal role in embedding these AI-based counterparts within professional practices and entrusting their clients to them.

Compared to the prevalent view on AI-user trust dyads in prior work, these relationships mark a theoretically distinct form of trust in AI, as different vulnerabilities are involved (Lockey et al., 2021). Domain experts’ key vulnerability lies in the potential erosion of relational expertise, a core asset underpinning their professional authority (Pakarinen & Huising, 2023). The AI facets of autonomy and inscrutability (Berente et al., 2021) create trust tensions regarding this key vulnerability—contradictory effects, where each facet may potentially increase or decrease the willingness of domain experts to be vulnerable to the actions of the AI-based counterpart (Vanneste & Puranam, 2024). AI-based systems, due to their autonomy—i.e., the ability to act without human input (Berente et al., 2021)—can take over routine tasks at scale and speed, freeing up domain experts’ time for complex problem solving (Grønsund & Aanestad, 2020) or cultivating client relationships. However, autonomy may lead to concerns about job displacement, especially by sidelining domain experts in client relationships (Strich et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2023). AI-based systems also tend to be inscrutable, relying on complex algorithms that few can interpret (Berente et al., 2021). These algorithms can enable superior performance (Asatiani et al., 2021), which could strengthen the client relationship when domain experts suggest an AI-based system to a client. However, domain experts’ ability to explain recommendations to clients may be impacted (Schwartz et al., 2011). Thus, for domain experts, AI facets cause trust tensions of replacement vs. complementarity and opacity vs. performance, which render trust building in AI-based counterparts particularly challenging. While scholars have called for broadening the focus beyond user trust to include triadic relationships to advance research on trust in AI (Glikson & Woolley, 2020; Lockey et al., 2021; Lukyanenko et al., 2022), our knowledge remains incomplete. Accordingly, in this study, we investigate the following research question: How do domain experts in triadic relationships navigate trust tensions when confronted with AIbased counterparts?

To find answers to this research question, we investigated the robo-advisory context in traditional banking. Robo-advisors, “digital platforms comprising interactive and intelligent user assistance components that use information technology to guide customers through an automated (investment) advisory process” (Jung et al., 2018a, p. 81), can be particularly characterized by the AI facets of autonomy and inscrutability (Berente et al., 2021; Ge et al., 2021). This context is promising for our investigation, as it is representative of settings in which AI-based counterparts are introduced alongside domain experts who are expected to build trust in these systems, even though they face the aforementioned trust tensions around their relational expertise (Altrock et al., 2023). We conducted a case study over more than 5 years, involving 89 interviews as well as secondary material and observations at EuroBank, a large traditional financial institution that has developed, introduced, and scaled the robo-advisor EVA. Applying grounded theory analyses to longitudinal data (Langley, 1999), we examined how EuroBank domain experts responded to EVA and how their perceptions and behaviors changed over time. We found that they engaged in relational interpretation, whereby they framed the perceived opportunities and threats associated with AI facets against the backdrop of their related relational networks (comprising the triadic relationship with customers and the robo-advisor), and relational adaptation, whereby domain experts changed their relational networks as a response. Our findings indicate that domain expert trust building proceeds over three stages that engender specific sequences of relational interpretation and adaptation to navigate the opacityperformance and replacement-complementarity trust tensions. Initially, domain experts underscored hypothetical relational threats posed by the AI-based system, drew attention to its potential imperfections, and restricted interactions with it (leading to avoided vulnerabilities). Subsequently, based on interactions with human experts and peers who had experience with the AI-based system, domain experts mitigated the perceived threats by foregrounding human support and human input-output controls in their thinking and by building additional human interactions around the AI-based system (leading to safeguarded vulnerabilities). Eventually, based on first experiences gained with their clients using the AI-based system, domain experts started self-experimenting with EVA. They explored the opportunities associated with the AI facets, came to appreciate the robo-advisor’s distinct characteristics, and hybridized their relationships with clients to make better use of those characteristics (leading to accepted vulnerabilities).

Our study makes two major contributions to the literature on trust in AI. First, we challenge the prevailing dyadic lens of the AI-user relationship (Glikson & Woolley, 2020; Yang & Wibowo, 2022). By advancing a triadic perspective, our work enriches the theoretical understanding of trust in AI, acknowledging the complex interrelationships among multiple trustors (Saffarizadeh et al., 2024). We discuss how trust-building interventions yield ambivalent outcomes across different trustors, revealing both distinct and interrelated effects that are critical for a more nuanced conceptualization of trust in AI. Specifically, our study indicates that AI facets and the associated trust tensions are not fixed, but malleable and shaped in interaction. Second, going beyond existing technology-centric perspectives, where trust building primarily hinges on the design, operations, and performance of AI-based systems (Glikson & Woolley, 2020), our work offers a more comprehensive view on trust building in AI, shaped by influences going beyond the technical AI artifact. In particular, we highlight the importance of the key processes of relational interpretation and adaptation, which afford a novel relational perspective on the concepts of trusting beliefs and behaviors (Hengstler et al., 2016; Lee & See, 2004; Thiebes et al., 2021). The analysis of the different sequences of relational interpretation and adaptation over time reveals the powerful role of the social context (Sergeeva et al., 2017), preceding and shaping trust building in AI-based counterparts. Social interactions create a safe space for domain experts to gather first experiences, which leads them to cautiously open their networks to the AI-based counterpart before they expose themselves and their clients directly to it. Finally, these insights provide practical guidance, particularly for cultivating trust in AI in complex triadic constellations.

## Background

## Trust in AI and Domain Experts in Triadic Relationships

According to prior research, trust in AI-based systems is expressed in terms of whether and how users interact with AIbased systems (Dzindolet et al., 2003; Vuori et al., 2025). This AI trusting behavior relating to system or feature use is based on trusting beliefs that users form toward the AI-based system (Lee & See, 2004; Thiebes et al., 2021). Prior work regularly refers to AI trusting beliefs, such as performance, purpose, and process (Glikson & Woolley, 2020; Hengstler et al., 2016; Lee & See, 2004; Thiebes et al., 2021). Performance refers to a system’s ability to achieve users’ goals (Wang et al., 2023). Purpose refers to the degree to which “the system is used within the realm of the designer’s intent” (Lee & See, 2004, p. 59)—why the system was developed. Process refers to the degree to which a system’s algorithms fit the situation and the users’ goals (Wang et al., 2023). The trust-building process portrayed in prior works thus involves users evaluating the AIbased system to form trusting beliefs (Vanneste & Puranam, 2024), then deciding upon usage and a trust calibration based on the usage experience (Lee & See, 2004; Thiebes et al., 2021). As an influence on this process, initial work has found that users may transfer trust from other technologies or providers onto an AI-based system (Renner et al., 2022; Saffarizadeh et al., 2024). Even though this knowledge is valuable, current research focuses primarily on initial and short-term perspectives, while longitudinal perspectives are scarce (Yang & Wibowo, 2022). Existing work indicates that depending on the specific form of representation (embedded, robotic, or virtual AI), initial trust in AI might be high or low and can develop over time in the opposite direction through user experience with the AI artifact, its operations, and performance; this dynamic is moderated by the level of machine intelligence (Glikson & Woolley, 2020).

Additionally, prior work has uncovered factors that help humans build trust in AI-based systems (Hoff & Bashir, 2015; Vanneste & Puranam, 2024). Here, some research has indicated that social influences, such as norms, values, or cultures, alter user evaluations (Yang & Wibowo, 2022). However, the majority of work on trust-building factors foregrounds technical aspects. For instance, Hengstler et al. (2016) found that performance beliefs are influenced by perceived operational and data security, process beliefs are associated with the system’s cognitive compatibility and usability, and purpose beliefs form around a contextualized system design. More systematically, a comprehensive review of trust in AI summarizes the following trust-building factors: AI’s tangibility, transparency, reliability, immediacy behaviors, and anthropomorphism (Glikson & Woolley, 2020). These factors mainly relate to the design and interface of AI-based systems and assume a direct user-system interaction (Glikson & Woolley, 2020; Lockey et al., 2021).

In sum, current research on trust in AI emphasizes two main perspectives. First, it follows a technology-centric view, linking trust building to the AI-based system’s design, intelligence, and performance, as well as measures to improve them (Glikson & Woolley, 2020; Thiebes et al., 2021; Vanneste & Puranam, 2024). One such group of actors comprises domain experts (Lockey et al., 2021). Prior research has portrayed domain experts in different ways with regard to AI-based systems (see Appendix A). For instance, in many contexts, domain experts directly use AI-based systems, inter alia, by utilizing the output generated by the system to improve or reflect upon their decision-making (Abdel-Karim et al., 2023). In other settings, domain experts have been deemed responsible for the quality of the data associated with AI-based systems, as they provide knowledge inputs or evaluate the outputs of such systems (Grønsund & Aanestad, 2020; van den Broek et al., 2021). While these domain experts are, in some way, directly interacting with the system (either during build time or run time), the perspective of domain experts who are not interacting with the system but are expected to act alongside AI-based counterparts while taking care of their clients is not well understood. Their vulnerability not only includes their professional skills and knowledge but also the interactions through which they are developed, applied, and evaluated (Pakarinen & Huising, 2023).

These domain experts operate within triadic relationships with AI-based systems and their clients as users (see Figure 1). This triad constitutes a network of trust (Söllner et al., 2016), where trust must be established not only between experts and users (Schwartz et al., 2011) but also toward the AI-based system, given its role and inherent vulnerabilities (Rai, 2020; Vanneste & Puranam, 2024). While the AI-based system-user trust relationship (relationship “a” in Figure 1) has been widely studied, the trust relationships between domain experts and AI systems (relationship “b”) and between domain experts and users vis-à-vis AI-based systems (relationship “c”) remain underexplored (Lukyanenko et al., 2022).

## Domain Experts’ Trust Tensions When Faced with AI-Based Counterparts

For domain experts, relational expertise is the foundation of their professional authority and autonomy (Pakarinen & Huising, 2023). Accordingly, any threat to relational expertise represents a key vulnerability (Lockey et al., 2021). When confronted with AI-based counterparts in triadic relationships, domain experts face trust tensions with regard to their relational expertise due to the AI facets of inscrutability and autonomy (Berente et al., 2021). Regarding these AI facets, advisory domain experts may perceive opportunities and threats that may increase or decrease their willingness to be vulnerable to AI-based counterparts.

## The Opacity-Performance Trust Tension Caused by Inscrutable AI-Based Systems

The performance potentials of AI-based systems frequently come at the expense of difficulties in understanding why systems produce a certain output (Vanneste & Puranam, 2024). High-performing AI models are often based on inscrutable machine learning (ML) techniques, such as deep neural networks that challenge the comprehension and explanation of their decisions and actions (Gunning et al., 2019). This creates a need “to balance the performance benefits of flexible AI models with the risks that inscrutable models can entail” (Asatiani et al., 2021, p. 325).

Inscrutable systems produce a level of opacity (Vanneste & Puranam, 2024) that makes it challenging to discern how these systems work (Burton et al., 2020). This can undermine human trust (Glikson & Woolley, 2020; Leavitt et al., 2024). To overcome the opacity issue for users, prior work has described the importance of measures that increase the transparency and explainability of the AI artifact (Rai, 2020) or of algorithmic brokers who can translate AI-based systems for potential users (Kellogg et al., 2020; Waardenburg et al., 2022).

In triadic relationships, opacity is even more problematic for domain experts, as it may threaten their relational expertise, which is expressed in diagnosing, explaining, and recommending solutions by drawing on their knowledge and skills in interaction with clients (Cruciani et al., 2021; Schwartz et al., 2011). Without fully understanding AI-based systems, domain experts may struggle to call upon their expert knowledge and may find it challenging to “be able to explain and justify decisions to other stakeholders” (Lockey et al., 2021, p. 5468), their clients, in particular. In such contexts, domain experts would be compelled to assume the role of translators who mediate between opaque algorithmic procedures and human understanding (Leavitt et al., 2024). However, in contrast to AI experts or algorithmic brokers (Kellogg et al., 2020), domain experts draw on legal, medical, or financial knowledge, but typically do not possess technical AI knowledge to explain the behavior of AI-based systems to their clients (Altrock et al., 2023). Thus, their inability to understand and explain AI-based systems may threaten their expert position in client relationships (Anthony et al., 2023; Rai, 2020).

However, inscrutable ML techniques regularly contribute to systems’ overall performance. ML algorithms in AI-based systems enable them to make decisions with high effectiveness and efficiency (Sturm et al., 2021), process more data in a shorter period, and identify relationships in high-dimensional spaces (Wang et al., 2023). They often outperform humans in analysis or decision-making situations (Constantinides et al., 2024), such as court bail decisions or the detection of chronic lung diseases in computed tomography scan data (Jussupow et al., 2021; Kleinberg et al., 2018; Marchant & Covey, 2018). Moreover, AI-based systems are designed to make decisions grounded in formal rationality, that is, through programmed logical and mathematical procedures aimed at optimizing predefined goals (Knight, 2017). This stands in contrast to human decision-making, which often struggles to achieve such levels of consistent rationality (Raisch & Krakowski, 2021).

In triadic relationships, well-performing AI-based systems may preserve or even enhance domain experts’ relational expertise, as high-quality recommendations can strengthen expert-client relationships (Cruciani et al., 2021; Schwartz et al., 2011). Importantly, since domain experts employ a multitude of such relationships, they stand to broadly benefit. Thus, when leveraging the functional capability of AI-based systems (Rai et al., 2019), domain experts must simultaneously entrust their clients to the system while knowing that they themselves do not fully understand the system. It is unclear how domain experts navigate this opacity-performance trust tension.

## The Replacement-Complementarity Trust Tension Caused by Autonomous AI-Based Systems

Autonomy refers to an AI-based system’s “capacity to act independently, without direct input from its creators or users” (Saffarizadeh et al., 2024, p. 647). The potential of autonomous AI-based systems to complement human capabilities is linked to the threat of replacement (Grønsund & Aanestad, 2020; Wang et al., 2023), as these systems have increasingly taken over tasks previously undertaken by human actors (Glikson & Woolley, 2020). While this phenomenon may devalue the role of human actors in some cases (Wang et al., 2023), studies also report that, through AI support, “human capacity is freed to take on non-routine tasks and capacity and productivity can increase” (Grønsund & Aanestad, 2020, p. 2).

Autonomous AI-based systems pose a significant threat to trust development when they assume human tasks in ways that displace workers or render professional roles redundant (Pachidi et al., 2021; Strich et al., 2021), thereby undermining human willingness to trust the system (Vorobeva et al., 2022). AI-based systems comprise logical and mathematical procedures that can potentially replace humans in a variety of tasks (Pakarinen & Huising, 2023; Raisch & Krakowski, 2021; Vorobeva et al., 2022). Human actors distrust systems they perceive as threatening their work, triggering reactions such as resistance and noncooperation (Kellogg et al., 2020).

For domain experts in triadic relationships, the threat of replacement is even more evident. While experts are often rather skeptical toward AI-based systems operating in their own domain of expertise (Glikson & Woolley, 2020), in triadic relationships, autonomous systems not only act on their own but also interact with clients autonomously, potentially leading to disintermediation by taking the domain expert out of the loop (Altrock et al., 2023; Kellogg et al., 2020). In their relationships, domain experts interact frequently with their clients to enrich and strengthen their relationships (Schwartz et al., 2011). As they draw on relational expertise, this represents a threat to the expert position of domain experts in triadic relationships, which critically depends on these interactions (Pakarinen & Huising, 2023).

Conversely, autonomy can potentially allow AI-based systems to complement human work (Grønsund & Aanestad, 2020). Autonomously taking over well-defined, standardized tasks can free up resources to help humans focus on the complex, tacit aspects of their work (Jussupow et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2023). In triadic relationships, domain experts form a multitude of relationships, in which each relationship involves several tasks with varying demands. Domain experts can use the strengths of AI-based systems in processing and analyzing large amounts of data to support work related to their individual domain knowledge, freeing up time to focus on complex problem-solving or the value-adding aspects of their relationship work with their clients (Altrock et al., 2023). Thus, when leveraging AI-based systems’ autonomy, domain experts must simultaneously entrust their clients to the system, knowing that they are being partially taken out of the loop.

In sum, prior research on trust in AI-based systems has primarily followed a technology-centric perspective on trust building and almost exclusively focused on the dyadic AIbased system-user relationship (Glikson & Woolley, 2020; Lukyanenko et al., 2022; Yang & Wibowo, 2022), while the perspective of domain experts in triadic relationships as trustors is largely absent in the literature. For these actors, AI’s inscrutability and autonomy create opacity-performance and replacement-complementarity trust tensions relevant to their relational expertise. Our research explores how domain experts navigate these trust tensions, contributing to a more comprehensive understanding of trust building in AI.

## Methodology

We conducted a longitudinal case study using grounded theory as the main analytical strategy (Langley, 1999) and building on the principle of emergence from data (Glaser & Strauss, 1967). Our initial goal was to understand the consequences of AI-based systems on established customerfacing organizations and their human experts. We purposefully selected EuroBank, a traditional financial institution and one of the biggest players in the European market. EuroBank stands out as a revelatory case (Yin, 2009) because, despite its long history, the bank managed to develop and successfully launch its very own robo-advisor, EVA. During the project, we engaged in a dialogue between the literature and the empirical setting (Monteiro et al., 2022), refining our broad research goal into a specific focus on how domain experts navigate trust tensions when confronted with AI-based counterparts.

## Research Setting

Our study focuses on the robo-advisor EVA, which EuroBank introduced in response to perceived new market demands. EVA was developed and introduced by a team in the digital innovation unit, the EVA team, in close collaboration with EuroBank’s investment office and support functions. EVA was later introduced into EuroBank’s sales function, where human financial advisors—the domain experts in our empirical case— were organized (see Figure A1 in the transparency materials for an overview of the organizational chart).<sup>2</sup>

The incorporation of the AI-based robo-advisor changed the work of the domain experts in EuroBank’s sales organization (see Appendix B to learn more about the research context of robo-advisory). Before the introduction of EVA, domain experts, who were tasked with building and managing longterm relationships with their clients and were not compensated per product sale, would meet their clients in their EuroBank branch office and discuss their personal and financial circumstances and their future plans in a structured process during personal conversations. In these conversations, domain experts used IT tools to support documenting and managing the workflow of the advisory practices, which also created paper-based forms and documentation materials. Based on their analysis of the client situation, domain experts developed a financial strategy and, mostly in follow-up conversations, suggested a portfolio of different investments according to their customers’ characteristics and preferences. Domain experts contacted their clients regularly to analyze and adjust these portfolios to changing market or personal conditions. Additionally, domain experts and their clients often developed more personal relationships.

With the introduction of EVA, domain experts could point their clients to the robo-advisor portal embedded in EuroBank’s online banking services to automatically invest and rebalance clients’ investments. Rather than having a personal conversation with a domain expert to identify personal circumstances and preferences, clients entered their data into the AI-based system themselves. Based on clients’ risk preferences, EVA compiled asset portfolios. EVA’s algorithm continuously monitored individual customer portfolios and automatically reallocated asset classes based on the system’s market assessment—without major human involvement. Clients were informed about reallocation decisions in their online banking accounts and via email. Thus, potential interactions between domain experts and clients were reduced or transferred to the AI-based system, especially when clients had no other investments with EuroBank.

EVA autonomously adjusted portfolios based on the aforementioned risk assessment and integrated market development expectations. For that purpose, the algorithm learned from historical market trends, subjective market assessments by human experts, and past investment performance. While the algorithm was intelligible to some technical members of the EVA team, it remained opaque to most of the clients and domain experts, because several investment strategies and calculations were intertwined and dynamically evolving. To make investment decisions automatically, EVA followed portfolio-optimization theory supplemented by a Bayesian approach to asset allocation, using prior return estimates and market predictions (Beketov et al., 2018). It employed a value-at-risk (VaR) approach to account for an individual customer’s preferences (Beketov et al., 2018). Customers specified their individual target VaR in the onboarding procedure and could change it later, if needed. The VaR approach was central to the automated portfolio strategy for the customer’s portfolio in the different asset classes available, for example, ETFs on stocks, bonds, cash, real estate, and commodities. EVA’s algorithm automatically rebalanced assets based on current and predicted future market developments by drawing on a plethora of data sources, including past developments, market predictions, and EuroBank’s proprietary market assessments. If EVA detected a discrepancy between the actual and contractually stipulated risk levels, it autonomously generated a specific recommendation for reallocation during its overnight processing cycle. A portfolio manager reviewed this recommendation, and if the manager agreed to the reallocation, it was carried out to reposition the investor’s portfolio within the desired risk corridor. EVA’s design aligned with state-of-the-art robo-advisors (Jung et al., 2018a; Zhang et al., 2021). Figure H1 in the transparency materials provides an illustration of how EVA operates.

## Data Collection

We collected data at EuroBank over a period of 62 months (see Figure B1 in the transparency materials for the timeline of the case and our data collection). Data collection started with interviewing the EVA team to gain an initial understanding of the case. We then sampled interviewees in other departments to capture the perspectives of sales, product design, regulatory context, technology, and strategy on EVA. During this process, we learned about the central role of domain experts and their relationships with clients in the sales function for the success of the robo-advisor, which further informed our sampling approach. We interviewed domain experts, such as advisors and senior advisors, as well as branch managers and regional sales managers, who had an overview of many domain experts in their area.

We employed both substantive theoretical sampling (e.g., we sampled domain experts for interviews based on whether they had already interacted with the EVA team or not, and triangulated domain expert views with branch manager interviews, documents, and observations) and extensive theoretical sampling (e.g., engaging with a telephone support unit, experts in other investment products, and EuroBank’s competitors) (Gregory et al., 2015). After the EVA team helped us identify branches that had successfully adopted EVA and those that were struggling to adopt EVA, we used a snowballing approach to identify further domain experts in different regions who had different types of experience with EVA. We visited bank branches and observed sales practices to better understand advisors’ descriptions of their practices.

We conducted a total of 89 interviews, our primary data source, 22 field observations, and collected more than 100 secondary data documents, primarily to triangulate the interview findings (see Table C1 in the transparency materials for an overview of the interviewees). We analyzed internal and market data on EVA and observed internal project meetings and external sales events, such as launch workshops for customers. We document the questions from our generic interview guides in Section D of the transparency materials. In total, we wrote 251 analytical memos to document our findings and conceptual development.

The insights gathered through the first wave of interviews indicated that trust by domain experts is subject to temporal dynamics, which spurred our interest in how and why trust in AI by domain experts evolves over time. Accordingly, we decided to collect longitudinal data (Langley et al., 2013). In particular, interviewing the same domain experts multiple times at different time points allowed us to track developments and understand changes in domain experts’ perceptions of, behaviors toward, and relationships with the AI-based counterpart and the associated vulnerabilities over time. We also went back to the EVA team several times to clarify open issues around events related to EVA and to understand contextual and organizational developments and their implications for domain expert trust building. We further interviewed clients to triangulate advisors’ views and understand how domain expert trust and the AI-based system were perceived.

During data collection, we also learned about the phases preceding EVA’s introduction. For instance, two years prior to EVA’s launch, EuroBank experimented with a predecessor robo-advisor, InvestmentGuru, which was terminated after one year. We took a retrospective approach to understanding these early developments (see Figure B1 of the transparency materials). As interviewees tend to filter out events that do not align with their mindset (Poole et al., 2000), we took three measures to improve the validity of these reports. First, we asked informants about specific events rather than abstract concepts. Second, we verified individual reports by asking multiple informants similar questions. Third, we used a plethora of secondary data to triangulate our interview data (Miles & Huberman, 1994). For instance, we were provided with descriptions of the client personas used for developing EVA, the presentations through which EVA was introduced to domain experts, presentations that domain experts developed to explain their advisory practices, and all the email templates and documents that were used to inform clients about EVA.

## Data Analysis and Process Theorizing

To analyze our rich and varied longitudinal data (Langley et al., 2013), we utilized a combination of process theorizing strategies (Langley, 1999). In particular, we employed grounded theory as our focal analytical strategy, but following process study role models (van den Broek et al., 2021; Waardenburg et al., 2022), we made use of narrative and visual mapping strategies as preliminary and exploratory steps beforehand. Multiple iterations of data collection, analysis, and comparisons with literature were intertwined (Charmaz, 2006).

## Narrative Strategy

In each iteration, based on interviews with the EVA team and other stakeholders who could deliver some overview (e.g., EuroBank managers) as well as secondary data (e.g., press releases, news articles, internal slide sets), we aimed to construct an event chronology regarding the evolution of EVA within EuroBank (Berends & Deken, 2021). This chronology helped us develop an overview of the overall process (Eisenhardt, 1989) and resulted in timeline figures (see Figure B1 in the transparency materials for the final version) and the case narrative (which varied in detail and focus over time, see Findings section for the final version). Importantly, however, this approach served only as a “preliminary step aimed at preparing a chronology for subsequent analysis—essentially, a data organization device that can also serve as a validation tool” (Langley, 1999, p. 695).

## Visual Mapping Strategy

In each iteration, the case narrative of the overall development of EVA at EuroBank provided an overarching frame for our analysis. We identified that a key theme was the building of trust that domain experts developed toward EVA. From the interviews, we found that this trust changed over time and that the sequences of events seemed to matter to explain this development (Langley et al., 2013). To make sense of this development in the rich data, as an exploratory step, we decided “to construct a visual map that portrayed how certain activities triggered specific events” (Waardenburg et al., 2022, p. 65) in each iteration. Ultimately, these process drawings were further condensed into data reduction tables (following the logic of Vaast & Pinsonneault, 2022), revealing the progression of specific domain experts in their trust building over time (see the final version in Section G of the transparency materials). This visual mapping strategy helped us to make sense of the different stages and triggers for our emerging process model.

## Grounded Theory Strategy

In each iteration, complementing the preliminary narrative and explorative visual mapping strategies, we employed grounded theory as the focal analytical strategy (Langley, 1999) to understand how domain experts built trust in EVA over time. In particular, each domain expert served as an instance with their trust building as a process that could be traced and compared with others (Langley et al., 2013). Thus, we examined “not just one process story, but several, allowing theoretical ideas to be tested and deepened” (Langley et al., 2013, p. 7). This approach led to the gradual development of our key concepts and categories (Langley, 1999), guided by the analytical approach of Gioia et al. (2013). We began the analysis with open coding— identifying initial concepts in the data and grouping them into categories, while ensuring that the open codes remained close to our data (Wiesche et al., 2017). This process led to lists of firstorder concepts, with multiple corresponding empirical observations (Gioia et al., 2013). Based on this first-order analysis, an informant-centric analysis, we engaged in axial coding as a second-order and theory-centric analysis, to frame concepts and themes (Gioia et al., 2013), which was informed by identified connections to and blind spots in the literature. As the third step, we distilled the emergent concepts into “aggregate dimensions” related to the trust-building process of domain experts—three stages of domain expert trust building, in particular. We continuously iterated between the empirical concepts and the relevant literature to substantiate our categories and identify the relationships between and among them (Wiesche et al., 2017). We ultimately identified the process of how domain experts navigated trust tensions as central to their trust building (Langley et al., 2013). In each iteration, we made use of the Gioia data structure template (Gioia et al., 2013) to structure and present our findings (see the final data structure in Figure E1 of the transparency materials). Finally, we connected our emerging categories with the data reduction tables and visual maps developed beforehand. This allowed us to allocate categories to different stages and develop a generalizable process theory (Langley et al., 2013) (see Figure 3).

Our thematic analysis evolved across different iterations (see Figure 2 for a summary). In the first two iterations, we identified the emerging patterns that the EVA team used to (1) tailor the robo-advisor system design to align with EuroBank’s processes, and (2) support the robo-advisor in the sales organization. Our analysis revealed that the AI facets of inscrutability and autonomy caused a lack of trust by domain experts. In Iterations 3 and 4, we uncovered the domain expert–client–robo-advisor triadic relationship as important in the trust-building process. By comparing data slices (see Tables F1 and F2 in the transparency materials), in Iteration 5, we identified two central patterns in the trust building process of how domain experts perceived (i.e., “relational interpretation”) and responded (i.e., “relational adaptation”) to the AI-based system. We found that domain experts made use of these key processes to react to the AI facets of inscrutability and autonomy, which spurred interest in further inquiry. In this iteration, we used the AI facets to engage deeper with the AI literature and identified trust tensions comprising opportunities and threats connected to autonomy and inscrutability (Asatiani et al., 2021; Grønsund & Aanestad, 2020; Wang et al., 2023). In Iteration 6, oscillating between the literature and our data, we identified clear links between the inductively derived concepts and the AI trust tensions. In particular, we found that relational interpretation was caused by a trigger and was related to the domain expert’s framing of trust tensions, whereas relational adaptation represented a response to these tensions, resulting in particular conditions regarding the key vulnerability of domain experts, that is, relational expertise. This focus helped to sharpen our understanding of the process of domain experts’ trust building in three stages, which resulted in avoided, safeguarded, and accepted vulnerabilities.

## Findings

## Case Narrative

When EuroBank introduced EVA, the domain experts were confronted with the robo-advisor only from afar. There was no particular support directed toward domain experts or specified communication about how they could embed EVA into existing advisory processes. In addition, in the beginning, there was no technical solution for domain experts to sign clients directly into EVA in the branches. Clients could sign in independently, without needing to contact the domain expert. While the domain experts were used to discussing financial investments with their clients regarding their preferences, providing recommendations, and then organizing their execution, EVA handled all these aspects, with only minimal initial input from the client at the beginning and no involvement of the domain expert at all. EVA’s detailed functioning, particularly how and why the algorithm predicted necessary rebalancing, remained elusive to most of the domain experts, precluding them from explaining EVA’s moves to clients. While other investment alternatives required follow-up interactions between the domain expert and the client to discuss potential portfolio adaptations, EVA executed reallocations automatically by itself. Accordingly, fears about potential consequences started to emerge among most of the domain experts.

The EVA team quickly realized that unlocking EuroBank’s potential, driven by its widely dispersed network of domain experts, required more than just designing a good product. Besides providing better technical integration into the branches, they held road shows to present EVA to domain experts in the field. In the presentations, members of the EVA team personally emphasized how EVA could be applied by domain experts in the branches to actually achieve client retention and growth. These activities sparked interest among domain experts, particularly when the EVA team began providing a stage to peer domain experts who had already successfully implemented EVA with their clients. Moreover, members from the EVA team offered to personally attend joint workshops and conversations involving domain experts and their clients. Domain experts also learned about further human components, with EuroBank’s investment experts providing inputs to the algorithm and supervising the algorithm’s decisions. Over time, more and more domain experts started to approach selected clients to talk about EVA, often together with experts from the EVA team. EuroBank later also lowered the bar for its domain experts to experiment with EVA themselves. The domain experts who later decided to do so regularly had positive experiences. This self-experimentation, inter alia, helped them get a better grasp of EVA and spurred their motivation to integrate it into their client relationships, further driving their confidence in integrating EVA into their advisory practices and offering it on a larger scale.

Post-launch, the number of domain experts recommending EVA steadily increased. As a sign of normalization, after several years, the bank’s strategic management decided to transfer EVA to its regular product management portfolio.

EuroBank reduced the more specialized efforts by the EVA team over time, as management considered EVA to be established and successful, a perception widely shared among the domain experts.

## How Domain Experts Navigated Trust Tensions in Robo-Advisory at EuroBank

Our case analysis revealed that domain experts navigated trust tensions—opacity vs. performance and replacement vs. complementarity—via two key processes. First, they engaged in relational interpretation, whereby they framed the trust tensions (i.e., the perceived opportunities and threats associated with the AI facets of autonomy and inscrutability) against the background of their related relational networks (comprising the triadic relationship with clients and the robo-advisor). Second, they engaged in relational adaptation, whereby domain experts responded to relational interpretation by strengthening existing relationships, forming new ones, or deliberately distancing themselves from certain relationships. We found that domain expert trust was built over three stages, each engendering a particular sequence of relational interpretation followed by relational adaptation (see Table 1). Tables F1 and F2 in the transparency materials illustrate how relational interpretation and adaptation evolved over time. Each stage was started by a trigger that served as an impulse for relational interpretation to begin. Each sequence of relational interpretation and relational adaptation resulted in consequences in relation to coping with the key vulnerability of domain experts, that is, their relational expertise involving two components—professional knowledge/skills and the interactions in which they manifest. Table 1 provides an overview of the three stages. Figure E1 in the transparency materials depicts our data structure, including our key concepts (Gioia et al., 2013).

## Stage I: “Fearful Exclusion”

Isolated market introduction and rollout of EVA as initial trigger: Although robo-advisory was an emerging trend in the financial services industry, most domain experts at EuroBank had no prior experience with it when they first found out about the bank’s plans to develop a robo-advisor. At the beginning of market introduction, the domain experts working in decentralized branch offices were not involved in the initiative or in the preceding development phase. Initially, there was no particular support directed at the domain experts. It was not even possible for domain experts to sign clients in directly. At this point, there were rarely any insights available to the domain experts on the potential role that EVA could play in the client relationships. The domain experts only obtained basic information about EVA via EuroBank’s intranet rather incidentally.

Navigating the replacement-complementarity trust tension: In this initial stage, the replacementcomplementarity trust tension stemming from EVA’s autonomy was particularly salient. An EVA team member recalled: “That was the initial reaction of many [domain experts]—do I now get replaced by an automatic system?” Even though EVA had always been planned by strategic management and developed by the EVA team as an offering to complement personal advisory practices carried out in the branches, most domain experts confronted with the emergence of EVA did not perceive such potential but instead felt that their position in client relationships was being called into question by an intelligent autonomous system. In particular, these domain experts feared that if clients got wind of EVA and decided to use it, they would lose touch with them. A regional sales manager described his perception of the many advisors he supervised: “At the beginning, there was a lot of fear among the [domain experts]. They were saying things like, ‘Maybe the [client] doesn’t need me anymore when he [or she] can also do some inputs on the computer himself.’” Importantly, the domain experts pointed out that EVA differed from mere self-service online depot platforms, which existed before. Most clients appreciated consultations with domain experts before creating orders through such platforms. EVA, as an AI-based counterpart characterized by its autonomy, instead would interact with clients on its own and conduct automatic portfolio rebalancing. As the head of the telephone unit, who later supported domain experts with EVA, noted: “Theoretically, once a [client] has [EVA], he never has to talk to you again. … They [domain experts] are afraid that something will be taken away from them—the [client] relationship.”

Accordingly, building upon the limited points of contact they had with EVA, the domain experts built a pessimistic relational interpretation in which they perceived EVA’s autonomy and the associated potential to be replaced as a menace in the context of the triadic relationship in which they would be embedded. Following this interpretation, the domain experts engaged in a defensive relational adaptation by preventing interactions with anything related to EVA. In particular, the domain experts built a “wall of silence” around EVA and tried to direct clients’ attention toward other alternatives. As one advisor explained:

We first try several other things, to be honest. Only when a [client] is very cost sensitive and you have the feeling that he has a high digital affinity or when he says, “Don’t you have a digital investment solution?,” then you pull this out.

Navigating the opacity vs. performance trust tension: The domain experts who cautiously started to inform themselves about EVA additionally encountered challenges in comprehending EVA and how it functioned and thus started to navigate the opacity-performance trust tension stemming from EVA’s inscrutability. One senior domain expert explained: “It might be clever and everything, but as an advisor, I need to understand what I am talking about in front of the customer. Period.” The domain experts assumed high cognitive efforts when dealing with the robo-advisor, fearing they would struggle to explain it to their clients. Their pessimistic relational interpretation received further impetus, as they perceived opacity as a threat in the context of the triadic relationship. An EVA team member noted: “Domain experts could not understand the algorithm. Its complexity was a major source of insecurity.”

Accordingly, in the resulting relational adaptation, most domain experts searched for further ways to avoid interactions with EVA. First, they expressed their perceived doubts regarding the benefits of EVA for clients. Although the actual performance of EVA remained unclear to the domain experts at this point, they felt the need to differentiate themselves from it and point to its potential problems. One domain expert noted, “look, it is all databased. But data can be false. … So, I would rather stick to what I know and what I can control.” Second, when clients were insistently interested in EVA, the domain experts would explicitly externalize interactions that clients might have with EVA from their own practices and just send a link to the EVA website for clients to sign up themselves. Meanwhile, the domain experts focused their efforts on their known practices. In particular, they intensified their established relationships even more:

It is not that we hide [EVA] ... If the [client] wants only that, he or she can have it. But our focus is really set on other things that require our expertise as personal financial advisors, and EVA is something else.

Consequences: During the first stage, most of the domain experts developed a relational interpretation projecting high risks involved with the robo-advisor’s facets, which created a cognitive distance from EVA. The domain experts then manifested this distance behaviorally through relational adaptation targeted at preventing interactions with EVA and its perceived threats. The consequence of this sequence of relational interpretation and adaptation can be described as avoided vulnerabilities. The key vulnerability of domain experts lies in their relational expertise, comprising both professional skills and knowledge, and the interactions through which these competencies are used. Through their perceptions and actions in this first stage, the majority of the domain experts protected their established set of skills and knowledge, as well as client interactions, from the perceived negative influences and aimed to preserve the status quo. Accordingly, the domain experts aimed to create distance with EVA and the vulnerabilities it brought:

In the initial conversations with [clients], I make it very clear that there are two paths—one with and one without me as a [domain expert] on your side. If you decide on EVA, you need to do it by yourself. Then, I am the wrong [domain expert] for you.

The avoidance of vulnerabilities went hand in hand with avoiding gaining experience with EVA.

## Stage II: “Controlled Opening”

EVA team and peer interaction as trigger: A new iteration of relational interpretation and subsequent relational adaptation was triggered when EVA team members and peers experienced with EVA approached and interacted with domain experts. These interactions provided the domain experts with new insights on the potential role EVA could play in their client relationships. First, the EVA team took several measures, including personal conversations with domain experts to clarify the algorithm and its human inputs and oversights and to disseminate relative performance outcomes (i.e., comparing EVA’s performance with that of alternative types of investment). Second, via special road shows, the team gave presentations that illustrated EVA and how it could be integrated into domain expert practices. Additionally, the EVA team members approached individual branches and offered to visit them and explain EVA to all or selected domain experts and their clients on-site. The domain experts reported that through such meetings, they could personally relate to these individuals. One domain expert stated:

I was at one of these [events] for no specific reason, just wanted to see what end users were up to. There, I met an [EVA team member]. He gave me some details … and we scheduled a call. This was a sympathy thing; no strings attached. Not a sales order from head office. This is why I agreed.

Accordingly, experts from the EVA team described how, after such personal encounters, the domain experts “had a face” that they could connect to the topic, which positively influenced their view on it. Third, the EVA team speculated that sharing peer experiences would be very important in convincing the domain experts. Accordingly, those domain experts who were already successful in embedding EVA in their practices, representing exceptions among their colleagues, were offered a platform to present their experiences and acted as role models. As one domain expert recalled: “One day [head of EVA team] called me and asked me if I would do an online presentation about EVA for other [domain experts]. He knew that I was really into [EVA]. So, I did that several times.” Another domain expert role model, reflecting on having online appointments with other branches, stated: “In these calls, I made clear how EVA not only benefits the [client], but also the [domain expert]. That alleviated some fears, I guess.” Importantly, in a few instances, when the domain experts perceived a lack of support from the AI expert team or peers, for example, because they encountered EVA when it was “only” supported by regular product management, they did not proceed to the next stage of trust building.

Navigating the replacement-complementarity trust tension: At this stage, the domain experts were still encountering the replacement-complementarity trust tension. The EVA team’s presentations demonstrated how domain experts could successfully integrate EVA without losing control of client relationships. The practices shared by peers underscored how the domain experts did not render themselves redundant by recommending EVA but were able to further leverage their relational expertise, even though the robo-advisor was acting autonomously. It was important for the domain experts to see how their colleagues had found a way to integrate EVA and be successful without damaging their client relationships. One domain expert explained: “I was very happy to get to know my colleague [Name], who is a [domain expert] like myself, but also quickly became an expert and role model about EVA.” Thus, the domain experts extended their learning about the relational consequences via the experience of others, which led to less pessimistic relational interpretations: “It took some time for most domain experts, but we learned that despite automation [provided by EVA], we are still relevant [to our clients].” Accordingly, they began to partially open their relational networks for EVA but also applied safeguards by extending their own interactions with clients. One domain expert explained why he felt the need for some backup: “You are accountable to the [client]. You do not want the [client] to be knocking on your door angrily for recommending something that did not work.” On the one hand, the domain experts tried to leverage their professional competence and apply it to assess EVA with clients in a critical yet open manner: “We sit down with the [client] and we say, ‘Let’s see how [EVA] performed,’ and we check if the VaR is correctly specified for the goals of the customer or if we should change that.” On the other hand, the domain experts aimed to intensify interactions with their clients in areas not covered by EVA, such as insurance and real-estate investments, thus making it clear that even if EVA worked well, which could partially reduce the need for interactions between clients and domain experts, they would still have enough client interactions. One domain expert noted, “Maybe the customer needs a loan, or insurance, or another type of investment. We try to stay in touch with customers to learn about their needs in these areas.”

Navigating the opacity vs. performance trust tension: The more contact the domain experts had with EVA, the more they faced the opacity-performance trust tension. However, domain experts who were affected by the EVA team members’ activities changed their assessment of EVA and arrived at a more differentiated relational interpretation. First, the way in which the EVA team presented the algorithm to the domain experts created a new perception based on the human inputs and controls for the algorithm, as one EVA expert described: “It is good for the [domain experts]<sup>3</sup> that they know that [EuroBank] looks over it. That alleviated a lot of concerns.” One domain expert explained his view as follows: “I would not underestimate the human component. That is a key strength of [EVA]—that humans are still there working in the background. This, not the algorithm, creates trust and sympathy.” Thus, even though the majority of the domain experts still did not comprehend the algorithm, they “masked” the algorithm’s opacity by emphasizing the human input and output controls. Second, the EVA team’s communication of performance results, along with peer advisor reports, helped reduce the domain experts’ uncertainty about outcomes, as explained by one regional sales manager: “Meanwhile, we have a track record. We can see how EVA performed for our [clients] when the markets did this or that. We know how it performed in comparison with classic investment management solutions or multi-asset funds.” Aside from very few exceptions that articulated that they and their clients had different expectations, the vast majority of the domain experts were convinced by the performance when they hypothetically evaluated it “through the eyes” of their clients. Based on the resulting less pessimistic relational interpretation, the domain experts started to carefully introduce EVA, primarily through EVA team members, but they also created a “safety net” of interactions with these actors. The domain experts wanted to make sure that if they encountered trouble with EVA, another human would be there to help. One domain expert stated: “Now that we have this human support, the complexity of the algorithm is okay for me.” An EVA team member explained how domain experts felt “good when they can tell their [clients], ‘Here is an expert from [EuroBank head office] I brought for you. He can answer all of your questions.’” Similarly, the domain experts realized that they could be supported by an EVA team member during direct client conversations. The domain experts felt comfortable with this expertise and help, which decreased their perceived cognitive effort: “When it comes to the, let’s say, the mathematical models behind EVA, if I know someone who perfectly knows the engine, it feels great to have such a person at my side, just in case.” The domain experts introduced the robo-advisor to clients only when they perceived there was a low risk to the client relationship. The outcomes of these cautious first encounters with EVA signaled to the advisors that EVA could be a part of their work, that its opacity was somehow manageable, and that it would not replace them. Interestingly, when we interviewed clients to validate the domain expert’s perspective, most agreed with the ongoing importance of their advisors: “Of course, I want to keep my relationship with Mr. [Domain Expert Name]. He understands my personal situation and helps me manage my financial concerns. It is very personal and long-term. [EVA] is not hurting that.”

Consequences: In the second stage, the vast majority of the domain experts still did not emphasize opportunities for performance and complementarity. Instead, still aware of the opacity and replacement threats, through interactions with specific human actors (i.e., EVA team experts and advisor peers), they learned a new perspective. In particular, they emphasized human interventions, which mitigated perceived opacity threats, and they saw the potential for retaining client interactions, which mitigated perceived replacement threats. Building on these perceptions, they became open to using EVA in selected client relationships, accompanied by additional interactions with EVA experts and clients. Accordingly, the perceptions and actions in the second stage resulted in safeguarded vulnerabilities. Thus, their professional knowledge, skills, and interactions were more exposed than in the first stage, yet they were also protected by intensified social interactions acting as countermeasures. That way, the domain experts felt that they would be able to preserve their relational expertise, even though EVA was in place. This created a greater willingness to be vulnerable to EVA under controlled circumstances. A senior domain expert explained it as follows: “To be honest, I first approached [clients] whom I thought could be interested and whom I knew trusted me so much that I felt safe to test this out.” Under these controlled circumstances, the domain experts cautiously opened their client relations to EVA while also ensuring that they could keep their established way of working with their clients.

## Stage III: “Opportunistic Teaming”

Self-experimentation with EVA as a trigger: The third stage was triggered by the domain experts themselves. At this point, through the perceptions and actions in the second stage, most of the domain experts had gained initial experience with their clients using EVA and felt that they remained relevant to their clients. One domain expert stated, “You need to experience what EVA does. What it does to your [client] and to you as [a domain expert].” After they learned about the nonmanifestation of the initial fears they had, the domain experts started to more actively explore EVA’s opportunities—for example, by setting up accounts for themselves and putting themselves in their customers’ shoes:

At the beginning, EVA was just a product to me. … But when I signed in myself, I saw what it was like, how it reacted, and how it performed. That gave me more security to talk with [clients] about it, and for me, it changed to a much more positive view of EVA. Meanwhile, I also convinced my family and friends!

Through these interactions with EVA, they gathered new insights on the potential role that EVA could play in client relationships. The former head of the EVA team recalled his impression from dozens of domain expert-client conversations he witnessed: “I can really tell … that it makes a difference when the [domain expert] uses EVA personally. You can feel a whole other level of commitment and also credibility.” Importantly, as a few exceptions among the domain experts showed, when the domain experts perceived EVA’s market or technical performance to be not convincing enough during their self-experimentation or their clients’ interactions with EVA, they did not proceed to higher levels of trust.

Navigating the opacity vs. performance trust tension: In the third stage, while the opacity-performance trust tension was still salient, we observed how the domain experts increasingly downplayed the opacity threat. One domain expert stated: “I also do not know exactly how the coffee machine works, but I use it every day!” The domain experts thus started to accept EVA’s traits, even without additional safeguards. As one domain expert expressed, “I know that such things can also go wrong sometimes, but I am completely fine with that.” This acceptance of potential threats was connected to domain experts’ increasing awareness of EVA’s performance. The domain experts extended their own positive experiences with the roboadvisor to their client relationships to highlight its value: “I am a [EVA user] myself. I can draw from this experience as a [user] that it performs well, also technically.” Accordingly, the domain experts emphasized performance opportunities and submitted to an optimistic relational interpretation. Following this interpretation, they more broadly opened up their relational networks for EVA through relational adaptation. As one domain expert illustrated:

For us, it is important that the things we are recommending perform well for the client. And EVA has proved its performance to me in competition to alternatives, so, yes, of course, I am very positive about recommending it in my everyday practice.

As this quote indicates, the domain experts at this stage aimed to leverage EVA’s performance at scale by integrating it widely into their related relational networks. One domain expert told us: “When I realized the potential we have with EVA, I systematically called my list of [clients] to tell them about it.”

Navigating the replacement vs. complementarity trust tension: In addition, we found that while the replacementcomplementarity trust tension was still salient, domain experts no longer opposed threats related to replacement but valued the opportunity of complementarity. A senior domain expert explained how “having an algorithm that automatically monitors investments and rebalances is something that you should have in your portfolio as [a domain expert].” One domain expert noted: “I was afraid that the [client] may not need me any longer, as all he might want to do is EVA. Instead, I learned that it is a complement to what I am doing with the [client].” Domain experts increasingly viewed EVA as an inherent element of their advisory practices and as a valuable contribution to their relational networks. One domain expert referred to EVA as a “little colleague that doesn’t annoy me.” Another domain expert stated: “My colleagues made fun of me because I was so into EVA. One day, they changed the name on the sign on my door to EVA [Name].” Increasingly, domain experts recognized how they could profit from the complementarity that EVA offered. One domain expert described that he employs EVA to help clients who are still on the way to building amounts of capital that would qualify for more complex personal advisory: “I sometimes say EVA is my little sister, and she takes care of a lot of things for me before the big brother takes over.” With this relational interpretation involving a framing of teamwork and integration, the replacement threat seemed acceptable because everything would remain in “one team.”

Following this quite optimistic relational interpretation, the domain experts engaged in relational adaptation, where they opened their relational network by teaming up strongly with EVA in front of clients. The domain experts fostered identification with EVA in customer conversations: “What I am doing quite often is that I put my smartphone on the table and show [the clients] my EVA depot. To me, that is a bit of authenticity.” This strong identification with EVA also led to changes in client relationships. In particular, domain experts started to hybridize their client interactions with EVA, with some parts being taken over by EVA and others remaining with the domain expert: “These colleagues say, ‘All of that is the job of the robo-advisor, and I am completely fine with that.’” Accordingly, they aimed for a division of labor between EVA and themselves:

I would say these [domain experts] use the system for themselves. They send [the clients] home to let them do everything by themselves online. So, they keep their personal time investment small to have more time to serve even more [clients]. So, I would say they have completely understood the system and used it for their benefit.

Accordingly, they identified options to adjust their relationships with clients by focusing their efforts on discussing the overall personal situations of clients and working out more complex solutions. At the same time, EVA could take care of other things automatically in the meantime. Furthermore, the domain experts started using EVA to create, develop, and maintain relationships: “I called all [the clients] that I hadn’t had a very intense relationship with because they did not want to come into our branch very often. When they decided on EVA, I had a new connection with them.” Thus, the domain experts increasingly recognized EVA as an efficient way to “secure” clients without needing to invest too much personal effort.

Consequences: In the third stage, based on a sense of security created by initial client experiences with EVA and new insights gathered through their self-experimentation, domain experts started to recognize and value the opportunities of performance and complementarity and broadly opened their relational networks to team up with EVA to leverage them. The perceptions and actions during Stage III resulted in accepted vulnerabilities, where domain experts exposed their professional knowledge, skills, and interactions to the threats of opacity and replacement. The threats were no longer seen as something they needed to guard against but were accepted as part of EVA. The fact that these threats had not yet materialized helped ease concerns. More importantly, the potential opportunities associated with EVA outweighed the perceived risks for most domain experts. Thus, the domain experts at this stage aimed to leverage the relation to EVA to complement their professional knowledge and skills and hybridize client interactions for their benefit. Interestingly, we noted that with this higher level of trust, the domain experts also developed high expectations regarding EVA and a desire to leverage the opportunities even more: “It’s maybe time for an update of the user interface, so that I get fewer questions about this or that,” and regarding potential performance improvements, “for me, [EVA] could even be more aggressive in its investments.” Still, during Stage III, the domain experts had further increased their willingness to be vulnerable to EVA and established it as a standard element in their advisory practices.

## Discussion

In what follows, we first present our generalized theoretical model of domain expert trust building in AI-based counterparts. Then, we discuss the two major contributions of our work to the research on trust in AI. Finally, we provide a reflection on the boundary conditions and generalizability and on the managerial implications of our work.

## Process of Domain Expert Trust Building in AI-Based Counterparts

Building on the findings of the case, we developed a theoretical model of the trust-building process of domain experts in AI-based counterparts (see Figure 3 above). Our findings suggest that domain expert trust building proceeds over three stages that engender specific sequences of relational interpretation and adaptation. Initially, triggered by an isolated introduction of the AI-based counterpart—that is, without prior involvement of domain experts and without a specific support infrastructure targeted at them—domain experts substantially emphasize potential threats and try to exclude the AI-based counterpart from entering their relationships with clients. They keep the AI-based counterpart at bay, leading to avoided vulnerabilities and prevented interactions. Accordingly, external stimuli are required to catalyze further trust-building processes. Specifically, according to our theory, the second stage is triggered by interactions between domain experts and humans speaking for the AI-based counterpart, such as AI experts and peers with experience in embedding it in their client relationships, which provides new insights. Domain experts then reassess and mitigate the perceived threats by emphasizing human support and recognizing ways to retain client relationships. They cautiously begin to open their networks, contingent upon the presence of human AI experts who could act as mediators and ensure a human touchpoint in AI-mediated client exchanges, leading to safeguarded vulnerabilities. Through this controlled opening, domain experts gain initial experience with clients using the AI-based counterpart, which helps mitigate initial apprehensions and sparks curiosity to more directly explore its capabilities. This self-experimentation marks the transition to the third stage, in which domain experts increasingly come to recognize and emphasize the unique opportunities associated with the AI-based system, which gradually outweigh perceived threats. At this juncture, they begin to actively team up with the AI-based system, leveraging and emphasizing its strengths in client relations. At this stage, domain experts broadly open their networks, as the perceived opportunities of the AI-based counterpart outweigh the threats that come with it, leading to accepted vulnerabilities.

## Beyond a Dyadic Perspective on Trust in AI

As a first contribution to the literature on trust in AI, our study advances a triadic perspective that significantly extends existing frameworks by capturing previously underexplored interrelationships. While prior research has predominantly conceptualized trust in AI as a dyadic user-AI system relationship (Glikson & Woolley, 2020; Yang & Wibowo, 2022), this view is insufficient when multiple human trustors are involved—each with distinct vulnerabilities (Saffarizadeh et al., 2024). In particular, AI facets, such as autonomy and inscrutability, do not uniformly affect human trust, but introduce specific trust tensions involving both threats and opportunities, depending on the trustor’s position and exposure (Leavitt et al., 2024; Lockey et al., 2021; Vanneste & Puranam, 2024).

Prior work has illustrated measures aimed at achieving higher AI user trust by mitigating threats related to inscrutability or autonomy—such as humanizing AI-based systems via anthropomorphic design elements or improving transparency via explanations (Glikson & Woolley, 2020; Rai, 2020). These measures, however, can have unintended effects on domain experts in triadic relationships. While the measures might enhance user engagement and understanding, domain experts may view them as diminishing their own professional value, potentially heightening fears of replacement and lowering their trust in the system. Furthermore, while increasing the technical capabilities of an AI-based system is said to increase user trust (Vanneste & Puranam, 2024), it could induce difficulties for domain experts to explain how the system achieves this performance (Anthony et al., 2023). Such interrelationships remain invisible from a dyadic view, which overlooks the triadic structure involving both users and domain experts.

These insights underscore the critical need to differentiate between distinct types of trustors (Leavitt et al., 2024), taking into account the particular vulnerabilities and specific trust tensions they encounter (Lockey et al., 2021). For AI users directly affected by system design, operations, and outcomes, key vulnerabilities include financial loss, health risks, or legal consequences. These vulnerabilities create trust tensions around autonomy and inscrutability—for instance, between potential performance gains and fears of losing control or facing uncertainty (Constantinides et al., 2024; Rai, 2020; Saffarizadeh et al., 2024; Vanneste & Puranam, 2024). Domain experts in triadic relationships instead experience trust tensions that are linked to their relational expertise, which shapes how they engage with their clients and assert their professional authority. Specifically, the complementarity-replacement tension affects their positioning in the evolving triadic relationship, while the performance-opacity tension concerns the nature and quality of their interactions with clients. Autonomy thus influences the extent to which critical interactions can occur, as increased automation risks sidelining experts by excluding them from the decision-making loop (Lockey et al., 2021; Strich et al., 2021). Inscrutability limits access to the knowledge and skills needed in these interactions, as domain experts rely on professional domain knowledge rather than technical knowledge (Lockey et al., 2021). These tensions raise fundamental questions about the role and relevance of domain experts (Strich et al., 2021). While prior work has indicated that relational expertise would not be substantially at risk from the diffusion of AI-based systems (Pakarinen & Huising, 2023), the triadic perspective reveals that inscrutability and autonomy can present vital threats to this key domain expert vulnerability by potentially affecting the established nature, quality, and extent of client relationships. Thus, we need to consider the simultaneous presence of multiple trustors and their specific vulnerabilities to better understand trust in AI.

Despite the differences between trustors, our findings highlight a bidirectional relationship between users and domain experts with regard to AI trust tensions. First, domain experts play a crucial role in shaping user trust by endorsing, scrutinizing, and regulating interactions with AI-based systems. Their trust in AI influences how users perceive and engage with these technologies and their facets. In particular, domain experts can influence how trust tensions are perceived by users (Leavitt et al., 2024; Vuori et al., 2025). Second, user adoption of the AIbased system serves as a valuable input in (re)framing AI trust tensions on the part of domain experts, fostering their own confidence in the system. This reinforcement extends our knowledge of the complex nature of AI trust formation in triadic relationships.

Whereas prior work often portrays AI facets as more or less objectively given (e.g., Berente et al., 2021), our work indicates that AI facets and the associated trust tensions are not “just out there,” but are malleable and shaped through interaction. We found that the framing of threats and opportunities related to autonomy and inscrutability for the triadic relationship varied depending on whether and how the AI-based system was embedded in social interactions. While prior work has indicated differences in the perception of AI facets—for instance, due to different educational backgrounds (Kellogg et al., 2020; Leavitt et al., 2024)—our work suggests the relevance of relational constellations. Our results indicate that AI facets do not have a stable or linear effect on trust (Vanneste & Puranam, 2024). Instead, framing the opportunities and threats associated with AI facets is embedded in a context and produced in relation to others. The way that AI facets are perceived directly affects whether and how domain experts integrate AI-based counterparts into their professional networks. Our study thus reveals a dynamic duality: Perceptions of AI facets both influence and are influenced by the relational structures within which they are embedded.

In sum, adopting a triadic perspective underscores that trust in AI cannot be meaningfully understood in isolation from the specific trustors and the prevalent relational contexts. This broader lens enables a more nuanced grasp of the situated, dynamic, and negotiated nature of AI-related trust.

## Beyond a Technology-Centric Perspective on Trust-Building Processes in AI

Our second contribution is a deeper understanding of how trust building in AI develops over time that accounts more specifically for the influences of the social context (Sergeeva et al., 2017), both in the assessment of the AI-based system’s potential consequences and in creating security in interactions with the system. Earlier studies have focused on technical factors, arguing that trust is shaped by how well the system performs, how it is designed, and how users interact with it (Glikson & Woolley, 2020; Thiebes et al., 2021). Our work uncovers a more nuanced trust-building process shaped by influences going beyond the technical AI artifact.

When faced with an AI-based counterpart, as a first key process in navigating trust tensions, domain experts engage in relational interpretation. When forming trusting beliefs, they do not primarily assess the system’s design, technical performance, or accuracy. Instead, they reflect on what the system does to their relationships—especially with clients. This shifts the focus of trust from the AI artifact itself to the social consequences of its use. Traditional AI trust beliefs— such as those related to performance, process, and purpose (Lee & See, 2004)—still matter. However, in triadic relationships, domain experts consider them in light of their relational responsibilities. For instance, they judge performance not for themselves but for how it serves their clients (Hengstler et al., 2016). They assess the process with the goal of recommending and explaining the system’s reasoning (Lockey et al., 2021). And they interpret the purpose based on how it affects their client relationships, not just the system’s intended function (Thiebes et al., 2021). Relational interpretation thus offers a socially grounded view of trusting beliefs that complements more technology-centric views.

Following this relational interpretation, as a second key process in navigating trust tensions, domain experts engage in relational adaptation, whereby they open or close their related relational network for AI-based counterparts. Relational adaptation represents a novel form of trusting behavior, going beyond technology-centric behaviors, such as feature or deep structure use (Benlian, 2015; Yang & Wibowo, 2022). Domain experts play a complex role, acting as gatekeepers who shape whether and how the interactions of other actors with AI-based systems occur. Their decisions can either foster collaborative synergies or erect relational boundaries that limit system integration. In this way, relational adaptation also reflects implicit power and control in the diffusion of AIenabled work and human-AI teaming (Wang et al., 2023). This extends our understanding of trusting behaviors in the context of AI-based systems (Burton et al., 2020; Dzindolet et al., 2003).

Our longitudinal analysis, covering multiple sequences of relational interpretation and adaptation, shows that domain experts build trust in AI-based counterparts through a staged process. This process reveals variations over time in how domain experts navigate the trust tensions of opacity vs. performance and replacement vs. complementarity. Domain experts first use relational interpretation and adaptation to keep the AI-based counterpart and its perceived threats away from their established client relationships. Then, they build on specific social interactions to interpret and adapt to the AI-based counterpart in a way that does not endanger their established position in their relational network. Finally, they come to value its unique traits and adapt their position in the relational network to make even more use of them. Prior work has underscored that AI-based systems are increasingly viewed as actors in relational networks that need to be trusted (Anthony et al., 2023). Our study demonstrates how domain experts first engage in relational interpretation to construct a projected role for the AIbased counterpart within their relational network and subsequently perform relational adaptation to integrate the AIbased counterpart in a manner that aligns with this projection. This integration process, evolving from initial exclusion to ultimately teaming up with a new actor, resembles trust building in human professional actors rather than trust building in IT systems we know about from the past (Glikson & Woolley, 2020; Lewicki & Bunker, 1995).

Analyzing the changes in trust building over time reveals an important dynamic. People make use of social interactions to alleviate potential threats of AI-based systems. Direct technology exposure helps to explore potential opportunities, both of which drive trust. Importantly, sequence matters in this development. We find that trust does not begin with the AIbased system itself, but with people who are related to it. When confronted with the AI-based counterpart “alone,” its autonomy and inscrutability seem to create uncertainties and fears among domain experts (Rai, 2020; Saffarizadeh et al., 2024), leading them to exclude it from their networks. Therefore, in their early trust building, domain experts seek security in social interactions. Instead of emphasizing the algorithm’s technical characteristics, domain experts render the system more relatable by embedding it within specific social interactions—foregrounding the human actors who supervise or speak for the algorithm in client interactions. This observation indicates that domain experts transfer trust from humans onto AI-based systems (Renner et al., 2022; Saffarizadeh et al., 2024). Importantly, however, they not only build upon such actors in their evaluation but also temporarily embed them into their relational networks to represent the AIbased counterpart in important interactions. In contrast to prior work, we show that this embedding grants a more active role to human actors in the social context (Sergeeva et al., 2017) as well as domain experts interacting with them and highlight the transient nature of trust transfer as a facilitator for more direct trust formation.

Interestingly, in our study, the domain experts initially diverted attention from the AI-based system’s technical characteristics, thereby cultivating a psychologically safe space for early engagement. This environment facilitated the accumulation of initial experiences, which in turn enabled a more direct and appreciative exploration of the system’s capabilities. This trajectory aligns with recent findings suggesting that individuals benefit from initial humancentered dialogues to process AI-related anxieties before developing more constructive interactions with the technology (Vuori et al., 2025). Technology exposure thus appears to be valued only when a particular level of trust is already established. This perspective seems especially compelling in high-stakes environments where domain experts, situated within triadic relationships, bear responsibility for others in significant areas of life (Vanneste & Puranam, 2024) and likely initially need social interactions to start building trust in AI.

Importantly, we found that the triggers of the trust-building process, providing new insights on the potential role the AIbased system could play in client relations, came from developments outside the triadic relationship. The domain experts were “pulled into” the second stage by AI experts and peers who approached them. Later, having established a certain level of trust, the domain experts “pushed” more actively to learn more about the AI-based counterpart. They thus stepped out of their role to gain more direct experience, being users themselves at this point in time (Sergeeva et al., 2017), and then built on this experience inside the triad. This observation indicates that higher levels of trust in AI, just as in humans (Mayer et al., 1995), require direct exposure. The path to this, however, is influenced by interactions with the social context.

In sum, as a key implication, moving beyond a technologycentric lens reveals a critical insight: Influences from the social context not only accompany but often precede and shape trust building in AI-based systems. Therefore, trust is unlikely to be built from purely technical capabilities and can, at least initially, be increased by changes in the social contexts, irrespective of the technical AI artifact. Therefore, trust in AI should be conceptualized through a more holistic framework in which psychological and social perspectives complement technical views.

## Boundary Conditions and Generalization

Our theorizing and the implications that we derived are valid within certain boundaries. First, based on the assumption that trust in AI-based systems evolves differently depending on the type of trustor, we focused on triadic relationships in which domain experts and users clearly differed in their vulnerabilities. Our theorizing may not be valid for cases in which this condition is not fulfilled. Furthermore, we focused on domain experts embedded in a triadic relationship with an AI-based counterpart and humans for whom they were professionally responsible. Our theory may not hold when domain experts have no such relationships and solely evaluate the quality and performance of an AI-based system. Further, the AI artifact in our case was clearly designed to interact with the user. Other designs that let domain experts directly interact with the system may produce other effects and may lead to an alignment of perspectives.

Second, our theorization of the trust-building process emerged from the temporal sequence of observed events. Grounded in the assumption that trust is dynamic, our longitudinal data collection enabled us to capture shifts in contextual conditions and evolving trust perceptions among domain experts. Our theorizing may be less applicable to more static contexts. Furthermore, our theory may not extend to contexts where key developments are reversed—that is, where deploying firms first target the particular interests of domain experts before releasing AI-based systems to their clients. If firms first foster interactions between domain experts, AI experts, and the system, trust building on the part of domain experts might evolve differently.

Within these boundary conditions, our work can be generalized beyond our specific research context. The model of trust building in AI by domain experts in triadic relationships extends to other professional domains facing new AI-based counterparts (Anthony et al., 2023). Similar trust tensions related to inscrutability and autonomy can be expected to emerge in professions where domain experts manage client relationships and handle high workloads, including routine tasks, while adapting to AI-based counterparts with these facets. For instance, in the healthcare domain, doctors are increasingly confronted with AI-based systems that could take over parts of their diagnostic or treatment work (Bohr & Memarzadeh, 2020). These systems can interact autonomously with patients and, depending on the specific design, operate in a manner that is not fully obvious to medical professionals (Lockey et al., 2021). A further practical example is the legal context, where human lawyers may be confronted with robo-lawyers independently interacting with their clients (Marchant & Covey, 2018).

Finally, especially with the widespread diffusion of generative AI applications, teachers in various kinds of schools struggling with limited capacities might also consider recommending such tools to their students (Tossell et al., 2024). They may need to weigh the potential benefits of introducing a high-performing tool and being freed up for more complex or personal interactions against the threats of losing the ability to explain, at least partially, and, in the worst case, damaging their relationship with students.

Beyond our immediate empirical context, the processes of relational interpretation and adaptation identified in this study likely hold broader relevance, particularly in light of the increasing emphasis on relational expertise in professional domains (Pakarinen & Huising, 2023) and the positioning of AI-based systems as relational actors within professional networks (Anthony et al., 2023). Our theoretical account suggests that professionals whose roles depend heavily on interpersonal dynamics, such as physicians, evaluate AI counterparts not only through the lens of their own needs and those of their patients, but also with regard to the relational fabric connecting them (Lockey et al., 2021). This implies that relational interpretation and adaptation serve as key mechanisms for navigating emerging trust tensions, evolving dynamically in response to social influences. Likewise, teachers, whose professional responsibilities are deeply embedded in their relationships with students, may engage in similar relational work when confronted with AI-based counterparts entering the classroom context.

## Recommendations for Managerial Practice

This study has important implications for practice. We found that simply implementing an AI-based system in triadic relationships is unlikely to result in domain experts developing trust. Instead, we found that trust building was substantially increased through interactions between domain experts, AI experts, and peer domain experts. When a firm has a large percentage of domain experts who are skeptical of or inexperienced with AI-based systems, it should invest in AI experts who can accompany them while AI is at play, as well as in the dissemination of peer domain expert experiences. Relatedly, when firms seek to build domain experts’ trust in AI-based systems, they should seek charismatic, competent, and committed AI experts to represent and speak for the algorithm. However, a certain dependence on these AI experts can emerge and pose a problem if they leave the company. This aspect, however, underscores the importance of the social context when introducing AI-based systems. Additionally, we found that trust in AI-based systems developed most strongly when domain experts had the chance to experiment with the system themselves after they observed how their clients’ use of the system influenced their client relationships. Therefore, organizations should lower barriers to self-experimentation and encourage broader adoption and communicate the benefits of AI-based systems for professional relationships— for example, through customer testimonials or interviews. While domain experts may have reservations initially, our work indicates that, although their practices may change and involve a division of labor with AI-based counterparts, they remain relevant for their clients and their home organizations. Still, a fundamental requirement for building trust is the development of high-performing AI-based systems that aim to complement rather than replace human expertise.

## Limitations and Future Research

Our study has certain limitations, many of which, however, also open up promising avenues for future research. Obvious restrictions arise from our single case study research design. EuroBank is a traditional bank with a focus on personal advisory services based in Europe. Therefore, its approach to robo-advisory and its strategic rationales might be different from born-digital fintechs or banks in other parts of the world. In addition, as domain expert trust in AI-based systems is an important yet overlooked and complex topic, we opted for a grounded theory investigation built mainly on qualitative data sources. Future studies should complement our work with different approaches, such as survey-based measurements or field experiments (Yang et al., 2025).

Furthermore, the particular AI artifact in our case, the roboadvisor EVA, is a specific AI instantiation, for example, with regard to its representation (Glikson & Woolley, 2020). In particular, the system did not include design elements such as avatars and other anthropomorphic elements or chatbot features (Hildebrand & Bergner, 2021). Other instantiations could include generative AI-based systems such as finetuned large language models for financial advisory. Such systems could interact conversationally with clients to provide investment advice, thereby mimicking expert-client interactions more closely and potentially appearing highly autonomous and inscrutable from the domain expert’s viewpoint. Although it seems likely that, just as in our case, domain experts could ultimately recognize that their role remains vital and that such systems could support them by handling routine inquiries, freeing up the capacity for more nuanced advisory work, trust building might be affected by variations in the technical AI artifact. Furthermore, the algorithm in our case was influenced and supervised by humans. Future research could investigate how domain expert trust building evolves with different representations without human interventions or for systems characterized by other AI facets beyond autonomy and inscrutability (Berente et al., 2021). Finally, the context we investigated, involving human financial advisors as domain experts and their relationships with their clients, is, of course, specific. These relationships are typically enduring and require punctual interactions. There are other contexts, such as that between a doctor, a patient, and an AI-based system, where relationships are shorter yet involve more frequent, intense interactions. In such cases, trust building might develop differently. Future research on domain experts’ trust in AIbased counterparts should investigate further triadic relationship contexts.

Relatedly, the progression through the trust-building model might be sensitive to contextual conditions. For instance, a few exceptions in our data on domain expert trust building indicate that domain experts with preexisting experience with robo-advisors or a particular affinity to AI-based systems in general may exhibit a higher baseline trust (Thiebes et al., 2021). We also found that when both the domain experts and their clients had extraordinary expectations—for instance, with regard to performance, the domain experts more intensely blocked EVA from entering their networks. This indicates that individual preconditions matter, as do the characteristics of the related relational network. Furthermore, it is likely that further contextual preconditions (e.g., firm legacy, hiring practices) matter. In born-digital tech firms where AI literacy is comparably high (Pinski & Benlian, 2024), domain experts would probably reach higher levels of trust in a shorter time. In traditional firms, where AI literacy may be comparably lower, they are likely to need longer to reach such levels. Therefore, future work should scrutinize domain expert trust building under different contextual conditions.

## Research Context: Robo-Advisors and Their Relationship with Human Financial Advisors

Traditional financial advisory services involve the analysis and identification of client needs and objectives (e.g., risk profiling), the derivation of an investment strategy that suggests asset allocations, implementation of the strategy via suitable financial products, and monitoring and readjusting the portfolio (Cocca, 2016). Financial advisory is a sensitive topic for many people (Bruckes et al., 2019; Jung et al., 2018b). Accordingly, the aforementioned steps are regularly carried out in close personal interaction between the human financial advisor and the client. The human financial advisor guides clients through the process via multiple personal conversations where the advisor provides recommendations and helps execute the decisions made by the client (Jung et al., 2018a, 2018b).

Robo-advisors represent instances of AI-based systems (Berente et al., 2021; Hentzen et al., 2022), which are particularly characterized by the AI facets of autonomy and inscrutability, as “the customer’s investment goals/purposes, risk affinity/aversion and return/risk expectations are quantified by algorithms and automated processes on digital platforms” (Jung et al., 2018a, p. 81). Most financial service customers still prefer human financial advisors, as they feel more comfortable discussing personal and complex financial situations with another human with empathy, competence, and understanding (Hildebrand & Bergner, 2021; Jung et al., 2018a; Zhang et al., 2021). Therefore, organizations aim to implement robo-advisors in hybrid arrangements that combine the strengths of human financial advisors and robo-advisors (Beketov et al., 2018; Lee & Shin, 2018; Rühr, 2020; Zhang et al., 2021). These arrangements aim to leverage algorithmic power and cost efficiencies, while human financial advisors handle personal interactions, offer explanations, and react to the personal and emotional circumstances of the individual customer and her overall financial situation (Zhang et al., 2021). A key condition for this, however, is that both clients as users and human financial advisors need to build trust in AI-based systems. The perspective on human financial advisor trust building in this triadic relationship is currently absent in the literature (Altrock et al., 2023).

The trust tensions of opacity vs. performance and replacement vs. complementarity manifest in the robo-advisory context when human financial advisors, representative of domain experts in triadic relationships, confront robo-advisors as AI-based counterparts (Anthony et al., 2023). First, robo-advisors potentially provide high-performance solutions to human financial advisors’ customers. Robo-advisors have decreased costs (Ge et al., 2021), shorter reaction times (Brenner & Meyll, 2020), and they achieve comparable or better investment outcomes (Beketov et al., 2018) due to increased prediction accuracy and precision and less human bias and error (Zhang et al., 2021). However, prior literature portrays current systems as opaque to most humans (Jung et al., 2018b; Rühr, 2020), which may hinder domain experts from drawing on their professional knowledge and skills in interaction with their customers (Cruciani et al., 2021; Pakarinen & Huising, 2023).

Second, robo-advisors exhibit a high potential to complement domain experts’ advisory practices (Altrock et al., 2023). Robo-advisors are often described as missing the personal and social aspects of financial advisory services, which can be compensated for by domain experts (Lee & Shin, 2018). However, when using robo-advisors, human interaction, a key aspect of traditional financial advisory services, is replaced by an autonomous system that, if at all, interacts with the customer, but not necessarily with the domain expert (Jung et al., 2018a, 2018b). Experimental research indicates that once customers opt for robo-advisors, they tend not to seek the expertise of domain experts in the future (Brenner & Meyll, 2020).
