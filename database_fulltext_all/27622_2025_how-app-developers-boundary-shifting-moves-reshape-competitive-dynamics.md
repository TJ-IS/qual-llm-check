# HOW APP DEVELOPERS’ BOUNDARY-SHIFTING MOVES RESHAPE COMPETITIVE DYNAMICS<sup>1</sup>

Hengqi Tian Information Systems, Business School, University of Colorado Denve Denver, CO, U.S.A. {hengqi.tian@ucdenver.edu}

Arun Rai

Center for Digital Innovation and Computer Information Systems Department, Robinson College of Business Georgia State University, Atlanta, GA, U.S.A. {arunrai@gsu.edu}

As digital platforms evolve, app developers do more than passively participate; they actively reshape the competitive landscape through boundary-shifting moves (BSMs). By modularizing boundary resources at different levels of the technology stack, developers can provoke, delay, or neutralize competitive responses in hypercompetitive app markets. Drawing on an empirical analysis of mobile apps launched by leading Chinese internet firms, we uncover a striking asymmetry in how rival developers react to these moves. Specifically, higher-level app modules—those tailored to particular application domains—tend to delay rival responses, while lower-level technical modules—broadly applicable across domains—trigger faster competitive reactions. This effect is heightened among firms with a history of direct competition, as they respond more aggressively to changes at the lower levels of modularization. By foregrounding the strategic role of technology stack levels in shaping competitive interactions, our study advances a differential resourcing perspective and offers new insights into the dynamics of competition within digital platforms. These findings challenge the dominant host platform-centric and cooperative views on boundary resources, illuminating how app developers actively reshape platform ecosystems to gain temporary advantage.

Keywords: Boundary-shifting moves, digital platform, modularization, technology stack, competitive dynamics, competitive asymmetry, app developer strategy, platform ecosystem evolution

## Introduction

App markets—such as Apple’s App Store and Google Play—have become central arenas for commercializing digital innovations. As of January 2025, Apple’s App Store alone hosts approximately 1.907 million apps (42matters, 2025), offering solutions for nearly every facet of modern life. Yet this abundance has spurred intense intraplatform competition, where app developers closely track one another’s moves and swiftly adapt. Competitive advantages are often fleeting, as rivals quickly adapt and replicate innovations, making sustained differentiation difficult (James et al., 2023). In these hypercompetitive environments, competitive advantages often prove fleeting:

once an innovation appears, rivals can quickly replicate it, making sustained differentiation a constant challenge.

In response to these pressures, developers must continuously innovate and counter emerging threats. While regularly updating apps with new features is one strategy, developers can also extend their influence beyond a single app by strategically modularizing and sharing boundary resources. These boundary-shifting moves (BSMs) involve restructuring functional components at different levels of the technology stack, allowing them to provoke, delay, or neutralize competitive responses. For example, Google Maps provides higher-level application programming interfaces (APIs) that allow third-party apps, such as real estate and delivery services, to seamlessly integrate navigation features. In contrast, WeChat has transformed itself into an application platform atop iOS and Android, offering lower-level technical modules that enable thirdparty developers to build millions of mini-programs within the WeChat ecosystem. These contrasting examples underscore a critical challenge in app markets: How can developers strategically deploy BSMs at different technology stack levels to reduce or delay rival reactions, thereby gaining a temporary competitive edge?

Despite the growing importance of app developers as active competitors, digital platform research has largely focused on host platforms. Scholars have examined how platforms foster app diversity through architectural design, such as modularity (Tiwana, 2018; Tiwana et al., 2010) and openness (Karhu et al., 2018; Parker & Van Alstyne, 2018), as well as governance mechanisms that regulate developer behavior (Foerderer et al., 2018; Huang et al., 2022; Wareham et al., 2014). While intraplatform competition is often acknowledged as an outcome of increased app diversity, few studies have considered how app developers actively shape this competition (Tiwana, 2015, 2018). Although previous research acknowledges the existence of within-platform rivalry (Cennamo & Santalo, 2013; Foerderer et al., 2018), developers are often treated as passive complementors rather than strategic actors who deploy boundary resources to influence competition (Boudreau, 2012; Claussen et al., 2013). Consequently, the ways in which app developers themselves modularize boundary resources—particularly at different levels of the technology stack—remain underexplored.

In this study, we ask:

1. How do BSMs that modularize boundary resources at different levels of an app’s technology stack affect competitive responses by rival app developers?

2. How does prior competitive contact in app markets shape rival developers’ responses to BSMs?

We analyze mobile apps released by leading Chinese internet firms, operationalizing BSMs through software development kit (SDK) and basic library release notes. Our findings reveal a striking asymmetry in competitive responses: while BSMs consistently spark rival reactions, higher-level modules—those designed for specific application domains—tend to delay these responses, whereas lower-level modules—offering generic technical functionalities—trigger faster competitive reactions. This effect is amplified among firms with a history of direct competition, as they respond more aggressively to lowerlevel modularization.

By foregrounding app developers as strategic actors in intraplatform competition, our work extends the digital platform literature beyond the host-centric perspective. We propose a differential resourcing logic, revealing how developers strategically reshape competition by structuring boundary resources at different stack levels. These findings challenge the prevailing cooperative view of boundary resources and highlight how temporary advantages emerge and dissipate in hypercompetitive app markets.

## Theoretical Foundation

## Boundary Resources

Boundary resources in the digital platform context are “software tools and regulations that serve as the interface for the arm’s-length relationship between the platform owner and the application developer” (Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013, p. 174). Traditionally, host platforms have leveraged boundary resources to collaborate with app developers, cultivating open and distributed innovations that continually redefine the platform’s product boundaries (Yoo et al., 2010). Within this cooperative framing, prior research has largely examined how host-provided boundary resources influence app developers’ innovations (Dal Bianco et al., 2014; Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013; Soh & Grover, 2022; Xue et al., 2019). Although existing studies acknowledge that app developers can also act as providers of boundary resources for self-resourcing (Eaton et al., 2015; Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013), few scholars have systematically explored how app developers deploy boundary resources to compete dynamically with rivals (see Table 1). This aspect is particularly relevant, as the resulting open architecture can facilitate forking and intensify competition (Eaton et al., 2015; Karhu et al., 2018). While prior work, such as Karhu et al. (2018), has examined boundary resources as a defense against platform forking, these studies have not differentiated boundary resources across technology stack levels or assessed how these levels affect competitive interaction patterns.

Among the various types of boundary resources, including APIs, SDKs, rules, regulations, documentation, and developer forums (Dal Bianco et al., 2014; Eaton et al., 2015), our study focuses on modular components that provide access to resources at different technology stack levels (Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013; Xue et al., 2019). These modules are vulnerable to exploitation by rivals through cloning or forking, which can trigger competitive interactions (Karhu et al., 2018). To better capture these interactions, we draw on the competitive dynamics literature to model how boundary resource modularization shapes competition in hypercompetitive app markets.

## Conceptualizing Boundary-Shifting Moves

We define BSMs as the modularization of boundary resources by app developers, where encapsulated functions and accompanying resources are provided to third parties to spontaneously shift an app’s product boundaries and secure temporary competitive advantages (Eaton et al., 2015; Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013; Karhu et al., 2018). From a layered modular architecture perspective (Yoo et al., 2010), the architecture of an app comprises a technology stack, with functions and resources encapsulated in modules, ranging from fine-grained, lower-level technical functions (e.g., HTTP requests) to higher-level app functionalities that integrate these technical elements. By modularizing these functions and resources and making them externally accessible via APIs or SDKs, BSMs open an app’s architecture and transform an app into an application platform that supports external innovations, leading to continuous reshaping of the app’s product boundaries. Reflecting different choices regarding architecture openness and third-party resource provision, we distinguish between app functionality modularization (AFM) and technical functionality modularization (TFM) (see Table 2 for a comparison).

App functionality modularization: Encapsulating higherlevel app functions and resources within a specific application domain allows third-party developers to access and use these modules (Piccoli et al., 2022). These app modules hide the complexity of detailed operations and provide access to app functions and accompanying resources that are preconfigured and contextualized to a specific application domain (e.g., route navigation and payment). Third-party developers can easily orchestrate and integrate app modules into their own apps without needing intricate technical details or developing similar functionalities (Soh & Grover, 2022). Using Uber APIs, thirdparty developers can integrate ride-hailing app functions and resources into their stand-alone apps to achieve seamless ride requests. For example, by integrating the Uber API, consumers can directly schedule rides to event locations after booking tickets in the StubHub app.

Technical functionality modularization: Encapsulating lower-level technical functions and accompanying resources provides generic technical modules that serve as building blocks for third-party application development (Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013). Because the layered and recursive composition of digital technologies allows developers to build upon one another (Baiyere et al., 2023; Piccoli et al., 2022), app developers can initiate TFM to transform an app into a new application platform within the host platform. By restructuring an app’s architecture and providing generic technical modules, TFM grants third-party developers greater control over system operations and facilitates the (re)combination of technical modules to build applications (Xue et al., 2019). In this case, third-party developers need a deeper knowledge of how to bind and contextualize fine-grained technical modules to address user needs in a specific application domain (Baiyere et al., 2023). For the WeChat mini-program example, third-party developers must interpret technical details and determine how to combine APIs (e.g., routing and HTTP network requests) and user interface (UI) components to develop mini-programs for domains like games and education.

## Integrating Boundary-Shifting Moves With the Competitive Dynamics Lens

The study of competitive dynamics is the “study of interfirm rivalry based on specific competitive actions and reactions, their strategic and organizational contexts, and their drivers and consequences” (Chen & Miller, 2012, p. 137). Drawing from Schumpeter’s theory of creative destruction (Schumpeter, 1934) and the Austrian school of economics, which emphasize the dynamic market process, competitive dynamics studies contend that a specific and detectable competitive action, such as new product introductions, will provoke rivals counteractions to defend or improve their competitive position if the initiating action generates abnormal profits (Chen & Miller, 2012; Chen et al., 1992; Smith et al., 1991). To maximize the monopolistic period of competitive advantages, firms seek to reduce and delay competitive responses from rivals (Porter, 1980; Smith et al., 2005). Therefore, we consider (1) the number of rival responses, defined as the number of competitive responses introduced by rival app developers to a BSM, and (2) rival response time, defined as the time it takes for rival app developers to enact competitive responses to a BSM (Chen et al., 1992; Smith et al., 1991).

In modeling competitive interactions, we follow the process model to match BSMs with rival responses. Based on the creative destruction process (Schumpeter, 1934), the process model views the time between a focal firm’s two successive moves as the monopolistic window for the initiating move to generate advantages and for rivals to introduce competitive responses to erode such advantages (Ferrier, 2001; Nadkarni et al., 2019; Yu & Cannella, 2007). Any move by a rival that follows an initiating move is considered a competitive response. Yet, in line with the literature, we do not claim a strict one-to-one correspondence of a rival response to an initiating BSM. The merit here is to model the dynamic process in a highly competitive environment (Yu & Cannella, 2007), such as app markets, where app developers are in a constant race to proactively act and respond to one another’s moves (James et al., 2023).

Nonetheless, we further constrain rivals as apps that have introduced the same types of modules and thus have similar technological architecture (Cennamo, 2021), while rival responses are defined as the modularization of boundary resources by rivals in the same module type as the initiating BSM to defend or improve their competitive positions. This constraining approach helps ensure competitive interdependence in competing for third-party usage of similar app-based modules or technical modules. Meanwhile, rival apps, by overlapping on encapsulated functionalities, might have already competed for consumers in the app markets (Wang et al., 2018). As such, they are more likely to experience increased competitive pressures and share similar resources. We therefore include prior competitive contact (PCC) as a contingency factor, influencing a rival’s motivation and capability to respond to an initiating move (Chen, 1996) (see Figure 1). We define PCC as the degree of app market overlaps between a focal app and a rival app before a BSM introduction.

## Hypothesis Development

By modularizing app functions and accompanying resources in an app’s technology stack and offering external interfaces for access and integration, BSMs aim to stimulate and support third-party innovations, thereby extending the focal app’s reach to consumers (Yoo et al., 2010) and enriching the scope and scale of functionality available through the focal app (Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013). In competing for thirdparty developers, rivals tend to proactively respond to prevent long-term disadvantages. This is because once third-party developers adopt the focal app’s modules, their migration costs to use similar modules provided by rivals grow over time. Given intensified competition and high response motivations, the open architecture created by BSMs facilitates rivals’ strategic exploitation and forking (Karhu et al., 2018), leading to fewer barriers and an increased number of rival responses (Chen et al., 1996).

Nevertheless, depending on the technology-stack level for modularization, barriers to responding differ, resulting in differences in rival response time. AFM, which occurs at the higher level of an app’s technology stack, provides modules in which functions and resources are tailored to the specific requirements of a domain, such as payment (Piccoli et al., 2022). These higher-level modules cannot be directly repurposed by rivals; instead, they require substantial reconfiguration through the integration of lower-level technical components and complementary resources. Such requirements are barriers for rivals appropriating these modules and thus slow their responses. In contrast, TFM implemented at the lower levels provides generic technical building blocks to support third-party app development (Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013; Xue et al., 2019). These lower-level modules are technically oriented and fine-grained, so they require less effort to repurpose. Thus, it is easier for rivals to adapt them through a sequence of modular operators such as cloning and forking (Karhu et al., 2018), allowing for faster responses. Therefore, we propose the following hypotheses:

H1a: Boundary-shifting moves—both AFM at the higher level of the app technology stack and TFM at the lower level— increase the number of rival responses.

H1b: AFM (higher level of the app technology stack) increases rival response time, whereas TFM (lower level of the app technology stack) decreases rival response time.

A high prior competitive contact (PCC) indicates that focal and rival developers provide similar app functionalities and compete in many common app markets. For example, before WeChat introduced technical modules and transformed itself into a mini-program platform, WeChat and AliPay were already competing intensively in payment and social networking. In such conditions, since both apps serve similar consumer groups (Cennamo, 2021), rivals are particularly sensitive to any moves that could draw those consumers. Additionally, because BSMs can foster external innovations and create time-amplifying cross-network effects that lock in both third-party developers and consumers (Song et al., 2018), rivals experiencing a high PCC in app markets are likely to experience greater competitive threats and possess stronger response motivations.

Moreover, rivals overlapping significantly in app functionalities often share a similar development stage and analogous app functions and resources. This similarity reduces the adjustment costs needed to respond to AFM. Meanwhile, rival apps that offer similar app functionalities likely have comparable technological architecture and accumulate parallel design and development knowledge. With this related knowledge, these rivals typically have greater absorptive capacity (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990), enabling them to better exploit technical functions at lower stack levels and introduce similar technical modules in response to TFM. Consequently, with heightened motivation and fewer obstacles to responding, a higher level of PCC intensifies competition by prompting rival app developers to enact more responses and react more swiftly to BSMs. Thus, we propose:

H2: Rivals with a higher level of prior competitive contact in the app markets introduce (a) more and (b) faster competitive responses to BSMs, whether these moves occur at higher (AFM) or lower (TFM) levels of an app’s technology stack.

## Methodology

## Research Context and Sample Selection

We chose mobile apps released by leading Chinese internet firms as our research context mainly because they are most representative of modularizing boundary resources across technology stack levels. Beginning in 2011, these internet firms underwent mobile transformations and introduced APIs and SDKs that allowed third-party developers to access and integrate an app’s core features, such as in-app sharing. With the development of mobile technologies and intensifying competition in mobile apps, these firms gradually opened lower stack levels and transformed their major mobile apps into mini-program platforms. Such acknowledged competitiveness helped ensure competitive interdependency in our sample. Because the COVID-19 pandemic disrupted innovation and competition patterns in China after January 2020, we established our sample time bracket from January 2011 to December 2019. The unit of analysis is a BSM and the matched set of rival responses.

We further restricted our sample to 20 mobile apps<sup>2</sup> introduced by top Chinese internet firms offering APIs, SDKs, or basic libraries to access an app’s functions and resources. This allowed us to leverage the SDK and basic library release notes to operationalize BSMs. In our case, an SDK mainly includes APIs to access app functions and resources, documentation, and other tools to help developers integrate app modules, whereas a basic library provides foundational, common functions and resources to address developers’ basic programming needs. Because most firms have released dual iOS and Android app versions, and there is no uniform distribution channel for Android apps in China, we focused on iOS apps and collected app data from the App Store and two leading Chinese mobile analytics firms.<sup>3</sup> These data include app title, app ID, app category, developer name and ID, release date, status, app description, app release notes, app reviews, and app ranks. We also collected firm-level data, including top Chinese internet firm rankings (2013-2019) published by China’s Ministry of Industry and Information Technology, and investment data regarding our sample apps’ parent firms from ITJUZI.com, a comprehensive database on Chinese internet firms’ investment and funding.

## Variables and Measurement

## Operationalizing BSMs

To make third-party developers informed and allow necessary adjustments, SDK and basic library release notes serve as authoritative documentation that provides clear and precise details about changes made in a new SDK or basic library version. Therefore, we used SDK and basic library release notes, combined with a dictionary classifier and keyword search method, to operationalize the two types of BSMs at different stack levels. To distinguish new modules, we created a dictionary classifier. Because of the technical nature, module release notes are straightforward in pinpointing changes for a new SDK or basic library version, often featuring keywords and indicators like “[adding]” and “[fixes]” at the beginning of each line to highlight different types of module changes. Therefore, in developing the dictionary, we relied on an iterative process of empirical summaries from randomly selected module release notes, coupled with trial-and-error classifier runs. The process led to a dictionary of keywords about the addition, improvement, removal, and bug fixes of modules, as well as other technical adjustments.

After applying the dictionary classifier and validating results (98% accuracy, evaluated using 20% of randomly selected release-note items), we identified release notes related to new modules, which we further classified into app modules versus technical modules by applying a keyword search approach. Because an SDK mainly includes APIs to access focal functions and resources, we first compiled app module keywords and labeled new modules in the SDK release notes for AFM. Using these module keywords (e.g., food delivery, ride-hailing, payment, navigation), we manually searched the basic library release notes, further identifying those with matched keywords as AFM. We then cross-checked technical module keywords (e.g., framework, view components, basic APIs) and labeled new modules in the remaining basic library release notes for TFM. Accordingly, we measured AFM by the count of new app modules and TFM by the count of new technical modules introduced by focal app developers at time t.

## Matching BSMs With Rival Responses

Based on the process model of competitive interactions (Ferrier, 2001; Nadkarni et al., 2019; Yu & Cannella, 2007) and our constrained approach to model rivalry by module types, we implemented a matching procedure to identify competitive responses from rival apps that introduced the same type of modules as the initiating BSMs (t1) before the focal app’s subsequent BSMs (t2). Module types were determined based on the keywords of app modules and technical modules. Specifically, we first identified rival apps as those introducing the same type of modules as the focal app, not released by the same developers, and lacking any prior equity or ownership investments between the two apps’ parent firms before the move time at t1. Next, we labeled a rival move as a rival response if the module was the same type as the initiating move and occurred within the time window of the focal app’s two consecutive moves (t1-t2). After completing the matching process for each BSM, we measured the rival response number as the total count of rival moves that introduced the same type of modules as an initiating BSM before t2, and rival response time as the number of days elapsed between the date of an initiating BSM and the date of the earliest rival move that introduced the same type of modules. We also used fixed time windows (30, 60, 90, and 180 days) to conduct sensitivity checks; the results remained consistent.

## Measuring Prior Competitive Contact (PCC)

We measured PCC by calculating cosine similarity between feature vectors representing app functionalities, as detailed in the app release notes of both the focal app and its rival apps prior to the introduction of a BSM. The greater similarity in app functionalities indicates increased competition. To extract these feature vectors, we conducted data cleaning to remove duplicates, noisy text (e.g., greetings, adding new content, requesting feedback or app upgrades, bug fixes), and notes containing fewer than three Chinese characters. We then segmented the text using Jieba (a widely used Chinese text segmentation module), removed stop words, and performed part-of-speech tagging. Retaining only nouns and verbs (Wang et al., 2018), we then applied term frequency-inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) in combination with singular value decomposition (SVD) to represent these feature vectors.

## Control Variables

We controlled for characteristics of focal apps, responding rival apps, and the competitive environment that might influence competitive interaction patterns (Smith et al., 2005).

Focal app controls: If there are many modules available for a focal app, rival developers have more resources to exploit, making it easier to respond. Therefore, we controlled for the number of modules the focal app developers had introduced before initiating a BSM.

Responding rival app controls: (1) We measured rival move similarity as the cosine similarity between a focal move and matched rival moves, represented by feature vectors of the associated module release notes. (2) We included two measures for rival app developers’ competency. At the app-developer level, we considered the number of sibling apps a responding rival firm had before a BSM’s introduction time. At the firm level, because top Chinese internet firm rankings consider size, profitability, innovation capability, and growth potential, we included the inverse firm rank of a responding rival app developer’s parent firm on the top chart in the previous year. (3) Because performance declines can trigger searches to improve performance, we controlled for app rank growth in the Top Overall and Top Apps charts, as well as rankings within an app category 1 month prior to the date when a rival app responded. (4) We included days since the last update of an SDK or basic library version to control for influences of rivals’ release schedules.

Competitive environment controls: Given that competition intensity can affect competitive interaction patterns, we controlled for the number of rivals that had introduced the same type of modules as the initiating BSM. We also controlled for app market dynamism, which shapes developers’ motivation to respond, by measuring top app churn—the count of unique apps that appeared on the top 1,500 overall rankings in the current and prior months (Bresnahan et al., 2015). We report descriptive statistics of the variables used in the main estimation in Appendix A.

## Empirical Model Specification

Because we had two dependent variables that capture the number and timing of rival responses to a BSM, we specified the models for each outcome separately. To investigate the effects of BSMs on the number of rival responses, we first logtransformed this number and used ordinary least squares (OLS) regression with robust standard errors to correct for heteroscedasticity. The baseline specification is as follows:

$$

$$

where controls represent the set of variables for the focal app, responding rival apps, and the competitive environment in a BSM (AFM or TFM)-rival response pair, and ε is the unobserved error term, assumed to be orthogonal to other independent variables.

Our data on rival response time, measured by the time until the earliest rival response, is considered duration data and may involve right-censoring if no responses are observed within a finite time window. In such cases, survival analysis is the most appropriate method. Instead of directly modeling survival time, survival analysis models the conditional hazard rates by incorporating information on the duration before an event (failure), thereby accounting for right-censoring issues and generating more accurate estimates (Allison, 2014). Applied to our study, an event is the occurrence of a rival response to a BSM, and the survival time is akin to the time until a rival response event occurs. In particular, we used the accelerated failure time (AFT) model because it provides direct interpretations of changes in survival time (Harhoff & Wagner, 2009) rather than relative risks for event failure. This allowed us to intuitively assess the impacts of BSMs on rival response time. We specified the baseline AFT model and the associated hazard model as follows:

where T represents rival response time, X denotes our BSM explanatory variables (AFM and TFM) and the set of control variables. We further specified u to follow a log-logistic (LL) distribution and used LL regression because the hazard functions from this model best fit our data, which exhibited nonmonotonic hazard rates that rise initially and then decline over time.

To address endogeneity issues, we first applied a commonly employed two-step Heckman procedure (Heckman, 1979) and extended the baseline models to include self-selection corrections to account for bias due to inherent differences among app developers who introduce BSMs versus those who do not. In the first stage, we considered factors from the platform, rival apps, consumers, and the focal app that might influence the focal app developer’s decision to introduce BSMs. We modeled these factors using a probit model (see Appendix B), from which we calculated the inverse Mills ratio (IMR), a nonselection hazard. The error term of the probit model is assumed to be normally distributed with a zero mean and correlated with the error terms in our main estimation models. This correlation suggests that rival responses are connected to underlying aspects of BSMs, such as complexity. To address potential endogeneity concerns, we included the IMR as a control variable in the second-stage models.

Second, we adopted an instrumental variable (IV) strategy that uses peer-effect IVs derived from sibling apps’ BSMs.

Specifically, we calculated the number of app modules and technical modules introduced by selected sibling apps one month earlier as IVs for AFM and TFM. These sibling apps were chosen based on a restrictive set of criteria: they were released by the same firm as the focal app, shared highly similar app features with the focal app but minimal feature similarity with rival apps, and had released the same types of modules as the focal app but not the rival apps. This approach isolated the direct spillover effect of sibling apps’ BSMs on the focal app alone—rather than on rival apps—thereby satisfying the exclusion restriction. Because sibling and focal apps share similar architectures and target the same consumer and developer groups, the modularization of boundary resources by sibling apps can influence the focal app’s motivation and capability to adopt similar or distinct strategies. By contrast, since sibling apps and rival apps do not operate in the same product markets or offer the same types of modules, they are not direct competitors under our moveresponse matching approach. Consequently, the impact of sibling apps’ BSMs on rival responses should be nonsignificant. Moreover, the validity of our peer-effect IVs was confirmed by weak-instrument tests: the first-stage Fstatistics, 11.23 and 57.96 for the main and moderation models, respectively, exceeded the critical threshold of 10 for weak instrument tests.

## Results

We further added interactions between BSMs (AFM and TFM) and prior competitive contact (PCC) in the models to uncover how rivals with different levels of PCC responded differently to BSMs. We mean-centered and standardized variables before calculating the interaction terms. In H2, we hypothesized that rivals with a high level of PCC in the app markets with the focal app introduced more competitive responses (H2a) in a shorter time (H2b). As shown in Table 3, in rival response number models, the coefficient for AFM × PCC is positive and significant (0.153 at p < 0.01 in Model 3) yet not significant in the 2SLS model (Model 4), whereas the coefficients for TFM × PCC are positive and significant (1.647 at p < 0.01 and 1.782 at $p < 0 . 0 1$ in Models 3 and 4, respectively). Such positive moderating effects indicate that the positive relationships between TFM and the rival response number were strengthened by a high level of PCC, meaning that rivals with extensive prior competitive contact in the app markets enacted more responses to modularization occurring at lower technology stack levels. Therefore, H2a is partially supported.

Turning to rival response time, the coefficients for AFM × PCC are negative and significant (-0.346 at p < 0.01 in Model 7 and -0.368 at p < 0.01 in Model 8), indicating that AFM’s delaying effect on rival response time is reduced by a high PCC. Similarly, the negative and significant coefficients for TFM × PCC (-1.808 at p < 0.01 in Model 7 and -1.819 at p < 0.01 in Model 8) suggest that TFM’s speeding-up effect on rival response time was intensified by a high PCC. Taken together, these results support H2b by showing that rivals with a high level of PCC in the app markets responded faster both to AFM and TFM, with especially quick responses to TFM at lower technology stack levels. This is indicated by the magnitude of PCC’s moderation effect on TFM and rival response time, which was almost four times larger than that on AFM.

## Implications

Departing from the predominant host platform-centric perspective in digital platform research, our study draws attention to intraplatform competitive interactions among app developers who strategically modularize boundary resources across their app technology stacks. The first major contribution of this work is to surface a differential resourcing logic of how boundary resources at higher or lower levels of the technology stack foster external innovations. The literature has broadly studied them as mechanisms for sharing resources and controlling third-party innovations (Eaton et al., 2015; Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013; Karhu et al., 2018). By focusing specifically on boundary resource modularization and distinguishing among different stack levels, our study elucidates that boundary resources at each level represent distinct module types, which require different knowledge from third-party developers. Specifically, higher-level app modules contain functions and resources situated within a particular domain (Piccoli et al., 2022). Third-party developers can integrate these modules directly, primarily needing to understand “what” to integrate (know-what). In contrast, lower-level technical modules are less contextspecific, requiring third-party developers to understand “how” to effectively (re)combine and contextualize technical blocks for emerging application domains (know-how) (Baiyere et al., 2023). This conceptual clarity of modularization across stack levels advances our understanding of how varying boundary resource types stimulate external innovations.

While some research has acknowledged that boundary resources can change dynamically (Eaton et al., 2015; Karhu et al., 2018), our second major contribution is to show that BSMs intensify competition among app developers and create asymmetric competitive interaction patterns. We find that modularizing boundary resources prompts more rival responses overall but affects their timing differently, depending on which stack level is exposed. Higher-level modules are harder to repurpose, providing a longer-lasting advantage. Lower-level technical modules, however, can be quickly replicated, eroding competitive advantages more rapidly. This asymmetry is especially pronounced among app developers with extensive competitive histories, as lower-level modules offer greater flexibility (Dal Bianco et al., 2014), raising the stakes for rapid reactions. By revealing these stack-level differences in rival response frequency and speed, we move beyond a purely cooperative view of boundary resources and demonstrate how they can be strategically exploited for (albeit temporary) advantages.

Our third major contribution extends the digital platform literature by directing attention to within-platform competition, an area often overlooked by previous research (Tiwana, 2015, 2018). Although Cennamo and Santalo (2013) and Foerderer et al. (2018) have acknowledged competitive dynamics among complementors, our study shows how app developers within a single host platform strategically modularize boundary resources to either delay or accelerate rival responses. Unlike the prevailing focus on how platform architectural design and governance mechanisms foster a greater variety or quantity of app innovations (Song et al., 2018; Tiwana et al., 2010), we demonstrate that app developers do more than launch new features for end users (James et al., 2023)—they also strategically modularize boundary resources at different technology stack levels to reshape competitive dynamics within a host platform.

Although our study provides new insights into intraplatform competition among app developers using boundary resource modularization, we acknowledge several limitations. First, we focused on 20 mobile apps from leading Chinese internet firms, which offered a relatively representative and manageable sample. However, relying on a single context may limit our findings’ generalizability; future studies could replicate our approach in other markets or ecosystems and assess contextual variations. Second, we examined iOS apps because Apple’s App Store provides a uniform and authoritative channel. However, distinct platform governance mechanisms—for example, more flexible policies on Android—could alter how developers initiate boundary-shifting moves (BSMs). Investigations across multiple platform ecosystems would shed additional light on when and why developers pursue BSMs. Third, while our IV design isolates sibling apps’ BSM effects on the focal app, potential spillovers from sibling apps could still impact rival apps—even those lacking overlapping functionalities or modules. Future research might adopt alternative designs (e.g., natural experiments or policy shifts, event studies with matched samples, panel vector autoregression, or agentbased simulations) to more rigorously evaluate BSMs’ causal impacts and illuminate coevolutionary interactions among multiple developers. Such approaches can offer deeper insights into how boundary resource strategies both influence and are shaped by competition.

Beyond addressing these methodological issues, our findings open exciting questions about how and why boundary resource strategies develop within a platform. For instance, by strategically releasing higher- or lower-level modules, developers actively shape rival behaviors and competitive trajectories, suggesting deeper exploration of their timing and sequencing. Scholars could investigate when and how to modularize app-level versus technicallevel resources or examine the synergy between focal and third-party applications. Additionally, researchers could explore the host platform’s perspective—such as how governance rules or API policies affect third-party developers’ BSM strategies—to yield fresh insights into platform-owner dynamics and ecosystem orchestration.

In sum, our research demonstrates how app developers, by modularizing boundary resources at different technology stack levels, shape competitive responses and gain temporary advantages within a host platform. This perspective enriches the digital platform literature, which has historically centered on host platform governance rather than developer-driven strategic moves. By revealing this differential resourcing logic, exposing asymmetric competitive interaction patterns, and highlighting within-platform rivalry, we offer a fresh lens on how digital innovation unfolds through multilevel, interdependent relationships among platforms, app developers, and third-party innovators. We hope our findings spark new inquiries into the strategies, timing, and broader ecosystem dynamics of app development, thereby guiding scholars and practitioners in navigating the evolving boundaries of digital platforms.

## Appendix C

## Robustness Check Results

We conducted several robustness checks: (1) employing alternative estimation methods, (2) using a subsample that excludes observations without matched rivals, (3) adopting an alternative operationalization of rivals, and (4) removing rival responses occurring immediately after an initiating move. Overall, the robustness check results in Table C1 are consistent with our main findings.

(1) We used alternative estimation methods to ensure our results were not tied to a specific empirical model. For the rival response number, we used the negative binomial model, a count model, to check the results. For the rival response time, we applied the Cox proportional hazard model for sensitivity checks. As a semiparametric model, the Cox model does not require specifying the distributions of baseline hazard functions. However, because it estimates the hazard ratio of rival responses and does not offer direct inferences about time, we leveraged the coefficients from the Cox model to infer the direction of relationships between BSMs and rival response time. It is worth noting that the sign of the coefficients in the Cox model is reversed compared to the accelerated failure time (AFT) model.

(2) In our main move-response matching approach, we defined rivals as apps that have introduced the same type of modules as the focal app. Since there were five observations without matched rivals, we narrowed our sample by removing these observations.

(3) To reduce potential noise in claiming a rival move as a direct response, we further adopted more constrained ways to define rivals. On one hand, we operationalized rivals as those that have introduced the same type of modules and have high prior competitive contact in the app markets. On the other hand, recognizing that rivals may respond to a general trend observed across multiple competitors rather than directly to a move initiated by the focal app, we selected only two major rival apps for each module type to account for the influences of these conditions. We chose the major rival apps based on the number of modules introduced by each app.

(4) Considering that there might be parallel development contributing to uncertainty when identifying rival responses, we removed rival moves that were temporally close (within 7 days) to the initiating move because they may lack sufficient lead time for a reaction.
