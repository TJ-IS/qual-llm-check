# SEIZING GROWTH OPPORTUNITIES: A RISKY BUSINESS? EFFECTS OF CLOUD SOURCING ON MERGERS AND ACQUISITIONS<sup>1</sup>

Moksh Matta Department of Management Information Systems, Beedie School of Business Simon Fraser University, Vancouver, BC, CANADA {moksh.matta@sfu.ca}

Hyeokkoo Eric Kwon Division of Information Technology and Operations Management, Nanyang Business School Nanyang Technological University, SINGAPORE {eric.kwon@ntu.edu.sg}

Kiron Ravindran Department of Information Systems and Technology, IE Business School IE University, Madrid, SPAIN {kiron.ravindran@ie.edu}

Gautam Ray Department of Information and Decision Sciences, Carlson School of Management University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, MN, U.S.A. {gautamr@umn.edu}

Recent research has shown that enterprise information technology (IT) can drive strategic growth through mergers and acquisitions (M&As). An implicit assumption underlying this research is that firms own their IT infrastructure. Challenging this assumption, however, the emerging trend of cloud sourcing suggests that IT may be owned by third-party vendors. Since third-party ownership of IT can introduce significant transaction costs and operational inefficiencies, cloud sourcing, unlike in-house enterprise IT, may be considered unlikely to drive M&A growth. However, the unique combination of IT infrastructural and service flexibilities that cloud sourcing provides could help in reducing the risk of integration failure posed by M&As, thereby driving M&A growth. Grounded in the transaction cost economics and resource-based views of the firm, respectively, these arguments illustrate the conflicting theoretical viewpoints offered by prior literature. This study seeks to improve our theoretical understanding of the relationship between cloud sourcing and M&A growth by addressing the theoretical conflict. Analyzing a dataset of cloud sourcing deals and M&As comprising 4,075 observations from 673 firms, our research finds that highly standardized cloud services, i.e., SaaS (software as a service), public, and globalized clouds, have a positive impact on M&As, particularly in information industries. Overall, these results indicate that it is only under conditions of enhanced flexibility afforded by a standardized platform and informationally rich operating environments that cloud sourcing positively affects M&A growth. Support for the theoretical propositions is further established through interviews with industry experts and mechanism tests, which reveal that cloud sourcing has a positive impact, specifically on M&As requiring intensive integration, and is associated with reduced disclosure of M&A risks in annual reports. Finally, consistent with our view that cloud sourcing smooths post-M&A integration, this research also finds that firms with cloud sourcing have relatively stronger post-M&A performance.

Keywords: Cloud sourcing, mergers and acquisitions, integration risk, flexibility, standardization

## Introduction

There exists a long-standing tradition in information systems (IS) research exploring the strategic implications of information technologies (IT), such as improved product and service quality, enterprise agility, customer and supply chain relationships, and innovation (e.g., Dong et al., 2021; Lu & Ramamurthy, 2011; Rai et al., 2015). Another strategic implication that has piqued researcher interest is the impact of IT on firm boundaries. A substantial body of literature has examined the influence of IT on market diversification, vertical integration (e.g., Dewan & Ren, 2011; Gurbaxani & Whang, 1991; Ray et al., 2013), and, more recently, on mergers and acquisitions (M&As) (e.g., Benitez et al., 2018; Cao et al., 2022a). An implicit assumption in the burgeoning literature on IT and M&As, however, is that acquirers own their IT resources. IT resources refer to the combination of IT assets (infrastructure including hardware, software, networks, and personnel) and IT capabilities (competencies and practices) (Aral & Weill, 2007). The growing trend of cloud sourcing, where IT resources may be owned by third parties, however, challenges this assumption and motivates us to address calls for a deeper examination of the link between IT and M&As (Hedman & Sarker, 2015).

The fundamental challenge in pursuing M&As is the information asymmetry that exists between acquirers and their potential targets. This information asymmetry raises the risk not only of value-destroying adverse selection but also unsuccessful integration (Reuer, 2005; Shrivastava, 1986). The risk of unsuccessful integration can discourage firms from pursuing M&As (Larsson & Finkelstein, 1999; Sarrazin & West, 2011). Notwithstanding this risk, however, recent IS research has suggested that ownership of IT resources drives M&As. Cao et al. (2022a), for example, found that in-house enterprise systems (ES) help reduce firms’ internal coordination costs, encouraging certain types of M&As for achieving economies of scale. Benitez et al. (2018) found that having an in-house flexible IT infrastructure provides the adaptability needed for tackling integration challenges, thereby encouraging M&As.

However, prior literature on strategic flexibility has indicated that in addition to the adaptability offered by a flexible IT infrastructure, where IT infrastructure refers to the technical (hardware, software, and networks) and human assets that provide the foundation on which specific IT applications are run (Benitez et al., 2018; Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011), firms also need the freedom to coordinate and control the infrastructure for strategic activities (Sanchez, 1995). This freedom, however, can be diminished with a loss of resource ownership to third-party vendors (Hart & Moore, 1990; Weigelt, 2009). With constraints imposed on the coordination and control of IT resources through cloud sourcing, the risk of slow and ineffective M&A integration thus becomes salient. However, unlike flexible inhouse IT infrastructure (Benitez et al., 2018), cloud sourcing can raise concerns about a firm’s ability to tackle integration challenges. Yet anecdotal evidence suggests that cloud sourcing enables firms to potentially pursue more M&As. For example, we interviewed a head of enterprise architecture with expertise in cloud sourcing at a top global IT services company. He noted:

It would be more comfortable for you [the acquirer] to integrate with an acquired entity if you are on cloud … if you look at the whole cycle of your M&A … within 18 months you are supposed to bring the acquired entity onto your platform … in that journey you’ve got a lot of things to do … procure new software … hardware, lineup the whole migration pathway, lift and shift the applications and data and integrate it … with you being on the cloud … its much easier. ... In a scenario where you are on premises … there is a lot of pain in this migration … so a lot of M&As are put aside.

Overall, whether and how cloud sourcing affects M&As remains an open question. To address the question, drawing upon the capability-based view of the firm (Barney, 1999), this study argues that cloud sourcing, in contrast to in-house IT and traditional IT outsourcing (ITO), uniquely combines IT infrastructural flexibility with IT service flexibility. IT service refers to the supplementary business (price and contract adjustments) and technical (resource development, provisioning, maintenance, and recovery) support provided by ITO vendors to address implementation and ongoing frictions (Chang et al., 2017; Gopal & Koka, 2012). While IT infrastructure flexibility affords firms the ability to rapidly commit IT assets to alternative courses of action, IT service flexibility based on pay-as-you-go pricing, dynamic service level agreements (SLAs) and on-demand provisioning of scalable systems (Iyer & Henderson, 2010; Mell & Grance, 2011), enables firms to overcome the resource coordination and control constraints arising from the loss of IT ownership. Consistent with this argument, as a baseline, cloud sourcing can be expected to increase the intensity (count) and value (deal price) of M&As by affording flexibility, which reduces the ex ante risk of integration failure.

Building on the core argument, this study posits that the flexibility provided by cloud sourcing diminishes when firms eschew standardized cloud services. Flexibility derives from the economies of scale and complementarities that are afforded by having a unified and standardized platform (e.g., Boh & Yellin, 2006; Markus & Loebbecke, 2013). The customization required by some cloud services, however, can erode these sources of flexibility. Consistent with the argument, this study posits that highly standardized cloud services—i.e., (1) software as a service (SaaS), (2) public, and (3) globalized cloud services (serving broad geographic markets)—have a positive impact on M&As.

Finally, drawing on prior literature which suggests that the strategic role of IT differs across industries (Banker et al., 2011), this study also examines the impact of cloud sourcing on M&A activity across three industry groups—(1) information industries, where IT can facilitate richer information flows that support decision-making; (2) automation industries, where IT can replace human labor; and (3) transformation industries where IT can facilitate innovation in products, services and business models (Dehning et al., 2003; Kim et al., 2017). The risk of M&A integration failure arises from an information asymmetry between the acquiring firm and its target (Reuer, 2005; Shrivastava, 1986). Consistent with their strategic imperative to use IT for improving information flows, firms operating in information industries can use cloud sourcing to enhance their information about potential targets. Thus, this study further posits that highly standardized cloud services will have a positive impact on M&As, particularly in information industries.

Analyzing a dataset comprising 4,075 observations from 673 firms, this study reveals that ownership of IT infrastructure is not a prerequisite for M&As. Rather, our findings indicate a significant positive link between de-ownership of IT infrastructure through cloud sourcing and the intensity and value of M&As. Analyses leveraging the heterogeneity of cloud services and firms’ operating industries provide consistent support to our proposed mechanism—that the flexibility afforded by cloud sourcing helps to diminish the ex ante risk of integration failure. When this flexibility is undermined through a choice of less-standardized cloud services, the positive link between cloud sourcing and M&A activity becomes insignificant. Similarly, when the diminished ex ante risk of integration failure afforded by cloud sourcing is muted, as in the case of automation and transformation industries, the positive link between cloud sourcing and M&A activity also becomes insignificant. Consistent with our theoretical proposition, findings from our post hoc analysis also reveal that firms with cloud sourcing exhibit higher post-M&A performance compared to firms without it.

This study contributes to three streams of IS literature. First, it contributes to the literature on IT outsourcing (e.g., Han & Mithas, 2013) by conceptualizing cloud sourcing as a service innovation with characteristics that are distinctive from traditional ITO and by demonstrating its strategic value as a driver of enhanced M&A activity and performance. Second and more fundamentally, it contributes to the literature seeking to understand the influence of IT on firm boundaries through M&As (e.g., Benitez et al., 2018). This study demonstrates that a flexible IT infrastructure enables M&As even when the IT infrastructure is owned by third-party vendors, provided the vendors afford capabilities that help to overcome the transaction costs that accompany de-ownership. Furthermore, this study illustrates the underlying mechanism through which cloud sourcing affects firm growth through M&As—namely through an assurance of reduced M&A integration risk. Notably, this stands in contrast to the mechanism by which firms’ in-house IT is expected to spur M&As through the reduction in firms internal coordination costs (Cao et al., 2022a, 2022b).<sup>2</sup> Third, it contributes to the literature on digital infrastructure and platforms (e.g., Rai & Tang, 2014) by demonstrating the value of standardization in the context of cloud services through its positive impact on M&As.

## Literature Review

## IT Outsourcing and Strategic Value

Research has shown that ITO, a phenomenon where firms shift components of their IT infrastructure—such as applications, data centers, networks, and staff—away from a hierarchical mode towards a market mode of governance delivers significant production cost advantages (Oshri et al., 2009). However, because ITO can also be accompanied by a loss of ownership of IT resources, its ability to generate strategic value is less certain (Kotlarsky et al., 2018; Lacity et al., 2017). Loss of ownership is accompanied by a depreciation in clients’ internal capabilities and an inflation in vendor-related transaction costs (Hart & Moore, 1990; Weigelt, 2009). Strategic value generation in traditional ITO, therefore, relies on relationship-specific investments from vendors in technological, contractual, and relational capabilities (e.g., Ethiraj, 2005; Levina & Ross, 2003).

Cloud sourcing, born out of the growing trend towards servitization, involves the sale and provisioning of IT resources as a service over the internet to avail economies of scale and scope (Bharadwaj et al., 2013; Iyer & Henderson, 2010). Like traditional ITO, the depreciation in clients’ internal resources and the inflation in vendor-related transaction costs, both arising fundamentally from a loss of ownership of IT resources, can also afflict cloud sourcing (Choudhary & Vithayathil, 2013; Hoffman & Woods, 2010). However, unlike traditional ITO vendors, relationshipspecific investments in technological, contractual, and relational capabilities are typically eschewed by cloud vendors in favor of capabilities that are common across clients. Cloud vendors provision standard technologies and contracts through arm’s-length relationships (Armbrust et al., 2010; Mell & Grance, 2011).

Notwithstanding this departure from relationship-specific investments by cloud vendors, however, recent research suggests that firms can indeed generate strategic value from cloud sourcing. A limited number of studies have found that cloud sourcing fuels organic growth through innovation in products and services (e.g., Jin & McElheran, 2017; Kathuria et al., 2018; Malladi & Krishnan, 2012). Notably, however, when using cloud capabilities for organic innovation, clients can still use their knowledge of internal systems to make their own relationship-specific investments and carefully plan resource usage (e.g., Han & Mithas, 2013; Lee et al., 2019). In contrast, in the case of inorganic growth through M&As, clients lack knowledge about the internal systems of potential targets they seek to acquire (Chen et al., 2018; Ragozzino & Reuer, 2011). Making relationship-specific investments with vendors or planning for fluctuations in resource demand is therefore less plausible.

In summary, for several reasons, which are grounded in the transaction cost economics (Williamson, 1981) logic of the firm and discussed above, the ability of cloud sourcing to drive M&A activity remains a priori unclear. First, the loss of IT ownership through cloud sourcing can introduce vendorrelated coordination and control costs, which may reduce M&As by introducing internal inefficiencies into client organizations. Second, relationship-specific investments made by traditional ITO vendors, which clients consider crucial for the achievement of strategic outcomes, are more muted under cloud sourcing. Third, clients’ knowledge of internal systems of potential targets, which are also considered crucial for integrating external resources from ITO vendors, is limited owing to the information asymmetry that exists with potential M&A targets. Notwithstanding these transaction cost concerns, however, prior literature suggests that cloud sourcing affords clients certain capabilities that can potentially drive inorganic growth through MA&s, thereby warranting a deeper theoretical and empirical examination.

## IT and Organizational Boundaries

Exploring the relationship between IT and firm boundaries, a significant body of literature has demonstrated that by reducing internal and external coordination costs, in-house investments in IT can motivate firm diversification and influence vertical integration (e.g., Dewan et al., 1998; Gurbaxani & Whang, 1991; Hitt et al., 1991; Ray et al., 2009). Unlike these organic modes of growth, however, the potential to create synergistic value through acquisition and integration of potential targets remains the fundamental motivation behind inorganic growth through M&As (Graebner et al., 2017). Integration is the process of combining the distinct and independent processes, structures, and systems of the acquiring and target firms into a unified whole (Barki & Pinsonneault, 2005). While speedy and effective integration can generate synergistic value, ineffective integration can result in poor performance and financial losses for acquiring firms (Larsson & Finkelstein, 1999). Integration with potential targets, thus, poses a significant ex ante risk for firms (see Table 1). With the risk of integration failure and financial losses looming, firms are naturally less likely to pursue or to spend excessively on a seemingly value-destroying activity, thereby diminishing both the intensity and value of pursued M&As.

What makes this ex ante risk especially severe is the information asymmetry between acquirers and potential targets, which “creates barriers in the pre-acquisition evaluation process” (Chen et al., 2018, p. 330). Since information about the target is asymmetrically distributed between buyers and sellers, it is expensive for buyers to aggregate the knowledge and understand all aspects of their prospective targets (Ragozzino & Reuer, 2011). Moreover, even when targets are willing to disclose information about their internal systems, the information may not be credible (Reuer, 2005). Without an understanding of how acquirers’ capabilities can be leveraged for speedy and effective integration, the ex ante risk of integration failure remains salient, diminishing the intensity and value of pursued M&As. As Shimizu and Hitt (2004, p. 57) noted: “Uncertainty regarding the future potential of strategic initiatives often makes even effective managers hesitant to initiate.”

Despite these challenges, prior literature suggests that investments in IT capabilities can motivate acquirers to pursue M&A activity (Sarrazin & West, 2011). For example, by reducing internal coordination costs, enterprise systems can encourage M&As (Cao et al., 2022a). Likewise, by enabling acquirers to quickly commit IT assets to alternative courses of action, flexible IT infrastructure can help acquiring firms to better tackle the integration challenges, thereby encouraging M&As (Benitez et al., 2018). This literature, however, implicitly assumes that firms always own their IT infrastructure. It thus largely overlooks the resource coordination and control constraints that arise with third-party ownership of IT infrastructure, as well as their potential to adversely affect the pursuit of M&As, given the challenges involved in integration and value generation (e.g., Cao et al., 2022b; Du, 2015; Tanriverdi & Uysal, 2011). Our study contributes to the growing literature on IT and M&As by examining this theoretical assumption through a widespread phenomenon involving a loss of IT ownership: namely, cloud sourcing.

## Hypothesis Development

From an organizational capability perspective (Barney, 1999), cloud sourcing provides firms with strategic flexibility (Bharadwaj et al., 2013; Kathuria et al., 2018; Lacity & Reynolds, 2014). Firms with flexible assets (i.e., assets that can be easily adapted, redeployed, or reconfigured) that offer strategic options to quickly pursue alternative actions and the freedom to coordinate and control the deployment of those assets towards those actions are considered to have strategic flexibility (Sanchez, 1995; Shimizu & Hitt, 2004).

Consistent with the first dimension of strategic flexibility, IT infrastructure flexibility is an IT capability that provides clients with strategic options to quickly commit IT assets to alternative courses of action (Benitez et al., 2018; Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011). Similarly, consistent with the second dimension of strategic flexibility, IT service flexibility is an IT capability that provides clients with increased freedom of coordination and control over those IT assets (Lusch & Nambisan, 2015; Piccoli et al., 2022).

By affording IT infrastructural flexibility, cloud sourcing can reduce the client’s ex ante risk of M&A integration failure. Cloud sourcing can enable clients to address the resource adjustment needs of M&A integration with an IT infrastructure that is highly scalable and adaptable due to its modularity, interoperability, and connectivity. Clients can quickly commit IT assets to alternative courses of action in response to change (Byrd & Turner, 2000; Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011). Through IT infrastructural flexibility, cloud sourcing provides clients with a safeguard against any inaccurate evaluation of asset usage that may occur due to the information asymmetry that exists with potential M&A targets, thereby improving their ability to deal with asset fluctuations. As Sarrazin and West (2011, p. 2) note, firms “with flexible, adaptive architectural platforms are less likely to experience post-merger integration difficulties … assuring synergies are realized and ultimately increasing the deal-making capacity of acquirers.” Thus, with the ex ante risk of integration failure reduced, firms with cloud sourcing may be driven to increase the intensity and value of the M&As they pursue.

Focusing solely on the IT infrastructural flexibility afforded by cloud sourcing, however, ignores another important aspect of the phenomenon, which fundamentally distinguishes it from similar capabilities provided by in-house IT (e.g., Benitez et al., 2018)—namely, the lack of firm ownership over IT infrastructure. Ownership of resources is considered valuable as it grants firms residual rights and serves as a safeguard against transaction costs that arise from contractual and relational hazards in interfirm settings (Hart & Moore, 1990). By forgoing IT ownership through cloud sourcing, firms thus subject IT infrastructural assets to various vendorrelated transaction costs. These costs can limit the firms ability to freely coordinate and control IT assets (Weigelt, 2009). Given the importance of this freedom for tackling ambiguity, contradictions, and unanticipated problems in resource demand during M&A integration (Cao et al., 2022b; Tanriverdi & Uysal, 2011), firms with cloud sourcing may avoid pursuing more and higher-value M&A deals, despite having IT infrastructural flexibility.

However, it is important to note that in addition to providing IT infrastructural flexibility, cloud sourcing also affords another important capability—IT service flexibility, i.e., the ability to rapidly change the terms, extent, nature, and scope of services following the initial contract (Kathuria et al., 2018; Susarla, 2012). IT service flexibility in cloud sourcing arises from unique features like pay-as-you-go pricing, flexible SLAs, and virtualized and elastic resources with on-demand and selfservice provisioning (Bharadwaj et al., 2013; Mell & Grance, 2011). It is specifically aimed at mitigating vendor-related transaction costs such as lengthy price negotiations, knowledge transfer, contract design, resource provisioning, and coordination costs (Lusch & Nambisan, 2015; Piccoli et al., 2022). Despite the loss of IT ownership, IT service flexibility can thus enable clients to freely coordinate and control assets for M&A integration, thereby reducing the ex ante risk associated with it. With the ex ante risk of integration failure reduced, firms with cloud sourcing will be driven to pursue more M&As given the strategic benefits they offer, i.e., innovation synergies, access to new customers and markets (Datta et al., 1992; Rabier, 2017; Tang & Zhao, 2023), and the ability to invest more heavily in the M&As being pursued.

## Nature of Cloud Services and M&A Activity

We posit that the flexibility benefits afforded by cloud sourcing for tackling M&A integration will diminish when firms eschew standardized cloud services in favor of those that entail customization. A firm may be motivated to eschew standardized cloud services due to the need to make externally sourced resources more compatible with internal systems and with other external stakeholders considered crucial for growth and survival (e.g., institutions, business partners, etc.) (Dyer & Singh, 1998; Garud & Kumaraswamy, 1995). Consistent with this view, several studies have highlighted how firms customize external IT resources to achieve internal and external compatibilities (e.g., Han & Mithas, 2013; Lee et al., 2019; Rai et al., 2015). Such customization, however, while improving the efficiency of resource utilization for internal activities, can diminish a firm’s ability to subsequently integrate the assimilated resources with third parties (Steensma & Corley, 2000; Weigelt, 2009).

Firms can eschew standardized cloud platforms through their choice of cloud services. To achieve internal compatibility in terms of the service type, compared to SaaS, which provides more standardized ready-to-use applications for end users,

PaaS (platform as a service) and IaaS (infrastructure as a service) enable customization, as they provide developers with access to the middleware and computing resources needed to build applications (Susarla et al., 2010; Xiao et al., 2020). In terms of the deployment models, compared to public clouds, which provide more standardized off-site IT resources owned and operated by cloud vendors, private and hybrid clouds entail more customization, as they are typically located on-site and partially or wholly operated by the client (Armbrust et al., 2010; Hoffman & Woods, 2010). To achieve external compatibility, compared to highly globalized services, which serve broader geographic markets<sup>3</sup> and are already standardized in congruence with different operational and institutional environments, localized cloud services support more customization<sup>4</sup> (Gozman & Willcocks, 2019; Loebbecke et al., 2012).

The prior literature on digital infrastructure and service platforms suggests that eschewing standardized cloud services can undermine the benefits offered by IT infrastructure flexibility for tackling M&A integration. First, clients lose the economies of scale and scope offered by standardized infrastructures (Allen & Boynton, 1991; Malhotra et al., 2005). As Boh and Yellin (2006, p. 174) note: “Reducing the heterogeneity of infrastructure components would enable … economies of scale”; however, “supporting a large number of different IT platforms complicates systems development and increases maintenance costs.” Quickly adapting and committing IT assets to alternative courses of action based on changing demand thus becomes difficult with a nonstandardized infrastructure. Additionally, clients also lose potential architectural complementarities that could be realized by bringing acquisition targets onto a common standardized platform. Common platforms, with their standardized interfaces, protocols, and formats, allow for seamless interoperability and connectivity across client modules and their acquisition targets (Lusch & Nambisan, 2015; Piccoli et al., 2022).<sup>5</sup> However, by introducing architectural heterogeneity and frictions, customization undermines the possibility of realizing such complementarities (Markus & Loebbecke, 2013). As Lindgren et al. (2021) note regarding an analogous context of ERP integration across the branches of a multinational firm: “Local system users created and implemented workarounds to facilitate its adoption and use, ultimately thwarting the potential gains of the system’s underlying global standard.”

Similarly, eschewing standardized cloud services can also undermine the benefits offered by IT service flexibility for tackling M&A integration. The benefits of IT service flexibility fundamentally derive from having a broader scope of options to choose from and a lower cost of reconfiguring those options (Drnevich & Croson, 2013). Cloud customization, however, makes reconfigurations less feasible and more expensive by engendering the interfirm transaction costs linked to resource de-ownership, ultimately limiting the scope of exercisable service options (Susarla et al., 2010; Xiao et al., 2020). As Bharadhwaj (2000, p. 172) notes: “A non-integrated IT infrastructure dominated by system incompatibilities severely restricts an organization's business choices.” Moreover, even effectively utilizing the exercisable service options that do exist requires a deep understanding of the vendor’s infrastructural capabilities (Susarla, 2012). However, heterogeneity across the cloud infrastructure can impede learning and undermine clients’ ability to fully utilize the service flexibility offered (Henfridsson & Bygstad, 2013). Finally, with cloud customization, clients also lose the governance complementarities promised by service flexibility. A common cloud platform, with flexible pricing and contractual provisions, allows clients and their acquisition targets to easily coordinate the development of controls for governing platform resources (Grover & Kohli, 2012; Rai & Tang, 2014). However, by engendering idiosyncratic governance hazards and rigid controls that are distinctive from acquisition targets, cloud customization by clients (Ceccagnoli et al., 2012; Huber et al., 2017) undermines the possibility of realizing such governance complementarities.

Overall, we argue that cloud services that are highly standardized will be relatively more effective in increasing the intensity and value of M&As because they provide greater flexibility benefits that reduce the ex ante risk of integration failure. In contrast, services that require customization may fail to provide clients with an assurance of reduced ex ante risk of integration failure because they suffer from many of the same limitations that afflict traditional ITO—e.g., limited flexibility and heightened vendor-related transaction costs linked to resource de-ownership. Thus, we hypothesize:

H1: Highly standardized cloud services—i.e., (1) SaaS, (2) public, and (3) globalized cloud services—have a positive impact on M&As.

## Nature of Operating Industries

The strategic role of IT innovations like cloud sourcing within firms can vary depending on their operating industries (Banker et al., 2011; Dehning et al., 2003; Kim et al., 2017). In information industries, IT innovations are used for improving information flows that support decision-making. In automation and transformation industries, IT innovations are used primarily for replacing human labor and facilitating innovation in products, services, and business models, respectively.

We posit that the reduced risk of M&A integration afforded by cloud sourcing will be especially salient for firms operating in information industries. The risk of M&A integration failure noted earlier arises from an information asymmetry between the acquiring firm and its target. It is expensive for acquirers to aggregate the knowledge and understand the internal systems of target firms (Reuer, 2005; Ragozzino & Reuer, 2011). Without this understanding, the ex ante risk of integration failure remains salient, threatening M&As (Chen et al., 2018). Consistent with their strategic imperative to leverage IT for improving information flows, however, firms operating in information industries can use cloud-based flexibilities to enhance the availability and flow of information about potential targets. As a principal at Deloitte Consulting LLC noted: “In an M&A transaction, companies may not initially have much access to their target’s data … companies can use cloud-based … platforms to merge due diligence information from multiple sources … improve the scrutiny of relevant information.”<sup>6</sup> Thus, we expect the risk-mitigating effects of cloud sourcing to be relatively stronger for firms operating in information industries. Since firms operating in automation and transformation industries eschew using IT innovations to gain an informational advantage over their environment, we expect those effects to be more muted in these industries.

Notably, the risk-mitigating effects of cloud sourcing on M&As are likely to be muted in automation and transformation industries for another reason. The internal systems of firms in these industries remain in a state of constant flux, and, when used for automation, IT results in the continuous decomposition and redesign of processes and structures (Feigenbaum & Gross, 2024; Grover et al., 1999; Sandberg et al., 2020). Similarly, when used for transformation, IT frequently gives rise to new business models through which firms manufacture and distribute their products and services—again inducing change in processes and structures (Yoo et al., 2010). These activities require significant coordination and control over external IT vendors. The heightened vendor-related transaction costs (see the IT Outsourcing and Strategic Value subsection above) posed by these activities can erode the assurance of smoother M&A integration that cloud sourcing affords. Thus, we hypothesize:

H2: Highly standardized cloud services—i.e., (1) SaaS, (2) public, and (3) globalized cloud services—adopted in information industries have a positive impact on M&As.

## Data and Variables

## Data

Our primary data source for ITO deals is the International Data Corporation (IDC), a prominent provider of market intelligence and advisory services. IDC monitors global outsourcing deal announcements, which typically follow the following format: “Client C has opted to outsource technology T to service provider V for N years at a cost of D dollars annually.” This data source thus provides extensive information on client firms, including location and industry, as well as detailed data on every ITO deal, such as signing date, contract duration, contract value, and deal type. Deal type information allowed us to determine if the deal was a traditional ITO or cloud sourcing deal, and revealed the deployment strategy chosen for the cloud sourcing deal (i.e., public, private, or hybrid). An earlier version of this dataset extending from 1989 to 2009 has been used in prior studies (e.g., Bapna et al., 2016; Ravindran et al., 2015). In this study, we focused on ITO deals involving North American public companies from 2010 to 2017.

We subsequently integrated the ITO deal data with M&A information sourced from the SDC Platinum Mergers & Acquisitions database.<sup>7</sup> This database has been widely utilized in M&A research (e.g., Tanriverdi & Uysal, 2011). The M&A dataset encompasses information on acquirers and acquirees, as well as details of their M&A transactions, including the value and objectives of each M&A deal. In accordance with the data processing methods employed in prior M&A studies (e.g., Cao et al., 2022a, 2022b), we removed all M&A deals categorized as repurchases, self-tender offers, recapitalizations, buybacks, exchange offers, spin-offs, or divestitures (Shenoy, 2012). In addition, we also excluded deals in which either acquirers or targets belonged to the public sector, as well as those involving targets classified as holding companies. To account for the role of IT investment in M&As, we employed another dataset from the Aberdeen CI database, a resource commonly referenced in IT investment research (e.g., Nagle, 2019). Additionally, to control for factors affecting M&A activity, we extracted diverse information on client firms from the Compustat database, including sales, asset value, number of employees, market valuation, financial performance, dynamism, munificence, organizational slack, diversification, and vertical integration. Lastly, we utilized the Aberdeen CI database to generate controls related to enterprise systems, acknowledged in the literature (e.g., Cao et al., 2022a, 2022b) as influential factors in M&A activity. Our final, integrated dataset comprised 4,075 observations from 673 firms, covering the period of 2010 to 2017.

Control variables: We accounted for a range of firm characteristics, which could influence firms’ M&A. To take account of IT investment, following prior studies (e.g., Nagle, 2019), we combined the value of the IT hardware with three times the value of the IT labor at the firm.<sup>8</sup> To some extent, firms’ IT investment can also address the ex ante informational costs of M&A and thus promote M&A growth. Size indicates each firm’s sales in a year. Larger firms would have greater ex ante informational costs and ex post integration frictions, resulting in fewer M&A activities. At the same time, they are also expected to exhibit stronger motivation for M&A growth from economies of scale and scope. Market valuation was measured by a firm’s market-to-book ratio in each year. It was calculated by the ratio of the market value of equity to the book value of equity (Anderson & Mansi, 2009). Firms with higher market valuation were considered to have greater growth opportunities, which can lead to more M&A activities. Research has suggested that prior M&A experience and prior financial performance affect firms’ M&A activities (Tanriverdi & Uysal, 2011). We included each firm’s cumulative number of acquisitions for the last five years and return on equity in the previous year in our models.

We also incorporated controls for dynamism and munificence, which characterize the industry conditions confronting the focal firm and might influence its M&A activity (Cao et al., 2022a). Dynamism, representing the volatility and unpredictability of changes within the industry, was measured by the fluctuation in industry sales (Keats & Hitt, 1988). Munificence, which indicates the growth opportunities within an industry (Dess & Beard, 1984), was captured through the increase in industry sales (Keats & Hitt, 1988). Additionally, we accounted for organizational slack, defined as the previous year’s cash in hand divided by total assets, given its potential to facilitate M&A activity. We also controlled for both the diversification and vertical integration of firms, recognizing their possible association with the types of M&A (Cao et al., 2022a). Related and unrelated diversifications were captured using an entropy measure, which accounted for the number of different industries a firm operated in and the distribution of sales across these industries, based on two-digit SIC codes (Dewan et al., 1998). Vertical integration, quantified through the three-step methodology proposed by Fan and Lang (2000), reflects the degree to which a firm oversaw production processes spanning from raw materials to final products. Lastly, we addressed the potential roles of enterprise systems (ES) in M&A by incorporating two additional control variables, namely ES extensiveness and ES standardization (Cao et al., 2022a, 2022b). ES extensiveness reflects the scope of different ES modules implemented firm-wide, while ES standardization denotes the compatibility of these modules across various organizational units.<sup>9</sup> Table 3 provides definitions for the key variables and their data sources. ## Baseline Model: Cloud Sourcing and M&A Activity

We first examined the association between cloud sourcing and the intensity and value of M&As using the following regression model:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & M \& A I n t e n s i t y _ {i t} \mathrm{or} M \& A V a l u e _ {i t} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} C l o u d _ {i t} + \\ & \beta_ {2} N o n C l o u d _ {i t} + X _ {i t} + I n d _ {i} + F Y _ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t}, \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

where $X _ { i t }$ represents the control variables, including ITI, size, market valuation, prior M&A experience, prior financial performance, dynamism, munificence, organizational slack, related and unrelated diversification, vertical integration, and ES extensiveness and standardization; ?????? and $\ b { F Y } _ { t }$ indicate the two-digit SIC industry-fixed effects and year-fixed effects (2010 to 2017), respectively.<sup>10</sup> The mean variation inflation factors of the different models were around 1.24, well below recommended limits, suggesting that multicollinearity was not a problem (Greene, 2011). In Harman’s (1976) one-factor analysis, no single major factor emerged, and the first factor accounted for only 16.2% of the total variance, implying that common method bias was not a significant concern. We began by using ordinary least squares (OLS) to estimate each model. Since firms in our sample could have multiple observations over the data period, residuals were autocorrelated. Thus, we used robust standard errors clustered at the firm level to account for correlation within observations from the same firms but in different years.

Model 1 in Tables 4 and 5 presents the results for M&A intensity and M&A value, respectively. The results demonstrate that cloud sourcing is positively associated with both the intensity and value of M&As. Specifically, a 1% increase in cloud sourcing is associated with a 0.033% increase in M&A value, which translates to an annual increase of \$0.125 million (i.e., \$374.2022 million × 0.0333%) in M&A deals, based on the average M&A value in our dataset (Appendix Table A1). At the same time, however, the results reveal that general IT investment and non-cloud-related outsourcing deals (i.e., traditional ITO) are not significantly related to the intensity and value of M&A. These findings underscore the unique contribution of cloud sourcing, compared to in-house IT and traditional ITO, in affording capabilities that drive M&As.

## Heterogeneity in Cloud Services

## Evidence From the Type of M&As Pursued

Earlier, we posited that cloud sourcing drives M&As by reducing the ex ante risk of integration. If this proposed mechanism is valid, we argue, cloud sourcing will drive firms to especially pursue M&As that are more integration intensive. Prior literature indicates that M&As offering operational rather than financial synergies are considered more integration intensive (Karim & Mitchell, 2000; Rabier, 2017). Operational synergies refer to gains achieved through the combination of resources, the strengthening of operations, and the expansion of products, services, and markets. Realizing operational synergies involves the reconfiguration of organizational resources underlying R&D, supply chain, marketing, and information systems, whereas financial synergies refer to gains achieved solely through the consolidation of the acquirer’s and target’s financial structure, in the form of, for example, tax savings and capital costs. Due to the combination and reconfiguration of resources it involves, integration intensity is relatively greater in M&A targets offering operational synergies. Thus, consistent with our proposed mechanism, we would expect to see a stronger impact of cloud sourcing on M&As that offer operational rather than financial synergies.

Interestingly, while the effects of other cloud services (PaaS, private, and localized cloud) on financial M&As were insignificant, we found that IaaS cloud services had a negative and significant effect. This result underscores the high level of customization and corresponding control and coordination costs associated with a nonstandardized cloud service IaaS poses. Financial M&As are expected to deliver immediate gains in cash flow through the combined financial structures of the acquired and target firms (Karim & Mitchell, 2000; Rabier, 2017). First, the high control and coordination costs imposed by IaaS can significantly undermine the expected cash flow gains. Second, the lack of standardization in IaaS cloud services can create control weaknesses and errors in the financial reporting structures of the combined firm, leading to serious market consequences (Tanriverdi & Du, 2020). Thus, firms using IaaS are likely to avoid targeting financial M&As.

## Evidence From Risk Disclosures in 10-K Filings

To provide further evidence for the proposed mechanism of cloud sourcing reducing M&A integration risk, we examined managerial discussions of risk in company 10-K filings. The Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) has required managers to discuss factors that make their securities risky in Item 1A of their annual 10-K filings since 2005. Among other risk factors, such as competition, intellectual property, regulations etc., the text also contains discussions on IS and M&A related risks. We analyze how cloud sourcing affects these risks.

The extraction of risk factors is carried out using a simple word embedding approach, where an initial set of seed words representing specific risks are identified and then subsequently used to develop an expanded dictionary of cooccurring keywords with similar meaning (Li et al., 2021).<sup>12</sup> Enterprise IS related seed words were drawn from Gartner’s IT Glossary.<sup>13</sup> Based on these seed words, co-occurring keywords, which include IS, but also M&A-related words such as M&A integration, post-merger integration, postacquisition integration, integration activity and technology integration, were identified from Item 1A of all 10-K filings between 2010 and 2018. These keywords formed the final dictionary.<sup>14</sup> Our variable of interest—M&A risk— represents the frequency with which the dictionary keywords appeared in the risk disclosures of firms in our sample.

Our analysis examines whether cloud sourcing contributed to a decrease in M&A risk. Given the low likelihood of reporting M&A risks in 10-K reports in years devoid of M&A activity, we focused exclusively on years in which firms undertook M&As, estimating the following model:

## Cloud Sourcing and Post-M&A Performance

Our findings demonstrate that cloud sourcing can enhance the intensity and value of pursued M&As, which are considered to be of strategic importance for firms, as they create synergies for innovation and provide access to new customers and markets (Datta et al., 1992; Rabier, 2017; Tang & Zhao, 2023). Consequently, M&As are expected to deliver strong sales performance for the combined firm. Potential integration failures, however, can significantly undermine the ability of the combined firm to create synergies, to exploit its access to new customers and markets, and, ultimately, to deliver on this promise of strong sales performance. This raises an important question: While cloud sourcing reduces the ex ante risk of integration failure and drives M&As, as our results suggest, does it effectively prevent failures ex post (i.e., post-M&A) and contribute to strong sales performance?

To address this question, we compared post-M&A sales between firms that adopted cloud services and those that did not. For this analysis, we replaced our dependent variable with sales, while focusing exclusively on firm-year observations where at least one M&A deal occurred. This approach allowed us to assess the impact of cloud sourcing on sales and revenue during M&A years. Additionally, we incorporated M&A value as an additional control variable to account for the varying contributions of M&A deals of different sizes to sales. Model 1 of Table 11 presents consistent results: Firms with cloud sourcing exhibited higher sales in M&A years compared to nonadopters.<sup>15,16</sup> We extended this analysis by lagging the dependent variable (i.e., sales) by 1 to 7 years after the independent variables involving cloud sourcing, allowing us to examine its longer-term effect on post-M&A business outcomes. The results, presented in Models 1-7 of Table 11, indicate that firms with cloud sourcing continued to exhibit significantly higher sales for up to 5 years post-M&A. However, the effect weakened, becoming statistically insignificant in year 6. The results suggest that firms without cloud sourcing may require an additional 6 years to achieve comparable post-M&A sales growth—possibly due to extended post-M&A integration challenges. This suggests that the reduced risk of integration failure promised by cloud sourcing ex ante is indeed actualized by firms ex post. This result strongly establishes the notion that cloud sourcing is not just a costsaving innovation but also carries tremendous strategic value.

## Robustness Checks

## Potential Endogeneity

The endogeneity of the cloud sourcing variable $( \mathrm { e . g . , } C l o u d _ { i t } )$ can stem from omitted variable bias and reverse causality. To address the potential endogeneity, we replicated the main analyses using two-stage least squares (2SLS) regression analyses, an instrumental variable approach. Following prior studies (Kim et al., 2017; Saldanha et al., 2020), we utilized the industry average of the endogenous variable as an instrumental variable. The industry-level average is acknowledged as a valid instrument in the literature, given its strong influence on a focal firm’s IT strategy (e.g., cloud sourcing) and exogeneity to firm growth (e.g., M&A). We thus adopted the regional (i.e., state) average of the endogenous variable as an instrumental variable.<sup>17</sup> We empirically examined the quality of the instrumental variables (i.e., whether they have a high correlation with endogenous variables but no correlation with the error term). Specifically, the F-statistic of the excluded instrumental variables in the first stage shows that we can reject the null hypothesis that the instruments are weak $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ Furthermore, Wooldridge’s (2002) test of overidentifying restrictions also supports the validity of the instruments $( p >$ 0.1). The results collectively validate our instruments. Model 1 (Model 2) of Table 12 presents the 2SLS results for M&A intensity (M&A value), which are consistent with the OLS results. We also conducted the Durbin (1954) and Wu-Hausman (Hausman, 1978) tests, where the null hypothesis is that the variables presumed to be endogenous should be treated as exogenous. Both test statistics are highly insignificant (i.e., p = 0.67 and 0.67, respectively), suggesting that we cannot reject the null hypothesis of exogeneity. Consequently, 2SLS estimators would be less efficient compared to OLS, leading us to focus on the OLS results. Additionally, we also implemented the limited information maximum likelihood (LIML) model as an alternative instrumental variable approach and found consistent results (see Models 3 and 4 in Table 12).

While we demonstrated the robustness of our results against potential endogeneity using the 2SLS and LIML approaches, we further employed an alternative instrumental variable approach—the system generalized method of moments (GMM)—for the following dynamic panel regression models (Arellano & Bond, 1991; Arellano & Bover, 1995; Blundell & Bond, 1998):<sup>18</sup>

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & M \& A I n t e n s i t y _ {i t} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} M \& A I n t e n s i t y _ {i t - 1} \\ & + \beta_ {2} C l o u d _ {i t} + \beta_ {3} N o n C l o u d _ {i t} + X _ {i t} + I n d _ {i} + F Y _ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t}. \end{array}\tag{3a}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & M \& A V a l u e _ {i t} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} M \& A V a l u e _ {i t - 1} + \beta_ {2} C l o u d _ {i t} \\ & + \beta_ {3} N o n C l o u d _ {i t} + X _ {i t} + I n d _ {i} + F Y _ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t}. \end{array}\tag{3b}
$$

These dynamic models allowed us to directly account for the potential impact of past M&A activity on current M&A activity. Moreover, the system GMM estimator for dynamic panel regression models is widely recognized for effectively addressing potential reverse causality by leveraging internal instruments, the lagged values of endogenous variables (Leszczensky & Wolbring, 2022). To ensure the validity of our system GMM analysis, we conducted a series of diagnostic tests. The Hansen test for the overidentification of instruments confirms that the model is not overfitted $( p >$ 0.1). The difference-in-Hansen test supports the exogeneity of the instruments $( p > 0 . 1 )$ . The auto-regression (AR) tests for serial correlation indicate that AR(1) is significant $( p <$ 0.01), while AR(2) is not significant $( p > 0 . 1 )$ , verifying the absence of second-order serial correlation. These diagnostic tests confirm that our instruments meet the necessary conditions for applying the system GMM estimator at the 10% significance level. Models 5 and 6 in Table 12 present the results for M&A intensity and M&A value, respectively, and are consistent with the OLS results. These findings mitigate concerns regarding the potential endogeneity of cloud sourcing.

## Long-Term Effects

The influence of cloud sourcing on M&A activity may extend beyond the immediate year, potentially manifesting as a delayed effect. To explore these potential lagged effects, we replicated the main analysis while incorporating cloud sourcing deals from up to 5 preceding years as additional independent variables. Models 1-5 (Models 6-10) in Table 13 detail the relationship between M&A intensity (M&A value) in a year and cloud sourcing from up to the previous 5 years. The findings indicate that cloud sourcing played a significant role across various time frames, while non-cloud outsourcing remained inconsequential throughout the 5-year time span in relation to M&As. These supplementary findings demonstrate: (1) cloud sourcing plays a pivotal role in driving M&As for at least 3 to 4 years following a deal, and (2) noncloud outsourcing does not facilitate M&As, even when accounting for delayed effects, thereby reinforcing H1.

## Discussion and Conclusion

## Theoretical Contributions

This study makes several theoretical contributions. First and foremost, the study contributes to research on the organizational outcomes of IT outsourcing. Whether firms should develop their IT infrastructure in-house or buy it from third-party vendors remains one of the most fundamental questions in IS research. While a substantial body of research has examined the implications of this “make versus buy” decision on firm profitability, innovation, and stock market value (Kotlarsky et al., 2018; Lacity et al., 2017), its consequences on M&As, an increasingly popular mode of strategic growth for firms, have remined unexplored. Consequently, the understanding of the underlying mechanism through which ITO might influence firms’ M&A activities has also remained limited.

Our research addresses this knowledge gap by resolving an interesting theoretical conflict that is rooted in transaction cost economics and resource-based views perspectives of the firm and that characterizes the relationship between ITO and M&As. Our findings demonstrate that in conditions where potential flexibility afforded by outsourced IT dominate vendor-related transaction costs, i.e., when firms choose (1) cloud sourcing over traditional ITO, (2) standardized (SaaS, public, and globalized cloud services) over nonstandardized cloud services, and (3) and information over automation or transformation industries, the ex ante risk of M&A integration failure is reduced, thereby spurring M&A activity. These findings fundamentally advance our understanding of the underlying mechanism through which cloud sourcing drives strategic outcomes.

It is important to note that while prior IS literature has explored the strategic implications of having a flexible and standardized IT infrastructure, it has focused on internal or “in-house” IT infrastructure (e.g., Boh & Yellin, 2006; Cao et al., 2022a; Malhotra et al., 2005). The theoretical understanding of whether and how IT infrastructure that is flexible and standardized but externally sourced has thus remained limited. An important distinction between internal and externally sourced IT infrastructure is the transaction costs associated with third-party ownership of IT. These transaction costs can potentially erode any benefits that IT infrastructural flexibility and standardization might offer. Interestingly, our research demonstrates that externally sourced IT infrastructural flexibility and standardization can also deliver strategic value to firms because the IT service flexibilities offered by third-party vendors in the form of cloud services can help to overcome the interfirm transaction costs associated with third-party ownership. Thus, our research makes an important theoretical contribution: It establishes externally sourced IT infrastructure flexibility and standardization as effective antecedents of strategic growth.

Our research also shows how the effectiveness of externally sourced IT infrastructure flexibility and standardization in driving M&As is enhanced (1) under conditions of low information asymmetry between the acquirer and target (i.e., in information industries), and (2) when the underlying motivation for M&As is operational integration. In doing so, our research makes another important theoretical contribution: It establishes interfirm information asymmetry and interfirm integration intensity as important boundary conditions in the relationship between externally sourced IT infrastructure flexibility/standardization and strategic growth.

Second, and more specifically, this study contributes to research on the organizational impact of cloud sourcing. A limited number of studies have demonstrated how cloud sourcing can fuel expansions in a firm’s products and services through internal innovation (e.g., Jin & McElheran, 2017; Malladi & Krishnan, 2012). However, the impact of cloud sourcing on expansion in products and markets through external channels like M&As is yet to be fully explored. An empirical investigation is warranted because, unlike internal innovation, where firms can still leverage their knowledge of internal systems for effectively utilizing cloud capabilities, in the case of M&As, firms lack knowledge about the internal systems of the potential targets they seek to acquire (Chen et al., 2018; Ragozzino & Reuer, 2011). Thus, it has been unclear whether firms can successfully deploy cloud sourcing for M&A success. Our research addresses this important empirical gap in the extant literature on the organizational outcomes of cloud sourcing. Our findings demonstrate that in addition to its positive impact on internal innovation shown by prior studies, cloud sourcing can also deliver externally oriented growth through M&As. In doing so, our study underscores the value of the cloud as a strategic rather than just a cost-saving technology.

Third, this study contributes to research on IT and firm diversification. A vast amount of literature has examined the implications of investments in enterprise IT on firms’ strategic growth through organic modes of diversification (e.g., Dewan & Ren, 2011; Ray et al., 2013). Recently, IS scholars have also begun to explore the impact of enterprise IT on inorganic diversification through M&As (e.g., Benitez et al., 2018; Cao et al., 2022a). An implicit assumption in these studies, however, is that ownership of IT infrastructure is a prerequisite for M&As. This literature specifically investigates the impact of “in-house” IT on M&As. However, the growing trend of cloud sourcing, where IT infrastructure may be owned by third parties, challenges this assumption and calls for a deeper understanding of whether and how “outsourced” IT might influence M&As. Complementing this growing literature on IT and M&As, our research thus demonstrates that ownership of IT infrastructure is not a prerequisite for M&A activity. Our findings reveal that outsourced IT, which provides significant infrastructural and services flexibilities, as embodied in the cloud, can overpower the transaction costs associated with de-ownership and still drive M&As by reducing the ex ante risk of integration failure.

## Managerial Contributions

This study also makes important managerial contributions. First, owing to its value-generating potential, there exists a strong underlying motivation for managers to pursue M&As. However, the risk of integration failure often serves as a barrier, preventing them from doing so (Graebner et al., 2017; Larsson & Finkelstein, 1999; Shrivastava, 1986). For managers interested in realizing synergistic value through

M&As, our research illustrates the need to invest in cloud sourcing. As our findings reveal, cloud sourcing can not only reduce the risk of integration failure ex ante, warranting the pursuit of greater and higher-value M&As, but it can also ensure effective integration ex post and therefore deliver strong organizational performance following M&As.

Second, when investing in cloud sourcing, managers are expected to choose from among several cloud service options. For managers facing this choice who are interested in realizing synergistic value through M&As, our research also illustrates the need to invest in more standardized cloud services. The findings from this research reveal that the superior flexibility offered by standardized cloud services, i.e., SaaS, public, and globalized clouds, significantly reduces the ex ante risk of integration failure, driving more M&A activity.

Third, underlying the decision to invest in cloud sourcing is a more fundamental choice made by managers—that of “buying” versus “making” their IT infrastructure. For managers interested in realizing synergistic value through M&As and considering the classic “make versus buy” decision with regard to their IT infrastructure, our research illustrates the need to reconsider investing in traditional ITO deals. The external sourcing of IT infrastructure often comes with the burden of significant vendor-related coordination and control costs. Our research shows that the infrastructural and service flexibilities afforded by the cloud can overcome this burden and enable strategic growth through M&As. However, given that these flexibilities are often largely missing or limited in traditional ITO deals, our findings indicate that such deals may not be able to deliver M&A growth.

Fourth, the integration risk posed by M&As stems from the information asymmetry that exists between acquirers and targets, with acquirers having limited information about the internal systems of potential targets. For managers interested in realizing synergistic value through M&As, our research emphasizes the need to invest in cloud sourcing, especially when operating in information rather than automation or transformation industries. Firms in information industries have a natural strategic imperative to use IT innovations like cloud sourcing to gain an informational advantage regarding their environment. The risk-mitigating effects of cloud sourcing, thus, are likely to be stronger in these industries, as the cloud can be used to improve knowledge and understanding about potential targets.

Finally, M&As can be pursued to achieve operational synergies with target firms for an expansion in products, customers, and markets through a deep operational integration, or they can be pursued solely to achieve immediate gains in cash flow through the combined financial structures of the acquired and target firms (Karim & Mitchell, 2000; Rabier, 2017). For managers interested in value creation through M&As, our research illustrates the need to invest in cloud sourcing especially when pursuing M&As for operational synergies. Our findings show that cloud-based flexibility is a particularly powerful strategic tool for effectively tackling the integration challenges posed by operational M&As. Managers with the ability to deploy this strategic tool can thus confidently pursue an expansion in products, customers, and markets through operational M&As without worrying too much about the risk of integration failures.

## Limitations and Future Research

Notwithstanding these contributions, this study does have some limitations, which provide avenues for future research. First, this study relies on secondary data from multiple sources and demonstrates that cloud sourcing drives M&A activity by reducing the ex ante risk of integration failure. We provide evidence in support of this theoretical rationale by considering (1) the types of M&As pursued (integrationintensive operational M&As versus financial M&As), (2) the disclosures of M&A risk in company 10-K filings, and (3) interviews with industry experts. Future studies could employ survey instruments for a more nuanced understanding of managerial perceptions of M&A integration risk and how the adoption of cloud sourcing influences those perceptions. A survey-based approach could enable researchers to further unpack and improve the theoretical understanding of the types and dimensions of integration risks that the cloud can effectively reduce.

Second, although this study focuses on M&As as a growth strategy, there are other ways in which firms can grow and diversify, such as vertical integration and alliance partnerships (e.g., Dewan, 2011; Ray et al., 2013). Future studies could explore the impact of cloud sourcing on these alternative modes of strategic expansion. Doing so would further improve our understanding of the strategic value that the cloud provides and the mechanisms through which it contributes to overall firm growth. Third, while this study does explore the heterogeneous effects of different cloud services, it remains agnostic to the nature of the underlying vendors that provide those services. Future studies could also examine how the nature and heterogeneity among cloud vendors influence the client’s ability to extract strategic value from its cloud capabilities.

Finally, this study argues that the strategic value of cloudenabled flexibility lies specifically in enabling acquirers to efficiently integrate with any type of IT system posed by targets. However, this integration efficiency can naturally vary, depending on the specific nature of the IT system. Due to the limited availability of data on the internal IT systems of the target firms in our sample, exploring the effects of cloud sourcing on M&As across different types of IT systems (including cloud) deployed by the targets was unfortunately not feasible. Future studies could examine this boundary condition, which would further bolster the theoretical mechanism proposed in this study. Consistent with our theoretical mechanism, we conjecture that the positive impact of cloud sourcing on M&As will be stronger when targets also subscribe to similar cloud services because the post-M&A integration frictions would be lower.

## About the Authors

Moksh Matta is an Assistant Professor of management information systems at the Beedie School of Business, Simon Fraser University. He earned his M.Sc. in management information systems from the University of Arizona and his Ph.D. in MIS from the University of British Columbia. His research broadly explores how organizations can improve the adoption of new technologies and enhance value from technology governance and management practices. His work has been published in MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, and other leading outlets in the field.

Hyeokkoo Eric Kwon is an associate professor in the Department of Information Technology and Operations Management at Nanyang Business School, Nanyang Technological University, Singapore. He holds a Ph.D. in management engineering (information systems) and a B.S. in aerospace engineering from KAIST. His research examines how AI and digital innovations transform traditional business practices. His research has been published in MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Marketing Science, Journal of Operations Management, Journal of Management Information Systems, Production and Operations Management, and npj Digital Medicine. He is a recipient of the INFORMS ISS Gordon B. Davis Young Scholar Award and the AIS Early Career Award. He serves as an associate editor for Information Systems Research.

Kiron Ravindran is an associate professor of information systems and the Information Systems & Technology Area chair at IE Business School, IE University, Madrid, Spain. He earned his Ph.D. in information systems from the University of California, Irvine, and holds a post-graduate diploma in management (international business) from the S. P. Jain Institute of Management and Research. His research draws on engineering, management, and economics, with a focus on sourcing strategy, IT governance, contract design, organizational social networks, and the economics of information systems. His research has been published in MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, and other leading journals in the field.

Gautam Ray is a professor in the Department of Information and Decision Sciences at the Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota. He holds a Ph.D. in business administration from the University of Pittsburgh. His research focuses on the strategic and organizational value of information systems, particularly in areas such as IT governance, digital transformation, and data analytics. Gautam’s work has been widely published in leading journals, including MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Management Science, and Journal of Management Information Systems. He has also served on the editorial boards of MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, and Management Science, and has been recognized with multiple Best Paper and Best Reviewer awards.
