# SOCIAL MEDIA MODERATION AND CONTENT GENERATION: EVIDENCE FROM USER BANS<sup>1</sup>

Xiaohui Zhang Sheldon B. Lubar College of Business, University of Wisconsin–Milwaukee Milwaukee, WI, U.S.A. {xhzhang@uwm.edu}

Zaiyan Wei Mitch Daniels School of Business, Purdue University West Lafayette, IN, U.S.A. {zaiyan@purdue.edu}

Qianzhou Du Faculty of Business for Science and Technology, School of Management, University of Science and Technology of China Hefei, CHINA {qianzhoudu@ustc.edu.cn}

Zhongju Zhang W. P. Carey School of Business, Arizona State University Tempe, AZ, U.S.A. {zhongju.zhang@asu.edu}

The rise of inappropriate content (e.g., misinformation, spam, hate speech, etc.) has become a major concern for social media platforms. To deal with such challenges, platforms adopt various strategies to moderate the content on their websites. This study focuses on user bans, a common but controversial moderation strategy that suspends rule-violating users from further participation on a platform for a predetermined period. Specifically, we investigated the impacts of user bans on banned users’ content-generating behavior (both quantity and quality). Leveraging reactance theory, we formalized our hypotheses relating users’ behavioral reactions to this content moderation strategy. We implemented multiple empirical designs to analyze data from a major social media platform. Our results show that users provided more answers, on average, after bans were lifted. In contrast, we found that the quality of the content (measured by linguistic features and content appropriateness) decreased after user bans. Furthermore, we found that platform recognitions, such as badges and recommendations, alleviated individuals’ reactance toward bans. Specifically, users who have received platform recognitions reduced inappropriate postings and improved the quality of their content after bans. Lastly, we explored the heterogeneous effects of user bans for different banning causes and repeated bans. Our research is among the first to evaluate the effectiveness of user bans and has important implications for content moderation on social media.

Keywords: Social media, platform moderation, user bans, reactance theory

## Introduction

Over the last decade, social media and related digital platforms have evolved as major outlets for many entities to distribute and consume news and information. According to the Pew Research

Center, in 2024, 72% of Americans rely on social media platforms to keep them informed.<sup>2</sup> The literature has also documented that the content on social media platforms significantly affects people’s political viewpoints (Levy, 2021), career choices (Huang & Zhang, 2016), financial decisions (Deng et al., 2018), etc. Despite the profound impacts, inappropriate content on social media sites, including but not limited to spam, racism, and illegal/malicious information, can lead to nonnegligible economic losses and negative consequences for society (Ananthakrishnan & Tucker, 2022). With the increasing volume of inappropriate content, it is critical that social media platforms effectively moderate their users and user-generated content (UGC).

Social media moderation encompasses actions taken by platforms to uphold community norms and minimize anti-social behavior (Wise et al., 2006). Depending on the target, moderation can be classified into content moderation or user moderation (Jiménez Durán, 2022). Content moderation focuses on managing the content by, for example, monitoring and identifying inappropriate material or removing or altering content to prevent it from influencing users (Srinivasan et al., 2019). However, recent studies suggest that content moderation alone may not effectively influence the behavior of the users who create such content (Jiménez Durán, 2022). In contrast, user moderation directly targets user activity on the platform. For instance, platforms may require users to familiarize themselves with community rules or provide specific personal information (Matias, 2019). This study focuses on user bans, a common user moderation strategy. User bans restrict individuals who violate community rules from participating on the platform, typically for a specified period (Chang & Danescu, 2019).

User bans are widely used,<sup>3</sup> yet their effectiveness remains underexplored. Most research has focused on restrictions targeting communities or subcommunities that limit users’ ability to form collective groups. Studies have shown mixed outcomes: Banning a group can increase the cost of inappropriate behavior, often leading to greater compliance with platform rules (Chandrasekharan et al., 2017) and may discourage future participation (Chang & Danescu, 2019). However, in some cases, such bans can also provoke an increase in toxic content (Chancellor et al., 2016). Additionally, Mudambi and Viswanathan (2022) highlighted spillover effects, where banning toxic reddit.com groups influenced the behavior of affected active users across the platform.

Unlike community bans, individual user bans operate on a smaller scale but are perceived as more personal, severe, and less transparent (Myers West, 2018). These bans restrict specific users from participating in activities across the entire platform rather than just within certain subcommunities. While their high severity and individual focus set them apart, the lack of transparency often makes user bans controversial. Table 1 summarizes the differences between user bans and other moderation strategies. Despite their widespread use, research on individual user bans is limited. Whether these bans effectively reduce inappropriate content and foster healthier participation on social media platforms remains unclear. Thus, our first objective is to evaluate the impact of individual user bans on social media.

Besides punitive strategies like user bans, social media platforms often employ incentive-based approaches, such as awarding badges or featuring user content, to encourage knowledgesharing and creativity (Gallus, 2017). One natural question is whether such carrot-and-stick strategies help users comply with moderation efforts. Platform recognitions can enhance users sense of belonging, potentially mitigating negative feelings toward user bans. This may lead to different behavioral responses compared to users who do not receive such recognitions (Kuem et al., 2020). Therefore, our second objective is to investigate whether receiving platform recognitions influences the effectiveness of user bans. With these two goals in mind—evaluating the effectiveness of user bans and understanding the moderating role of platform recognitions— we formally pose the following research questions:

RQ1: Whether and how do user bans influence individual users’ content generation (quantity and quality) on social media platforms?

RQ2: Does the status of platform recognitions impact the effects of user bans?

We base our research hypotheses on reactance theory, which suggests that when individuals perceive or experience a loss of freedom, they exhibit psychological reactance—a motivational state aimed at restoring that freedom (Brehm & Brehm, 2013). This reactance can result in two types of outcomes: behavioral outcomes, where individuals take direct actions to regain autonomy (e.g., engaging in restricted activities), and subjective outcomes, which include internal responses such as frustration, anger, or hostility toward the source of the restriction (Rosenberg & Siegel, 2018). In our context, when users are temporarily banned, they lose their freedom to participate on the platform. This deprivation triggers reactance: Behavioral reactance may affect the user’s likelihood of generating content (impacting quantity). In contrast, subjective reactance may result in hostility toward the platform, potentially altering the qualitative attributes of their content. Reactance intensity depends on the nature of the restriction, including the costs of noncompliance and the user’s relationship with the platform (Graupmann et al., 2012; Rosenberg & Siegel, 2018). Consequently, the effects of user bans likely vary based on users’ engagement and connection to the platform.

To answer the research questions and empirically test our hypotheses, we collected and analyzed data from Zhihu, a leading question-and-answer (Q&A) platform in China that enforces community rules through various moderation strategies, including temporary bans. Users who violate platform rules may be banned from 1 day to 2 weeks. While banned, users can browse the platform but are restricted from posting questions and answers or engaging in other activities. We monitored a random sample of 34,258 active Zhihu users between September

2020 and January 2021, tracking their daily activities, such as posted questions and answers, whether they were banned, etc. Among these users, 7,376 (21.5%) experienced at least one temporary ban during the study period. We compiled a user-byweek panel dataset and used these banning events to investigate the effects of user bans. Our primary analysis employed a differences-in-differences (DID) design combined with propensity score matching (PSM). To address potential endogeneity concerns, we incorporated advanced methods, including look-ahead propensity score matching (LA-PSM) (Bapna et al., 2018) and a social network instrumental variable (IV) (Wei et al., 2021). These analyses yielded consistent and robust findings, ensuring the reliability of our results.

Our findings reveal several key insights. First, users who were banned for the first time during our study period tended to generate more content after the ban was lifted (Q1—quantity). Specifically, these users posted 12.7% more answers in the 6 weeks following their first ban than their expected posting behavior without the ban. However, we found no significant changes in content quality, on average (Q1—quality). Second, platform recognition status significantly moderated the effects of bans on both content quantity and quality (Q2). We observed that users with platform recognitions, such as badges or highlighted answers, reduced the quantity of their answers while improving the quality of their contributions after the bans were lifted. Additionally, the effects of the bans varied depending on the cause of the ban and the frequency of repeated bans.

This research makes several theoretical and practical contributions. First, it complements prior research that has primarily focused on content moderation strategies (Srinivasan et al., 2019). While existing studies on user moderation often examine the community or regional level (Chandrasekharan et al., 2017; Hobbs & Roberts, 2018), our work evaluates user bans at the individual level, offering a more granular understanding of their effects. Second, we challenge the assumptions of analytical models on platform moderation mechanisms (Liu et al., 2022). These models often assume that moderation has homogeneous, static, and universally effective impacts. In contrast, our findings highlight the need for dynamic models that account for irrational responses, long-term effects, and user heterogeneity. Third, our empirical findings lend support to reactance theory, particularly regarding the moderating role of the user’s relationship with the platform (Rosenberg & Siegel, 2018). The influence of platform recognitions on reactance outcomes underscores the importance of these relationships in shaping user responses. Practically, our results offer actionable insights for platform operators seeking to improve moderation strategies. Understanding the duration of reactance periods and identifying effect boundaries (e.g., bans being more effective for users with platform recognitions) can help operators implement more targeted and efficient governance practices.

## Literature and Research Hypotheses

## Social Media Moderation

Research on social media moderation has investigated the effectiveness of different moderation strategies. There are primarily two types of moderation strategies studied in the literature: proactive and reactive (Habib et al., 2019). Proactive strategies prevent users from posting misinformation and harmful content. Appropriate proactive strategies can help online communities maintain desirable environments and promote more contributions from their users (Wise et al., 2006). For example, Matias (2019) showed that making community rules visible to newcomers can effectively reduce online harassment and unruly behaviors. This norm learning process increased newcomers’ compliance rate (to community rules) by more than 8%, leading to a drastic 70% increase in their participation rate. The content filter is another effective proactive moderation strategy; however, Seering et al. (2017) pointed out that such a strategy is inflexible and discourages user participation.

The literature has also explored reactive strategies in which moderation occurs quickly after the posting of misinformation or other harmful content. Srinivasan et al. (2019) showed that removing such content can reduce immediate inappropriate behaviors. Past studies have also shown that after their content is removed, most users do not accept the deletion and express frustration (Jhaver et al., 2019). They also found that reading community guidelines and explaining the deletion can effectively increase users’ acceptance of content removal. Research has also investigated user-initiated moderation like “blacklists” created by individual users. Jhaver et al. (2018) found that such user-created blacklists can efficiently protect users from harmful interactions (with other users on blacklists). However, as they are initiated and maintained by certain individual users, the blacklisting method does not scale and hence cannot achieve platform-wide moderation.

A recent stream of the literature has focused on the impacts of restricting users’ freedom of participation on digital platforms, which is the closest in spirit to our study. The literature argues that the primary goal of user restrictions is to deter potential future repetitions of inappropriate conduct (Chang & Danescu, 2019). Most previous studies have examined the impact of restricting users’ activities at the community or subcommunity level. Chandrasekharan et al. (2017) showed that after banning two subreddits on Reddit, many active members of the banned subreddits became inactive or even deleted their accounts. Some members joined other subreddits and reduced their hate speech usage. Similar effects of banning subreddits are found in Mudambi and Viswanathan (2022). Relatedly, Hobbs and Roberts (2018) documented that users were more likely to join other censored communities after Instagram was banned in China.

At the individual user level, Chang and Danescu (2019) conducted a descriptive analysis and found that 18% of the users banned by Wikipedia violated the community rules again, and 30% left the community altogether. Through quasi-interviews, Myers West (2018) found that people perceive user bans differently from other social media moderation strategies regarding personal orientation, severity, and transparency. While people perceive content moderation and community-level user moderation as being topic-targeted, they often interpret user bans as personal. Also, user account suspensions are generally considered the most stringent form of moderation because they lead to the full deprivation of participation on the entire platform. In addition, people tend to complain about the opacity of user bans. Past research has also revealed the controversial legal nature of user bans due to freedom of speech concerns (Celeste, 2021).

There are also theoretical studies discussing the relationship between social media moderation and platform profitability (Bhargava, 2022). Liu et al. (2022) explored the economic incentives of UGC moderation. In their model, the platform profit is assumed to be generated mainly from users posting and reading content, and UGC moderation affects the quantity and quality of the content. Their theoretical findings indicate that the optimal moderation intensity depends on the platform’s revenue model (advertisement vs. subscription). Madio and Quinn (2021) investigated platform profitability from the perspective of attracting advertisers. In their model, advertisers’ entry decisions are based on user population and the safety of the content, and these two aspects are influenced by content moderation. They found that the optimal moderation intensity depends on the congruence of users’ and advertisers’ preferences. Explicitly, these two theoretical studies both assume that users either comply with the moderation or leave the platform altogether.

Compared with the existing studies, ours is the first systematic empirical work to explore the impact of individual user bans on their subsequent behaviors. In addition, since our research context, zhihu.com, represents a major social media platform that utilizes user bans regularly, we were able to examine more seemingly random user ban events than other studies. Our study also empirically checked the assumptions of prior analytical research. Lastly, in terms of methodology, we had an advantage over previous studies in that we were able to devise state-of-the-art empirical methods to alleviate endogeneity concerns.

## Reactance Theory

We relied on reactance theory to develop our research hypotheses. Reactance theory is a psychological theory about freedom and control; it posits that individuals resist threats as a way to maintain their sense of autonomy (Brehm & Brehm, 2013). According to the theory, freedom is an important and pervasive facet of people’s lives. Threats to or the loss of freedom motivate people to restore it. The core construct of reactance theory is the motivation to restore threatened freedom, i.e., psychological reactance. It has four major components: freedom, the loss of or threat to freedom, the arousal of reactance, and the restoration of freedom. Varied combinations of the four components, together with different attributes, lead to different behaviors. Rosenberg and Siegel (2018) provide a comprehensive survey of reactance theory.

According to reactance theory, experiencing freedom restrictions motivates people to restore the lost freedom. The aroused psychological reactance manifests in two ways: behavioral outcomes and subjective outcomes (Brehm & Brehm, 2013). These two aspects are integral parts of reactance and are not independent or separate. The behavioral outcomes often manifest as increased engagement with the restricted activity. In the context of social media moderation, it has been shown that implementing content censorship promotes people’s search for restricted information (Hobbs & Roberts, 2018). The subjective outcomes refer to people’s increased hostility toward the source of the threat. For example, customers generally consider reviewers with persuasive intentions to be less trustworthy (Lei et al., 2021).

Furthermore, the literature shows that the magnitude of reactance and its related outcomes depend on the noncompliance cost and the individual’s relationship with the source of the threat (Brehm & Brehm, 2013). First, a higher cost of noncompliance will lead to a lower intensity of reactance (Haselhuhn et al., 2012). Second, the focal individual’s relationship with the source of the threat also affects the arousal of reactance. From a group boundary perspective, Graupmann et al. (2012) found that people show little to no psychological reactance when their freedom is restricted by in-group members. In sharp contrast, restrictions from out-group individuals often provoke strong reactance.

## Research Hypotheses

On social media platforms, the literature has argued and demonstrated that users contribute content due to two primary motivations: intrinsic and image-related (Toubia & Stephen, 2013; Wei et al., 2021). Intrinsic motivations pertain to a user’s desire for self-expression, such as the satisfaction derived from sharing thoughts or experiences and providing answers to others’ questions. Image-related motivations revolve around managing reputation and signaling status on the platform. Guided by these motivational aspects, we argue that restrictions on content generation, such as user bans, significantly (1) suppress users’ freedom to fulfill their intrinsic need for selfexpression and (2) hurt their autonomy in managing their reputation on the platform. Such restrictions are likely to trigger banned users’ psychological reactance, which will motivate them to restore their lost freedom and autonomy on the platform (Rosenberg & Siegel, 2018).

As pointed out by reactance theory, the aroused psychological reactance may lead to behavioral outcomes. In our context, once a user is lifted from a ban, one immediate behavioral outcome of such reactance is an increase in content generation, driven by two main factors. First, users may engage in compensatory behaviors to address their perceived loss of freedom (Liu et al., 2019). During the ban, their ability to express themselves—i.e., the intrinsic motivation to contribute content—is suppressed. Their image-related motivation, or reputation, may also suffer because of negative perceptions associated with the ban. To compensate, users are likely to increase their content generation after the ban is lifted, aiming to restore their selfexpression and repair any reputational damage. Second, the arousal of psychological reactance itself prompts resistance to the perceived threat to autonomy (Powers & Altman, 2023). Social media users often post content to signal their presence and affirm their autonomy in the online environment (Huang & Zhang, 2016). By increasing their content output after the ban, users reassert their sense of free will, alleviating the negative emotions caused by the prior restriction. Thus, we hypothesize:

## H1: Users will generate more content on the social media platform after a ban is lifted.

The hypothesis above formalizes the average effect of user bans on the quantity of content generation. We argue that this effect can vary according to the user’s platform recognition status. Platform recognitions, such as badges or platform content recommendations, are performance-based rewards from the platform. Such recognitions denote a user’s closeness to the platform (or their sense of in-group membership) and their elevated reputation within the platform. Users with platform recognitions often establish stronger ties with the platform, i.e., receiving platform recognitions strengthens a user’s sense of belonging to the in-group (hereafter, in-group sense) (Gallus, 2017). Additionally, because of their better-established reputation (Goes et al., 2016) and deeper attachment to the platform (Kuem et al., 2020), users with platform recognitions often place greater value on the opportunities afforded by the platform to express themselves. Therefore, the cost of damage to their reputation and the loss of self-expression caused by a user ban is heightened—resulting in higher noncompliance costs. In contrast, the noncompliance costs and in-group sense may be lower for users without platform recognitions. These factors, i.e., the in-group sense and noncompliance costs, can change the magnitude of reactance and its associated outcomes (Brehm & Brehm, 2013).

First, as established by reactance theory, individuals exhibit lower or no reactance toward restrictions imposed by ingroup sources of threat (Graupmann et al., 2012). Thus, the in-group sense held by users with platform recognitions would be expected to reduce their drive to restore autonomy through increased posting. In contrast, users without platform recognitions are likely to exhibit stronger reactance and post more to the platform in order to restore autonomy. Second, users with platform recognitions are more likely to adopt self-censoring behaviors in content generation after a ban is lifted (Hayes et al., 2005). Due to the higher noncompliance costs, they will tend to carefully weigh the risk of triggering another ban before each potential post, ensuring compliance with platform rules. This selfcensoring process will likely constrain the quantity of content generation. Together, these arguments suggest that users with platform recognitions may generate less content after bans are lifted. Despite the existence of the motivations to restore one’s damaged reputation and self-expression through posting more content, these motivations are likely dominated by the arguments above (for users with recognitions), especially the primed awareness of ban risks and the stronger in-group sense. Conversely, for users without platform recognitions, their reactance (induced by user bans) will be less (or not at all) mitigated by the ingroup sense or non-compliance costs, and they will be likely to exhibit the behavioral outcomes of reactance, i.e., generating more content. Collectively, we propose:

H1a: Users with platform recognitions will generate less content on the social media platform after a ban is lifted.

## H1b: Users without platform recognitions will generate more content on the social media platform after a ban is lifted.

Psychological reactance also manifests in subjective outcomes, i.e., hostility toward the source of the threat. In our context, the loss of freedom caused by a user ban may trigger emotional and cognitive responses that undermine the quality of content generated after the ban is lifted. First, user bans may provoke negative emotional states, particularly anger and frustration, which are central to reactance (Rosenberg & Siegel, 2018). These emotions can lead to a hostile mindset toward the platform, interfering with the cognitive processes necessary for generating high-quality content (Rains, 2013). For example, past research has demonstrated that content created under anger-driven states often demonstrates reduced helpfulness (Yin et al., 2021). Another study showed that social media users are prone to post content that may be considered inappropriate under a negative emotional state (Jhaver et al., 2019). Second, psychological reactance heightens self-focus on personal grievances, depleting the attentional resources available to users for content generation (Rosenberg & Siegel, 2018). This cognitive depletion compromises users’ ability to construct well-reasoned arguments and valuable contributions. The arguments above suggest that user ban-induced reactance decreases the helpfulness, argument quality, and appropriateness of the content, decreasing the quality of the content as a whole. Thus, we hypothesize:

## H2: Users will generate content of lower quality on the social media platform after a ban is lifted.

We further argue that the impacts of user bans on content quality also vary according to the user’s platform recognition status. First, users with platform recognitions experience less psychological reactance due to their in-group sense, which will mitigate negative emotions like anger and frustration (Graupmann et al., 2012). This emotional stability will minimize the cognitive interference and attentional depletion associated with reactance (Rosenberg & Siegel, 2018). As a result, the content generated by users with recognitions will likely remain helpful and appropriate. In other words, in contrast to users without recognitions, their content quality would not be expected to decrease (due to negative emotions). Second, while user bans are likely to trigger hostility among users without recognitions, users with recognitions will view bans as in-group threats, which can activate group development behaviors, such as offering resources to the group (Jonas et al., 2009). Thus, users with recognitions may put more effort into generating content of higher quality. Additionally, due to higher noncompliance costs, users with platform recognitions are more likely to engage in deliberative self-censoring in the content generation process. This process can reduce inappropriate content; the efforts to restore reputation in this process can also increase content quality. In contrast, the subjective outcome (i.e., reduced content quality) of users without recognitions will be less (or not at all) mitigated by the in-group sense or by noncompliance costs. We thus propose the following hypotheses on content quality:

## H2a: Users with platform recognitions will generate content of higher quality on the social media platform after a ban is lifted.

H2b: Users without platform recognitions generate content of lower quality on the social media platform after a ban is lifted.

## Research Context and Data

## Zhihu and User Bans

We tested our research hypotheses using data from zhihu.com, the largest Q&A platform and the equivalent of Quora in China. Zhihu was established in 2011 and has since evolved as China’s largest peer-to-peer network of knowledge, expertise, and insights. We chose to examine Zhihu for several reasons. First, Zhihu is one of the largest social media platforms, with over 400 million registered users (as of the end of 2021). It has over 100 million monthly active users, providing over 240 million answers.<sup>4</sup> Second, user bans are widely adopted on the Zhihu platform. In our data, even during the relatively short time window of September 2020 to January 2021, about 21.5% of a random sample of active users had been banned on the platform. Finally, unlike other major social media platforms (such as X and Reddit), the user ban status is publicly observable on Zhihu. We took advantage of this data availability to obtain rich information from the platform.

On its official page on user bans, Zhihu specifies four groups of activities on the platform that can lead to temporary bans:<sup>5</sup> (1) violations of national laws; (2) unfriendly activities such as intimidating speech and racism; (3) spam advertisements, including those generated by machines; and (4) malicious activities such as posting “fishing content” to bait other users. Under each group, there are more specific scenarios that Zhihu regards as a violation of its community rules. From the API,

Zhihu provides four types of labels for user bans: unfriendly, illegal, advertisements, and others. A typical process of banning a user proceeds as follows: After a site administrator identifies or another user reports that a user has engaged in a violation, Zhihu reviews the case. Then, if the violation is confirmed, the account is banned from all activities except browsing site content. Depending on the severity of the violation, a user account can be banned for 1 day to 2 weeks (or in extreme cases, their account may be permanently deleted). Figure 1 shows the profile page of a user being banned (or suspended) for 1 day. At the top of the page, we can see that the user is suspended and the ban will be lifted after 1 day.

In addition to allowing users to post and answer questions, Zhihu also facilitates social networks among its users. A Zhihu user can follow and be followed by other users (without the need for mutual consent). In this research, we call the user who follows the other party in a following relationship the “follower” and refer to the other party as the “followee” (Wei et al., 2021). The followee’s activities on the platform, e.g., posting and answering questions, will appear on the follower’s news feed. Although most of the content on Zhihu is questions and answers, users can also write blogs. In addition, Zhihu users can comment on and “like” (known as “upvotes” on other platforms) others’ content (blogs, answers, etc.). Unlike social media platforms such as X and Facebook, the content and interactions among users on Zhihu are centered on sharing knowledge and insights.

## Data and Samples

Our data collection period was between September 17, 2020, and January 21, 2021. The sample, generated from a standard snowballing algorithm, contained 167,900 users and their daily records during the period. Among all sampled users, 34,258 made at least one contribution to the platform in this period (e.g., providing answers, posting blogs, etc.). Therefore, we relied on this subset of active users in our analyses. Out of all active users, 7,376 (or 21.5%) had records of being banned during our data collection period. To test our research hypotheses, we relied on the first ban (in our study period) for users experiencing multiple bans.<sup>6</sup>

We obtained a rich set of information for each user. We traced all answers posted by each platform user both before and during the data collection period. We focused on answers but not on other activities because, despite being an online social network, Zhihu’s primary content contributions continue to be in the Q&A format (Cao et al., 2023). We also continuously recorded the number of followers and the number of followees a user had so that we could calculate the number of new followers and new followees they gained in each time frame (daily, weekly, etc.). In addition, we scraped other time-variant information, such as user tenure (days since registration), “Excellent User” badge status (to indicate whether a user has made high-quality contributions on the platform), the number of answers highlighted by the platform (due to the high quality of those answers), and the number of upvotes they obtained.

From the data, we constructed a user-by-week panel for our empirical analyses. First, for user i, we determined whether the user was ever banned during the study period and constructed the 1(Has ban)<sub>i</sub> dummy variable. Then, for each observation of user i in week t, we assessed whether the observation occurred after a ban and constructed the 1(Post ban)<sub>it</sub> dummy. The key independent variable for this study is the interaction between 1(Has ban)<sub>i</sub> and 1(Post ban)<sub>it</sub>. Governed by our research hypotheses, we examined five sets of outcome variables: (1) whether a user posts any answer in each week (posting indicator 1(# New answers<sub>it</sub> ≥ 0), binary), (2) the weekly number of answers posted (# New answers<sub>it</sub>), (3) the weekly number of votes received (# New upvotes<sub>it</sub>), (4) the linguistic attributes of these answers, and (5) their appropriateness. We also constructed a series of control variables according to the literature. Previous research has demonstrated that a user’s experience (User tenure<sub>it</sub>), activities (# Answers<sub>it</sub>), reputation (# Upvotes<sub>it</sub>), and social network (# Followers<sub>it</sub> and # Followees<sub>it</sub>) exert significant influence on the user’s content generation (Wasko & Faraj, 2005). We controlled for each variable’s cumulative value by the time in the regression to account for the influence of the user’s historical attributes. In addition, Wei et al. (2021) showed that short-term social interactions (changes in social network and receiving likes) also affect a user’s content generation; therefore, we controlled for # New followees<sub>it</sub> and # New followers<sub>it</sub> to account for the influence of a user’s recent social activities. Because a user is prohibited from engaging in any activities on the platform (except browsing pages) spanning the banned period, we removed their observations for the weeks during the ban. For example, suppose User A was first banned for 3 days during the first week of January 2021. We would have deleted their observations from that week but kept all their other observations (including in the weeks after the ban was lifted). Overall, there were 586,554 user-week level observations in the full sample. Table 2 provides the summary statistics of all variables used in our empirical analyses. We report the correlation matrix in Appendix A.

## Empirical Analysis and Findings

## Empirical Design

To test our research hypotheses, we relied on our panel data and leveraged the events of first bans by the platform to construct a differences-in-differences (DID) design. Specifically, our sample contained users (21.5% of the whole sample) who had experienced user bans during our study period (September 2020 to January 2021), in addition to others who had not been banned by the platform during this period. Since these user ban events are not random, we first relied on a propensity score matching (PSM) algorithm to generate a comparison group of users (from the group who had never been banned). User bans are often linked to factors like platform experience, content posting, and social interactions (Chang & Danescu, 2019). We matched users based on the corresponding covariates: user tenure (indicating familiarity with the platform), the number of questions and answers (reflecting content posting), upvotes received, and follower/followee numbers (representing

social interactions). Specifically, we conducted one-to-one nearest-neighbor matching on the propensity score without replacement. We matched the 7,376 treated users to 7,376 control users; overall, our main sample contained 14,752 users. The unmatched 19,506 control users were excluded from the analysis. The matching process reduced the number of observations from 586,554 to 235,525. In a series of robustness checks, we showed that the results were consistent with the original sample and samples with additional matching covariates or alternative methods. We also validated that unobserved variables did not significantly affect our matching results.<sup>7</sup>

To further alleviate endogeneity concerns, we conducted a series of robustness checks and devised multiple alternative empirical strategies, including LA-PSM and IV; we report the findings in later sections.

Using the PSM-generated sample, we tested our proposed hypotheses. Our regressions included individual user-fixed effects and week-fixed effects to control for both timeinvariant user characteristics (e.g., personal habits) and platform-wide temporal changes (such as website design updates). We also included a set of user-specific, time-variant control variables. Equation (1) below is our main regression specification (to test our main hypotheses, H1 and H2):

$$
Y _ {i t} = \nu_ {i} + \beta_ {1} \cdot 1 (\mathrm{Afterlifting}) _ {i t} + \gamma^ {\prime} \mathbf {Z} _ {i, t - 1} + \mu_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t},\tag{1}
$$

where $\nu _ { i }$ are user-fixed effects, and $\mu _ { t }$ are week-fixed effects. As we used this two-way fixed effects specification, user and week-fixed effects absorb the treatment group and period effects from the basic DID specification. 1(After lifting)<sub>it</sub> is our key independent variable; it is the interaction between 1(Has ban)<sub>i</sub> and 1(Post ban)<sub>it</sub>, i.e., 1(After lifting)<sub>it</sub> = 1(Has ban)<sub>i</sub> × 1(Post ban)<sub>it</sub>. The dummy variable equals 1 if and only if the user was banned during our study period and the observation week is after their first ban. For users who never experienced a user ban in our study period, 1(After lifting)<sub>it</sub> is 0 for all time periods. 1(After lifting)<sub>it</sub> also takes 0 for users who experienced user bans in the weeks before their first bans. The coefficient $\beta _ { 1 }$ captures the effects of our interest on the outcome variable $Y _ { i t } . \mathbf { Z } _ { i , t - 1 }$ is a vector of time-variant control variables introduced in the data section.

As our dependent variables differ in data type, we employed various estimation models. For the binary outcome (binary posting indicator), we used probit and logit regressions. For the count variables (weekly number of answers and votes), we applied Poisson pseudo-maximum likelihood (PPML) and negative binomial to the original counts, as well as ordinary least squares (OLS) for log-transformed counts. All three methods for these count variables yielded the same results qualitatively. We focused our quantitative interpretations on the PPML estimates, given PPML’s ability to address zeroinflation biases (Gong et al., 2018). For robustness checks on the count variables, we likewise report only the PPML results. Regarding linguistic attributes and appropriateness, which are continuous variables derived via content analyses, we used OLS. Finally, we clustered all the standard errors at the individual user level to control for potential serial correlation in our panel data.

Besides the average effects of user bans, we also hypothesized that the effects would depend on a user’s specific platform recognition status (H1a-b and H2a-b). On Zhihu, there are two primary indicators of platform recognitions: the status of the “Excellent User” badge,<sup>8</sup> 1(Excellent User) , and the number of answers highlighted by the platform, # Highlighted answers<sub>it</sub>. Zhihu and other social media platforms widely adopt badges and platform-highlighted elements. On Zhihu, badges serve as a symbolic reward for a user’s significant contributions and reputable status, whereas highlighted answers reflect Zhihu’s endorsement and promotion of a user’s content. To test for heterogeneous effects, we conducted subgroup analysis, in which we divided our matched sample into two subsamples: users with platform recognitions (470 users in total, 304 with the Excellent User badge only, 137 with highlighted answers only, and 29 with both recognitions)

and users without platform recognitions (14,282 users). We estimated Equation (1) using each subsample. We also estimated an alternative regression that used users’ platform recognition status as a moderator, i.e., adding an interaction term between the recognition status and $1 ( A f t e r l i f t i n g ) _ { i t } .$ . The moderating analyses generated the same results qualitatively. In our context, platform recognitions may correlate with the ban status and individual users’ content generation at the same time. As a result, the findings of the moderating analysis may have been biased. Pearl and Mackenzie (2018) suggest that subgroup analysis is more appropriate in such situations. Thus, we only reported and interpreted the results from the subgroup analyses.

## Effects of User Bans on Content Generation: Quantity

## Effects on the Likelihood of Posting Answers

We first examine the effect of user bans on the likelihood of posting answers on the platform after the lifting of the first ban. The dependent variable, 1(# New $a n s w e r s _ { i t } \ \ge \ 0 )$ indicates that user i posted at least one answer on Zhihu in week t. We estimated probit and logit regressions for this binary indicator; the results are reported in Table 4. We can see that the coefficients associated with the lifting dummy, $1 ( A f t e r l i f t i n g ) _ { i t } .$ , are both positive and statistically significant in the two specifications. In other words, consistent with H1, which predicts that users will post more after their (first) ban is lifted, we found that users were more likely to provide answers after the ban was lifted. Numerically, we found that the probability of posting at least one answer increased by about 3% to 7% (compared to before the ban).

## Effects on the Number of Answers Provided

Our primary measure for the quantity of content generation was the weekly number of answers posted by a user on Zhihu (# New answers<sub>it</sub>). To estimate Equation (1) for this count measure, we used OLS, PPML, and negative binomial models (log-transformed count for OLS and original count for the latter two), as outlined in the empirical design. The results from all three models are qualitatively consistent. Panel A of Table 5 presents the estimated coefficients of the lifting dummy. Consistent with the impacts on the probability of answering questions, users posted significantly more answers in the weeks following the lifting of their first bans. The PPML results suggest an approximately 12% increase in the weekly number of answers (compared to before the ban), providing further evidence supporting H1.

## Heterogeneous Effects by Platform Recognition Status

H1a and H1b propose that the effects of user bans on the quantity of content generation vary depending on whether users have platform recognitions. A user’s platform recognitions reflect their relationship with and attachment to the platform, influencing noncompliance costs and perceptions of group boundaries. Consequently, users with platform recognitions are expected to produce less content after a ban, while users without recognitions will likely generate more.

## Effects of User Bans on Content Generation: Quality

Our empirical findings thus far show that users tend to generate more content after a user ban. In this section, we report our findings on how user bans affect the quality of a user’s content contribution after the ban is lifted. Synthesizing previous studies regarding online content quality and our research context, we measured content quality based on three dimensions: peer evaluations (Huang et al., 2019), linguistic attributes (Liu et al., 2020), and content appropriateness (Mudambi & Viswanathan, 2022).

## Effects on Content Quality Measured by Peer Evaluations

Peer evaluations (e.g., likes and upvotes) are commonly used to measure content quality because they reflect both the popularity of a post and others’ assessment of its quality (Huang et al., 2019). In this study, we measured peerevaluated content quality using the number of upvotes for a user’s answers posted in a week (# New upvotes<sub>it</sub>). To test H2, we estimated Equation (1) using this quality measure. We then assessed the heterogeneous effects proposed in H2a and H2b through subgroup analyses similar to those in the previous section. In addition to the controls in the quantity analysis, following Huang et al. (2019), we also controlled for the number of answers each user posts weekly to ensure that our estimates captured per-post effects.

All results are reported in Table 6. Panel A reports the main effect of user bans on # New upvotes<sub>it</sub>. All estimated coefficients of the lifting dummy are insignificant. On average, no evidence indicates significant changes in the peerevaluated quality of a user’s content after a user ban is lifted. Therefore, H2 is not supported when using peer evaluations to measure content quality. Turning to the subgroup analyses, Panel B shows that for users with platform recognitions, the lifting of a user ban led to a significant increase in peerevaluated content quality (all three estimated coefficients are positive and significant). The PPML estimate in Column 2 indicates a roughly 98.6% increase in weekly upvotes (compared to before the ban), which strongly supports H2a. In contrast, Panel C reveals no significant effect for users without platform recognitions, suggesting that user bans do not meaningfully alter the peer-evaluated quality of posted content for these users. Hence, H2b is not supported when using peer evaluations as the quality measure.

## Effects on Content Quality Measured by Linguistic Attributes

Previous research also highlights the importance of objectively assessing the argument quality of content (Otterbacher, 2009). Linguistic attributes are widely adopted to measure argument quality. Liu et al. (2020) proposed evaluating argument quality across various dimensions, including informativeness, objectivity, and accuracy. A sufficient amount of information is essential for high-quality content; therefore, content length is commonly used as a measure of informativeness. Objectivity reflects whether an argument is unbiased and impartial, with higher objectivity generally indicating higher quality. Subjectivity and sentiment analyses are typical methods for evaluating content objectivity (Liu et al., 2020). Accuracy, on the other hand, relates to credibility. Accurate content is typically supported by concrete data and expressed in a logical manner (Otterbacher, 2009).

We leveraged a Chinese dictionary developed by Huang et al. (2012) for linguistic inquiry and word count (LIWC) to extract measures of the aforementioned quality measures. More specifically, we focused on word count, subjectivity, number of cause words, negative emotion, and numerical data. For each linguistic characteristic, we calculated the average value per answer posted by each user in a week (i.e., continuous measures). We then estimated Equation (1) with the five linguistic characteristics as the dependent variables using OLS. Table 7 reports the results. Each column corresponds to a linguistic characteristic.

As shown in Column 1 of Table 7, the average answer length decreased by about 26 words after the first ban was lifted (compared to before the ban). This result indicates a decrease in the answers’ informativeness and suggests that although, on average, users posted more answers after experiencing a ban, they collectively put less effort into each answer. Regarding the subjectivity of answers, Column 2 of Table 7 shows that the answers provided by users after a ban was lifted were more subjective (or, equivalently, less objective). Likewise, Column 3 shows that users used words with more negative emotions after the ban (compared to before the ban). These two results (Columns 2 and 3) combined suggest a decrease in content objectivity, likely stemming from users’ defensive mindset after a ban. Furthermore, as shown in Columns 4 and 5, after the first ban, users’ answers contained fewer cause words (such as “because,” “therefore,” etc.), compared to before the ban, indicating fewer logical expressions and less use of concrete numerical data. Overall, these linguistic changes suggest that bans discourage users from producing effortintensive, objective knowledge while encouraging more emotionally driven, less logically coherent responses. We interpret these findings as evidence that user bans lower the argument quality (informativeness, objectivity, and accuracy) of answers provided by individual users. In other words, bans lead users to produce a higher quantity of answers at the expense of argument quality. Taken together, H2 is supported when using linguistic attributes to measure content quality.

## Relative Time Models

An important assumption for a valid DID design in our context is that there exist relatively parallel trends between users who experienced at least one user ban and those who did not. Table 11 reports the coefficients of time dummies from estimating relative time models in the sense of Angrist and Pischke (2008). Specifically, we included time dummies indicating 5 weeks before the first ban and 7 weeks after (i.e., two additional dummies indicating 6+ weeks before and 8+ weeks after it, respectively). The regression equation is:

$$
Y _ {i t} = \nu_ {i} + \sum_ {\tau = - T} ^ {N} \beta_ {\tau} D _ {i \tau} + \gamma^ {\prime} \mathbf {Z} _ {i t - 1} + \mu_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t},\tag{2}
$$

where $D _ { i \tau }$ are the relative time dummies. The coefficients $\beta _ { \tau }$ capture how a user’s content contribution pattern changes over time.

Column 1 of Table 11 reports the estimation results of Equation (2) with # New answers<sub>it</sub> as the dependent variable. First and most importantly, it shows that most of the pre-ban coefficients are not statistically significant, suggesting that the two groups of users are indeed comparable in terms of the quantity of content generated before the first ban. In other words, there is a parallel trend in providing answers between the two groups. Second, in sharp contrast, the coefficients of the post-ban dummies are positive and statistically significant immediately after the lifting, indicating that users increased their contributions right after being allowed to post content on the platform. Lastly, we also notice that the positive effects are insignificant after 7 weeks (including the 7 week and 8+ week dummies), suggesting that users’ contribution levels drop back to their pre-ban levels in the long run.<sup>10</sup>

Column 2 of Table 11 reports the results with # New upvotes<sub>it</sub> as the dependent variable. As user bans only have significant impacts on peer-perceived quality for users with platform recognitions, we only report the results of the robustness checks for this subgroup of users. We observe similar patterns to those in the quantity analysis. All pre-ban coefficients are statistically insignificant, indicating that the two groups of users are comparable in terms of peer-evaluated quality. After the first ban was lifted, newly generated content from the users with platform recognitions received more upvotes, which can be interpreted as increased peer-evaluated content quality. The effect lasted for about 6 weeks. We also checked the results using LIWC attributes and content appropriateness as quality measures. Appendix B reports the results. The majority of preban coefficients (61 out of 66) are statistically insignificant, with a few low-magnitude (compared to the main effects) violations in weeks relatively far away from the ban.

## Rosenbaum Bounds Test on Matching Sensitivity

The PSM method matches the “treated” (i.e., those banned by Zhihu during the study period) and comparison groups on observable variables; however, it cannot handle potential bias caused by unobserved variables. If the unobserved variables influence the treatment assignment and the outcome simultaneously, the observed effect might be biased. To address this concern, we conducted the Rosenbaum bounds test (Rosenbaum, 2002) to assess the influence of unobserved variables on our estimations. The Rosenbaum bounds test evaluates the magnitude of hidden bias that needs to be present to nullify or flip the estimated effects. Such magnitude is represented by the sensitivity parameter Γ. Individuals with the same observed characteristics may differ in the odds of receiving the treatment by Γ. A large Γ suggests that the result is less sensitive to unobservable variables. The Rosenbaum bounds test result (Table 12) indicates that the unobservable variables need to change the odds of differential treatment assignment by at least 56% to nullify the estimated effect, i.e., $\Gamma \geq 1 . 5 6$ . This result is of a similar magnitude to previous research (Zhang et al., 2022). Therefore, we conclude that our results are unlikely to have been driven by unobservable factors.

## Endogeneity

Our main analyses rely on a PSM-adjusted DID method. Using a PSM algorithm, we first constructed a comparison group of users who were similar to those being banned during our study period. Then, our DID estimation incorporated both individual user-fixed effects and time-fixed effects to control for unobservables. We devised two state-of-the-art empirical methods to further alleviate endogeneity concerns and repeated our main analyses.

## DID With Look-Ahead Propensity Score Matching (LA-PSM)

Following Bapna et al. (2018), we adopted LA-PSM as an alternative matching approach (to our main PSM discussed earlier). The advantage of LA-PSM, compared with traditional PSM, is that the traditional approach matches on observed characteristics and thus may potentially suffer from biases due to the omission of unobserved factors (although our sensitivity analyses demonstrated that our results were not very sensitive to such omission). In contrast, LA-PSM has the advantage of accounting for certain time-invariant unobservables (Bapna et al., 2018). Based on traditional PSM, the LA-PSM method retains only the “control” units that become “treated” in the future. In our context, our main PSM determined the individuals who were similar to a banned user but had not been subject to a ban themselves. Built upon this, our LA-PSM process further identified individuals who were later banned by the platform. We continuously tracked the sampled users from the end of the main observation time window, January 21, 2021, to March 1, 2021. We identified 1,039 users who received their first ban during this look-ahead period; these users formed the control group in the LA-PSM analysis. They were then matched (via PSM) to 1,039 users from our original treated sample. This matched sample, consisting of 2,078 unique users (1,039 treated and 1,039 control), yielded 17,355 observations.

## An Instrumental Variable (IV) Approach

The LA-PSM method is useful for accounting for certain unobserved characteristics; however, one may argue that timevariant factors that affect individual users differently may exist. To further control for such endogeneity concerns, we followed Wei et al. (2021) and adopted an IV method. Wei et al. (2021) argued that in a network setting, the characteristic of a user’s second-order neighbors is a useful instrument for the focal user’s activities. In our context, we proposed using the number of second-order followees (a user’s followees followees who are not currently immediate connections) who had ever experienced banning. Specifically, for an observation corresponding to user i in week t, the instrumental variable is the number of user i’s second-order followees who were ever banned before week t.

The IV we propose is relevant because individuals with similar characteristics (observed and, more importantly, unobserved) are more likely to form connections in a network—i.e., homophily (McPherson et al., 2001). In Zhihu’s context, users who have been banned on the platform are more likely to form connections with (following or being followed by) others who are also more likely to be banned.

This “homophily” phenomenon applies not only to the firstorder followees (or followers) but also to higher-order neighbors (such as followees’ followees). On the other hand, since the outcome of interest is a user’s content generation on Zhihu (answers provided, etc.), we argue that the validity of our instruments relies on the assumption that a Zhihu user does not provide answers to help their followees (the users they follow) follow other users (whom the focal user does not follow or know). For example, suppose user i only follows user j, and user j only follows user k on the platform (so user i does not follow user k and user k does not follow user i), the assumption is that user i does not provide answers to help user j follow user k. We believe that this is reasonable in Zhihu’s setting, and this assumption helps establish the exclusion restriction and exogeneity of our proposed instrument.

We then estimated Equation (1) with the instrument using a twostage least squares (2SLS) specification. We report the regression results on content quantity in Columns 2 and 3 of Table 14. Column 2 reports the first-stage results, and Column 3 presents the second-stage results. First, our first-stage results suggest that the IV is relevant, and our method is unlikely to be subject to weak instrument issues. Specifically, the coefficient associated with the instrumental variable is positive and statistically significant, suggesting that the banning status is indeed positively correlated between a user and their second-order followees. The values of the F-statistics from the first-stage regression are much greater than 10 (the rule of thumb for the nonexistence of weak instrument concerns). Second, using the instrument, we continued to find that a user contributed more after their first ban was lifted, which is qualitatively the same as our main findings. Leveraging the IV, we also replicated previous analyses on peerevaluated content quality. Columns 2 and 3 of Table 15 report the results, which are qualitatively consistent with the main analysis. In Appendix E, our IV analyses for linguistic attributes (Table E2) and content appropriateness (Table E4) show patterns broadly comparable to our main analyses.

## Repeated User Bans

In the previous analyses, we dated the banning event using the first ban the user experienced (during the study period). A Zhihu user could have been banned multiple times during our study period. In the sample, the average number of bans among those who had ever been temporarily banned is 2.53, and the median number is 1. One may naturally wonder whether the effects we discovered persist over multiple bans. It is reasonable to expect that the strength of reactance (to the loss of freedom) may decay as the number of temporary bans increases.

In Table 17, we report a series of regressions. The first column corresponds to the regression using the first ban to date the event. Compared with the main analysis (Table 5), where we kept all observations after the first ban, in the regression of Column 1 in Table 17, we kept only the observations up to the week of the second ban. Similarly, the regression reported in the second column uses the second ban to date the event. To remove the impact of the first ban for such users (who were banned more than twice in our period), we only kept the observations between the first ban and the third ban (if any). The other regressions in Table 17 were conducted similarly. The results lend support to our conjecture. Quantitatively, a user increased the number of answers after the first ban (and before the second ban) by about 13.6% (compared to pre-ban numbers). This estimation is slightly larger than what we observed in the main analysis. The increase dropped to about 10% for the second ban. Starting from the third ban, the impact was not statistically significant or even became negative. The decline in content quality was also mainly exhibited after the first few ban experiences. These findings align with reactance theory’s premise that reactance diminishes when people perceive their lost freedom as irretrievable. In cases of repeated bans, this perception may lead to a decrease in content generation, as individuals recognize the futility of regaining their freedom of participation.

## Discussion and Conclusion

With the growing influence of social media in many areas, social media moderation has become increasingly important.

Our understanding of the effectiveness of various moderation methods has been limited to content moderation or constrained to the group level. Ours is among the first studies to investigate the effectiveness and impacts of individual-level user bans. Taking advantage of a unique panel dataset to identify the impacts of user bans on individual users’ contentgeneration behavior on a social media platform primarily devoted to Q&A content (Zhihu), we found that (1) after the first ban was lifted, users provided more answers; (2) on average, the answer quality did not improve, with the answers becoming shorter, more subjective, more negative, and less logical; (3) users with platform recognitions (badges, highlighted answers) showed less reactance to the moderation; and (4) the impacts are likely short term. Table 21 summarizes our findings. These findings demonstrate that temporary user bans are not a one-size-fits-all strategy for all users on the platform. In some cases, user bans may backfire and induce low-quality content. These results have a number of theoretical and practical implications.

## Theoretical Implications

On a theoretical level, this study enriches the research on the effectiveness and impacts of social media moderation. First, we expand the scope of this research stream by focusing on a prevalent yet understudied moderation strategy: user bans. While prior studies have primarily examined content moderation (Chancellor et al., 2016; Jhaver et al., 2018) or group-level user moderation (Chang & Danescu, 2019; Mudambi & Viswanathan, 2022), user bans differ in their personal orientation, severity, and transparency (Myers West, 2018). To our knowledge, this is the first paper to systematically evaluate the effectiveness of user bans as a moderation strategy. Unlike findings that content moderation fails to significantly alter users’ subsequent behaviors (Jiménez Durán, 2022), our findings indicate that user bans lead to immediate changes in content generation behaviors. Similarly, while group-level moderation tends to encourage compliance (Chandrasekharan et al., 2017), we find that user bans provoke reactance behaviors. These findings highlight the unique effects of user bans compared to other moderation strategies.

Additionally, our unique dataset and empirical strategies make significant contributions to this research stream. Previous studies on social media moderation have typically relied on selfreported qualitative data, often capturing a sample of users particularly concerned with moderation practices (Jhaver et al., 2018; Myers West, 2018). In contrast, we assembled a comprehensive panel dataset that tracked all user bans over the study period. By collecting data daily (rather than relying on static records at the end), we included all observations, even for users who left the platform, which helped to mitigate potential biases that could have been caused by missing data. In addition, we employed multiple state-of-the-art empirical strategies to address endogeneity concerns, going beyond the descriptive or qualitative approaches commonly used in prior research. These methods enhance the robustness and validity of our findings.

Furthermore, our empirical findings have implications for theoretical studies on platform governance (Bhargava, 2022). This literature focuses on the relationship between governance strategies and platform welfare, with user moderation serving as a key tool (Liu et al., 2022; Madio & Quinn, 2021). While analytical models often assume that the likelihood of participation will decrease when users experience social media moderation, our findings challenge this assumption, revealing an increase in the generation of low-quality content after user bans. We recommend that future theoretical models account for such reactance outcomes to better understand the broader welfare impacts of platform governance. Additionally, our findings on the heterogeneous impacts of user bans according to the user’s platform recognition status suggest that future research should consider the interactions between simultaneously employed platform governance strategies. Lastly, while many studies have modeled platform governance as a single-shot game, our results indicate that the influence of user bans evolves over time and varies across repeated bans. Ignoring these dynamic patterns may limit theoretical insights, underscoring the need for future studies to incorporate the dynamic aspect of governance strategies.

Finally, our findings have implications for reactance theory. First, by examining behavioral and subjective responses (changes in content quantity and quality) following a clear freedom restriction (user bans), our study provides empirical results within the context of social media platforms that are largely consistent with predictions derived from reactance theory (Rosenberg & Siegel, 2018). Further, the observed temporal patterns of reactance—characterized by initial spikes followed by subsequent declines—suggest that reactance evolves over time. These dynamics challenge the static assumptions often made in reactance studies, and call for models that account for the time-sensitive nature of behavioral and emotional responses to perceived threats to freedom.

## Practical Implications

Our research offers valuable user moderation guidelines for social media operators. Specifically, our findings highlight the need for caution when applying user bans as a moderation strategy, as bans can provoke substantial reactance from the majority of users and can further degrade the overall platform environment. However, for users with platform recognitions, user bans appear to effectively reduce inappropriate content and enhance content quality. Therefore, platform operators should consider combining punitive measures, such as user bans, with reward-based strategies, such as expanding recognition coverage or offering incentives for improved behavior. For example, in addition to user bans, since July 2024, Zhihu has started offering additional features (e.g., the right to edit questions and higher priority on feedback) for users to maintain good records of following platform rules.<sup>11</sup> Such comprehensive approaches can help mitigate the reactance triggered by user bans.

Our findings reveal that the impact of user bans diminishes over time, highlighting the need for prompt and strategic follow-up interventions. For example, providing specific, constructive feedback on the reasons for the ban can help users understand the behavior that led to the action, reducing reactance and the likelihood of repeated violations. Similarly, incorporating behavioral nudges—such as reminders about community guidelines or notifications recognizing positive contributions—can reinforce desired behaviors and mitigate negative reactions. By adopting tailored and multifaceted moderation strategies, platform operators can minimize the adverse consequences of bans while fostering a healthier and more sustainable platform environment.

## Limitations and Future Extensions

We acknowledge several limitations of this study. First, user bans are an outcome of users’ inappropriate behaviors, which could introduce selection bias. Even though we employed multiple empirical strategies to address such concerns, there are likely remaining differences between the observed sample and the population. Due to ethical reasons, a randomized controlled trial (RCT) is impossible in this setting. Second, our research context, Zhihu, is a platform that mainly operates in China. Cultural differences might impact the reactance arousal process. Moreover, national censorship policies can significantly influence user behavior and reactions to moderation. For instance, users in highly censored environments may view bans as expected and comply more readily. In contrast, those in less regulated contexts may perceive bans as unjust and react more strongly. We encourage future research to examine the impact of user bans across countries with differing levels of censorship and cultural norms. Third, we explored users’ behaviors on a single platform only. As people generally participate in multiple social media platforms, we call for future research investigating the spillover effect of user bans across platforms, e.g., a user’s behaviors on X when the user is banned on Reddit.
