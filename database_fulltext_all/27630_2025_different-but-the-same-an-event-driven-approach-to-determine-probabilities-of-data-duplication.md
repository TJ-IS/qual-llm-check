# DIFFERENT BUT THE SAME? AN EVENT-DRIVEN APPROACH TO DETERMINE PROBABILITIES OF DATA DUPLICATION<sup>1</sup>

Bernd Heinrich Faculty of Computer Science and Data Science, University of Regensburg Regensburg, GERMANY {bernd.heinrich@ur.de}

Mathias Klier Faculty of Mathematics and Economics, University of Ulm Ulm, GERMANY {mathias.klier@uni-ulm.de}

Andreas Obermeier Faculty of Mathematics and Economics, University of Ulm Ulm, GERMANY {andreas.obermeier@uni-ulm.de}

Alexander Schiller Faculty of Computer Science and Data Science, University of Regensburg Regensburg, GERMANY {alexander.schiller@ur.de}

The importance of data quality assessment, in general, and duplicate detection, in particular, has been recognized in both research and practice. Duplicates are known to cause critical problems in many domains, including customer relationship management, data management and data warehousing, fraud detection, production, and healthcare. Such duplicates are caused by events that are typically associated with data patterns in the records. For example, duplicates in customer databases caused by the event “relocation of a customer” characteristically exhibit dissimilar values for address-related features, while the values of the other features (e.g., name-related features) tend to be highly similar. Analyzing duplicate-related events and recognizing such patterns seems particularly promising for duplicate detection. However, existing approaches do not take advantage of this and neither consider events nor recognize their associated data patterns when detecting potential duplicates. In this paper, we introduce events as causes of duplicates and, on this basis, propose a novel probability-based approach for duplicate detection. Our approach assigns the probability of being a duplicate to each analyzed pair of data while avoiding limiting methodical assumptions of existing approaches. The evaluation on seven different datasets shows that our approach is able to outperform existing approaches and that the models learned can be transferred between datasets of the same domain without further labeling or repeated model learning.

Keywords: Duplicate detection, entity resolution, record linkage, probabilistic model, data quality

## Introduction

To gain competitive advantages, companies’ decision-making is increasingly based on large amounts of data (Breuker et al., 2016; Ngai et al., 2017). However, poor data quality (DQ)

prevents companies from achieving the best possible value from their data (Baesens et al., 2016; Moges et al., 2016; Shankaranarayanan & Zhu, 2021). Indeed, companies need to spend a significant part of their data analytics efforts to ensure DQ, often about 80% of the working time (Gupta et al., 2021).

As a result, DQ has been identified as a crucial success factor for decision support systems and a particularly relevant field for both research and practice (Legner et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2018; Marsden & Pingry, 2018; Padmanabhan et al., 2022; Parmiggiani et al., 2022; Peng et al., 2023).

One of the most prevalent reasons for poor DQ is duplicates (Bai et al., 2012; Binette & Steorts, 2022; Christophides et al., 2020; Fan, 2015; Koumarelas et al., 2020a), pairs of data that represent the same entity, such as a person or an object (Draisbach & Naumann, 2011). Duplicates are known to cause a large variety of problems, such as misjudgments of customers (Bleiholder & Schmid, 2015), incorrect strategic and operational decisions (Armony & Plambeck, 2005), additional operational expenses (Draisbach, 2012), and resource misallocation (Binette & Steorts, 2022). Thus, duplicate detection has been recognized as critical in many domains, including master data management, data warehousing, customer relationship management (CRM), fraud detection, production, and healthcare (Ayabakan et al., 2017; Elmagarmid et al., 2007; Fan, 2015; Hua & Pei, 2012). The importance of duplicate detection is also supported by the statements of the experts we interviewed (see the Applicability Check section for details). The experts found that duplicates have a variety of harmful consequences, including incorrect decision-making (“... it is fatal if there are duplicates in the data. It leads to wrong conclusions, performance indicators and so on”), additional costs (“... last year, 350,000 letters were returned to us as undeliverable since outdated addresses were used for duplicates”), and incorrect reporting (“I cannot determine my customer count correctly, which I however need to report”).

In addition, duplicate detection has many applications in research. For example, in many research contexts, it is necessary to identify the same real-world item across datasets and platforms. The data can then be synthesized, allowing for novel and interesting analyses. For example, Nie et al. (2022) matched hotel data from six different sources, Heinrich et al. (2021) aggregated item data across two restaurant portals, and Fang (2022), Huang et al. (2017), and Proserpio and Zervas (2017) linked customer reviews (for restaurants or hotels, respectively) from different review platforms. Similarly, it is often necessary to match users on different online platforms (e.g., Lausen et al., 2020; Tian et al., 2022) or to perform product matching for cross-platform analyses (Park et al., 2021). In such cases, duplicate detection can help facilitate the necessary matching, i.e., identifying the same item based on its different descriptions across different data sources. Another important application is detecting and removing duplicate research samples after retrieving samples from different sources prior to data analysis (e.g., Dehning et al., 2003; Racherla & Mandviwalla, 2013; Yang et al., 2012).

The literature provides well-known approaches for duplicate detection. Focusing on probabilistic approaches (for a discussion, see the Related Work section), a seminal framework for duplicate detection has been presented by Fellegi and Sunter (1969) and serves as a foundation for various such approaches (e.g., Steorts et al., 2016; Xu et al., 2019). Yet probabilistic approaches typically incorporate decision rules based on (conditional) independence or monotonicity assumptions and are often confined to syntactical assessments of the examined data. In particular, they do not consider events causing duplicates and associated data patterns.

However, duplicates in datasets appear after certain events. For instance, Schönfeld (2007) showed—based on the analysis of a company’s customer database with more than 20 million customers—that every year, about 2 million customers relocated and 60,000 got divorced, resulting in many duplicates. Such underlying events causing duplicates are associated with specific data patterns of (dis)similarities regarding the feature values of the duplicate records. For example, a duplicate record is often created after relocation as an event, when the customer is stored once again as a record with their new address—here, typically only the address-related features differ. Thus, the two stored records form a duplicate which exhibits a pattern distinct from patterns of duplicates caused by other events such as marriage, divorce, typo, or the abbreviation of names. For duplicate detection, targeting the data patterns associated with events causing duplicates seems promising.

While the patterns associated with underlying events provide an indication, it remains uncertain whether two records exhibiting a certain pattern indeed form a duplicate. We argue that the principles and knowledge base of probability theory are appropriate and valuable, providing well-founded methods to describe and analyze such situations under uncertainty. Thus, we (1) propose a theoretical foundation for duplicate detection, including formal and precise definitions of duplicates and duplicate-related events, and, based on this foundation, we (2) introduce a probability-based approach for duplicate detection grounded on duplicate-related events and their associated data patterns. Our approach allows to assign the probability of being a duplicate to each analyzed pair of data. This leads to several advantages: (a) the results of our approach are directly interpretable as duplicate probabilities, (b) the evaluation using seven different datasets from the person master data domain and the product data domain shows that our approach is able to outperform existing approaches, (c) the learned models can be transferred between datasets of the same domain without further labeling or repeated model learning. The need for a new approach is also supported by our expert interviews, where several participants revealed the necessity for a substantial improvement regarding the duplicate detection task (see Applicability Check section).

Our approach is useful in terms of two general objectives: First, as it assesses the probability of being a duplicate for pairs of data, it enables the verification of pairs of data with a high probability and the subsequent merging of the verified pairs into one single (“golden”) record. This is important when duplicates directly impede business goals, or violate laws or regulations (e.g., in the financial services industry), and thus cannot be tolerated. Second, there are also situations in organizations where duplicates can or even should be generally tolerated (i.e., no cleanup of duplicates), but it is still important to identify or know about these duplicates. For example, a company’s customer may intentionally create and maintain two separate accounts (e.g., for different shipping addresses or personal purposes). However, it may still be very important for the company to know that the two accounts belong to the same person. In CRM, it is essential to know a customer well and comprehensively, i.e., which contracts, orders, or revenues a customer has in total (across all accounts). The integrated evaluation of all a customer’s accounts in CRM (without technically merging these accounts) enables a more comprehensive view of the customer (the so-called 360° view of a person). This ensures, for example, that customers are not offered products in one account that they have already recently rejected explicitly in another account. This “linking” of accounts can benefit the organization even if it does not clean up the duplicates in its customer database.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. In the next section, we discuss related work on probabilistic duplicate detection and identify the research gap. Subsequently, we introduce the theoretical foundation and our probability-based approach for duplicate detection. Then, we analyze the performance of our approach with respect to different evaluation settings and performance metrics and outline our applicability check. Finally, we summarize, discuss implications for research and practice, and provide an outlook for further research.

## Related Work

Literature provides well-known approaches for duplicate detection (for surveys, see Binette & Steorts, 2022; Christen, 2012a; Christophides et al., 2020; Papadakis et al., 2020a) and distinguishes two major strategies: probabilistic and deterministic duplicate detection (Binette & Steorts, 2022; Tromp et al., 2011). While deterministic approaches allow some flexibility (e.g., with respect to mismatches of the considered pair of data), they employ completely deterministic rules, meaning that each pair of data is strictly classified into one of the (typically) two classes—“duplicate” or “no duplicate”—without any disclosure of uncertainty, probability, or confidence (Binette & Steorts, 2022). Thus, deterministic approaches can lead to problems such as difficulties in interpreting results and overconfidence. For example, a pair of data may fulfill a certain deterministic rule and thus be definitely classified as a “duplicate,” while a minimally different pair of data may fulfill another deterministic rule and thus be definitely classified as a “nonduplicate.” This lack of uncertainty estimate or confidence (e.g., in the form of a probability) hampers use in downstream tasks (Binette & Steorts, 2022)—a fact that speaks in favor of probabilistic approaches. In addition, commonly used deterministic approaches often rely on very complex handcrafted rule sets (Hettiarachchi et al., 2014). Most importantly, studies comparing deterministic and probabilistic approaches for duplicate detection have consistently shown that probabilistic approaches provide superior results (Avoundjian et al., 2020; Binette & Steorts, 2022; Tromp et al., 2011). This is because of the flexibility of probabilistic approaches, which, in contrast to deterministic approaches, allows them to balance between false positives and negatives based on the data at hand (Tromp et al., 2011). Thus, we focus on probabilistic approaches.

A seminal framework for probabilistic duplicate detection traces back to Fellegi and Sunter (1969). This work serves as a foundation for various further probabilistic approaches (e.g., Belin & Rubin, 1995; Steorts, 2015; Steorts et al., 2016; Thibaudeau, 1992; Xu et al., 2019). These approaches rely on a probabilistic model classifying given pairs of data into one of three mutually exclusive classes: the set of duplicates, the set of non-duplicates, and the set of pairs requiring manual review. This classification is based on syntactical agreements and similarities of the considered records’ feature values. To quantify the agreements and similarities, a comparison vector is introduced. Its components are values between 0 and 1 according to the outcome of comparisons performed on the feature values of a pair of data. These comparisons might lead to binary outcomes (e.g., “value of a feature agrees or not”) or continuous outcomes (e.g., “Jaro similarity measure of the values of a feature”). For example, consider a pair of data in a customer database where the feature values are completely different for all features. The comparison vector would show that all features disagree (e.g., it would be filled with zeroes). Based on the comparison vector, two terms ?? and ?? are formed. Precisely, the terms ?? and ?? represent probabilities of observing the comparison vector, conditioned on the considered pair of data being a duplicate or a non-duplicate, respectively. For instance, on condition that the pair of data is a duplicate, the probability (represented by the term ??) of observing the comparison vector filled with zeroes from above is estimated to be small. In the same way, the term ?? represents the probability of observing the comparison vector filled with zeroes on the condition that the pair of data is a non-duplicate. Subsequently, based on the terms ?? and ?? as well as further assumptions (see below), a score—itself not to be interpreted as a probability—is calculated and used for the classification as duplicate, non-duplicate, or requiring manual review according to thresholds.

In this regard, probabilistic approaches rely on two key assumptions: (A1) the classification is confined to syntactical agreements and similarities without considering events causing duplicates, and (A2) the classification incorporates decision rules based on (A2a) (conditional) independence or (A2b) monotonicity assumptions. These assumptions lead to misclassifications (Belin & Rubin, 1995; Thibaudeau, 1992; Xu et al., 2019):

Regarding (A1), not considering the underlying events can lead to false negatives because the semantics behind possible disagreements or low similarities of feature values are not grasped. For instance, in the case of duplicates caused by relocations, address-related feature values usually disagree. These disagreements or low similarities are represented in terms of low or zero values for the corresponding entries of the comparison vector. Approaches based on such syntactical comparisons are prone to incorrect classifications of such pairs as non-duplicates. This leads to potential misclassification of duplicates resulting from relocations or, more generally, other underlying events. To the best of our knowledge, none of the existing approaches reveal and take advantage of the fact that duplicates result from events. In our research, we consider and explicitly model duplicate-related events that cause duplicates as part of our novel theoretical foundation.

With respect to (A2a), the decision rule presented by Fellegi and Sunter (1969) relies on conditional independence assumptions regarding the agreements and similarities across features. This means the agreement/similarity in one feature or vector component is assumed to be not correlated with the agreements/similarities in other features or vector components, given that the considered pair of data is a duplicate or nonduplicate. Many subsequent approaches continue to use these conditional independence assumptions even though they have been shown to be violated in reality (Belin & Rubin, 1995; Thibaudeau, 1992; Xu et al., 2019). For instance, people form households. Because household members often have identical last names, this leads to inherent dependencies between address-related features and the feature Last Name (Thibaudeau, 1992). Therefore, approaches relying on conditional independence assumptions tend to lead to invalid results since these assumptions are violated in reality.

Selected works have tried to alleviate the (conditional) independence assumptions. In particular, notable contributions have been presented by Thibaudeau (1992), Winkler (1993), Larsen and Rubin (2001), and Xu et al. (2019). Thibaudeau (1992) introduced a model tailored to account for certain dependencies among address-related features of non-duplicates based on an empirical correlation analysis. Winkler (1993) suggested including a specified small set of interactions, for example, all three-way interactions or a selection of interactions based on knowledge of some true duplicate statuses. This selection of modeled dependencies was taken up by Larsen and Rubin (2001). As these authors state, the selection of dependencies to be modeled relies on personal knowledge and experience. In addition, these three methods are only suitable for binary comparisons and do not cover continuous similarity-based comparisons (Lehti & Fankhauser, 2006). Moreover, the explicit modeling of all possible interactions between all features is computationally expensive because the number of parameters to be fitted rises strongly. The high number of parameters also fosters overfitting. Recently, Xu et al. (2019) used latent class models with a conditional dependence structure based on a gold standard to analyze approaches relaxing the (conditional) independence assumptions. They acknowledged that the approaches require significant human oversight and input (e.g., their manual configuration is context-specific and timeconsuming) and are computationally expensive. In summary, while these works relax the (conditional) independence assumptions hampering probabilistic approaches, they suffer from limitations regarding generalizability (e.g., by being context-specific and/or requiring inputs from experts) and computational complexity. By contrast, we develop an event-driven approach incorporating a different probabilistic model, which eliminates the need for defining independence assumptions. In particular, our approach does not need to outline conditional dependence structures or other constructs that hamper generalizability.

Regarding (A2b), other approaches (e.g., Lehti & Fankhauser, 2006; Ravikumar & Cohen, 2004) avoid (conditional) independence assumptions by replacing them with a monotonicity assumption. This monotonicity assumption states that higher similarities of feature values lead to a higher probability of being a duplicate and vice versa. However, this assumption is also violated in reality. For instance, two records that differ only in the feature value “Los Angeles” and “Beverly Hills” for the place of residence are highly likely to be a duplicate because “Los Angeles” is commonly used in place of the actual city name “Beverly Hills.” Indeed, despite this stark syntactical dissimilarity, they are more likely to be a duplicate than two other records with smaller syntactical dissimilarities like “Los Angeles”

and “Los Alamos.” Indeed, many pairs of data exhibit low similarities for a subset of features despite being duplicates (Christen, 2008a). Therefore, approaches relying on the monotonicity assumption may also lead to invalid results because of the assumption being violated in reality. Both the theoretical foundation and the approach presented here avoid the need for defining the assumption of monotonicity. This is especially important, as using this assumption hinders the detection of duplicates caused by duplicate-related events.

Finally, some further approaches aim to circumvent the (conditional) independence assumptions by modeling duplicate detection as a supervised classification problem (Binette & Steorts, 2022). These approaches rely on labeled training data with the labels stemming from crowdsourcing (e.g., Frisoli et al., 2019), manual linkage efforts (e.g., Bailey et al., 2020), or the results of unsupervised methods (e.g., Christen, 2007, 2008a). Here, the fact that the amount of available quality-assured training data is usually limited needs to be taken into account (Binette & Steorts, 2022). However, these approaches provide a binary outcome, disregarding the modeling of duplicate probabilities as well as events that cause duplicates, whereas both are focused on in our approach.

To conclude, existing probabilistic approaches for duplicate detection are grounded on syntactical agreement and similarities without considering the causative events. Moreover, most of these approaches are limited by independence or monotonicity assumptions. This may negatively affect duplicate detection performance, as the approaches are prone to false negatives because they fail to grasp the semantics behind disagreements or low similarities of (certain) feature values that indeed indicate duplicates (see the results from the comparison of our approach with competing state-of-the-art approaches in the Evaluation section). Finally, existing approaches do not assess duplicate probabilities and disregard the events causing duplicates, inhibiting deeper insights into the generation of duplicates.

## Theoretical Foundation

In this context, the detection of duplicates remains one of the most prevalent tasks, and our manuscript contributes to assessing DQ regarding duplication as potential DQ defect. To serve as a basis for defining duplicates and explicating how they are caused by duplicate-related events, it is crucial to first provide a definition of DQ.

Definition 1 (data quality): Both real-world entities (e.g., persons) and digital entities (e.g., virtual concepts) can be represented by data (e.g., data records in a relational database). Under certainty and perfect information, it would be known which entity is represented by the respective data and, in particular, which events have affected the entity space ??, the data space ??, or the transition between the entity space and the data space. Thus, with respect to Definition 4, the detection and cause of a duplicate would be decidable under certainty, i.e., they could be assessed in a straightforward manner (e.g., by means of binary classification).

However, such assumptions are not realistic, and we have to consider the fact that information is obviously not perfect and uncertainty arises (Ben-Haim, 2006). Based on notions in decision theory and probability theory, this uncertainty is caused by a lack of information. More precisely, it is not known which entity is represented by the respective data and, in particular, which events have affected the entity space ??, the data space ??, or the transition between the entity space and the data space. In the running example, it may be uncertain whether the two data representations (Anna, Smith, Poststr. 8, 58300, 08-16-1990) and (Anna, Smith, Hardtstr. 3, 44867, 08- 16-1990) represent the same real-world entity “Anna Smith” and whether these representations are duplicates caused by the event relocation. Similarly, duplicates caused by events concerning the transition between the spaces or manipulations of data stored in an information system may remain uncertain. Nevertheless, our idea is that even under uncertainty, a duplicate-related event leaves traces of its occurrence that can be recognized in the form of data patterns. Thus, such patterns can indicate whether a specific duplicate-related event has occurred and whether the pair of data is indeed a duplicate caused by this event.

Based on this foundation for detecting duplicates, we introduce an approach that is grounded on data patterns caused by events under uncertainty. More precisely, we aim to model the probability that a pair of data $( d _ { m } , d _ { n } )$ is a duplicate caused by duplicate-related events.

## Event-Driven Approach to Determine Probabilities of Data Duplication

First, we present the basic idea of our approach. Thereafter, we discuss how to recognize data patterns based on similarity vectors. Next, we introduce our model to determine duplicate probabilities based on duplicate-related events. Then, we extend our model to incorporate additional data. Finally, we discuss the transfer of once-learned models to other datasets.

## Basic Idea

Duplicates are caused by duplicate-related events. However, under uncertainty, these events are not always observable. Our idea is that even under uncertainty, duplicate-related events leave traces of their occurrence, which can be used to recognize specific patterns in the data. For example, for duplicates caused by relocations, the pattern can be as follows: The values for name-related features exhibit a high similarity or even a perfect match, while the similarity between address-related feature values is usually low. To detect such patterns in general, a training dataset with pairs of records is used, for which the duplicate-related events are known (i.e., these pairs are labeled). In Figure 2, outlining the basic idea of our approach, the first pair of records in the training dataset (ID ) is given by ((Fred, Moll, Paulstr. 9, 55239, 02-12-1999), (Fred, Moll, Markstr. 1, 86153, 02-12- 1999)), labeled with the event relocation. For each such pair, a similarity vector is determined, which contains pairwise similarities for the respective feature values.

For example, the first pair of records shows perfect matches for the values of the name-related features First Name and Last Name, as well as for Date of Birth (highlighted by the green vector components), while the values for the addressrelated features Street and $Z I P$ Code show very low similarity (highlighted by the respective red vector components). In this regard, we argue that the event relocation leaves traces that can be captured by a similarity vector for each pair of records. Moreover, other events or combinations of events leave distinct traces, e.g., as illustrated by the third pair (ID ), which is a duplicate caused by the event typo. While the traces for the same event are expected to be similar for different pairs of records, the respective values of the similarity vectors may show some (smaller) variation, as illustrated by the second pair (ID ). Here, the real person represented by both records actually relocated from one city to another, but the street names are, by chance, rather similar, which is reflected in a very low similarity with respect to the ZIP code (highlighted by the red vector component) and a moderate similarity with respect to the street (highlighted by the light red vector component), which is not as low, compared to the first pair (ID ). To deal with such potential variation in the similarity vectors, we consider data patterns $Z _ { l }$ by grouping similar similarity vectors of different pairs. For example, since the similarity vectors of the pairs with IDs , , and have similar values (denoted by a similar but not equal red and green coloration of the vector components), they can belong to the same data pattern $Z _ { 1 }$ . It is important to note that the pairs of records are grouped into the data pattern based only on their similarity vectors (for details, see below). Therefore, it is possible and natural that a data pattern includes both pairs of records being duplicates (possibly caused by one event or combinations of events) and nonduplicates. For example, while the records of the last pair (ID ) have perfect matches for the values of both namerelated features and Date of Birth, it is known (training dataset) that they still represent different real persons with the same name and date of birth who live in different cities. Thus, while the pair with ID also belongs to data pattern $Z _ { 1 }$ , unlike the pairs with IDs  and , the pair with ID is not a duplicate. Hence, besides the data patterns $Z _ { l } ,$ a number of ?? so-called duplication types $R _ { j }$ are modeled, which represent whether a pair of records is a duplicate caused by a certain event (in our example, relocation is represented as $R _ { 1 }$ and typo is represented as $R _ { 2 } )$ or combination of events, or whether the pair of records is not a duplicate $( R _ { J } )$ . Overall, by conceptualizing both the data patterns $Z _ { l }$ and the duplication types $R _ { j } ,$ each labeled pair of records in the training dataset can be assigned to exactly one data pattern $Z _ { l }$ and exactly one duplication type $R _ { j } .$ . For example, the pai with ID  belongs to data pattern $Z _ { 1 }$ and duplication type $R _ { 1 } ,$ while the pair with ID belongs to pattern $Z _ { 1 }$ and duplication type $R _ { J }$ . To address our central question whether a considered pair of records—e.g., ((Anna, Smith, Poststr. 8, 58300, 08-16-1990), (Anna, Smith, Hardtstr. 3, 44867, 08- 16-1990)) (see the right side of Figure 2, denoted by )—is a duplicate caused by a certain event (e.g., relocation or typo), separating data patterns $Z _ { l }$ from duplication types $R _ { j }$ is necessary. Precisely, while it is possible to determine the similarity vector for the considered pair of records and match it to the corresponding data pattern (e.g., data pattern $Z _ { 1 } ;$ see A. in Figure 2), we do not know about the occurrence of duplicate-related events (otherwise it would be possible to detect duplicates with certainty; see above). Indeed, under uncertainty, the duplication type $R _ { j }$ of the considered pair of records is unknown (see B. in Figure 2). Due to the prevailing uncertainty, we propose a random experiment where the sample space contains both data patterns and duplication types jointly as outcomes (see C in Figure 2). Based on the training dataset, the probability of each such outcome of the random experiment can be calculated (see D in Figure 2; details below). On this basis, it is then possible to infer a probability that a considered pair of records $\pmb { \ 6 }$ is a duplicate caused by a certain event. Thus, the result of our approach is directly interpretable in terms of a probability instead of providing a binary classification or an opaque score value. To effectively estimate this probability, it is necessary to recognize the data patterns based on training data with known duplicates and their causing events. This not only helps to infer duplicate probabilities, but it also allows for transferring once-learned patterns (i.e., the learned model) to related datasets without further labeling and retraining. Indeed, knowing the events that cause duplicates and their data patterns can reduce the effort of labeling and training on each dataset due to model transfer, especially in contexts with little labeled training data (Binette & Steorts, 2022; Christen, 2007).

## Recognizing Patterns Associated With Events Based on Similarity Vectors

We argue that each duplicate-related event leaves traces of its occurrence that can be used to detect specific patterns in the data. To systematically recognize such patterns, for pairs of data $( d _ { m } , d _ { n } )$ , we consider interrelations of the feature values $f _ { i } ( d _ { m } )$ and $f _ { i } ( d _ { n } )$ . Since many feature values may be relevant, we regard the feature values as part of the vectors $f ( d _ { m } ) =$ $( f _ { 1 } ( d _ { m } ) , \dots , f _ { I } ( d _ { m } ) )$ and $f ( d _ { n } ) = ( f _ { 1 } ( d _ { n } ) , \dots , f _ { I } ( d _ { n } ) )$ and map the interrelations in the form of similarities onto a ??- dimensional vector $\zeta \in \mathbb { R } ^ { U }$ with $U \in \mathbb { N } . ^ { 4 }$ The entries of ?? are values of similarity measure functions (Christen, 2012b). Here, it is important to note that our approach does not require or assume any kind of independence, monotonicity, or other specific relationship between the components of the similarity vector (see research gap). Rather, the similarity vector $\zeta$ allows for maximum flexibility to recognize data patterns. For example, to recognize the patterns associated with the event relocation $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ very similar first name, last name, and date of birth, but different street and ZIP code), the similarity vector in our running example includes Jaro-Winkler similarities (Winkler, 1990) of first names, last names, streets, and ZIP codes, as well as Levenshtein similarity (Levenshtein, 1966) of dates of birth. For the considered pair of data—((Anna, Smith, Poststr. $^ { 8 , }$ 58300, 08-16-1990), (Anna, Smith, Hardtstr. 3, 44867, 08-16- 1990))—this results in $\zeta = ( 1 , 1 , 0 . 6 3 , 0 . 4 7 , 1 )$ (for the calculation of the Jaro-Winkler similarities, see Appendix B).

While the traces for the same event are expected to be similar for different pairs of data, the respective specific values of the similarity vectors may show some variation. Therefore, we group similar similarity vectors of different pairs by categorizing the similarity vectors into disjoint multidimensional intervals so that each similarity vector belongs to exactly one such interval. Each multidimensional interval represents a particular pattern $Z _ { l }$ in the data. The categorization can be performed, for example, by drawing on the analysis of training data. One possibility is experts recognizing patterns based on (dis)similarities in the training data. Another way is to use rule mining to recognize patterns. Details on recognizing patterns $Z _ { l }$ are presented in Appendix C. For instance, in our example, the multidimensional interval representing pattern $Z _ { 1 }$ represents a typical case of a relocation of a person between two different cities. The interval is defined based on a similarity of 1 for the feature values of First Name, Last Name, and Date of Birth, and a similarity below 0.8 for the feature values of Street and ZIP Code. As another example, pattern $Z _ { 2 }$ represents a second typical case of a relocation, but one taking place within the same city. Thus, $Z _ { 2 }$ is defined analogously to $Z _ { 1 } ,$ , except for the similarity of the feature values of $Z I P$ Code, which is high (i.e., above or equal to 0.8) for the respective pairs. Of the 148 and 85 pairs included in $Z _ { 1 }$ and $Z _ { 2 }$ in the training data, 143 and 84 pairs are labeled to be duplicates caused by relocations (i.e., duplication type $R _ { 1 } )$ , while 5 and 1 are labeled to be non-duplicates (i.e., duplication type $R _ { J } )$ despite exhibiting the same patterns, demonstrating the uncertainty regarding the duplication type for data patterns. To address this uncertainty, we present a model based on a random experiment.

## Modeling Duplicate Probabilities

We denote a duplicate-related event by ??, where k is an element of the set of duplicate-related events $\{ 1 , 2 , \ldots , K \}$ with $K \in \mathbb N$ as the number of duplicate-related events. Further, we introduce duplication types $R _ { j } \ ( j \in \{ 1 , 2 , \ldots , J \}$ with $J \in \mathbb { N } )$ indicating whether a pair of data is

• a duplicate caused by a certain event (single-element subset of $\{ 1 , 2 , \ldots , K \} )$ 2

• a duplicate caused by a combination of events (subset of $\{ 1 , 2 , \ldots , K \}$ with more than one element),

• or not a duplicate at all (empty subset).

In this regard, a duplication type $R _ { j }$ represents a subset of $\{ 1 , 2 , \ldots , K \} , \mathrm { i . e . }$ , an element of $2 ^ { \{ 1 , 2 , \ldots , K \} }$ , resulting in $2 ^ { K } = J$ different duplication types. Finally, by $\{ Z _ { 1 } , \dots , Z _ { L } \}$ we denote the set of data patterns, with ?? ∈ ℕ as the number of patterns. Data patterns serve to capture the traces left by duplicaterelated events, which can be identified using similarity vectors. Precisely, similar similarity vectors are categorized into ?? disjoint multidimensional intervals so that each similarity vector is contained in exactly one multidimensional interval.

Due to the prevailing uncertainty, it is unknown which duplication type $R _ { j }$ a considered pair of data $( d _ { m } , d _ { n } )$ has. Therefore, we rely on probability theory and define a random experiment employing that each pair of data exhibits exactly one data pattern $Z _ { l }$ and has one duplication type $R _ { j }$ . Thus, each outcome of our random experiment is a tuple of a duplication type $R _ { j }$ and a data pattern $Z _ { l } ,$ , which leads to a probability space $( \Omega , 2 ^ { \Omega } , P )$ Here, the sample space $\Omega =$ $\{ ( R _ { 1 } , Z _ { 1 } ) , \ldots , \left( R _ { J } , Z _ { L } \right) \}$ } consists of all possible outcomes of the random experiment, $2 ^ { \Omega }$ is the event space, and ?? is the probability measure. With $2 ^ { K }$ different duplication types $R _ { j }$ and ?? different data patterns $Z _ { l } ,$ there are $2 ^ { K } \cdot L$ possible outcomes $( R _ { j } , Z _ { l } )$

In this probability space, a duplicate-related event k can be related to a respective probability-theoretic event $A _ { k } \in 2 ^ { \Omega }$ Precisely, $A _ { k }$ is the set of all tuples $\left( R _ { j } , Z _ { l } \right)$ such that ?? $\in R _ { j } ,$ i.e., all tuples where the event k is part of the duplication type $R _ { j }$ . Given that the pair of data $( d _ { m } , d _ { n } )$ exhibits data pattern $Z _ { l } ,$ the probability $P ( A _ { k } | Z _ { l } )$ that $( d _ { m } , d _ { n } )$ is a duplicate caused by k is then defined as:

$$
\begin{array}{l} P (A _ {k} | Z _ {l}) = \frac {P (A _ {k} \wedge Z _ {l})}{P (Z _ {l})} = \frac {P (\{(R _ {j} , Z _ {l})   |   k \in R _ {j} \})}{P (Z _ {l})} = \frac {\sum_ {R _ {j} \ni k} p _ {j , l}}{\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {J} p _ {j , l}} \\ \text { with } k \in \{1, \ldots , K \} \text { and } l \in \{1, \ldots , L \}. \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

Here, $p _ { j , l }$ is the (prior) probability for the outcome $( R _ { j } , Z _ { l } )$ of the random experiment, i.e., $p _ { j , l } = P ( ( R _ { j } , Z _ { l } ) )$ . These probabilities $p _ { j , l }$ are derived from a training dataset, where both data patterns and duplication types can be determined.

We illustrate the model and the calculation of the probabilities in our running example, focusing only on the two duplicate-related events relocation and typo (i.e., $K =$ 2). In this case, each pair of data has exactly one of four duplication types $R _ { j } ( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , J = 4 )$ :

$R _ { 1 } ,$ indicating a duplicate caused by relocation;

$R _ { 2 } ,$ indicating a duplicate caused by typo;

$R _ { 3 } ,$ , indicating a duplicate caused by the combination of relocation and typo;

• or $R _ { 4 }$ , indicating no duplicate.

Given these four duplication types $R _ { 1 } , \ldots , R _ { 4 } $ and, for example, ten data patterns $Z _ { 1 } , \dots , Z _ { 1 0 }$ , this leads to 40 possible outcomes of the random experiment: $\Omega =$ $\{ ( R _ { 1 } , Z _ { 1 } ) , ( R _ { 1 } , Z _ { 2 } ) , \ldots , ( R _ { 1 } , Z _ { 1 0 } ) , ( R _ { 2 } , Z _ { 1 } ) , ( R _ { 2 } , Z _ { 2 } ) , \ldots , ( R _ { 4 } , Z _ { 1 0 } ) \}$ To calculate the probability $p _ { j , l }$ for each outcome $( R _ { j } , Z _ { l } ) \in \Omega$ of the random experiment, we leverage the pairs of data in the running example with respect to the training dataset, which includes 13,540 pairs (only considering pairs with duplication types $R _ { 1 } , R _ { 2 } , R _ { 3 }$ and $R _ { 4 } )$ , given in Table 3. For these pairs, the duplication types and data patterns are determined.

The (prior) probabilities $p _ { j , l }$ can be obtained by assessing the respective relative frequency of pairs of data in the training dataset. The presented approach for the calculation of probabilities in the example is methodically based on the partitioning method (Darbellay & Vajda, 1999), which generally allows for the representation of real-valued variables by discrete variables (in our case, the real-valued similarity vectors are represented by discrete data patterns $Z _ { 1 } , \dots , Z _ { L } )$ . In fact, the main idea of our approach is to recognize data patterns based on similarities and use them for an event-driven probability estimation. To address this task, besides the partitioning method, a variety of other methods can be applied, such as kernel density estimation (Terrell & Scott, 1992). Kernel density estimation can be performed with a high degree of automation and little computational effort, and also avoids probability estimates of 0 and 1 due to inherent smoothing.

## Extending the Model to Include Additional Data

The probability determination in our approach can be supported by additional data, such as statistical data related to the feature values of the pair $( d _ { m } , d _ { n } )$ . For instance, ceteris paribus, young adults relocate much more often than older people (PostAddress, 2021). This information is valuable because it means that the event relocation that causes duplicates occurs more often for younger people. Likewise, ceteris paribus, pairs of data with the same very rare last names (like “Basler”) are more often duplicates than pairs of data sharing the same very common last names (like “Mueller”). Thus, determining duplicate probabilities may consider such additional data, which can be retrieved from both company-internal and -external sources. For example, external statistics include information on frequencies of relocations based on age, residence, or income shared by federal statistical offices or other information providers (Bundesamt für Statistik, 2023; PostAddress, 2021). Internal statistics might also reflect such frequencies (e.g., younger people relocate more often). Summing up, additional data can support the determination of probabilities.

## Transferring Learned Models to Related Datasets

As argued in the basic idea, patterns once recognized (i.e., the learned model) can be transferred to datasets in which the same or somewhat related events caused duplicates without requiring complete retraining. Here, the two datasets need to be sufficiently similar (i.e., semantically similar or identical features associated with the considered event need to be contained in both datasets). This is not unusual for datasets from the same domain because, for example, person master datasets usually contain features such as First Name, Last Name, and Date of Birth. The syntactical structure and format, however, do not need to be similar or identical, as it is possible to examine the datasets and preprocess them by mapping corresponding features, excluding features that only appear in one of the datasets from further consideration, and reformatting features or feature values. It is also reasonable to suppose that the same or somewhat related events cause duplicates within the same domain. For example, the event relocation is likely to cause duplicates in all datasets containing person master data. A model learned by our approach on a first dataset can then be transferred to a second dataset, for which no labeled training data is available: For the (preprocessed) first dataset, data patterns $Z _ { 1 } , \dots , Z _ { L }$ and a probability space $( \Omega , 2 ^ { \Omega } , P )$ are defined as described above. The goal is to determine whether a pair of data $( d _ { m } , d _ { n } )$ from the second dataset is a duplicate caused by event k. Therefore, we calculate the similarity vector ?? for $( d _ { m } , d _ { n } )$ . Due to the preprocessing of the datasets, it is possible to categorize ?? in one of the multidimensional intervals represented by $Z _ { 1 } , \dots , Z _ { L }$ —say, $Z _ { l _ { 2 } } .$ Our model for the first dataset determines the probabilities $P ( A _ { k } | Z _ { l } )$ for all data patterns $Z _ { 1 } , \dots , Z _ { L } .$ . Thus, the model in particular yields $P ( A _ { k } | Z _ { l _ { 2 } } )$ , representing the probability that $( d _ { m } , d _ { n } )$ is a duplicate caused by event k. In this way, it can be estimated whether a pair of data stemming from the second dataset with no labeled training data is a duplicate. We will demonstrate this transfer of learned models between related datasets and show its performance in the next section.

## Description of the Datasets

We based our evaluation on three datasets from the person master data domain and four datasets from the product data domain. The first two-person datasets (“INSUR1” and “INSUR2”) comprise real-world customer data from a German insurance provider. The third dataset is a publicly available census dataset (“CENSUS”),<sup>5</sup> which is well known from the literature on duplicate detection (Christen, 2008a; Koumarelas et al., 2020b; Loster et al., 2021). The insurance datasets serve as prime examples of real-world customer data. In fact, compared to non-company data or even synthetic data often used in the literature (Tancredi et al., 2020), the use of real-world datasets allowed us to evaluate our approach in a more practical setting, as the data has not been modified or transformed (e.g., sampled or anonymized). As the largest of our datasets, INSUR1 is wellsuited for evaluating the determined duplicate probabilities (E1) and classification performance (E2) of our approach in detail. Moreover, a second real-world dataset from the same domain (INSUR2) allowed us to evaluate the transfer of an already learned model (E3) in a setting with company data. To allow for an extended reproducible comparison with existing state-of-the-art approaches, we further included five publicly available datasets when evaluating (E2) and (E3): one from the person master data domain (CENSUS) and four from the product data domain (BABY, BIKES, BOOKS, and COSMETICS). The analysis of data from the product domain expands the scope of our evaluation beyond person master data. Please note that in our evaluation, we focus on nontrivial duplicates only (i.e., ?? ≠ ?? in Definition 4).

The insurance datasets consist of two distinct relations comprising person master data from a German insurance provider (INSUR1) and another insurance provider acquired by this company (INSUR2). In both relations, duplicates were a major source of DQ problems, which had been recognized in several DQ projects conducted by the insurers, involving careful and exhaustive manual searches for duplicates assisted by data experts. Thus, we are confident that for INSUR1 and INSUR2, all existing duplicates are labeled correctly, with the labeling by the insurer serving as the gold standard. The insurance datasets include the features First Name, Last Name, Street, ZIP Code, and Date of Birth. Moreover, we were able to draw on additional data regarding Age, Frequency of Last Name, and Frequency of First Name to support the determination of duplicate probabilities (see below for our rationale behind selecting this additional data).

The CENSUS dataset is based on data from the U.S. Census Bureau (Loster et al., 2021) and contains 841 data records with values for the features First Name, Middle Name, Last Name, Street, and ZIP Code. For the product data domain, we included the datasets BABY, BOOKS, and COSMETICS from Primpeli and Bizer (2020). These datasets have challenging characteristics, such as high schema complexity (i.e., many features form the patterns that are associated with a duplicate) and high sparsity (i.e., high number of missing values for some features).

Complementarily, we included BIKES as a dataset with dense data and low schema complexity. The datasets from the product domain contain different features, ranging from only three features (COSMETICS) to 15 features (BABY). The features include three different types: short strings (e.g., product names or titles), long strings (e.g., article descriptions), and numerical features (e.g., prices). Several features with identical or similar meanings occur in more than one dataset (e.g., Title or Article Name, Price, and Color). Thus, models can be trained to transfer them between datasets (E3) (for more details on the product datasets, see Primpeli & Bizer, 2020). All of these five datasets (CENSUS, BABY, BIKES, BOOKS, and COSMETICS) include a publicly available gold standard (Koumarelas et al. 2020b; Primpeli & Bizer, 2020) indicating all duplicates and non-duplicates. These respective gold standards were used without any changes.

Typically, while all possible pairs of data can be generated based on the relations (with the number of pairs growing quadratically with the number of data records), the vast majority of pairs do not exhibit any similarities at all. Blocking is a preprocessing technique often used in the literature (Christen, 2012b; Papadakis et al., 2020b) to reduce the number of pairs of data that need to be analyzed, resulting in feasible runtimes and more balanced datasets. In this line, the publicly available datasets CENSUS, BABY, BIKES, BOOKS, and COSMETICS already contain blocked pairs of data that were not modified at all for our analyses. More precisely, our approaches and the competing state-of-the-art approaches were all evaluated on the same set of pairs of data after blocking, which is all pairs contained in these datasets. For INSUR1 and INSUR2, we performed blocking by filtering based on feature value similarities, as is usual (Papadakis et al., 2020b). More precisely, only pairs with similarities above predefined thresholds for the features Date of Birth and First Name were considered for further analysis. The features and thresholds were chosen with the help of an internal expert from the insurer to ensure that no potential duplicates were missed. This resulted in 16,987 pairs of data from INSUR1 and 1,254 pairs of data from INSUR2, which were used for further analysis without any exclusions. As an additional measure to avoid mislabeled pairs in our analysis, we manually checked the pairs from INSUR1 and INSUR2 that were considered for further analysis after blocking. Again, all considered approaches, including competing approaches, were evaluated on the same pairs of data after blocking. To assess robustness with respect to the blocking thresholds chosen for INSUR1 and INSUR2, we also conducted the evaluation with stricter thresholds. This resulted in the exclusion of actual duplicates but did not qualitatively change the results presented below. Table 6 summarizes the characteristics of the datasets used for evaluation.

## Model Implementation and Competing Approaches

For evaluation purposes only, all duplicates from INSUR1 and INSUR2 were labeled together with an internal expert of the insurer who had supervised the respective DQ projects. The duplicates in INSUR1 were attributed to the events relocation, marriage, typo, and combinations. All duplicates in INSUR2 were attributed to the event relocation because the insurer focused on the detection of duplicates attributed to relocations to update customers’ address information. In CENSUS, the duplicates are known from the gold standard and were attributed to the events typo, abbreviation of first name, and change of first name associated with a gender change. Likewise, the duplicates contained in the datasets from the product data domain are known from the gold standard and were attributed to the events typo, inconsistent formatting during data capture (e.g., different currencies for prices), entry of (syntactically) different article names during data capture (e.g., abbreviations), entry of (syntactically) different article descriptions during data capture, and price change. In summary, the seven datasets contain (simpler) duplicates caused by typos, but also duplicates caused by more challenging events.

To recognize data patterns associated with events, our approach uses similarity vectors. For INSUR1, INSUR2, and CENSUS, we relied on established similarity measures from the literature (Christen, 2012b; Draisbach et al., 2019): Levenshtein similarity (Levenshtein, 1966) was used for numerical features (e.g., Date of Birth), while Jaro-Winkler similarity (Winkler, 1990) was selected for name-related features such as First Name. For the datasets from the product data domain, we relied on the similarities provided with the datasets (see Section 5.1 in Primpeli & Bizer, 2020), which include different similarity measures for short strings (e.g.,

Levenshtein similarity and Jaccard similarity on the token level), long strings (e.g., cosine similarity with tf-idf weighting), and numerical features (e.g., absolute difference). Based on these similarities, data patterns associated with the events were recognized manually. For the product datasets, for example, we found low similarities in the feature Price and high similarities in other features with respect to the event inconsistent formatting during data capture, and higher similarities across all features except for Article Name regarding the event entry of (syntactically) different article names during data capture.

To calculate the duplicate probabilities, we created two versions of our approach, using the partitioning method described in detail above (referred to as “Partitioning”) and kernel density estimation (“KDE”). Both versions use the same similarity vectors and training dataset. Further, to evaluate the benefit of additional data, we considered data on Age, Frequency of First Name, and Frequency of Last Name for INSUR1 and INSUR2. These additional data were chosen since the events relocation and marriage occur much more often for young people, and pairs of data sharing the same very rare names are more often duplicates, compared to pairs of data with the same very common last names. This third version of the approach uses KDE and will be referred to as “KDE with additional data.”

As the datasets from the product data domain include long string features that may be semantically similar while being syntactically different (e.g., product descriptions with different wording), we additionally created a version of our approach (referred to as “KDE with language model”) that adds feature similarities based on the large language model BERT (Devlin et al., 2019). We also examined GPT-3 (davinci) and a fine-tuned semantic similarity (AutoGluon Sentence Similarity). Here, BERT yielded the best results.

## (E1) Reliability and Discriminatory Power of the Determined Duplicate Probabilities

Intuitively, such a definition translates to the fact that if we consider all instances where a 90% probability is determined for an event of interest (e.g., a pair of records being a duplicate caused by relocation), then for 90% of these instances, the event should actually occur (Zadrozny & Elkan, 2002). In our context, reliability means that the determined duplicate probabilities should correspond to the actually observed relative frequencies of duplicates. A common way to evaluate reliability is the reliability curve, which means that the (binned) estimated probabilities are plotted against the observed relative frequencies based on the gold standard. Reliability can also be assessed quantitatively in terms of the reliability score, which is defined as the mean squared deviation from the diagonal weighted by the number of pairs of data in each bin (Murphy, 1973). Such an assessment is similar to expected calibration error (ECE) analysis (e.g., Kuppers et al. 2020, Naeini et al. 2015). We draw on INSUR1 as the largest dataset in the evaluation to evaluate the reliability of the probabilities determined by our approach in terms of the reliability curve and the reliability score based on a common fivefold crossvalidation. The left part of Figure 3 shows the reliability curves for the three versions of our approach. With the reliability curves following the diagonal closely and reliability scores of 0.0063% (Partitioning), 0.0172% (KDE), and 0.0043% (KDE with additional data), the results demonstrate that our approach provides reliable results for all three versions. The best fit is observed for KDE with additional data, showing the benefit of including additional data.

## (E2) Performance With Respect to Classification Into Duplicates and Non-Duplicates

In some fields, it is necessary to perform a binary classification into duplicates vs. non-duplicates. We evaluate the performance regarding such a classification using the common performance measures accuracy (i.e., the percentage of correctly classified pairs of data out of all analyzed pairs of data), precision (i.e., the percentage of true duplicates out of the pairs of data classified as duplicates), recall (i.e., the percentage of duplicates correctly classified as duplicate), and F1-measure (i.e., the harmonic mean of precision and recall). To determine the values for these performance measures, we draw on the probabilities estimated in the versions of our approach and classify pairs of data by assigning them to the class that is most likely according to the probabilities. We conducted fivefold cross-validations in which the datasets were split into five equally sized subsets (folds), as is common practice in evaluating the results of machine learning approaches (Arlot & Celisse, 2010; Berrar, 2019). Moreover, we used stratified random sampling (i.e., the five folds are formed randomly, but in such a way that the share of duplicates in each fold is similar), which is also common practice (Arlot & Celisse, 2010; Berrar, 2019). The model was then trained on four of these subsets (as one training dataset) and tested on the remaining one. This process was repeated five times, with each fold serving as the test dataset exactly once. All performance measures were based on the micro-average. This means that the confusion matrices (consisting of the true/false positives/negatives) were summed up for all five folds, and the resulting values were used to calculate the performance metrics. We classified the pairs of data using competing approaches in the same manner, where a fivefold cross-validation was also performed for supervised approaches. Table 7 summarizes the results by providing the F1-measure.

Our approach, regardless of the considered version, yields convincing results. For example, for INSUR1, a classification based on KDE with additional data yielded a precision of 93.37% and a recall of 85.35%, which aggregate to the F1- measure of 0.8918 shown in Table 7. Moreover, our approach outperformed the competing approaches on all datasets in terms of F1-measure. In particular, our approach was able to cope with the imbalance in the datasets and the different events underlying duplicates very well. In contrast, for the example of INSUR1, the unsupervised competing approaches only yielded F1-measures of 0.3099 (ECM) and 0.1034 (KMeans). This can mainly be attributed to the fact that the unsupervised approaches classified a very large number of pairs of data as duplicates, resulting in poor values for precision. Regarding the supervised competing approaches, Febrl (optimal threshold), Python RLTK (naive Bayes), and Microsoft AutoML struggled to identify a large proportion of duplicates contained in INSUR1. Further, the commercially available tool, despite being optimized for person master data, only achieved a low F1-measure of 0.1599 on INSUR1. AWS AutoGluon was able to achieve an F1-measure of 0.8797 on INSUR1, which is close to our approach. Yet, for other datasets, the differences in F1-measure are much larger. This may be an indication that the competing supervised approaches miss a large proportion of duplicates due to the misconception of duplicates as a homogeneous group, neglecting the patterns associated with the different underlying duplicate-related events.

Focusing on the example of INSUR1, KDE with additional data correctly identified 79.6% of duplicates attributed to the events relocation, marriage, or a combination of both. This underlines that our approach can identify duplicates caused not only by (simple) typos but also by complex events and their associated patterns. Indeed, considerably different patterns for the different events were recognized and used: For instance, in the product data domain, a pattern reflected that duplicates attributed to price changes exhibited high similarities between the values of all features except the feature Price.

As indicated by the higher F1-measures for KDE with additional data on INSUR1 and INSUR2, the inclusion of additional data can improve the detection of duplicates. Likewise, the inclusion of semantic similarities based on the large language model BERT resulted in improved classification performance for BIKES, BOOKS, and COSMETICS. This highlights the flexible and straightforward use of current language models as an advantage of our approach. These results support the performance of our approach with respect to classification in duplicates and non-duplicates (E2).

## (E3) Performance With Respect to Transferring Already Learned Models

As often cited in the literature and faced in many contexts (Binette & Steorts, 2022; Christen, 2007), there is usually only (very) little high-quality labeled training data available. Therefore, it is important that supervised approaches for duplicate detection are transferable between different application settings (especially within the same domain). In this line, we demonstrate the transfer of models in multiple settings: (1) from INSUR1 to INSUR2, (2) from INSUR1 to CENSUS, and (3) within the datasets of the products domain. We first focus on (1) and (2) and the models trained using KDE on INSUR1.

For (1) INSUR2, the transfer of a KDE model trained on INSUR1 resulted in an F1-measure of 0.7118, and 121 of the 148 duplicates were correctly detected (see Table 8). In contrast, when trained on INSUR1, competing supervised duplicate detection approaches and automated machine learning approaches were hardly able to detect any duplicates (i.e., 3 (optimal threshold), 7 (naive Bayes), 11 (AWS AutoGluon), and 0 (Microsoft AutoML) correctly identified duplicates). Thus, these approaches were not able to generalize from INSUR1 to detect duplicates in INSUR2. This is unexpected since AWS AutoGluon performed well on INSUR1 for all duplicate-related events. However, as the automated machine learning approaches represent black boxes, one cannot easily infer why they are not able to generalize between different datasets. Regarding (2), KDE, trained only on labeled instances from INSUR1 and applied to CENSUS, also yielded a high F1-measure (0.8449) and outperformed all competing supervised approaches. Even when compared to results reported in the literature based on training with the gold standard labels from CENSUS, the F1-measures achieved by KDE trained on INSUR1 are competitive: Christen (2008a) reported an F1-measure of 0.808, and Koumarelas et al. (2020b) reported 0.80. The most recent results reported by Loster et al. (2021) refer to an F1-measure of 0.86-0.87 using 80% training data (i.e., equivalent to the amount of training data of a fivefold cross-validation) and an F1-measure of 0.76-0.77 using 20% training data (F1-measures extracted from graphs using WebPlotDigitizer). Please note that these approaches, while highly relevant, could not be evaluated on other datasets because their implementations are not publicly available.

To further investigate why 52 duplicates from CENSUS were not correctly classified by KDE trained on INSUR1, we performed a detailed failure analysis. We found that many of these duplicates were caused by events not apparent in INSUR1, especially abbreviations of first names (e.g., “J” and “Jeffrey”) and changes of the first names associated with a gender change (e.g., “Angela” and “Mike”). Thus, the model trained on INSUR1 and transferred to CENSUS had not learned these events and their patterns, resulting in false negatives. We therefore conducted an iteration, labeling 24 training instances that were duplicates caused by these events. This could be done quickly, with little manual effort. Then, we performed a fivefold cross-validation, where for each fold we used INSUR1 as well as the newly labeled data contained in the fold (so that training data and test data did not intersect). Table 8, row “KDE (after one Iteration)” shows that with little labeling effort, the results of a transferred model of our approach can be further improved. This case demonstrates that even if only a subset of duplicate-related events can be determined right away, it is possible to apply the approach with this subset, detect duplicates caused by these events, and improve the results in further iterations.

## Applicability Check

To assess the ecological validity of our research in terms of (1) importance, (2) accessibility, and (3) suitability, we conducted an applicability check with practitioners. We followed the approach of Rosemann and Vessey (2008) and consulted other studies for reference (Li et al., 2020; Lukyanenko et al., 2019). Overall, we recruited a total of 12 data management and/or data analysis practitioners for three focus groups of four participants each. For the focus groups, we designed a set of 10 open-ended interview questions. During the focus group interviews, we presented the prepared materials before initiating a discussion of the 10 questions. Each focus group interview lasted approximately 75 minutes and was recorded and transcribed. In a nutshell, the key findings from the focus group interviews are as follows.

Regarding (1) importance, all practitioners shared the opinion that duplicate detection is of very high relevance. While practitioners also pointed out that duplicates do not always need to be merged and cleaned up, they still emphasized that it is important to be aware of their existence. In this context, a senior business analyst from the insurance industry shared a striking example of how digital transformation projects in CRM can fail if a consistent and comprehensive view of the customer cannot be provided. Here, the duplication of customer accounts made it impossible to provide customers with a complete view of their contracts. As a result, a new platform could not be launched, underscoring the importance of recognizing that single real-world persons have multiple customer accounts, even when direct merging of duplicates is not desired. The discussions further showed that duplicates have a negative impact in a variety of contexts, such as customer data, supplier data, materials management data, product data, accounting data, IoT data, and documents containing textual data. Further, the practitioners discussed a wide range of consequences of undetected duplicates, with erroneous decision-making, additional costs, and technical performance issues being the most prominent. Moreover, they disclosed that their companies already conduct initiatives to detect duplicates, mostly manually, but partially also toolsupported. Still, the majority of respondents stated the need for considerable improvement.

With respect to (2) accessibility, all practitioners emphasized that they found the proposed approach for duplicate detection comprehensible (“Yes, I found it well understandable”). The majority also expressed that they would be interested in applying the approach themselves (“I can also imagine using it for vehicle data”). Moreover, the practitioners were optimistic that duplicate detection can be grounded on an event-driven approach and saw potential further use cases for event-driven approaches in data (quality) management.

Regarding (3) suitability, the practitioners were convinced that event-driven duplicate detection would make sense for the data they deal with, including customer data, but also other kinds of data, such as materials management data and product data. Discussed events were manifold and included changes of product names, changes of products (e.g., machines, robots) after repair, typos, inconsistent data capturing, erroneous updates, data integration, data migration, and data transformation. Furthermore, the practitioners considered it feasible to identify at least the majority of events in a real use case. Indeed, there are several ways to identify events, including analysis of the data itself and examination of known duplicates, interviewing responsible staff or experts, and consulting further data (e.g., about previous data integrations). For example, a process data analyst at a large construction company stated: “One would really ask the person that is responsible for the process because they know the data really well. ... They can sketch a pretty much complete picture of the events.” The practitioners also highly valued the transferability of the approach and considered it promising to alleviate the high effort currently required when preparing data for duplicate detection.

To summarize, the discussions supported the (1) importance, (2) accessibility, and (3) suitability of our approach. Indeed, the discussions showed that duplicate detection is considered a highly relevant problem in various business contexts, with a significant need for support from novel approaches. Accessibility was supported, as the practitioners found the proposed approach comprehensible and were interested in using it despite only a brief explication. Finally, the practitioners considered our approach suitable for duplicate detection in different business contexts and highly valued its transferability.

## Discussion, Implications, and Conclusion

Duplicates are an important and intensively discussed topic in both research and practice. We present an event-driven approach to determining duplicate probabilities for pairs of data. Existing approaches do not analyze the events that cause duplicates, which affects their performance and prevents deeper insights into the generation of duplicates. Our approach is based on a sound theoretical foundation to determine the probability of a pair of data being a duplicate, explicitly models duplicate-related events, and does not rely on limiting assumptions. The evaluation on seven datasets shows that our approach is able to outperform existing approaches. Furthermore, it demonstrates the performance of our approach in the absence of dataset-specific training data when transferring already learned models to new duplicate detection settings. Our work has several implications for research and practice.

First, our work provides a sound theoretical foundation for duplicate detection based on formal and precise definitions. In particular, our research is grounded on definitions of (intrinsic) DQ as well as entities and data representations, taking an objectualist view (Fine, 1982). Our work also formally defines a representation function that maps each data representation to the corresponding entity it represents. This allows for a careful definition of duplicates, incorporating and reconciling existing definitions proposed by research on probabilistic duplicate detection, mainly based on the seminal work of Fellegi and Sunter (1969). Previous research has not focused on the causes of duplicates when detecting them, leading to a misconception of duplicates as a homogeneous group of pairs of data. In contrast, we explicitly consider and model duplicate-related events that cause duplicates. While it is usually unknown whether and which duplicate-related events actually occurred, they leave traces of their occurrence that can be used to recognize specific patterns in the data. These patterns allow for the careful detection of duplicates. Contrary to the assumption of a homogeneous group of duplicates, these patterns can differ substantially depending on the underlying event. Thus, our work enables a deeper understanding of duplicates and their potential causes.

Second, the grounding of our approach on duplicate-related events and their associated data patterns enables a promising novel way to detect duplicates. This is supported by the strong performance of our approach and the fact that it was able to detect duplicates formerly hidden to existing approaches. Indeed, as a further analysis of the evaluation results shows, existing approaches and our approach succeed in detecting duplicates when there are only minor differences between the data (e.g., caused by typos). However, our approach can also detect duplicates exhibiting patterns where certain feature values differ substantially (e.g., address-related features for relocations), which are hard to identify using existing methods. Consequently, our foundation constitutes a valuable starting point to enhance existing duplicate detection approaches by accounting for duplicate-related events. Furthermore, the identification of duplicate-related events (e.g., based on data analysis and staff interviews) was also assessed to be feasible in practice (see the Applicability Check section). Moreover, since our approach provides an indication regarding the causes of duplicates, this information can be used to reduce or avoid duplicates associated with these events in the future (e.g., by adopting data acquisition processes to account for possible duplicates caused by mishearing or poor data formatting). Likewise, when resolving a duplicate, it is beneficial to know whether the duplicate is caused by, for example, a relocation or a typo: While in the case of a relocation, the data representation stored last is more likely to represent the current (correct) state of the customer in the real world, in the case of a typo, it is not clear whether it occurred during the initial or the second data acquisition. Indeed, practitioners in our applicability check shared that knowing about the events that cause duplicates would allow them to optimize processes to prevent the creation of further duplicates.

Third, based on probability theory, the result of our approach is directly interpretable as the probability of a pair of data being a duplicate. This is in contrast to existing approaches that provide either binary assignments (e.g., Koumarelas et al.,

2020b) or score values (e.g., Xu et al., 2019), both of which can be difficult to use in practice: Binary assignments, on the one hand, cannot represent the actual uncertainty associated with duplicate detection and do not provide a measure of the degree of confidence with respect to the classification into duplicates and non-duplicates; thus, they express a certainty that does not exist. Score values, on the other hand, are typically neither interval-scaled nor bounded from above and are thus hard to interpret and evaluate. Our results in terms of probabilities offer a clear, meaningful interpretation and can be evaluated directly in terms of reliability (see the Evaluation section). Further, these probabilities can be used for decisionmaking and can be directly included in a decision calculus. For instance, the decision threshold for taking action to resolve a possible duplicate (e.g., by contacting a customer) can be calculated based on the duplicate probability, the average cost of contacting a customer, and the estimated cost of potentially not resolving a duplicate, which is hardly possible with binary assignments or score values. Indeed, practitioners in our applicability check saw value in the fact that our approach yields interpretable probabilities instead of binary decisions. For example, an IT consultant concluded: “Also because compared to other duplicate detection approaches it is much more meaningful, not least due to the probability you get.”

Fourth, models, once trained to detect duplicates based on duplicate-related events and data patterns, can be transferred to other datasets within the same domain without further labeling or repeated model learning. Indeed, our evaluation shows that models learned by our approach yield consistently strong performance across different datasets from the same domain. This further supports our general idea and theoretical foundation, as it turns out that the patterns associated with duplicate-related events remain robust within a domain. The opportunity of such a transfer also evidently provides significant advantages in practice, which was supported by the practitioners in our applicability check. Indeed, transferability reduces or removes the need for labeling and repeated model learning, thus saving time and effort. This could lead to the creation and deployment of already evaluated learned models to detect duplicates per domain, which could directly or with minor adaptations be transferred to other datasets from the respective domain. This would enable a straightforward application of the approach to a wide range of domains in practice and could also lead to a deeper understanding of duplicate-related events. On this basis, scientific and public “libraries” of learned models could be established in the future. For example, such a library would allow for the successive discovery of duplicate-related events and their patterns within each domain. Moreover, comparing events across domains could also lead to new insights regarding their generalizability (i.e., certain events might occur in several domains while others may be domain-specific). Existing approaches for duplicate detection could, on this basis, be compared regarding their ability to uncover duplicates resulting from different duplicate-related events, allowing for a reproducible evaluation, which is necessary for wellfounded duplicate detection in practice but often lacking in the literature (Christophides et al., 2020). Finally, such a library could be used to explore common structures in patterns associated with duplicate-related events within and even across domains. Here, strategies from model-based transfer learning could be used to uncover these common structures (Zhuang et al., 2021).

Finally, from a practical perspective, our approach needs to be economically feasible. There is a large body of literature on economically oriented DQ management that focuses on the economic feasibility, efficiency, and benefit vs. cost aspects of DQ and addresses this topic generally or for specific use cases or settings (Batini & Scannapieco, 2016; Timmerman et al., 2023; Valencia-Parra et al., 2021). Here, benefits and costs are weighed accounting for possible risks, while benefits, costs, and risks are highly context-specific. Following such typically context-specific economic considerations and to provide a first step towards analyzing the economic feasibility of our approach, we have quantified the effort (in person-days) for applying our approach based on our experience with the INSUR1 and INSUR2 datasets. Our approach could be applied to a dataset from a new domain (i.e., INSUR1) within 13 person-days, and an existing model could be transferred to another dataset (i.e., transfer from INSUR1 to INSUR2) in only 3 person-days. Based on this analysis of needed effort, as well as the statements of the experts in the focus group interviews, we are very confident that the application of our approach is economically viable in many contexts.

Our work also has limitations that provide a starting point for future research. First, our theoretical foundation is grounded on duplicate-related events causing duplicates. In our evaluation, we identified these events for seven different datasets, mainly based on a manual review by data analysts and domain knowledge. Future research should thus explore ways to identify duplicate-related events in an automated manner. Second, our research demonstrates that current language models can be helpful for duplicate detection based on unstructured data (see the results of KDE with language model) but did not further analyze under what circumstances which specific language model is useful. Thus, we encourage future research to explore ways to identify potentially useful language models in a systematic way. Finally, while the paper at hand is grounded on the theoretical foundation of duplicate detection based on the definition of DQ, it does not explore links to other DQ dimensions in detail. For instance, a duplicate attributed to relocation provides the additional information that the address of the more recent data representation is more likely to be current (i.e., the older representation may be the outdated one).

This reveals a currency defect that would be hard to identify without a duplicate detection approach that examines duplicaterelated events. Thus, our work may open doors for future research to study such links between duplicate detection and other DQ dimensions. Finally, future research might explore whether event-driven approaches are also beneficial for assessing these other DQ dimensions by generalizing the findings of our work.

## Appendix C

In the proposed formalism, each similarity vector belongs to exactly one of the data patterns $Z _ { 1 } , \dots , Z _ { L }$ . In this line, a finite set of data patterns has to be determined. One way is that experts recognize patterns based on (dis)similarities. For example, experts may acknowledge that duplicates caused by the event relocation typically have matching first and last names, while the street differs and the ZIP code may differ depending on whether the relocation is within the same city or not. Based on such considerations, training data can be used to (manually) determine thresholds for the (Jaro-Winkler) similarity for each feature. In this way, for example, the following two patterns (representing a typical case of a relocation of a person between two different cities or within one city) can be defined:

$$
\text { Pattern } Z _ {1} \text { if: } \quad \text { sim(First   Name) } = 1, \text { sim(Last   Name) } = 1, \text { sim(Date   of   Birth) } = 1, \text { sim(Street) } <   0. 8, \text { and } \text { sim(ZIP   Code) } <   0. 8
$$

$$
\text { Pattern } Z _ {2} \text { if: } \quad \text { sim(First   Name) } = 1, \text { sim(Last   Name) } = 1, \text { sim(Date   of   Birth) } = 1, \text { sim(Street) } <   0. 8, \text { and } \text { sim(ZIP   Code) } \geq 0. 8
$$

Identifying appropriate data patterns algorithmically has the advantage of alleviating the need for manually setting thresholds to construct the multidimensional intervals.
