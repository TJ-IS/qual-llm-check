# ALGORITHMIC SOCIAL INJUSTICES: ANTECEDENTS AND MITIGATIONS<sup>1</sup>

Hüseyin Tanriverdi and John-Patrick Olatunji Akinyemi McCombs School of Business, University of Texas at Austin Austin, TX, U.S.A. {huseyin.tanriverdi@mccombs.utexas.edu} {johnpatrick.akinyemi@mccombs.utexas.edu}

A key assumption in data science is that the fairness of an algorithm depends on its accuracy. Antecedents that create accuracy problems are expected to reduce fairness and cause algorithmic social injustices. We theorize why complexities in ground truths, IT ecosystems, and statistical models of algorithms can also generate algorithmic social injustices, above and beyond the indirect effects of antecedents, through the mediation of accuracy problems. We also theorize technology design and organizational mitigation mechanisms for taming such complexities and reducing algorithmic social injustices. We tested the proposed theory in a sample of 363 matched pairs of problematic and problem-free algorithms. We found that complexities in ground truths affected algorithmic social injustices directly rather than through the mediation of accuracy problems. Failures in complex IT ecosystems of algorithms did not affect the likelihood of algorithmic social injustices, but they caused damage directly and indirectly through the mediation of accuracy problems. Failures in complex statistical models significantly increased algorithmic social injustices both directly and indirectly through the mediation of accuracy problems. The results indicate that agentic algorithms produce social injustices not only through accuracy problems but also through complexities in their ground truths, IT ecosystems, and statistical models. The proposed complexity taming mechanisms are effective in reducing algorithmic social injustice risks through (1) the user organization’s quality in managing the algorithm’s stakeholders, (2) designing algorithms with a large scope of human-like interaction capabilities, (3) the developer organization’s algorithmic risk mitigations, and (4) the user organization’s algorithmic risk mitigations.

Keywords: Machine learning, artificial intelligence, algorithm, complexity, social injustices, bias, accuracy, ground truth, multiple ground truths, mitigation, control, irreducible uncertainty

## Introduction

Injustice anywhere is a threat to justice everywhere. – Martin Luther King, Jr., 1964

Social injustice—defined as the condition where people have unequal access to valued resources, services, and positions in society (Kerbo, 2012)—has become increasingly prevalent in digital technologies. Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) algorithms promise to reduce social injustices (Purdy, 2020). However, some AI and ML algorithms have unintentionally learned, reproduced, and amplified them, aggravating social injustices ( U.S. Congress, 2022; Eubanks,

2018). Understanding the root causes of social injustices in AI and ML algorithms is crucial for designing interventions that reduce both their likelihood and impact. Identifying effective mitigations for these injustices is essential not only for fostering trust in algorithms but also for achieving business goals and contributing to the broader societal objective of reducing social injustices. Thus, we study: (1) antecedents of algorithmic social injustices, and (2) organizational mitigations for algorithmic social injustices.

An algorithm is considered intelligent if it learns from patterns in big data and dynamically adjusts its behaviors and outcomes (Benbya et al., 2021). Algorithmic social injustice, also referred to as “algorithmic bias” or “algorithmic unfairness,” is defined as an algorithm’s systematic and unfair treatment of certain individuals or groups (e.g., protected classes) in favor of others when allocating access to valued resources, services, and opportunities in society (Friedman & Nissenbaum, 1996) without a justified reason for such unequal access (Kordzadeh & Ghasemaghaei, 2022).

A common assumption in data science is that accuracy problems reduce fairness and cause algorithmic social injustices (De‐Arteaga et al., 2022). Accordingly, mitigations for algorithmic social injustices focus on statistical methods for addressing accuracy and fairness problems. However, as the literature review in Appendix Table A1 shows, the link between accuracy and fairness is not straightforward. Some studies suggest a trade-off, where higher accuracy comes at the expense of fairness (Gardner et al., 2022; Liang et al., 2022; Valdivia et al., 2021), while others indicate that both can be improved simultaneously (Friedler et al., 2019; Rodolfa et al., 2021).

Another assumption is that accuracy issues can mediate the fairness effects of other factors. For example, accuracy problems may arise from (1) issues in the ground truth (GT) data used for training, (2) glitches in the IT systems that collect data, or (3) models failing to represent complex decisionmaking processes adequately (De‐Arteaga et al., 2022). The accuracy problems can, in turn, lead to algorithmic social injustices. However, the mediating role of accuracy in fairness outcomes has yet to be theorized and tested. The lack of a clear theoretical framework and empirical research on the relationships between antecedents, accuracy problems, and algorithmic social injustices limits organizations’ ability to design effective mitigations. We aim to address these gaps.

Intelligent algorithms are agentic systems (Baird & Maruping, 2021). They operate as part of complex adaptive sociotechnical ecosystems that present fundamental, irreducible uncertainty, which is distinct from reducible uncertainty because of the lack of information and irreducible statistical error due to randomness. Fundamental uncertainty is a property of complex systems whose smart agents interact in nonlinear ways to produce emergent, unpredictable behaviors (McDaniel et al., 2003). Randomness, i.e., the lack of pattern or predictability in events, can be quantified with probabilities and modeled statistically. In contrast, fundamental uncertainty cannot be quantified or modeled. Randomness is a “known unknown,” whereas fundamental uncertainty is an “unknown unknown” (Daniel & Daniel, 2018). Statistical mitigations designed to address known unknowns in fairness and accuracy problems are inadequate for addressing unknown unknowns arising from complexities in algorithms’ ground truths, IT ecosystems, and statistical models. If unaddressed, unknown unknowns can create algorithmic social injustices. This motivates us to theorize organizational mitigations for taming complexities in GTs, IT ecosystems, and statistical models of intelligent algorithms.

In theorizing the antecedents, we build on complexity science to explain how and why complexities in an algorithm’s GT, IT ecosystem, and statistical model are likely to increase algorithmic social injustice risks. In theorizing the mitigations, we use three taming principles from complexity science (Clarke & Stewart, 1997). We explain how the following complexity taming mechanisms can reduce algorithmic social injustices: (1) improving the quality of relationships among the algorithm’s stakeholders, (2) aligning the goals of the stakeholders, and (3) increasing the stakeholders’ capacity to take joint action.

We tested our theory in a sample of 363 matched pairs of algorithms. Each pair contained one algorithm that experienced a big enough problem to be noticed and publicly discussed by the algorithm’s stakeholders and a matching algorithm whose characteristics were similar to those of the problematic algorithm but whose stakeholders did not report any major problems.

The results on the antecedents indicate that intelligent algorithms produce social injustices directly through fundamental uncertainties arising from the complexities in their GTs, IT ecosystems, and statistical models. The indirect effects through the mediation of accuracy problems receive partial support for model failure only. Specifically, the lack of a well-established GT or the multiplicity of competing GTs can increase algorithmic social injustice risks directly by creating complexities and fundamental uncertainties for algorithms’ learning processes and decision tasks. Complexities in IT ecosystems of algorithms do not directly affect the likelihood of algorithmic social injustices. But they lead to IT failures, which cause damage both directly and through the mediation of accuracy problems. Failures in statistical models due to complexity increase the likelihood, scope, and impact of algorithmic social injustices both directly and through the mediation of accuracy problems.

As for results on the mitigations, designing an algorithm to have a large scope of human-like interaction capabilities (vision, speech, cognition, emotion, and other sensory capabilities) significantly reduces the likelihood, scope, and impact of algorithmic social injustices. Beyond such technology design, both user organizations’ quality in managing stakeholders of algorithms and user and developer organizations’ respective organizational mitigations for algorithm risks significantly reduce the likelihood and impact of algorithmic social injustices.

## Background on Structural Inequalities and Fairness In AI

Crenshaw’s intersectional approaches, Bourdieu’s social capital theory, Giddens’s structuration theory, and social stratification and class theories from Weber to Durkheim to Marx, all acknowledge an unequal distribution of resources between people (Morrison, 2006). There are structural inequalities along group characteristics such as gender, race, religion, socioeconomic status, etc. Throughout history, class inequalities have stemmed from economic differences in wealth (Engels & Wischnewetzky, 1845), with close associations between race and class (Du Bois, 1935). Some inequalities emerged in the era of colonialism when colonizers exploited the colonized region’s resources and imposed their religion, economic and cultural practices, and languages onto the colonized people (Zajko, 2021). In the 18th century, Linnaeus (1758) developed a taxonomy classifying people into four races based on physical, ethnic, and geographical differences, a classification that has evolved into today’s notion of race (Blumenbach, 1969). Given the deep tradition of historical and structural inequalities in society (Payne et al., 2019), today’s technological social inequalities likely reflect the existing structural inequalities, at least in part (Kennedy et al., 2021; Martin, 2019).

## Digital Technology and Social Inequality

People’s unequal access to valued resources, services, and societal positions also manifests in the design and usage of digital technologies. Designers of digital technologies have been predominantly white males (Brown, 2014). Underrepresentation of other races and genders has led to underrepresentation of the excluded groups’ values, needs, and preferences in digital technologies, thus reinforcing the existing inequalities. The digital divide literature has identified a connection between unequal access to digital devices and economic inequality, referring to it as the first-level digital divide (van Deursen & van Dijk, 2019), in contrast to the second-level digital divide, which is associated with a lack of technological competencies and difficulties using digital technologies (van Dijk, 2020). Further, datasets used by algorithms do not adequately represent members of protected classes who may have a limited presence in the digital world. While mainstream groups present data oceans, excluded and protected classes are “data deserts” for algorithms.

## Fairness in AI

The foregoing review implies that people’s lack of access to algorithms’ ecosystems (e.g., digital technologies, data, developers, interface, and interaction capabilities) can unfairly constrain or undermine some groups’ meaning-making capacities. Moreover, gaps in the collective interpretive resources of some groups can give other groups an unfair advantage (Fricker, 2007). Interpreting algorithms’ fairness or social justice requires an understanding of what it means for an algorithm to be fair (Friedler et al., 2021). There are three barriers to addressing this question. First, there are many definitions of fairness, and there is no consensus across and even within different ontological approaches (Mehrabi et al., 2021). Second, the many fairness metrics that data scientists use are incompatible (Hutchinson & Mitchell, 2019). Third, there are computational limits to developing fair algorithms in certain settings (Teodorescu et al., 2021). We organize the complex landscape of fairness notions under three disciplinary notions.

Statistical notions of fairness: The data science community uses statistical notions of fairness. In a review of over 140 papers, Mehrabi et al. (2021) found that each definition can be described as giving similar predictions to similar individuals, treating different groups equally, or obtaining the best properties of groups’ and individuals’ fairness notions. The community delineates bias and discrimination and demonstrates how each is a source of unfairness (Hutchinson & Mitchell, 2019). Yet there are situations where discrimination, defined as human prejudice based on sensitive attributes, is justified and explained by attributes specific to the context (Mehrabi et al., 2021). Fairness is addressed by evaluating the handling of protected attributes with statistical criteria: e.g., demographic parity, equalized odds and opportunity, and fairness through unawareness (Hutchinson & Mitchell, 2019; Teodorescu et al., 2021).

Procedural and distributive notions of fairness: Organizational scientists and economists focus on procedural and distributive notions of fairness. Procedural fairness refers to the consistency, neutrality, precision, revocability, and representativeness of the procedure used by an algorithm to produce its decisions (Marcinkowski et al., 2020; Morse et al., 2022). Distributive fairness refers to how algorithmic decisions optimize equal benefits for different stakeholders and distribute scarce resources equitably among them (Morse et al., 2022).

Stakeholder notions of fairness: In all notions of fairness reviewed, the subjective perceptions and judgments of an algorithm’s stakeholders ultimately play a key role in the evaluation of whether an algorithm’s decisions treat the stakeholders fairly or not (Morse et al., 2022). Thus, we adopt stakeholders’ perceptions of algorithmic fairness. Building on stakeholder theory, we define the salient stakeholder of an algorithm as any group or individual who affects or is affected by the achievement of an algorithm’s objectives (Mitchell et al., 1997).

## Theoretical Foundations and Hypotheses

Intelligent algorithms are agentic information systems that have the capacity to learn, adapt, and act autonomously (Baird & Maruping, 2021). We build on complexity science in viewing agentic algorithms as complex adaptive systems (CAS). An agentic algorithm’s CAS is made up of various connected and interdependent sociotechnical elements. Sensory information technologies, IT infrastructures, and IT applications collect big data on user behaviors. Statistical and computational models of algorithms process big data inputs. Users and other stakeholders generate big data inputs for algorithms with their dynamically changing behaviors. Algorithms learn from the big data inputs to dynamically update their learning and decision rules. The learning processes transfer the rights and responsibilities of human agents to algorithms or vice versa (Baird & Maruping, 2021). By learning human agency, algorithms become agentic IS that can make decisions autonomously, even in unstructured situations. The interactions among the agentic elements are reciprocal, nonlinear, and produce fundamental uncertainties that lead to unexpected, unpredictable outcomes (Anderson, 1999; Holland, 1995). Even if managers have perfect knowledge of the past states of the CAS, they cannot form subjective probabilities or relative frequencies for future states due to unpredictable behaviors and nonlinear interactions of the autonomous agents (Daniel & Daniel, 2018).

We build on these ideas to explain how complexities in GTs, IT ecosystems, and statistical models drive algorithmic social injustice risks. Risk is defined as the likelihood and impact of loss (Tanriverdi & Ruefli, 2004). Thus, we define algorithmic social injustice risk as the likelihood and impact of an algorithm’s systematic and unfair treatment of certain individuals or groups (Friedman & Nissenbaum, 1996).

## Ground Truth Status and Algorithmic Social Injustice Risks

Ground truth is the sum of all data collected, checked, and labeled in the context of a specific decision task for training an algorithm on how to make decisions on that task and calibrating its decision criteria, rules, and boundaries (C3.ai, 2022; Kozyrkov, 2021; Lexico, 2021).

Some algorithms have decision tasks with no well-established GTs or stakeholder groups and hold conflicting GTs about the decision tasks. Such algorithms face complexities and fundamental uncertainties in their learning processes and decision tasks. When there is a lack of a well-established GT, developers do not know or understand decision criteria, rules, or boundaries. Until the scientific process creates new knowledge to establish the GT for the decision tasks, developers cannot effectively supervise the algorithm’s learning process. They may have to resort to the use of unsupervised learning methods. Unlike supervised learning, unsupervised learning has relatively few fairness metrics. This limits developers’ control over what the algorithm learns. If the algorithm learns biases and structural inequalities in the data, developers may not notice them until after victims notice and report the biases.

Alternatively, instead of using unsupervised learning, some developers attempt to reduce fundamental uncertainties in the decision tasks by asking the crowd to label the data and create the GT. However, in the absence of well-established decision criteria, rules, and boundaries, labelers resort to their own perceptions of truth and embed their implicit biases into GT labels (Babaei et al., 2021). The algorithm learns the resulting biases and treats some stakeholder groups systematically unfairly (Lopez, 2021).

Different labeler groups who hold multiple, irreconcilable GTs (Gordon et al., 2022) also interact in unpredictable ways during the GT elicitation process. For instance, one stakeholder group may label a social media post as hate speech, while another labels it as free speech (Cinelli et al., 2021; Kitchens et al., 2020). Most machine learning approaches view labeler disagreements as noise that must be eliminated rather than as useful signals. They attempt to eliminate the “noise” by a majority vote (Schaekermann et al., 2016). In a majority vote, the largest group’s view overrides the minority groups’ views and causes the algorithm to treat the excluded minority groups unfairly.

Some algorithmic decision tasks have well-established GTs (Smyth et al., 1994). By following established GT acquisition methods, developers can acquire the GT and reduce the uncertainty faced regarding the GT. However, mistakes can still happen. If GT labels are wrong, for instance, misclassification might be the correct action, and accuracy problems can emerge (Cooper et al., 2021). Accuracy problems, in turn, can lead to algorithmic social injustices (Gardner et al., 2022; Liang et al., 2022; Valdivia et al., 2021). Thus, accuracy problems can mediate the link between the GT status and algorithmic social injustices.

H1: GT status of an algorithm affects algorithmic social injustice risks.

H1a: The lack of a well-established GT increases algorithmic social injustice risks.

H1b: Multiple conflicting GTs increase algorithmic social injustice risks.

H1c: Accuracy problems in an algorithm mediate the effect of the GT status on algorithmic social injustice risks.

## Failure in the Algorithm’s IT Ecosystem and Algorithmic Social Injustice Risks

An IT ecosystem of an intelligent algorithm refers to IT infrastructures, IT applications, and IT services that collect, process, and store big data for the algorithm. Sensory information technologies (e.g., accelerometers, microphones, cameras, LIDAR) collect input data. Communication technologies transfer the sensed data to an algorithm. Storage, security, and privacy technologies enable the algorithm to manage the data. The connections, dependencies, and interactions among such IT elements make an algorithm’s IT ecosystem complex. Complexity presents fundamental uncertainties and leads to the emergence of unexpected IT failures at the ecosystem level (Tanriverdi & Du, 2020). Some ecosystem-level IT failures affect marginalized populations disproportionally, wrongly depict them to algorithms, and amplify preexisting social injustices (Lopez, 2021).

For instance, Fitbit’s fitness tracking algorithm initially relied on optical sensors to read vital signs from a person’s skin and accelerometers to sense motion (Feehan et al., 2018). Optical sensors failed to read vital signs accurately from darker skin (Shcherbina et al., 2017). Thus, the IT failure disproportionately affected Black individuals. The victims expressed discontent about the inaccurate readings from their Fitbits and the subsequent injustices they experienced. Fitbit’s accelerometer readings were initially also inaccurate for disabled or older people who walked with a walking aid or had a disease-related mobility limitation (Feehan et al., 2018). Due to the IT failures, the fitness algorithm made systematic errors in estimating the fitness levels of darker-colored races, disabled people, and the elderly, and amplified the preexisting social inequalities against them. Emergent IT failures are properties of complex IT ecosystems, and they present fundamental uncertainties for the accuracy and fairness performance of algorithms.

H2: IT failure in an algorithm’s IT ecosystem increases algorithmic social injustice risks.

H2a: Accuracy problems in an algorithm mediate the effect of an IT failure on algorithmic social injustice risks.

## Failure in the Algorithm’s Statistical Model and Algorithmic Social Injustice Risks

We define a failure in an algorithm’s statistical model as the model’s inability to adequately represent the complex reality of a decision task. As the British statistician George Box stated, “all models are wrong, but some are useful” (Box, 1979). All statistical models of algorithms are “wrong” in the sense that they are reductionist representations of the complex realities they attempt to represent. Some models are useful because their reductionist choices might adequately represent the complex reality.

The complexity of a decision task presents fundamental uncertainties for developers. For instance, developers may not know or understand which variables should be included or excluded in the reductionist model and how the intricate interactions might work between the included and excluded variables and affect outcomes. Such fundamental uncertainties can lead to discrepancies between the complex reality and its modeled version in the algorithm and create new social injustices.

For instance, in Idaho, Medicaid used an algorithm to automate health benefits allocation to individuals with a disability. The algorithm unfairly targeted 4,000 Idahoans and cut their benefits by as much as 30% (Stanley, 2017). A classaction lawsuit was filed against the state of Idaho’s Medicaid. The court found that the algorithm produced arbitrary results for many people. In Arkansas, a similar algorithm unfairly treated Medicaid users. The algorithm’s model took into account only a fraction of a lengthy list of items that human assessors used to rely on when making resource allocation decisions: “it turned out that a small number of variables could matter enormously for some people” (Lecher, 2018). With fewer support hours, individuals with cerebral palsy had to change their lifestyles. Some family members had to quit their jobs to care for loved ones with cerebral palsy due to the algorithmic injustices their family suffered (Lecher, 2018). These are examples of new social injustices that did not exist before the adoption of the algorithm.

A few studies found associations between model failures and AI bias (Anderson et al., 2019; Buolamwini & Gebru, 2018; Obermeyer et al., 2019), but they did not test if model failure had a direct effect on bias and or an indirect effect through the mediation of accuracy problems. We hypothesize both direct and indirect effects to capture social injustices stemming, respectively, from the complexity of the modeling task and social injustices due to model accuracy problems.

H3: A failure in an algorithm’s model increases algorithmic social injustice risks.

H3a: Accuracy problems in an algorithm mediate the effect of model failure on algorithmic social injustice risks.

## Complexity Taming Principles

Complexity science offers three generic principles for taming complexity in systems: (1) improve the quality of relationships among the elements of the complex system, (2) align the goals of the elements, and (3) increase the capacity of the elements to take coordinated, system-level action (Clarke & Stewart, 1997). We build on these principles to theorize organizational mitigations for taming complexities in an agentic algorithm’s sociotechnical ecosystem.

## User Organization’s Stakeholder Management Quality and Algorithmic Social Injustices

Algorithmic decisions potentially affect vast numbers of stakeholders who hold diverse sets of values, interests, and objectives. User organizations can potentially focus on the most salient stakeholder groups to tame complex dynamics among the stakeholders served. A salient stakeholder is defined as one with a power, legitimacy, or urgency relationship with an algorithm (Mitchell et al., 1997).

Salient stakeholders can have a variety of moral values and objectives, which may be difficult to reconcile or irreconcilable. To improve the quality of relationships among salient stakeholders, the user organization must proactively seek compromises. If an algorithm emphasizes the objectives and preferences of one group of salient stakeholders (e.g., men only, whites only, non-disabled users only, or right-handed people only), then algorithmic decisions could become less inclusive of the needs, preferences, and values of the other stakeholder groups (e.g., females, non-white race groups, disabled people, left-handed people, etc.). The excluded groups can perceive the algorithm’s decisions to be unfair against themselves. User organizations can mitigate such algorithmic injustice risks by improving stakeholder relationships. We build on stakeholder theory to define the quality of stakeholder management as the extent to which a user organization of an algorithm: (1) creates value with and for an algorithm’s salient stakeholders, (2) seeks to understand their social and political surroundings, (3) learns with and from them to create value opportunities, and (4) takes an integrative view of salient stakeholders by focusing on their interconnections (Freeman et al., 2017).

The quality of stakeholder management can satisfy the complexity of science principle of improving the quality of relationships among elements of a complex system. Discussing the diverse moral values, objectives, and decision criteria of the salient stakeholders and jointly seeking compromises can allow an algorithm to update its learning to remain inclusive of the salient stakeholders and make decisions that do not unfairly treat any of them.

H4: User organization’s stakeholder management quality reduces algorithmic social injustice risks.

## Algorithm’s Scope of Human-Like Interaction Capabilities and Algorithmic Social Injustices

Algorithms often face a complex external environment comprising a broad spectrum of disabled and non-disabled users. Whether an algorithm’s interaction capabilities are exclusive or inclusive of the broad spectrum of users could affect algorithmic social injustice risks. For instance, many chatbots provide only text-based interactions. They exclude disabled users who cannot use keyboards or touchscreens. According to complexity science, an organization needs to increase its internal complexity to match the external complexity (Page, 2010). Developers can accommodate a broad spectrum of disabled and non-disabled users in the external environment by endowing an algorithm with a broader scope of human-like interaction capabilities such as vision, speech, emotion, cognition, and other sensory capabilities such as touch, smell, and taste (Lake et al., 2017). In the chatbot example, endowing a chatbot with speech or vision capabilities can increase the chatbot’s inclusivity of disabled users and reduce those users’ perceptions of systematic and unfair treatment. For example, the famous physicist Stephen Hawking, who suffered from amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), gradually lost voluntary control of muscles and speech, severely limiting his ability to interact with computer systems. However, algorithms that offered touch, speech, vision, and eye-movement-based interaction capabilities were inclusive of him. He used a thumb switch and a blink switch attached to his glasses to control his computer. By squeezing his cheek muscles and “blinking,” he was able to scan and select characters on the screen to compose speeches, surf the internet, send emails, and “speak” through a voice synthesizer (Access Computing, 2021). Designing a broad scope of human-like interaction capabilities can accommodate the needs of a broad spectrum of disabled and non-disabled users and reduce algorithmic social injustice risks (Costanza-Chock, 2020).

H5: A large scope of human-like capabilities reduces algorithmic social injustice risks.

## Developer Organization’s Governance and Controls and Algorithmic Social Injustice Risks

We define organizational governance and controls as a set of organizational structures, roles, policies, and procedures designed to reduce organizational risks (Tanriverdi & Du, 2020). In response to the proliferation of algorithmic problems such as social inequality, various organizational governance and control mechanisms have been proposed to mitigate algorithmic risks. Some examples include board-level oversight of algorithmic risks (Silverman, 2020); algorithmic transparency (Hosanagar & Jair, 2018); algorithmic accountability (U.S. Congress, 2022); algorithmic audits (Guszcza et al., 2018; Morse et al., 2022); the use of FACT (fairness, accuracy, confidentiality, and transparency) principles by data science teams (RDS, 2022; van der Aalst et al., 2017); the diversity of data science team members’ backgrounds, experiences, and perspectives (Moore et al., 2021); and governance and controls over the inputs, processing logic, and outputs of algorithms (Babel et al., 2019). However, their effectiveness has not been validated.

A developer organization of an algorithm is an organization that develops algorithms for internal use or sale to user organizations. Developer organizations often develop a portfolio of algorithms. For example, Google has developed a rich portfolio of algorithms, including advertising, searchmatching, recommender, content moderation, photos, autonomous driving algorithms, etc. We frame the governance and controls of developer organizations at the level of the developer’s overall portfolio of algorithms rather than any specific algorithm.

Governance and controls can potentially reduce social inequality risks by improving the quality of relationships among stakeholders, aligning stakeholders’ goals, and building a capacity for stakeholders to take coordinated action in the design and use of algorithms. We illustrate the proposed nomological relationship by focusing on one of the proposed governance and control mechanisms, i.e., board-level oversight of algorithmic risks (Silverman, 2020).

Board-level oversight of algorithmic risks elevates the management of algorithmic risks to the highest organizational structure in the firm: i.e., the board of directors (Silverman, 2020). A board-level committee or a board member is charged with managing algorithmic risks. Many developers relegate algorithmic risk management to lower levels in the hierarchy, e.g., data science teams. This is problematic because an algorithm is a complex ecosystem with many stakeholders, including technologists, data scientists, users, regulators, the general public, etc. (Clarke & Stewart, 1997). Data scientists may not have full visibility into all stakeholders and their objectives, needs, preferences, and values. Moreover, a data science team may not know how the trade-offs among the conflicting objectives of powerful stakeholders ought to be made. The data science team may end up training an algorithm to optimize the objectives of some stakeholders at the expense of other stakeholders and thereby create social inequalities. Board-level oversight of algorithmic risks can potentially tame these challenges and reduce the likelihood of algorithmic social injustices because the board has better visibility of all stakeholders. The board also has decision rights to make tradeoffs among the conflicting objectives of the stakeholders. The board is better positioned to improve the quality of relationships among the stakeholders, balance their conflicting interests, achieve goal alignment, and foster coordinated action on algorithm design and use.

Similarly, other governance and controls can enable a developer organization to maintain high-quality relationships and goal alignment as the algorithm’s ecosystem dynamically changes and evolves. Some developers announce governance and controls symbolically and do not follow through to implement the controls substantively. Substantive governance and controls are critical for reducing algorithmic social injustice risks.

H6: Developer organization’s substantive governance and controls reduce algorithmic social injustice risks.

## User Organization’s Governance and Controls and Algorithmic Social Injustice Risks

Algorithmic social injustices often emerge in user organizations because developer organizations may not detect them during the development and test stages. A user organization is an organization that adopts an algorithm developed by a developer organization and implements it in its business operations to make decisions.

A user organization’s governance and controls at the level of a specific algorithm can also affect whether algorithmic social injustices are likely to emerge or not during the usage phase of an algorithm. We focus on whether a user organization uses the following governance and control mechanisms: (1) Is the user organization committed to the ethical use of the algorithm? (2) Is the user organization transparent about how it uses the algorithm and how the algorithm makes decisions? (3) Does the user organization accept accountability for decisions made by the algorithm? Like the logic of the developer’s governance and controls, the user’s governance and controls can increase the quality of relations among stakeholders of the user organization’s algorithm. They can align the stakeholders’ goals and enable them to take coordinated action in response to unexpected, surprising challenges emerging during the usage phase of the algorithm.

H7: The user organization’s substantive governance and control mechanisms reduce algorithmic social injustice risks.

## Methods

## Sample and Matching Criteria

We used a sample of problematic algorithms from the AI Algorithmic and Automation Incidents and Controversies (AIAAIC) repository, an independent public interest initiative managed by a team of journalists and data scientists. For each problematic algorithm, the AIAAIC repository includes news articles or other sources discussing the alleged problem. Journalists validate the credibility of the sources. Data scientists validate whether the technologies discussed in the sources are indeed algorithms that have problems. The editorial team follows a governance process and applies four criteria (relevance, impact, credibility, and volume) to distinguish between “issues,” which are just potential concerns, and “incidents,” which are problems that already happened and caused negative impact. The AIAAIC repository has been used in peer-reviewed publications (Longoni et al., 2023), industry reports (Nestor et al., 2023), and EU policy reports (Molinari et al., 2021). We supplemented this repository by searching for additional problematic algorithms in Google, Factiva, EBSCOhost, Web of Science, and Google Scholar. We addressed the limitations of the AIAAIC repository by using conceptually grounded definitions for key concepts such as intelligent algorithm and algorithmic problem types (e.g., bias, IT failure, model failure, privacy breach, cybersecurity breach).

## Inclusion Criteria

We used the following criteria to decide if a problematic algorithm in the AIAAIC repository qualified for inclusion in our study:

Algorithm: The problematic algorithm had to meet the definition of an intelligent algorithm (Benbya et al., 2021). It had to have the capability to learn from patterns in big data inputs and change its behavior over time based on changes in big data inputs.

Problem type: The problematic algorithm had to have one or more problems theorized in this study—i.e., bias, IT failure, or model failure. Although our study focused on nonmalicious operational IT failures, the repository also contained malicious IT failures, such as privacy and cybersecurity breaches in algorithms. We included malicious IT failures as controls.

Realized or potential problem: The problematic algorithm had to have a realized problem. We excluded entries that discussed potential algorithmic problems that had not yet emerged.

Usage status: When the problem emerged, the algorithm had to have been used with actual data and users during at least a pilot study or trial run, if not in full production. Entries that discussed problems detected during the ideation phases of algorithms were excluded.

Developer organization: The developer of the problematic algorithm had to be an organization. If an individual developed an algorithm, it was excluded.

User organization: The organization in which the algorithmic problem emerged had to be known. Entries that failed to specify or did not have a user organization were excluded.

Locations of user organizations: The user organization of the problematic algorithm had to be incorporated in the U.S. We excluded user organizations from international locations.

## Matching Criteria

We formed a matched pair of problematic and problem-free algorithms. For each problematic algorithm, we searched for a problem-free competitor algorithm whose characteristics were very similar within the 12-month period before the emergence of the problematic algorithm’s problem. For instance, if Google Search was the problematic algorithm, Microsoft Bing was a competitor, and we checked to see whether it satisfied the following matching criteria:

Timing of match: The matching algorithm had to be in use as of the year of the problematic algorithm’s problem emergence. All matching criteria had to be satisfied as of that year.

Problem status: The matching algorithm had to be free of bias, IT failure, model failure, privacy breach, and cybersecurity breach problems. Stakeholders of the problemfree match did not discuss any of these problems within 12 months prior to the emergence of the problematic algorithm’s problem.

Function: The model of the problem-free algorithm had to be in the same functional category as the problematic algorithm (e.g., voice assistant algorithm, recommender algorithm, search-matching algorithm, etc.).

Application domain: The problem-free match had to be in the same application domain as the problematic algorithm (e.g., insurance, healthcare, HR, social media, transportation, etc.).

On-platform/Off-platform status: The problem-free match had to have the same on-platform/off-platform status as the problematic algorithm. On-platform algorithms had to work on multisided platforms (MSP); off-platform algorithms could not operate on an MSP.

For-profit/not-for-profit status: The problem-free match’s user organization had to have the same for-profit/not-forprofit status as the problematic algorithm.

Public/private status: The user organization of the problemfree match had to have the same public/private sector status as the problematic algorithm.

Industry: The developer organization of the problem-free match had to have the same NAICS industry code as the developer of the problematic algorithm.

## Fidelity of Match

76% of pairs had an “exact” match, as the two algorithms were the same in all matching criteria. The remaining 24% had a “coarse” match, as the two algorithms met all criteria except their application domains being slightly different. For instance, the problematic algorithm was a bar examproctoring algorithm; due to the lack of a competitor in the bar exam context, we selected a very similar proctoring algorithm in the college exam context.

The final sample had 363 pairs of problematic and problemfree algorithms, i.e., 726 algorithms from 18 industry segments and 150 functional categories in the U.S. between 2006 and 2022. Explanatory and control variables were coded within the 12-month period prior to the emergence of the algorithmic problem. Table 1 summarizes the sample construction process. For illustrative purposes, Appendix Table A2 shows three pairs of matched algorithms.

## Coding Instrument Validation

We developed and validated a coding instrument for coding the study variables from the source documents. Definitions of variables were adapted from published literature or practitioner articles where no academic articles were available. Two Ph.D. students with degrees and professional experience in IS and strong training and research experience in data science served as independent expert coders who read the source documents to code the study variables by following the validated coding guidelines.

We established the reliability of the coding guidelines as follows. In each round of an iterative process, the two independent coders used the guidelines to code a small sample of algorithms (e.g., 20). We assessed the intercoder agreement rate after each episode of coding. After the first round, the agreement rate was 68%. After observing the two coders discuss the sources of coding discrepancies, it was clear that some variables were not tightly defined. We revised and tightened the definitions. After three rounds of iterations, the intercoder agreement rate increased above the 90% threshold for establishing the reliability of the coding instrument. In the coding of 726 algorithms, agreement rates between the two independent coders were 95% for the dependent and 94% for the independent variables. The remaining discrepancies were jointly discussed and resolved by the two coders. To further assess the reliability of the coding guideline, we introduced two additional independent coders (a human-computer interactions graduate student and an undergraduate information systems honors student). They coded a small sample of 32 pairs and achieved a 96% intercoder agreement rate, reinforcing the reliability of the coding guideline instrument. Appendix Table A4 provides supporting literature and definitions of the study variables.

## Dependent Variables

Risk is conceptualized in terms of likelihood and magnitude of loss (Tanriverdi & Ruefli, 2004). We used three dependent variables to measure the likelihood of algorithmic social injustices, the scope of social injustices, and the damage caused by algorithmic social injustices.

Algorithmic social injustices: Independent coders measured algorithmic social injustices with bias problems in an algorithm, i.e., by assessing whether the algorithm systematically and unfairly discriminated against certain individuals or groups of individuals in favor of others (Friedman & Nissenbaum, 1996). Federally protected classes were the target of algorithmic biases: i.e., age, sex (gender, pregnancy, sexual orientation, or gender identity), physical or mental disability, race, color, religion or creed, citizenship, national origin, or ancestry, and veteran status (Kappelman et al., 2019). As coders noted socioeconomic status as another target of algorithmic bias, we added it to the list. In source documents, coders looked for evidence that a stakeholder group claimed that the algorithm treated them systematically unfairly to reduce their access to valued resources, services, and positions. If such claims were made by one individual in isolation, it was not sufficient to conclude that the algorithm was biased. Coders triangulated the evidence by checking if two or more individuals within the same stakeholder group independently made similar allegations that the algorithm treated them systematically unfairly. After the coders measured if an algorithm had a given type of bias [1] or not [0], we took the logical OR of all types of algorithmic biases to measure whether the algorithm had a social injustice problem [1] or not [0].

Scope of social injustices: Some algorithms exhibited more than one type of algorithmic bias. We computed the total number of algorithmic biases to measure the scope of algorithmic social injustices.

## Independent Variables

Ground truth status: Independent coders evaluated the status of ground truth used to train an algorithm and calibrate its decision-making rules and boundaries before the emergence of a problem in the algorithm: no well-established GT [0], well-established GT [1], or multiple conflicting GTs [2]. Then, we used [0] as the base case and created two dummy variables to represent well-established GT [1] and multiple conflicting GTs [1], respectively. Appendix Table A5 presents the evidence used to code the algorithm’s ground truth status.

IT failure in the algorithm’s ecosystem: Independent coders read the source documents to assess if there was any evidence of an operational IT glitch or malfunction in the algorithm’s IT ecosystem prior to the emergence of the algorithmic bias [1] or not [0]. Appendix Table A5 presents the evidence used to code the IT failure variable.

Model failure in the algorithm: Data science literature attributes algorithmic biases to failures in statistical and computational models of algorithms (Mehrabi et al., 2021) (e.g., unrealistic or problematic assumptions, omission of important variables, variables that serve as proxies to protected classes, etc.). Independent coders read the source documents to assess if there was any evidence of model failure in the algorithm [1] or not [0]. Appendix Table A5 illustrates evidence used to code the model failure variable.

Stakeholder management quality: Independent coders reviewed the source documents to assess if there was evidence that the user organization: created value with and for at least 50% of the algorithm’s salient stakeholders [1] or not [0], sought to understand their social and political surroundings [1] or not [0], learned with and from them and used their criticism and feedback as value creation opportunities [1] or not [0], or took an integrative stakeholder view by focusing on the interconnections among the algorithm’s salient stakeholders [1] or not [0]. Appendix Table A6 presents the evidence used to code the newly developed stakeholder management quality scale. Cronbach’s Alpha, 0.69, is very close to the 0.70 threshold and thus satisfactory for a newly developed scale.

Algorithm’s scope of human-like capabilities: The AI community seeks to build systems that learn and think like people (Lake et al., 2017). Specifically, the AI community aims to endow machines with five human-like capabilities: (1) vision, (2) speech, (3) emotion, (4) cognition, and (5) senses such as touch, taste, etc. (Nirenburg, 2017). The independent coders first used categorical variables to measure whether each capability was present in each algorithm [1] or not [0]. Then, we computed the total number of capabilities to measure the algorithm’s scope of human-like capabilities. Appendix Table A7 presents the evidence used to code the scope of the algorithm’s human-like capabilities.

Developer organizations’ governance and controls: The independent coders reviewed the source documents of the algorithm’s developer organization to code if there was evidence indicating that the developer organization had governance and controls to mitigate risks of its portfolio of algorithms. For each mitigation, the following scale was used: no evidence of the mitigation [0], symbolic evidence of the mitigation [1], and substantive evidence of the mitigation [2]. This measurement was taken for a total of eight developer organization mitigations: (1) board-level oversight of algorithmic risks (Silverman, 2020), (2) commitment to ethical development and the use of AI (Leslie, 2019), (3) algorithmic transparency (Hosanagar & Jair, 2018), (4) algorithmic accountability (U.S. Congress, 2022), (5) algorithmic audits (Guszcza et al., 2018; Morse et al., 2022), (6) use of FACT (fairness, accuracy, confidentiality, and transparency) principles by data science teams (RDS, 2022; van der Aalst et al., 2017), (7) diversity of data science team members’ backgrounds, and perspectives (Moore et al., 2021), and (8) governance and controls over inputs, processing logic, and outputs of algorithms (Babel et al., 2019). Cronbach’s alpha for the eight items was 0.80, indicating sufficient reliability.

User organizations’ governance and controls: In line with our theory, we measured the user organization’s mitigations at the level of a specific algorithm. We used the same three-point measurement scale: no evidence of the mitigation [0], symbolic evidence of the mitigation [1], and substantive evidence of the mitigation [2]. Independent coders measured if the user organization had (1) committed to ethical use of the algorithm, (2) accepted accountability for the algorithm’s decisions, and (3) exhibited transparency about the variables the algorithm used to make decisions. Cronbach’s alpha for the three items was 0.71, indicating sufficient reliability. Appendix Table A8 presents the evidence used to code organizations’ governance and controls.

## Control Variables

To account for alternative explanations and potential endogeneity concerns, we included organizational-level and algorithm-level controls: privacy breach (present [1] or not [0]); cybersecurity breach (present [1] or not [0]); algorithm’s variety of stakeholders (count of the algorithm’s unique stakeholder groups); private/public status of the algorithm’s user organization (private [1], public [0]); for-profit/nonprofit status of the algorithm’s user organization (for profit [1], nonprofit [0]); on-platform/off-platform status of the algorithm (on-platform [1], off-platform [0]).

Algorithm’s decision support mode: We evaluated the algorithms in the sample on the level of human augmentation in the algorithm’s decision-making based on insights from Teodorescu et al. (2021). In realizing fairness in algorithm outcomes, Teodorescu et al. (2021) emphasized the importance of augmentation. If the algorithm processes a very high volume of transactions and makes a very high percentage of decisions in a short amount of time (e.g., credit card swipe approvals) and flags only a small percentage of those transactions as exceptions to be reviewed by humans (e.g., suspicion of fraud, or customer dispute), we coded it as automation [0]. We used automation as the base case and coded three additional dummy variables. If humans audited the algorithm’s recommendations, but the machine made the final decisions, we coded it as machine makes final decision [1]. If humans reviewed the algorithm’s recommendations and made the ultimate decisions for a very high percentage of the decisions, we coded it as human makes final decision [1]. Lastly, if the algorithm and humans delegated decision tasks to each other and both made non-negligible percentages of the decisions, we coded it as human-machine collaborates [1].

Industry similarity: If the developer and user organizations were in the same industry, we coded industry similarity as 1, otherwise 0.

Fairness goal: If the developer organization had set fairness as a goal for its algorithms, we coded fairness goal as 1, otherwise 0. Due to the multiplicity of fairness definitions, we only looked for whether the developer organization set fairness as a goal, regardless of the measures it might have used for fairness.

Accuracy problem measures the ratio of incorrect decisions to the total number of decisions recommended by the algorithm (Lebovitz et al., 2021; Morse et al., 2022). In the year of an algorithmic problem emergence, if the stakeholders of an algorithm were discussing any accuracy problems in the algorithm, we coded accuracy problem as 1, otherwise 0.

Fidelity of matching: If the problematic and problem-free algorithms matched exactly in all eight matching criteria, we coded fidelity of matching as 1. When the granularity of the functions or the application domains of the algorithms differed slightly but still matched, we classified it as “coarse matching” and coded fidelity of matching as 0.

## Estimation Models

The first dependent variable (DV-1), social injustice, is a dichotomous variable. Thus, we used a binary logistic model to estimate the likelihood of algorithmic social injustices. The second dependent variable (DV-2), scope of social injustice, and the third dependent variable (DV-3), damage to the user organization, are continuous variables. Thus, we used OLS regressions for them. One identification concern was organization-level unobserved heterogeneity. Some developer organizations (e.g., Google, Facebook, etc.) had multiple problematic algorithms in our sample. We clustered standard errors by unique developer organizations to account for potential unobserved heterogeneity.

Another concern was potential endogeneity. Developer and user organizations’ mitigations could correlate with the model’s error term. Both developer and user organizations may selfselect into implementing mitigations due to an omitted variable, and their decisions may subsequently affect algorithmic social injustices. This means that both variables might be endogenous and can bias estimation results. We used the instrumental variable estimation technique to address the potential endogeneity problem. A valid instrumental variable must be correlated with the endogenous variables but not directly affect the dependent variable. We used the following instrument: the first proposal of the Algorithmic Accountability Act (AAA) in the U.S. in 2019. While this proposal was not signed into law, it could have affected developer and user organizations adoption of mitigations by signaling that the government might hold companies accountable for algorithmic biases. The proposal was not likely to have had a direct effect on algorithmic social injustices. However, it could have had an indirect effect through its effects on developer and user organizations’ mitigations. Thus, we classified all problematic algorithms into two groups based on the year the social injustice emerged. If the year of emergence was on or before (vs. after) 2019, the year of the AAA, the developer, and user organizations of an algorithm would have had relatively low (vs. high) awareness of algorithmic bias risks and would have been less (vs. more) likely to implement mitigations. Because of how we matched problem-free and problematic algorithms, we classified each problem-free algorithm in the same way based on the year the social injustice emerged in the problematic algorithm. Results remained qualitatively the same in two-stage least squares (2SLS) analyses with this instrument variable.

## Results

Table 5 presents the direct effects of the hypothesized antecedents and the controls. The control for accuracy problems in algorithms significantly increased the likelihood, scope, and damage of algorithmic social injustices. Above and beyond the effects of accuracy problems, the multiplicity of GTs, IT failure, and model failure problems had additional effects on the dependent variables. Table 6 presents Sobel tests (Baron & Kenny, 1986) that assessed the mediation effects of accuracy problems between the antecedents and algorithmic social injustices.

Relative to algorithms with no well-established GTs, algorithms with well-established GTs significantly reduced the likelihood of algorithmic social injustices. This relationship was not mediated by accuracy problems in algorithms. Similarly, algorithms with multiple conflicting GTs significantly increased the likelihood and scope of algorithmic social injustices. Accuracy problems in algorithms did not mediate these relationships. The results partially support H1.

IT failures in algorithms’ IT ecosystems did not significantly affect the likelihood or scope of algorithmic social injustices, but they significantly increased the damage caused both directly and through the mediation of accuracy problems. These results partially support H2.

Model failures in algorithms had direct, significant positive effects on the likelihood, scope, and damage of algorithmic social injustices. They also had indirect effects on all three dependent variables through the mediation of accuracy problems. These results fully support H3.

As Table 5 shows, the user organization’s stakeholder management quality significantly reduced the likelihood of algorithmic social injustices and the magnitude of damage caused. These results support H4. An algorithm’s scope of human-like interaction capabilities significantly reduced the likelihood of algorithmic social injustices, the scope of social injustices, and the magnitude of damage caused to the user organization. These findings support H5. Both the developer and the user organizations’ governance and control mechanisms over algorithmic risks significantly reduced the likelihood of algorithmic social injustices and the magnitude of damage they caused. These results support H6 and H7.

## Discussion and Conclusion

This study generates new insights about the antecedent mechanisms through which algorithmic social injustices emerge. It also proposes and validates new technology design and organizational mitigation mechanisms for reducing algorithmic social injustices.

## Contributions to Research on Antecedents of Algorithmic Social Injustices

The results regarding the antecedents support our thesis that fundamental uncertainties—stemming from the complexities of GT data, IT ecosystems, and statistical models—directly contribute to algorithmic social injustices. In contrast, we found only partial support for the prevailing view that accuracy issues mediate the relationships between these antecedents and algorithmic social injustices. These findings advance IS research by identifying fundamental uncertainties as a primary cause of algorithmic social injustices, suggesting a need for new theoretical frameworks that focus on mitigating fundamental uncertainties. Current data science mitigations primarily address reducible uncertainties. IS research should complement them with organizational mitigations aimed at the fundamental uncertainties arising from the complexities of intelligent algorithms.

Contrary to the prevailing assumption, accuracy problems do not mediate the links between GT status variables and algorithmic social injustices. Instead, the absence of a wellestablished GT and the existence of multiple competing GTs have direct effects on algorithmic social injustices. These results warn IS researchers that existing data science methods for GT elicitation are inadequate for reducing algorithmic social injustices stemming from the fundamental uncertainties inherent in GT data. Until scientific advancements produce a consensus GT for a given decision task, IS researchers should refrain from developing intelligent algorithms for such tasks. Confusing fundamental uncertainties with reducible uncertainties could lead to the use of GT elicitation processes and the creation of labeled datasets for algorithm training. However, the fundamental uncertainties in these datasets can still introduce implicit biases, irreconcilable conflicts among labelers, and, ultimately, biased decision-making.

While IT failures within an algorithm’s ecosystem can lead to accuracy problems, these accuracy problems do not influence the likelihood or scope of algorithmic social injustices, nor do IT failures have a direct effect on such injustices. This challenges the prevailing view that IT failures are a primary cause of algorithmic social injustices. However, IT failures are not irrelevant. Rather, when they occur, they exacerbate the damage caused by accuracy problems, both directly and indirectly. These results call for further IS research on the governance and controls of intelligent algorithms’ IT ecosystems to ensure their reliable operation. A wellfunctioning IT ecosystem can mitigate the impacts of other antecedents that contribute to algorithmic social injustices.

Model failures, on the other hand, have both direct and indirect effects on algorithmic social injustices through the mediation of accuracy problems. This highlights that statistical models in intelligent algorithms contain both reducible and fundamental uncertainties. While data science methods can address reducible uncertainties, fundamental uncertainties remain largely unaddressed. These are the uncertainties that stem from the inherent complexity of the real world, which cannot always be captured in a model. Intelligent algorithms have agentic components endowed with rights and responsibilities to make autonomous decisions. However, the interactions among these autonomous components are not yet fully understood, particularly in terms of how they influence outcomes and each other. Developers face fundamental uncertainties when deciding which variables to include or exclude in statistical models. The discrepancy between the complex, multifaceted nature of reality and the simplified, reductionist models used by algorithms contributes to algorithmic social injustices, as critical factors may be overlooked or misrepresented. IS research must focus on developing organizational mitigations for taming these fundamental uncertainties and enabling algorithms to make fairer and more accurate decisions that better reflect the complexity of real-world issues.

## Contributions to Research on Mitigations for Algorithmic Social Injustice Risks

This study makes both theoretical and empirical contributions to the literature on mitigation mechanisms for algorithmic social injustices. Most existing research on mitigations focuses on developing statistical techniques to address known unknowns in fairness and accuracy issues within algorithms (De‐Arteaga et al., 2022). While the concept of unknown unknowns has also been acknowledged (Daniel & Daniel, 2018), statistical mitigations are insufficient to address these complexities. We began developing theory to address unknown unknowns arising from the complexities of GT data, IT ecosystems, and the statistical models underlying intelligent algorithms. While the fundamental uncertainties inherent in such complexities are assumed to be irreducible, our theory and results indicate that fundamental uncertainties can be tamed by enhancing the quality of interactions between the algorithm and its stakeholders, between the user organization and the algorithm’s key stakeholders, and within the organization’s own mitigation strategies for managing algorithmic social injustice risks. Future IS research could build on our theory and further theorize organizational mitigations for taming fundamental uncertainties arising from the complexities of intelligent algorithms.

Addressing external complexity in agentic algorithms: Agentic algorithms interact with diverse sets of users, including both disabled and non-disabled individuals. Algorithms that fail to account for the varied needs of these groups risk systematically excluding or unfairly treating protected classes, such as people with disabilities. Our theory and findings inform IS research that designing algorithms with a broad range of human-like interaction capabilities can improve inclusivity and reduce perceptions of unfair treatment. These design interventions tame the external complexity and fundamental uncertainties the algorithm faces and ensure fairer outcomes for protected classes.

Enhancing stakeholder management: Our theory and findings also inform IS research that effective management of relationships between a user organization and the algorithm’s stakeholders also plays a critical role in reducing algorithmic social injustices. In the absence of a well-established GT, or when multiple conflicting GTs exist, a high-quality stakeholder management strategy allows for negotiation and compromise on the decision criteria, rules, and boundaries of the algorithm. Although algorithms may not fully align with every stakeholder’s specific value, the transparency and involvement of stakeholders in the process reduce perceptions of unfair treatment. In our sample, organizations improved stakeholder relationship management by designing algorithms to create value for stakeholders, understanding their social and political contexts, and using stakeholder feedback as an opportunity for continuous learning.

Organizational mitigation mechanisms: Finally, our theory and findings inform IS research that, beyond the statistical mitigation mechanisms, the organizational mitigation mechanisms employed by both developer and user organizations proved effective in reducing algorithmic social injustices. Measurement properties of these new constructs indicate that the proposed governance and controls are covarying manifestations of a coherent organizational-level risk mitigation strategy for taming complexity dynamics in an algorithm’s complex sociotechnical system. Future IS research can build on our theory to further theorize organizational mitigations for algorithmic social injustices arising from fundamental uncertainties inherent in complex, intelligent, agentic algorithms.

## Implications for Practice

Intelligent algorithms not only replicate existing structural inequalities in society; they also amplify these inequalities at scale and create new ones. A key implication of this study for data science practice is that these outcomes are not solely attributable to issues of accuracy arising from ground truth acquisition processes, or to failures in IT and statistical models. Instead, they stem from the inherent complexities of these antecedents. Statistical mitigations addressing the “known unknowns” in algorithmic accuracy and fairness are insufficient to address the “unknown unknowns” arising from these complexities. Organizations should not relegate these issues to data science teams. They must supplement statistical mitigations of data science teams with organizational mitigations that address the multifaceted challenges within the sociotechnical ecosystems of algorithms.

Developers should also recognize that automating a decision is viable only when a well-established GT exists. When no clear GT exists, or when multiple conflicting GTs are present, algorithms face fundamental uncertainty in their learning processes and decision-making tasks. In such cases, refraining from automation may be a prudent approach.

Furthermore, designing algorithms with a broad scope for human-like interaction increases their internal complexity. However, this also enables algorithms to adapt to external complexities by accommodating the diverse interaction needs of users, promoting inclusivity.

## Boundary Conditions, Limitations, and Future Work

This study is subject to several boundary conditions and limitations. First, it focuses exclusively on biases as examples of social inequalities. Whether the theory and findings extend to other forms of social inequality remains an open question for future research. Second, the absence of a systematic database containing comprehensive data on a broad, crosssectional sample of algorithms, along with information about the mitigation mechanisms employed by their developers and user organizations, poses a limitation. The availability of technical documentation is also constrained. To address these gaps, we conducted extensive manual data collection using publicly available source documents. However, further testing and validation of our theory will be needed as alternative data sources become available. Third, our study could not measure variables for all the years during which an algorithm existed. Longitudinal analyses, made possible by emerging datasets, could shed light on how time-varying algorithm characteristics influence the emergence of algorithmic social injustices. Finally, this study focused only on the mediating role of accuracy in the relationships between antecedents and algorithmic social injustices. Our findings underscore a broader knowledge gap: the mechanisms beyond accuracy through which antecedents lead to algorithmic social injustices remain poorly understood. Addressing this gap represents a critical area for future research.

## About the Authors

His research focuses on firm-level risk/return implications of IT strategies. On the return side, he studies digital business strategies for surviving and thriving in complex, hypercompetitive, and disruptive business ecosystems. On the risk side, he studies the IT-related risks of firms, such as cybersecurity and privacy risks, algorithmic risks, ethical dilemma risks, and how IT governance and control mechanisms could mitigate them.
