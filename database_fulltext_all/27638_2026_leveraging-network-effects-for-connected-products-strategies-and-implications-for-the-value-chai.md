# LEVERAGING NETWORK EFFECTS FOR CONNECTED PRODUCTS: STRATEGIES AND IMPLICATIONS FOR THE VALUE CHAIN<sup>1</sup>

Zhuoran Lu Antai College of Economics and Management, Shanghai Jiao Tong University Shanghai, CHINA {luzhuoran@sjtu.edu.cn}

Yifan Dou School of Management, Fudan University Shanghai, CHINA {yfdou@fudan.edu.cn}

D. J. Wu Scheller College of Business, Georgia Institute of Technology Atlanta, GA, U.S.A. {dj.wu@scheller.gatech.edu}

Jian Chen Research Center for Contemporary Management, Key Research Institute of Humanities and Social Sciences at Universities School of Economics and Management, Tsinghua University, Beijing, CHINA {chenj@sem.tsinghua.edu.cn}

This paper examines how a value chain can strategically harness network effects in connected products. We consider a representative two-tier value chain, in which a manufacturer sells a connected product through a retailer. Consumers derive both a stand-alone value and a network benefit, which increases with the size of the user base and the strength of the network effect. We investigate two strategies to effectively leverage these network effects. First, we analyze network expansion through seeding, which involves providing the product for free to a targeted group of consumers. Our analysis shows that when the production cost is sufficiently low, seeding arises in equilibrium and exhibits a “bang-bang” pattern, with either the manufacturer or the retailer exclusively undertaking seeding. Under strong network effects, the manufacturer can induce retailer seeding through a quantity-forcing contract with a wholesale discount, thereby replicating the centralized outcome in which both firms are integrated and act as a central planner. Next, we explore engineering network effects through investment. The decentralized value chain results in less efficient investment than the centralized one, leading to lower social welfare. However, when both strategies are jointly employed, we uncover a striking result: The decentralized value chain can yield higher social welfare than the centralized one. This occurs because the central planner underinvests in network effects, as investment generates a positive externality for seeded users, whereas the manufacturer may overinvest to reduce the wholesale discount, potentially resulting in greater social welfare.

Keywords: Connected product, value chain, seeding, engineering network effects, pricing

## Introduction

The proliferation of the Internet of Things (IoT) and cloudbased infrastructures has led to an unprecedented level of connectivity across physical products, ranging from smart wearables to electric vehicles. Using digital technologies such as artificial intelligence (AI) and data analytics, firms can collect and process vast amounts of user data to continuously monitor, maintain, and improve product quality. For example, smart speakers can enhance their recommendation systems using voice data collected from users. As the user base expands and more data are accumulated, the product becomes increasingly valuable to all users, creating a self-reinforcing cycle of adoption and improvement. This phenomenon is known as the “data network effect” (Cusumano, 2022; Gregory et al., 2022).

Unlike traditional network effects, where value arises from direct interactions among users, the data network effect allows users to benefit from others’ data through the product network—even without any social connections. Moreover, product performance can continue to improve after purchase as additional usage data become available. Therefore, consumers’ willingness to pay depends critically on their expectations about the eventual (equilibrium) size of the user base and the value generated through such a network.

To monetize connected products by leveraging the data network effect, firms must strategically develop the user network. The literature suggests two common approaches to harnessing network effects. The first is the “network expansion strategy,” which focuses on increasing the number of users or nodes in the network (e.g., Gelper et al., 2021; Haenlein & Libai, 2017). The second is the “connection-strengthening strategy,” which aims to increase the intensity or value of connections among users (e.g., Aral & Walker, 2011; Dou et al., 2013). While these strategies are well-established in the software industry, applying them to connected products poses two key challenges. First, the non-negligible production costs of physical products often make it infeasible to employ the low-price (or even free) strategies, which are commonly used in software markets. Second, unlike software, connected products cannot be distributed virtually; rather, manufacturers typically rely on value chain partners, such as retailers, to deliver the product to consumers. These vertical relationships fundamentally shape how the network effect translates into economic value. Motivated by these observations, this paper asks: How can value chain participants for connected products leverage the data network effect through these two strategies?

To address this question, we develop a game-theoretical model in which a manufacturer sells a connected product through a retailer to a unit mass of consumers. Each consumer has a unit demand and an idiosyncratic stand-alone value. In addition, consumers derive an indirect network benefit generated by other users—for example, through the positive externalities of shared data. This network benefit increases with both the size of the user network and the strength of the network effect (e.g., the manufacturer’s data analytics capability). We consider two value chain structures to examine how the network effect affects value chain outcomes and efficiency. In the centralized case, the manufacturer and the retailer are vertically integrated and act as a central planner. In the decentralized case, the two firms choose their strategies sequentially and independently.

We first consider a baseline model, in which the manufacturer sets a wholesale price, and then the retailer chooses a market price. We show that, in both the centralized and decentralized cases, stronger network effects expand the user network and increase industry profits. Consistent with the classic literature on value chains, the decentralized case results in a smaller user network and a lower industry profit than the centralized case, due to the double marginalization problem.

We then explore how the value chain can more effectively leverage the network effect through the above two strategies in the Strategies for Leveraging the Network Effect section. We begin with the network expansion strategy, focusing on targeted seeding as a representative strategy. In the centralized case, the central planner chooses a (possibly empty) group of low-value consumers and offers them free products to build a “critical mass,” and then sets the market price. In the decentralized case, the manufacturer first chooses a group of low-value consumers to seed and sets the wholesale price; then, the retailer chooses its seeding volume among the remaining consumers and sets the market price. All firms face the same seeding cost, which is increasing and quadratic in the seeding volume. This setting echoes real-world practices for many connected products sold through retailers using giveaway campaigns to boost adoption, such as Philips Hue smart lighting devices,<sup>2</sup> Fitbit trackers,<sup>3</sup> and Sonos smart speakers.<sup>4</sup>

We show that seeding arises only when the production cost is sufficiently low. In the centralized case, the central planner optimally chooses a full-scale seeding volume so that the network reaches its maximum size when the network effect is sufficiently strong. In the decentralized case, the equilibrium seeding outcome exhibits a “bang-bang” structure: either the manufacturer or the retailer undertakes all the seeding, although both firms have the option to do so. This pattern stems from a key trade-off faced by the manufacturer: It can either seed consumers directly, or it can lower the wholesale price to induce the retailer to do so and thus save on seeding costs. The price cut needed to incentivize the retailer to seed decreases with the strength of the network effect. Consequently, when the network effect is sufficiently strong, the manufacturer sets a relatively low wholesale price to induce the retailer to choose the full-scale seeding volume (referred to as R-seeding). In contrast, under weaker network effects, the required price cut becomes too costly. As a result, the manufacturer undertakes seeding itself (referred to as Mseeding), choosing a moderate seeding volume (i.e., partial Mseeding) when the network effect is sufficiently weak. Importantly, to induce R-seeding, the manufacturer effectively uses a quantity-forcing contract, <sup>5</sup> offering a discounted wholesale price for the full-network quantity. This contract mitigates double marginalization, leading to the same market outcome and industry profit as in the centralized case. In contrast, if the equilibrium exhibits M-seeding or no seeding, the decentralized case always yields a lower industry profit due to double marginalization.

Then, we turn to the connection-strengthening strategy, under which both the central planner and the manufacturer can engineer the strength of the network effect by incurring investment costs before engaging in the pricing game of the baseline model. For example, Philips Hue smart lighting systems allow users to control lights through smartphones and create custom scenes; the product’s value increases as Philips develops more intricate lighting automations. We show that the central planner’s marginal return on investment is either below the socially optimal (first-best) level or equal to it when the resulting network effect is strong enough such that the network size is maximized. Consequently, the centralized case either exhibits underinvestment or achieves the social optimum, depending on the investment cost. In contrast, due to double marginalization, the manufacturer’s marginal return on investment is even lower than the central planner’s for weak network effects. However, for sufficiently strong network effects, the manufacturer’s marginal return exceeds that of the central planner, which coincides with the first-best level. This outcome is driven by two forces. First, double marginalization slows network expansion in the decentralized case, thereby extending the range in which the manufacturer can benefit from incremental investment. Second, once the network achieves its maximum size, the manufacturer can appropriate an increasing share of the additional surplus generated by further investment, as the retailer’s incentive to seed consumers increases with the strength of the network effect. Together, these two forces give rise to incentives for overinvestment. Since the decentralized case leads to either more severe underinvestment or overinvestment (meanwhile, the centralized case achieves the social optimum), it always yields lower social welfare than the centralized case when only the engineering strategy is employed.

Finally, in the Combined Use of Seeding and Engineering section, we examine the combined use of these two strategies and show that the two strategies are complementary in expanding the user network and enhancing profitability. In both the centralized and decentralized cases, as long as the user network remains below its maximum size, combining the engineering strategy with seeding yields a higher marginal return on investment than relying on engineering alone. Consequently, the investment level increases, leading to a larger user network and a higher industry profit. More interestingly, when both strategies are employed, the decentralized case can yield higher social welfare than the centralized case under R-seeding—in stark contrast to the welfare ranking, when only the engineering strategy is employed. The reason is twofold. First, unlike in the absence of seeding, the central planner’s marginal return on investment is less than that in the first-best case. This is because investment benefits both paying and seeded users, yet the central planner appropriates surplus only from the former. In other words, investment generates a positive externality for seeded users, leading to underinvestment. Second, to induce R-seeding under sufficiently strong network effects, the manufacturer offers the retailer a discounted wholesale price, and the stronger the network effect, the smaller the required discount. Meanwhile, under R-seeding, the manufacturer can capture surplus from both paying and seeded users through the retailer. Together, these facts provide the manufacturer with an additional incentive to invest, leading to overinvestment. If the underinvestment in the centralized case outweighs the overinvestment in the decentralized case, then the decentralized case will generate higher social welfare.

This welfare result suggests that the conventional wisdom— that vertical integration is socially desirable since it eliminates double marginalization (e.g., Tirole, 1988)—does not directly apply to connected products with network effects. When both network expansion and connection-strengthening strategies are employed, an integrated value chain may fail to fully internalize the social return on product innovation. In contrast, in a decentralized value chain, the involvement of the retailer may not only facilitate efficient network expansion (e.g., through R-seeding) but may also incentivize the upstream manufacturer to invest more aggressively, potentially yielding higher welfare. Our analysis thus sheds new light on the welfare-enhancing role of retailers in the market for innovative connected products.

## Literature Review

This paper contributes to two well-established strands of literature. First, it relates closely to the extensive literature on network effects, a topic widely studied since the seminal works of Katz and Shapiro (1985), Rohlfs (1974), and Varian et al. (2004). Under network effects, early adopters generate value for later users, consistent with Metcalfe’s law (Metcalfe, 2013). This externality incentivizes firms to expand the user base through various business strategies, including price discrimination (e.g., Chien & Chu, 2008; Jing, 2007) and free offerings (Cheng & Tang, 2010; Jiang & Sarkar, 2009). Following this line of research, we focus on seeding strategies (Libai et al., 2005), where a segment of the market receives free products to stimulate broader adoption. Unlike much of the existing literature that assumes zero marginal production cost (e.g., Abhishek et al., 2016; Hu et al., 2022), we consider positive production costs and fully characterize how the production cost and the strength of the network effect jointly determine the equilibrium seeding outcomes.

Recently, the proliferation of connected products has spurred a growing literature on data network effects. While the use of user data for personalization and pricing is prevalent in practice (Varian, 2019), the optimal intertemporal strategy for leveraging such network effects remains underexplored. Hagiu and Wright (2023) developed a dynamic duopoly model in which firms improve their products by learning from user data and showed that firms’ learning functions critically shape their competitive advantages. In contrast, we study a value chain in which a manufacturer sells a connected product through a retailer and examine how firms’ strategies to leverage network effects affect value chain outcomes and overall efficiency. Moreover, whereas the learning function in Hagiu and Wright (2023) is exogenously given and driven solely by user data, our model allows firms to invest in data analytics capabilities, thereby endogenizing the learning function. Relatedly, Adner et al. (2019) proposed a “connectivity revolution” for connected products—a notion that aligns with our findings on engineering network effects. We show that such a revolution is more likely to emerge in a decentralized value chain, where the upstream manufacturer may invest aggressively to incentivize the downstream retailer to expand the user network.

Second, our paper contributes to the literature on vertical relationships within the value chain. It is well known that double marginalization can lead to inefficiently high prices, reducing total quantity and welfare. Recent studies have investigated this inefficiency in various settings, including performance-based advertising (Dellarocas, 2012) and SaaS markets (Demirkan et al., 2010). In our setting, however, when network effects are strong, the manufacturer may offer a wholesale discount to induce the retailer to seed, thereby eliminating double marginalization. Furthermore, the manufacturer’s desire to reduce this discount can cause overinvestment in the strength of the network effect. This may yield higher welfare than in a centralized value chain, which could lead to underinvestment. A related insight appears in Shang et al. (2016), who showed that double marginalization can play a positive role when a retailer shares demand information with competing manufacturers. Finally, Sun and Ji (2022) studied IoT-enabled channels in a similar context, but without network effects, as the value derived from IoT is independent of the size of the user base.

In line with the existing literature, we examine two typical strategies for leveraging network effects. The first aims to increase the number of “nodes” in the network, thereby amplifying network value according to Metcalfe’s law. This typically involves acquiring new users—especially those who would not otherwise purchase the product—by offering it for free, a practice commonly referred to as “seeding.” For example, Amazon U.K. has given away Apple AirTags, <sup>9</sup> and Apple distributed 20,000 AirTags in Japan in 2021 as a promotion.<sup>10</sup> Other examples, including Philips Hue smart lighting devices, Fitbit trackers, and Sonos smart speakers, are discussed in the Introduction. The second strategy focuses on strengthening the “edges” of the network to extract greater value from each connection. This can be achieved by enhancing connectivitybased features, thereby raising the value of user connections. In the following sections, we analyze these two strategies in detail.

## Strategies for Leveraging the Network Effect

## Leveraging the Network Effect Through Seeding

First, we consider expanding the user network through a seeding strategy. Specifically, each firm—including the central planner, the manufacturer, and the retailer—can give away products for free (i.e., seeds) to a fraction $\alpha \in [ 0 , 1 ]$ of consumers to build the user network, where ?? is also referred to as the seeding volume. These seeds help establish a “critical mass” of users, which is essential for enhancing the utility of other consumers and attracting them to purchase. Note that the user network includes both paying and seeded users, as all adoptions contribute equally to the network benefits.

Given consumer preferences, the most profitable seeding strategy is to provide seeds to consumers with $\theta \in [ 0 , \alpha ]$ , as this minimizes opportunity costs. We refer to this efficient approach as targeted seeding, which fully leverages the potential of seeding by focusing on consumers with the lowest willingness to pay. As shown in Appendix D, if firms are restricted to less efficient methods—such as uniform seeding, where all consumers are equally likely to be seeded—then seeding does not occur in equilibrium due to a cannibalization problem, even when seeding incurs no costs at all.

Moving forward, we compare the marginal returns on investment across three cases: the centralized case, the decentralized case, and the first best. This additional benefit provides the manufacturer with a marginal return that exceeds not only the central planner’s but also the first-best level.

Proposition 5 asserts that the decentralized case can outperform the centralized case in welfare only if R-seeding occurs, in which case the centralized case exhibits full seeding. This is because, given ??, the decentralized case can maximize industry profit only through R-seeding, mitigating double marginalization. However, the centralized case always achieves higher industry profit, as the manufacturer invests inefficiently relative to the industry-profit-maximizing level. Moreover, when the decentralized case achieves a higher investment level than the centralized case, both cases exhibit inefficiencies in two ways. First, both investment levels are distorted from the firstbest level. Therefore, for any investment level $b \in [ 0 , 1 )$ , both cases yield strictly lower social welfare than the first best, as illustrated in Figure 4b.

Proposition 5 has important implications for value chain efficiency and management. In contrast to the conventional wisdom that vertical integration is beneficial to eliminate double marginalization, Proposition 5 suggests that when both the seeding and engineering strategies are employed to harness network effects in connected products, the participants in a decentralized value chain can strategically transform their contract to mitigate double marginalization. This provides the manufacturer with an incentive to undertake more productive investments than the central planner, who underinvests in engineering network effects due to positive externalities. Consequently, the decentralized case may yield strictly higher social welfare than the centralized case, highlighting the potentially indispensable role of the retailer in achieving welfare improvements.

## Conclusion

As connected products draw increasing attention from researchers, this paper is among the first to explore their implications for the value chain. We develop a gametheoretical model in which a manufacturer sells a connected product through a retailer to consumers, who derive both a stand-alone value and a network benefit that increases with the user base and the strength of the network effect. Our analysis reveals that such network effects fundamentally reshape value chain dynamics, offering insights into three key aspects.

First, as physical products increasingly incorporate connectivity features, the resulting network effects create new sources of profit. In the Baseline Model section, we show that stronger network effects expand the user network and increase industry profit. When network effects are sufficiently strong, the user network reaches its maximum size, and the industry profit is maximized. However, the required level of network effects may be prohibitively high. To explore more effective ways of leveraging network effects, the Strategies for Leveraging the Network Effect section examines two typical strategies: (1) seeding to expand the user network, and (2) investing in connection strength. We show that seeding can accelerate network growth and improve profitability. In particular, under strong network effects, a decentralized value chain can replicate the market outcome and industry profit of a centralized (integrated) one through a quantity-forcing contract that eliminates double marginalization. In contrast, when only the engineering strategy is used, decentralization leads to more distorted investment relative to centralization. The Combined Use of Seeding and Engineering section further shows that the two strategies are complementary in expanding the user network and enhancing profits.

Second, our analysis shows that the strategic interaction in a decentralized value chain can induce more investment in connectivity features than centralization. When network effects are strong, the manufacturer may offer a quantity-forcing contract with a wholesale discount to incentivize the retailer to expand the network through seeding. The stronger the network effect, the smaller the required discount, which further motivates the manufacturer to invest in network strength. If the manufacturer becomes sufficiently efficient in investment—

e.g., due to improved data analytics capability or increased consumer willingness to share data—the resulting investment may exceed that under centralization and even surpass the social optimum. As connectivity becomes more pervasive, these dynamics are increasingly relevant across decentralized industries. In sectors such as robotics, autonomous vehicles, and voice assistants, combining seeding and connectivity investment can reshape industry outcomes and innovation patterns. As “always-on” connectivity becomes the norm, these effects are particularly salient in industries undergoing structural transformation (Adner et al., 2019; Siggelkow & Terwiesch, 2019).

Third, and building on the above, we show that a decentralized value chain can even yield higher social welfare than a centralized one. While decentralization may lead to overinvestment, centralization results in underinvestment because the positive externality for seeded users is not fully internalized. If the inefficiency from underinvestment outweighs that from overinvestment, the decentralized value chain leads to higher welfare. This result offers new insights into the regulation of value chains and the potential welfareenhancing role of retailers. In a connected economy, efforts to strengthen network effects not only benefit firms individually but also support broader market coverage and a more efficient division of labor between upstream and downstream players.

To conclude, our analysis points to several promising directions for future research. One natural extension is to investigate the impacts of consumer heterogeneity in both the contribution to and the benefits derived from usage data, as well as empirical tests of such heterogeneity. Another interesting extension is to examine data collection and algorithm training along the value chain, especially when connected products embed AI modules (Gurkan & de Véricourt, 2022). Other valuable extensions include privacy concerns (Sun & Ji, 2022), network topology (Dou et al., 2013), and the spillover effects of shared data on other products or future innovations. In addition, studying hybrid channel structures—such as firms selling both directly and through platforms—can also advance our understanding of the interaction between vertical relationships within value chains and network effects.

## Uniform Seeding

In this section, we consider the case where firms have limited capabilities in identifying and targeting consumers. In particular, we focus on the case where neither the central planner nor the value chain participants can target specific consumers. Instead, they must distribute seeds uniformly across all consumers, a strategy referred to as uniform seeding. We assume that uniform seeding incurs no cost beyond the production costs. Proposition D1 below characterizes the equilibrium seeding strategies under uniform seeding.

Proposition D1: Under uniform seeding, the equilibrium seeding volume is always zero in both the centralized and decentralized cases.

Proposition D1 states that under uniform seeding, irrespective of the market structure, seeding never occurs in equilibrium, even if it incurs no costs at all. Although the proof is somewhat involved and can be found below, the intuition is transparent. Whereas the explicit production cost might be negligible, there is always a nontrivial implicit cost of cannibalization. Specifically, with extra Δ units of seeding volume, $\big ( 1 - \widehat { \theta } \big ) \Delta$ of higher value consumers who would have purchased the product under the targeted seeding strategy now receive seeds, thereby leading to a $\cdot p ( 1 - { \widehat { \theta } } ) \Delta$ loss of revenue. Thus, the virtual marginal cost of uniform seeding is the sum of the marginal cost and the cost of cannibalization.

It turns out that in the centralized case, the virtual marginal cost of seeding is always higher than the marginal benefit, which is the central planner’s capability to charge a higher price due to the network effect. Therefore, the central planner chooses not to seed. In the decentralized case, the retailer’s marginal profit of seeding is the same as that of the central planner, except for that the marginal cost is given by the wholesale price, and thus, the retailer chooses not to seed either. In contrast, the marginal benefit of seeding is lower for the manufacturer due to double marginalization. Specifically the increase in the market price caused by higher seeding volume will only partially lead to the increase in the manufacturer’s revenue due to the negative externality of the retailer’s pricing. Therefore, the manufacturer has a lower incentive to seed consumers, thereby choosing not to seed.
