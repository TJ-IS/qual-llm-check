# THE FOG OF WARNINGS: HOW NON-SECURITY-RELATED NOTIFICATIONS DIMINISH THE EFFICACY OF SECURITY WARNINGS<sup>1</sup>

Anthony Vance Department of Business Information Technology, Pamplin College of Business Virginia Tech Blacksburg, VA, U.S.A. {anthony@vance.name}

Dave Eargle Independent Scholar, Denver CO, U.S.A. {dave@daveeargle.com}

C. Brock Kirwan MindCORE Neuroimaging Facility University of Pennsylvania, Philadelphia, PA, U.S.A. {bkirwan@sas.upenn.edu}

Bonnie Brinton Anderson and Jeffrey L. Jenkins Information Systems Department, Marriott School of Business, Brigham Young University, Provo, UT, U.S.A. {bonnie\_anderson@byu.edu} {jeffrey\_jenkins@byu.edu}

Users’ disregard of security warnings is a critical problem in cybersecurity. This problem worsens when people confuse security warnings with common, non-security-related notifications, which they learn to routinely disregard. We investigate this problem through the neurobiological phenomenon of generalization of habituation, where habituation to one stimulus transfers to another stimulus that shares similar characteristics. Generalization of habituation suggests that because of habituation to frequent notifications, people may also be deeply habituated to security warnings they have never seen before, leading to warning disregard. Furthermore, because generalization of habituation occurs unconsciously at the neurobiological level, this may occur even though a person can consciously distinguish security warnings from notifications. We address this problem through three experiments—two in the field and one using functional magnetic resonance imaging. These experiments demonstrate how generalization of habituation occurs and can be mitigated by differentiating warnings from notifications in terms of their visual appearance or mode of interaction. These findings provide guidance to software developers for designing warnings that resist generalization of habituation and promote greater warning adherence.

Keywords: Security warning, habituation, generalization, fMRI, field experiment, NeuroIS

## Introduction

Users’ disregard of security warnings—discrete communications designed to make the user aware of and take action in response to an immediate potential threat<sup>2</sup>—is a serious problem in cybersecurity because warnings are often the last defense before a person or organization is compromised (Acer et al., 2017; Weinberger & Felt, 2016). For example, numerous significant security incidents can be traced to a user opening a malicious file received as an email attachment or unwittingly downloaded from the internet, disregarding the associated security warning (see Figure 1a; MITRE, 2024).

The problem of security warning disregard is believed to be exacerbated by common non-security-related notifications, dialogs, alerts, and confirmations (hereinafter, collectively termed notifications) that have no security implications but share a similar look and feel with security warnings (Böhme & Köpsell, 2010). For example, Figure 1a is a security warning, while Figure 1b is a notification, yet both appear as pop-up dialogs, are similar in appearance, and share iconography and an “OK” button. However, while clicking “OK” in the notification of Figure 1b is of no consequence, choosing the same button in the warning in Figure 1a has resulted in major compromises for many organizations (Crowdstrike, 2024; Mandiant, 2024). This similarity between warnings and notifications is encouraged by the user interface (UI) design principle of consistency (Cooper et al., 2007; Krug, 2014) and reinforced by major software companies that provide development libraries and guidelines (e.g., Apple, 2025; Google, 2025; Microsoft, 2025). An unfortunate consequence of UI consistency is that “security messages often resemble other message dialogs. As a result, security messages may not stand out in importance and users often learn to disregard them” (West, 2008, p. 39).<sup>3</sup>

The security warning disregard caused by the blurring of security warnings with notifications is related to the neurobiological phenomenon of habituation, which is defined as a “decreased response to repeated stimulation” (Groves & Thompson, 1970, p. 419). Prior research has shown that there is substantially less attention-related brain activity in response to a specific warning after the second time it is viewed—which, in turn, leads to warning disregard behavior (Anderson et al., 2016a; Anderson et al., 2016b; Vance et al., 2018). However, these studies did not investigate the above problem of people confusing warnings with notifications, even when they can consciously discern that warnings and notifications belong to different contexts (i.e., security and non-security). Furthermore, because the nature and conditions of this problem are not understood, it is unknown which interventions will prevent security warnings from being confused with notifications.

We investigate this problem through an aspect of habituation previously unexplored in IS, known as generalization of habituation or simply generalization (Rankin et al., 2009; Thompson & Spencer, 1966). Through this process, which is common to all animal life (Thompson, 2009), habituation to one stimulus carries over to another that shares similar characteristics. When this phenomenon occurs, an organism exhibits a decreased response to the other stimulus, even if it is novel. Applied to the domain of security warnings, generalization suggests that, due to exposure to common notifications that share similar characteristics, people may already be deeply habituated to security warnings they have never seen before, leading to warning disregard. This may occur even though a person can consciously distinguish warnings from notifications, because habituation occurs unconsciously at the neurobiological level (Vance et al., 2018).

To address the warning disregard problem caused by similarities between notifications and warnings, the objectives of this study are (1) to explain how habituation generalizes from frequent notifications to security warnings and (2) to evaluate interventions to mitigate this problem. In pursuing these objectives, we answer the following research questions:

RQ1: How does habituation to frequent non-security-related notifications generalize to infrequent security warnings?

RQ2: How can generalization to security warnings be reduced?

We conducted three experiments in a web browser that displayed a notification and a variety of warnings to users. First, we conducted a field experiment to demonstrate that habituation to a frequent notification generalizes to a novel security warning in terms of decreased attention and higher warning disregard behavior. Furthermore, we showed that changing the visual appearance of the warning to make it less similar to the notification made generalization substantially less likely.

Second, we performed a functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) experiment to provide a neural measurement of the generalization effect observed in the field experiment and to rule out rival explanations of neural fatigue or increased cognitive engagement. In the fMRI experiment, we confirmed the behavioral results of generalization and demonstrated that generalization occurs neurobiologically in the brain, allowing us to rule out rival explanations. We also showed that generalization can be reduced by changing the mode of interaction of the warning. We define mode of interaction as the way a person interacts with a user interface (Reeves et al., 2004), such as using a mouse to click a button in a notification message (e.g., “OK” or “Cancel”). Warnings with a distinctive mode of interaction (e.g., dragging a slider widget with the mouse to select an option) showed significantly less generalization. Third, we performed an additional field experiment to disentangle the effects of changing the mode of interaction and visual appearance. We found that, while holding the visual appearance constant, changing the mode of interaction alone was effective in reducing generalization.

This study makes three primary theoretical contributions. First, we demonstrate that generalization from notifications to warnings does occur and leads to security warning disregard. We test this effect neurally and behaviorally and rule out several rival explanations. Second, we show that generalization can occur despite people being able to consciously determine that notifications belong to a nonsecurity context and warnings belong to a security context. Third, we develop two theory-driven interventions involving visual appearance and the mode of interaction of a warning and show that they effectively reduce habituation.

This study also makes several practical contributions. First, our interventions can be straightforwardly implemented by software developers without imposing any usability costs on users, such as delay or cognitive effort. Second, we show that the principle of UI consistency widely followed in practice can pose a security threat when applied to security warnings because it leads to generalization of habituation and warning disregard. Finally, our results show that the practical recommendations of past research to reduce habituation are incomplete without accounting for generalization.

## Literature Review, Theory, and Hypotheses

## Generalization and Security Warnings

Habituation to security warnings has been observed or inferred in several studies (see Amran et al., 2018; Vance et al., 2018 for a review). However, the phenomenon of generalization in the context of security warnings remains understudied. In addition, West (2008) noted that security messages often resemble notifications, leading to security warning disregard. Böhme and Köpsell (2010) observed that users’ automatic responses to notifications appear “to spill over from moderately relevant topics (e.g., EULAs) to more critical ones (online safety and privacy)” (Böhme & Köpsell, 2010, p. 2406). However, none of these studies empirically investigated this problem or evaluated possible solutions.

Generalization needs to be empirically investigated because the recommendations of previous research to reduce habituation to security warnings may not apply when generalization occurs. Primary among these is the recommendation (e.g., Akhawe & Felt, 2013; Krol et al., 2012; Vance, 2017; Weinberger & Felt, 2016; Zeng et al., 2019) to limit the appearance of warnings, such as “reducing the onslaught of interrupting security warning dialogs might help reduce the strain on users’ attention” (Bravo-Lillo et al., 2013, p. 1). Although this guidance is reasonable, it does not consider the impact of notifications that share similar characteristics with warnings and can appear many times throughout a user’s day (Pielot et al., 2018; Shirazi et al., 2014; Turner et al., 2019). Accordingly, limiting the appearance of warnings in such contexts will have little effect in the presence of generalization.

Another previous recommendation is to update the appearance of a warning with each presentation through a “polymorphic” design, which can substantially reduce habituation to a single warning (Anderson et al., 2016a; Anderson et al., 2016b; Vance et al., 2018). However, it is unclear whether a polymorphic warning is robust to generalization of habituation caused by frequent notifications. While some variations in a polymorphic design may have appearances that are sufficiently different from common notifications, others may be too subtle to resist the effect of generalization (Anderson et al., 2016b; Vance et al., 2018). More fundamentally, the conditions under which generalization occurs in the context of notifications and warnings remain unclear. Therefore, the interventions that are most effective for reducing generalization are unknown. Hence, research is needed to investigate how “other facets of habituation may influence the users’ responses to security warnings, such as … generalization” (Vance et al., 2018, p. 376). This study addresses these gaps in the literature.

## Generalization Theory and Hypotheses

Generalization can be understood as the failure to distinguish between different stimuli (Shepard, 1987). According to the dual-process theory of habituation (Groves & Thompson, 1970), when a person is initially exposed to a stimulus, a mental model of the stimulus is created. When the same stimulus is encountered again, the person automatically and unconsciously compares the stimulus with the mental model. If the stimulus and mental model match, behavioral responses to the stimulus are inhibited in favor of reliance on the model in the person’s memory (Thompson, 2009). However, when a novel stimulus that does not match the mental model is encountered, the inhibited behavioral responses are recovered. This process of habituation allows the brain to conserve resources efficiently to filter out irrelevant stimuli and respond to new stimuli.

However, according to the neuroscience literature, the increased efficiency of the neural response in habituation comes at the price of failing to distinguish among stimuli that share similar characteristics. Correspondingly, an inhibited response to one stimulus carries over or generalizes to another novel but similar stimulus (Thompson & Spencer, 1966). The degree to which the brain relies on an existing mental model instead of paying full attention to a novel stimulus depends on how frequently the person has been previously exposed to the stimulus and how similar the novel stimulus is to existing models (Rankin et al., 2009). Therefore, the stronger the habituation and similarity of the two stimuli, the more likely it is that habituation will generalize from one stimulus to another.

Generalization of habituation has been studied in humans in various contexts and ages, from image recognition in toddlers (Anderson et al., 2022) to cues to stop eating in adults (Epstein & Carr, 2021). A common theme across studies is that when generalization occurs, people become confused between the habituated and novel stimuli, even though they can consciously differentiate between the two. This happens because generalization of habituation occurs unconsciously at the neurobiological level (Groves & Thompson, 1970), leading people to mistake the two stimuli when deliberate attention is not paid (Lampinen & Moore, 2016). Thus, while habituation is specific to a single stimulus in a particular context, generalization applies across stimuli in different contexts.

Following the dual-process theory of habituation, we hypothesize that habituation to notifications will generalize to security warnings that share a similar visual appearance, even when the participants can consciously differentiate between the two. Maintaining visual consistency has long been considered a fundamental principle of UI design (Curtis, 1989). Hence, security warnings are typically visually similar to notifications (see Figure 1). The greater the visual similarity between a security warning and a notification, the greater the reliance on the mental model of the previously seen notification and the lesser the response time to the security warning, both in terms of attention and behavioral response. When evaluating a stimulus, people often notice big picture elements (e.g., visual appearance) before delving into small details (e.g., text; (Djamasbi et al., 2011). Thus, even though some information may differ across security warnings and notifications (e.g., text and icons), which allows a person to consciously differentiate between the two, visual similarity and other high-level information will increase reliance on the model in the individual’s memory. In summary, we predict the following:

H1: Habituation to non-security-related notifications will generalize to security warnings with a similar visual appearance.

The foregoing theory and hypothesis also allow for a corollary prediction. According to dual-process theory, generalization will not occur for a novel stimulus that is sufficiently distinct from an existing model in an individual’s memory (Thompson, 2009). In such cases, new stimuli will be recognized as novel and will be processed with full attentional resources (Rankin et al., 2009). Therefore, if a novel security warning is dissimilar to the mental model of a notification, people will be more likely to pay attention to the warning, and generalization will occur less. Accordingly, we hypothesize the following:

H2: Habituation to non-security-related notifications will generalize less to novel security warnings with a distinctive visual appearance.

We also theorized that differentiating the mode of interaction of a warning from common notifications would reduce generalization. According to schema theory (Rumelhart, 1980), schemas represent general knowledge about objects, situations, or sequences of actions. This phenomenon is often explained by the concept of muscle memory. Muscle memory, sometimes called skill or habit memory, refers to a type of procedural memory—that is, a type of unconscious memory that aids in performing repeated tasks efficiently—which occurs when a person performs a specific motor (e.g., muscle movement) task repeatedly. Through the repeated performance of a given motor task, long-term muscle memory is created for that task, which can then be performed more accurately and efficiently in the future (Krakauer & Shadmehr, 2006). For example, many types of athletes repeatedly practice an activity so that they can perform it well without conscious effort (Adkins & Boychuck, 2006).

Likewise, in computing tasks, the dominant UI paradigm for responding to a notification is the use of a mouse to click one of several labeled buttons (e.g., “OK” and “Cancel”; West, 2008), a paradigm we call “click to dismiss.” Due to this paradigm dominance, people often habitually respond to a message using muscle memory, even though it sometimes contradicts their conscious intentions (Bravo-Lillo et al., 2013). Thus, similar to visual habituation, people may also form high-level memory representations (or schemas) of how to interact with notifications and warnings.

However, schema theory also explains that changing schemas forces conscious engagement with a task (Rumelhart, 1980). Therefore, a person who can kick a soccer ball proficiently without conscious effort may not necessarily be able to hit a golf ball with the same automaticity. Rather, this would require attention and purposeful movements (James et al., 2013). Similarly, a person who can play a scale on a violin without conscious thought of finger placement may not necessarily be able to automatically do the same on a cello, given the differences between the instruments and how they are held. In the context of security warnings, changing the standard mode of interaction from “click to dismiss” to something distinctive (e.g., using the mouse to drag a slider widget to select an option) will break the schema of past responses to notifications and force people to respond consciously without relying on muscle memory (Rumelhart, 1980). Hence, people will not rely on the existing mental model, avoid automatically responding, and pay more attention to the warning. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

H3: Habituation to non-security-related notifications will generalize less to novel security warnings with a distinctive mode of interaction.

## Methods

We tested our hypotheses through three experiments following the guidelines of Kirwan et al. (2022) to complement NeuroIS experiments with behavioral experiments to enhance the overall internal, external, and ecological validity of the study. In Experiment 1, we conducted an online field experiment to test H1 and H2 by observing participants’ behaviors. In Experiment 2, we performed an fMRI laboratory experiment to test H1 via neural activation and to rule out rival explanations for neural fatigue and increased cognitive engagement. Furthermore, we tested H3 by showing that warnings with different modes of interaction from a notification exhibit less generalization of habituation. Finally, in Experiment 3, we performed an additional online field experiment to test H3 again, this time assessing the effect of changing the mode of interaction of a warning while holding the visual appearance constant. ## Experiment 1: Online Field Experiment

We designed an online experiment to establish whether habituation generalizes from notifications to security warnings (H1) and whether distinguishing the visual appearance of the warning can reduce this generalization (H2). We tested these hypotheses in the context of a web browser-based task. This allowed us to observe the effects of repeated browser notifications on actual browser warnings. It also had the advantage of observing participants in their natural environment and on their own computers, facilitating perceptions of risk to their devices and data.

Browsers show various notifications, such as meeting reminders, website and app push notifications (e.g., news updates or new email alerts), and extension notifications. In addition, browsers display important security warnings. For example, they can warn users when a website requests access to private information, when a website likely contains malware that may harm the user’s computer, or when a user requests to open a potentially dangerous file that was downloaded. Browser notifications and warnings often share a consistent look and feel (Firefox, 2024), which may exacerbate generalization. Hence, we deem the browser context appropriate for studying generalization and anticipate that the results of our experiments will apply to a variety of other applications and devices.

## Experiment 1: Participants

We recruited 609 participants via Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk). Following Steelman et al. (2014), all subjects were required to be based in the United States. The average age of the individuals was 36 years, and 53% of them were male. They were paid \$1.50 (\$1.00 upfront and \$0.50 bonus) for an approximately 5-minute task. To standardize the notification and warning messages, the participants were required to use the Firefox browser.<sup>4</sup>

## Experiment 1: Experimental Task

We followed a previously established experimental protocol designed to measure interactants’ disregard of security warnings on a web browser (Vance et al., 2014). However, with the approval of the local institutional review board, deception was used to conceal this purpose from the participants. Instead, subjects were instructed that the experiment was aimed at training a machine learning algorithm by manually classifying images from the web, a commonly performed task on MTurk (MTurk, 2017). Individuals were required to view images of Batman and classify them as either photographs or drawings (Figure 2).

![](/api/attachments/2YBPX28T/fulltext/images/d7d4741932060603d77f08fc1c8eaff27fff820e3fc5f88b0eaf8f474f836fce.jpg)

Figure 2. Browser-Based Image Classification Task

Typically, because security warnings and notifications must compete with time-sensitive work tasks in the real world (Jenkins et al., 2016), we simulated time pressure by giving the participants 10 seconds to classify each website image. Failure to classify the image was counted as an incorrect answer. Participants started with a bonus of \$0.50, which was decreased by \$0.05 with each incorrect classification. A status bar on the bottom-left corner of the screen showed the participants their current bonuses (Figure 2). However, in reality, all individuals received the full bonus at the end of the task, regardless of their performance.

After a short practice round, the subjects began classifying 75 images. In actuality, they classified at most 14 images to avoid behavior bias by anticipating the end of the experiment. A previous study conducted using similar stimuli showed decreased neural responses with 13 presentations of a stimulus (Anderson et al., 2016). Thus, we reasoned that habituation would be evident in many presentations. Each classification was followed by the display of an HTML5-style browser notification on the upper-left corner of the screen, which indicated the participant’s current classification performance (Figure 3a). Importantly, subjects had to click the “Continue” button on the performance notification before proceeding to the next image. In this way, they were naturally and repeatedly exposed to the performance notification throughout the experimental task.

## Operationalizations of Habituation

To demonstrate that habituation generalizes from notifications to security warnings (H1), it is first necessary to establish that habituation has occurred (Rankin, 2009). We operationalized habituation in two ways to serve as a dependent variable. First, we measured habituation in terms of the participants’ reaction time, measured as the time between the display of a notification or warning and when a user responded to it. As individuals become habituated, responses become more automatic, with faster reaction times. This is because as individuals become habituated, they “rely more on the mental model of the warning as opposed to the actual warning presented, resulting in a decreased response” (Anderson et al., 2016b, p. 722). In addition, reaction time has previously been demonstrated to accurately measure habituation in both IS (Anderson et al., 2016b; Eargle, 2017) and neurobiology (Mackworth, 1968).

Second, because our goal in this research was to ultimately improve security behavior, our second dependent variable measured habituation in terms of its behavioral effect on users’ disregard of security warnings. Following habituation theory articulated by Vance et al. (2018), as habituation occurs to warnings, warning disregard behavior increases.<sup>6</sup> We operationalized security warning disregard as a binary variable that measures whether a participant disregards the warning by clicking on the less safe option (Jenkins et al., 2016; Vance et al., 2014). For the warnings in this experiment, this means selecting “Don’t allow” (Figure 3b) or “Cancel” (Figure 3c-e).

## Operationalizations of Generalization of Habituation

Because generalization is fundamental to habituation (Thompson & Spencer, 1966), both these concepts are often assessed together (Rankin et al., 2009). We operationalized generalization as the difference between responses in two separate experimental conditions: (1) the warning is displayed after a person has been habituated to the performance notification through repeated exposures and (2) the same warning is shown without seeing a similar notification (Figure 4). We measured disparities in responses, both in terms of response time and warning disregard, as defined previously, for our operationalization of habituation. If the response to the warning is lower after habituation to the performance notification compared to the response when the warning alone is seen, this is partial evidence that habituation has been generalized from the notification to the warning (Rankin et al., 2009).

## Ruling Out Fatigue

A rival explanation for habituation and generalization is neural fatigue (Thompson & Spencer, 1966); that is, people have a lower response because they (or, more specifically, their neurons) have depleted metabolic resources after repeated exposures to a stimulus. Following the neurobiology literature, neural fatigue can be distinguished from habituation by displaying a novel stimulus that is considerably different from the stimulus to which a participant has become habituated (Rankin et al., 2009), which we term a novel foil. First, a person becomes habituated to stimulus A through repeated exposures. If the response to a novel foil F recovers to the level of the response of A when it was first presented, then habituation, not fatigue, was the reason for the diminished response to A. Novel foils are used across various memory paradigms for similar comparisons. In this case, the novel foil stimulus acts as a “baseline” or “reset” to rule out the overall general fatigue of responding.

This approach can also be used to rule out fatigue in the case of generalization. If subjects reveal a diminished response to A through repeated exposures and a weak response to a novelthough-similar stimulus B, but a separate condition depicts a strong response to F, then this is strong evidence that the diminished response to B is due to generalization of habituation, not to fatigue (Rankin et al., 2009).

We used a novel foil in the form of a dialog window with a duck image designed to appear different from the performance notification to make generalization of habituation unlikely (Figure 5). Subsequently, we compared the reaction times to the novel foil message under two experimental conditions: (1) The novel foil is first seen before a notification and (2) the same foil is first seen after a person has habituated to a notification through repeated exposures. If the response to the novel foil message before seeing notifications is no different from that after having habituated to repeated notifications, then habituation, not fatigue, diminishes the response to the notification and the generalization of this effect to warnings (Rankin et al., 2009).

## Testing H1: Whether Generalization Occurs

We tested H1 by examining two dependent variables: (1) the natural log of the reaction time to the security warning<sup>7</sup> and (2) whether users disregarded the warning. Reaction time was transformed with the natural log because it was skewed, and the transformation normalized the distribution for analysis (Hammouri et al., 2020). The test examined whether participants who received the warning after 14 exposures to the performance notification were more likely to respond faster to the warning and disregard it compared to individuals who responded to the warning first without exposure to the performance notification.

For this analysis, we fitted two models, one logistic regression for disregard behavior (“disregard”) and one linear regression for the natural log of reaction time (see Table 3). Each model had just one predictor: the exposure position at which the warning was displayed, specified as a dummy-coded fixed effect (“0” for a warning alone and “1” for after 14 exposures to performance notifications).<sup>8</sup> The estimate of the exposure position at which the warning was displayed was statistically significant in both models in the hypothesized direction. Thus, the participants who viewed the warning after repeated notifications were 1.62 times more likely to disregard it and responded approximately 22% faster (4.6 s vs. 3.6 s). This suggests generalization of habituation, supporting H1.

## Ruling Out Fatigue

We tested whether users’ reaction times were caused by fatigue instead of generalization of habituation. To do this, we compared the reaction times to the novel foil when it was shown alone with the reaction times observed after repeated exposures to the performance notification. This was tested using the reaction time model information in the “Foil × After notifications” row of Table 4. While the reaction time trended toward being faster when shown after notifications than when shown alone, it was not statistically significant. Therefore, these reaction time results do not support the rival explanation of fatigue for H1.

## Ruling Out a Lack of Discrimination Between Notifications and Warnings

Experiment 1 demonstrated that participants habituated to repeated performance notifications, and this habituation generalized to novel security warnings. However, an alternative explanation for these results might be that subjects could not really differentiate between the performance notification and the security warning (i.e., the security warnings were not perceived as novel stimuli). Hence, they responded to them in the same way. To test this rival explanation, we conducted two additional analyses.

First, in addition to our 10 experimental treatments listed in Table 2, we also collected data for an 11th treatment $( n = 6 0 )$ which included only the performance notification (i.e., no warning or novel foil). The purpose of this treatment was to compare participants’ responses to treatments with and without warnings. Additionally, our post-survey included a measure of concern that presented the message received by the participants (i.e., one of the messages listed in Table 2 or the performance notification): “On a scale of 0 to 10, how concerned did the following screen make you feel during the Batman image classification task?” Participants rated each warning an average of 6.10-7.45, and the average level of concern for warnings was similar, irrespective of whether participants saw the warning at Exposure 1 or Exposure 15. However, compared to any of the warnings, the average concern for the performance notification was significantly lower at 3.50 $( p \ < \ 0 . 0 0 1$ in all t-test comparisons). Furthermore, the average concern for the performance notification was no different from that for the novel foil. This indicates that people could differentiate between the performance notification and security warnings, as measured by their reported concern.

Second, we conducted a supplementary robustness test experiment to examine whether participants could (1) form a distinct memory representation of the performance notification (Figure 3a) and (2) discriminate between the memory representation of the notification and the novel security warnings used in Experiment 1 (Figures 3b-e). In other words, we tested whether the individuals could recognize two types of messages—general notifications and security warnings—even though the stimuli appeared similar.

For this purpose, we leveraged the robust cognitive neuroscience literature on mnemonic discrimination by using a variation of a well-validated paradigm known as the mnemonic similarity task (MST). The MST was originally developed to test how memory representations of images of physical objects interfere with each other in the medial temporal lobe of the brain (Kirwan & Stark, 2007). Stark et al. (2019) identified more than 100 publications that used the MST to test people’s ability to distinguish between specific memories (i.e., memory specificity) in a wide range of populations, including healthy older adults, young children, and patients with schizophrenia, dementia, and autism. The MST is designed and validated to test the ability of people to form detailed, specific memory representations of individual stimuli and later discriminate between exact repeats from the study phase, similar but not exact stimuli from the study, or novel stimuli. Thus, the task structure of the MST, particularly the inclusion of similar but not identical stimuli, is well-suited to help answer the question of whether the subjects could discern subtle differences between similar-looking notification and warning stimuli.

Participants: We recruited 31 participants (mean age: 23.5, age range 18-58; 20 women) from the university community who participated in exchange for either course credit or \$10. One person was excluded from the final analysis due to the equipment’s failure to record behavioral responses (final n = 30). The respondents gave written informed consent before the study began. All study protocols were approved by the local institutional review board. Following the consent procedure, participants completed the MST on a computer in a laboratory setting after viewing a brief instructional video.

Procedure: The MST consists of a study phase, in which users view separate stimuli for memory encoding, and a test phase, in which participants must discriminate between stimuli. In the study phase, subjects viewed 30 images of computer notifications similar to those that might be encountered during a normal computer interaction (e.g., a “low battery” notification on a laptop computer). One of the stimuli presented to participants was the performance notification from the online field experiment (Figure 3a). To encourage the attention and encoding of each stimulus, subjects were instructed to indicate, by pressing a keyboard button, whether each stimulus was from the MacOS or Windows operating system. Stimuli were displayed for three seconds, followed by a blank screen for 1.25 seconds. Users were encouraged to respond while the stimulus was being displayed.

Following the study phase, participants immediately completed the test phase, in which they were shown 15 exact repeats (targets), including the performance notification from the online field experiment; 12 modified versions of the study stimuli (e.g., changed from the “light mode” theme to the “dark mode” theme); and 18 novel distractor stimuli (notifications that were distinct from the study’s stimuli), including the four security warnings in Experiment 1 (Figure 3b-e). For each stimulus, subjects were instructed to respond via a keyboard button, pressing “old” for repeats, “similar” for modified stimuli, and “new” for distractor stimuli. Test stimuli were presented for 3 s, followed by a blank screen for 1.25 s, and individuals were again instructed to respond while the stimulus was being displayed on the screen.

## Experiment 1: Summary

Experiment 1 supported H1 by illustrating that habituation to notifications generalized to security warnings with a similar visual appearance in terms of both higher warning disregard and shorter reaction times. Experiment 1 also supported H2, depicting that generalization of habituation occurred less for warnings with a distinctive visual appearance. Corroborating H2, we found that the warnings that were visually similar to the performance notification (the “add-on permission” and “location request” warnings) experienced a significant increase in warning disregard and faster response times, while warnings that were visually distinct (the “open macro” and “save executable” warnings) did not notably change on these measures. Furthermore, the results of Experiment 1 also failed to support the rival explanation of fatigue, as they showed no change in reaction time in response to the novel foil when shown alone or after repeated exposure to notifications.

Additionally, a supplemental experiment ruled out a rival explanation of participants being unable to discriminate between notifications and warnings. Individuals could consciously discriminate between the performance notification and security warnings when the task demands and instructions required this. However, because generalization occurs unconsciously, when task demands did not explicitly require attention to stimulus differences, as in Experiment 1, participants were confused between the messages’ security and non-security contexts, leading to disregard of the warning.

## Experiment 2: fMRI Experiment

The results of Experiment 1 showed a clear overall pattern of generalization of habituation, as participants responded more quickly to the security warnings after being repeatedly exposed to performance notification over time (supporting

H1). However, while the rival hypothesis of fatigue was not supported, as indicated by the comparison between reaction times to the novel foil in Experiment 1, another rival explanation for H1 is that participants’ faster reaction times could have been due to their becoming more cognitively engaged in the task over time, and therefore, quicker to dismiss notifications and less accurate at responding to warnings.

We conducted a follow-up fMRI experiment to (1) further rule out rival explanations, (2) test H3 to determine whether varying the mode of interaction could mitigate generalization of habituation, and (3) examine the effect of generalization by measuring decreased neural activity rather than response time and warning disregard. Regarding purpose (3), according to the philosophy of science, integrating findings across the behavioral and neural levels of analysis can provide a more complete view of phenomena (MacDougall-Shackleton, 2011; Marr, 1982).

## Experiment 2: Participants

We scanned 25 participants (mean age: 24 years, 14 women) who underwent fMRI while receiving repeated exposures to notifications and occasional exposures to warnings to confirm habituation and generalization in neural activity.<sup>9</sup> All participants were right-handed, native English speakers, had normal or corrected visual acuity, and self-reported being free of previous neurological and psychiatric disorders, in addition to not currently using any psychoactive medications. Participants were recruited through a flier circulated in the university community and provided written informed consent prior to participation in the experiment. They were compensated with either \$20 or a one-fourth-scale 3D-printed model of their brains. All procedures were approved by the local institutional review board.

## Experiment 2: fMRI Protocol

We followed the guidelines provided by Dimoka (2012) for conducting an fMRI study. Upon arrival, participants were screened to ensure MRI compatibility. They were verbally briefed about the MRI procedure and the task and were then positioned lying on their backs in the scanner. Once positioned in the scanner, participants completed a brief tutorial explaining the image classification task. However, they were not briefed on the security warnings. All stimuli were presented on an MRI-compatible liquid crystal display (LCD) monitor placed at the head of the bore of the MRI scanner and viewed through a mirror mounted on the head coil. Behavioral responses were collected with a fiber optic button box because mousing causes detrimental motion artifacts in the fMRI data.

We first performed a brief (10 s) localizer scan, followed by a T1-weighted structural scan. Following these scans, we started the experimental task, which was completed in one functional run. We used psiTurk (Eargle et al., 2021) to record participant keypresses and to display and record timestamps for stimuli in a web browser visible to participants on the LCD monitor. Timestamps were later synchronized with those recorded using MRI scanner software. All ex post tests revealed that none of the subjects needed to be excluded (e.g., due to abnormalities or excessive movement).

## Experiment 2: Task and Design

Participants performed the same Batman image classification task from Experiment 1, albeit adapted as a within-subject, repeated-measures design, which is commonly used in fMRI studies to maximize the number of measurements taken from each subject to obtain reliable estimates of their hemodynamic response (Dimoka, 2012). In this design, the participants were presented with sets of 2-10 images to classify,<sup>10</sup> with each classification choice by the participant followed by a performance notification (Dale, 1999). At the end of each set, participants received one more image to classify, followed by either a randomly selected security warning or a random novel foil (see Figure 8 for the experimental design).

For Experiment 2, we used three security warning designs, which were all variations of the “Firefox add-on permission warning” from Experiment 1 (see Figure 3c) and differed in their mode of interaction. The first mode-of-interaction variation was the standard button press, matching the mode of interaction of the performance notification (see Figure 9a). The second variation required participants to press and hold one of two buttons to move a slider (Figure 9b), and the third featured a drag-and-drop interaction that again required participants to press and hold one of the two buttons to move the slider (Figure 9c). The latter two warning designs were chosen because of their distinctiveness from the click-todismiss paradigm in order to break the mental schema of interaction. Each of these three warnings was shown 15 times, for a total of 45 sets.

## Operationalization of Habituation and Generalization

We operationalized habituation as decreased neural activity in response to a warning, as measured indirectly by tracking changes in blood oxygenation level, which are driven by changes in the metabolic demands of active neural populations. This phenomenon is known as the blood oxygen level dependent (BOLD) effect, and its magnitude is proportional to the degree of underlying neural activation (Logothetis et al., 2001). By measuring the BOLD effect, researchers can identify distinct regions of the brain where activity correlates with specific cognitive processes (e.g., perception or memory retrieval) and evaluate the degree of activation in these regions. Habituation has been widely observed in terms of a decreased neural response to a stimulus in all biological sciences (Rankin et al., 2009). Previous work in IS has also demonstrated habituation in terms of decreased neural activity with repeated exposure to security warnings (Anderson et al., 2016b; Vance et al., 2018). BOLD activation might also decrease with repeated presentations if the neurons in these brain regions become fatigued. In the case of fatigue, activation should be low for all stimuli, regardless of novelty or similarity to previous stimuli. Thus, if activation for novel foils is greater than that for repeated notifications, this is evidence against fatigue.

Therefore, if (1) the decreased neural response to a repeated performance notification (2) carries over to a novel security warning that is similar in appearance to the notification but (3) the neural response to a novel foil is still high after repeated exposure to the performance notification, then this is strong evidence that generalization of habituation has occurred (Rankin et al., 2009).

Following our within-subject design, a common test of generalization is to assert whether the neural response is weaker for the first exposure to the novel stimuli than for the first exposure to the initial stimuli (Grizzard et al., 2015). Therefore, a comparison with the immediately preceding exposure of the initial stimuli is a more stringent version of this test.

## Experiment 2: Analysis

## Testing for Habituation and Manipulation Check

As all our hypotheses involved generalization of habituation, we first identified brain regions that demonstrated habituation to the performance notifications. This was achieved by comparing changes in neural activity in the brain (i.e., a linear contrast) across the first 15 presentations of the notification (thus matching the number of presentations of each type of security warning). Four clusters in the brain demonstrated a clear pattern of habituation in the form of significant linear decreases in response to repeated presentations of the performance notification. These regions were the bilateral ventral visual stream, left dorsal visual stream, bilateral dorsomedial prefrontal cortex, and right inferior frontal gyrus (see Table B1 in Appendix B). Visual information is processed along two separate pathways in the brain: the dorsal and ventral streams. The ventral stream supports object perception, while the dorsal stream supports spatial awareness. The ventral pathway, often referred to as the “what” stream, is crucial for object identification and recognition, helping us to understand and categorize visual stimuli (Goodale & Milner, 1992). It begins in the occipital lobe and continues into the inferior temporal lobe and plays a key role in recognizing shapes, objects, and faces. This pathway underpins our ability to interpret the content of what we see. The bilateral ventral visual stream has been identified as a site of habituation using fMRI paradigms similar to those in previous studies (e.g., Anderson et al., 2016b; Kirwan et al., 2020; Vance et al., 2018). As this is a well-established site of habituation to visual stimuli, we focused our subsequent tests for generalization on this cluster (Figure 11; Berry et al., 2017). This also demonstrated that our experimental design successfully manipulated habituation.

## Testing H1: Whether Generalization Occurs

Having established habituation in the ventral visual processing stream in response to performance notifications, we next examined whether habituation to the performance notification would generalize to security warnings with a similar visual appearance (H1). To test this, we examined fMRI activation for each participant’s first presentation of the button-press warning (Figure 9a) compared to its immediately preceding performance notification activation. We observed significantly lower fMRI activation for the button-press warning relative to its immediately preceding performance notification, which suggests that habituation generalized to the button-press warning (t(23) = -3.071; p = 0.0025 one-tailed; 95% CI [-,0.62, -0.12]; Cohen’s d = 0.059), supporting H1.

## Ruling Out Cognitive Engagement and Fatigue

We first found that the linear activation decrease identified in the visual ventral processing stream region failed to support the rival hypothesis of increased cognitive engagement in Experiment 1. As Experiment 1 used highly similar tasks and the window of the first 15 notification exposures from the Experiment 2 task aligned with the maximum number of exposures in the Experiment 1 task, the evidence of decreased neural activity over repeated exposures in Experiment 2 failed to support the rival hypothesis of increased cognitive engagement, which explains the faster security warning reaction times in Experiment 1. If that had been the case, cognitive engagement would have shown an increase in activation for the region tested in this experiment (Berry et al., 2017).

We then tested whether the observed decrease in fMRI activation was due to fatigue instead of habituation. We compared the mean activations for all performance notifications with those for all novel foils. We reasoned that if fatigue caused a decrease in activation in response to performance notifications, then it would also decrease activation in response to the novel foils, given that the foils were randomly interspersed among the performance notifications. This would lead to no significant difference in activations between the performance notifications and novel foils. However, activation for all novel foils was significantly greater than that for all performance notifications (t(23) = 4.50; p < 0.0005 one-tailed; 95% CI [0.094, 0.253]), consistent with habituation theory, not the explanation of fatigue (Figure 12, “performance” vs. “novel foil”).

## Testing H3: Whether the Mode of Interaction Mitigates Generalization

Next, we examined whether habituation to the performance notification generalized less to security warnings with a distinctive mode of interaction (H3). To test this, we compared the mean fMRI activation within the ventral visual stream cluster for all presentations of each warning type and for the performance notifications, collapsed across all trials, using the Batman image classification activations as the baseline (Figure 11). Because the slider and drag-and-drop notifications had similar modes of interaction (i.e., press and hold a button on a controller in the MRI scanner), we collapsed these notification types for this analysis. We found that activation for the buttonpress warning type (Figure 9a) was not different from that of the performance notification, indicating a generalization of habituation between these two messages and further strengthening H1. In contrast, activation was significantly higher for the slider and drag-and-drop warning types than for the performance notification (t(23) = 3.83; p < 0.0005 onetailed; 95% CI [0.03, 0.1]; Cohen’s d = 0.083), which suggests a resistance to generalization of habituation for these types. In summary, the warnings with a distinctive mode of interaction (slider and drag-and-drop) showed substantially less generalization of habituation than the warning with the same mode of interaction (button press) as the performance notification. Therefore, Experiment 2 supported H3.

## Experiment 2: Summary

The tests in Experiment 2 effectively ruled out rival explanations of fatigue and cognitive engagement for H1. First, the general trend of decreasing neural activity across the experimental task did not support the rival explanation of increased cognitive engagement. Second, because the participants showed a diminished neural response to the performance notification and the modally similar button-press warning, the neural response to the novel foil was significantly higher than the response to the remaining messages. This presents strong evidence of generalization and not fatigue (Rankin et al., 2009). Experiment 3 also supported H3 by showing that habituation to the performance notification generalized less to the slider + drag-and-drop warnings with distinctive modes of interaction.

## Experiment 3: Online Field Experiment

Experiment 2 provided evidence that security warnings with distinctive modes of interaction are more resistant to generalization of habituation, supporting H3. However, it is difficult to change the mode of interaction of a warning without simultaneously changing its visual appearance. For example, in Experiment 2, the warnings with the press-and-hold and dragand-drop modes of interaction were more visually dissimilar to the performance notification than the extension warning because of the differences in the UI widgets particular to those modes of interaction. Therefore, it is not possible from the results of Experiment 2 alone to distinguish between the effects of changing (a) the visual appearance and (b) the mode of interaction. To differentiate between these effects, we conducted another field experiment (Experiment 3) to test H3 while holding the visual appearance constant.

## Experiment 3: Participants

We recruited 241 participants from MTurk (mean age: 39 years, 54% male). Seven outlier participants were removed based on their extreme reaction times in the task, leaving 234 participants. As in Experiment 1, we followed the guidelines provided by Steelman et al. (2014), which required all participants to be from the United States. As in Experiment 1, the participants were paid \$1.50 (\$1 plus a \$.50 performance bonus) for completing a 5-minute task.

## Experiment 3: Experimental Task and Treatments

The experimental task for Experiment 3 was essentially the same as that for Experiment 1. However, in contrast to Experiment 1, the experiment involved only a performance notification and a permission warning. To hold the visual appearance constant as much as possible, we incorporated both a traditional button and a disabled slider widget into the performance notification and permission warning (see Figures 13a-b). In this way, we were able to keep the visual appearance constant, except for slightly fading a button or slider widget to indicate its disabled status. To increase the realism of this new notification design, we started with the standard Firefox HTML5-style notification and incorporated an actual slider from the Firefox setting dialogs, following the Firefox UI guidelines (e.g., for positioning of labels and length of the slider line). Our pilot testing indicated that the participants understood how to interact with the different configurations.

The participants were randomly assigned to one of four treatments (Table 6). ## Experiment 3: Analysis

## Testing for Habituation and Manipulation Check

As with Experiment 1, we verified that participants in the performance notification treatment experienced habituation. Figure 14 shows a clear pattern of habituation to the performance notification in terms of reaction time, with participants responding 18% faster at Exposure 29 than at Exposure 1 $( p \texttt { < } 0 . 0 5 )$ This demonstrates that the manipulation of habituation was successful.

## Testing H3: Whether the Mode of Interaction Mitigates Generalization

Similar to the H2 test in Experiment 1, we examined two dependent variables: (1) the natural log of reaction time to the security warning and (2) whether participants disregarded the warning. We fitted models for both dependent variables, with predictors representing the different warning types. Both models specified the main effect of the warning type and the interaction of each warning type with whether it appeared at Exposure 30. To facilitate model interpretation, neither the main effect of the exposure position nor the model intercept was specified. In Table 7, the estimates for each interaction term indicate whether evidence of generalization existed for that warning type.

The results show that the warning with the button enabled was 2.8 times more likely to be disregarded when shown at Exposure 30 than at Exposure 1. Participants also responded 32% faster to this warning when seen at Exposure 30 than at Exposure 1 (approximately 1.2 s faster).

By contrast, the warning with the slider enabled was not more likely to be disregarded at Exposure 30 than at Exposure 1 (the odds ratio was not significantly different from 1; see Figure 15). Likewise, participants did not respond faster to this warning at Exposure 30 than at Exposure 1 (see Figure 14). These results strongly indicate that habituation generalized less to warnings with a distinctive mode of interaction, supporting H3.

## Experiment 3: Summary

In summary, Experiment 3 revealed strong support for H3, which states that habituation to notifications will generalize less to novel security warnings with a distinctive mode of interaction. For the button-enabled warning with the same mode of interaction as the performance notification, the likelihood of warning disregard increased, while the reaction time decreased between Exposures 1 and 30. By contrast, the slider-enabled warning with a distinctive mode of interaction showed no significant difference between Exposures 1 and 30, neither for warning disregard nor reaction time. Most importantly, these results were obtained while holding visual appearance essentially constant.

## General Discussion

Past studies have shown that people habituate to individual security warnings. This study broadens past findings by showing that through generalization, habituation to notifications carries over to security warnings. In our study, this occurred despite participants being able to consciously discern that notifications belonged to a non-security context while warnings belonged to a security context. However, when task demands did not explicitly require conscious differentiation of the two, generalization of habituation occurring unconsciously at the neurobiological level led to participants confusing warnings with notifications, leading to warning disregard. These findings suggest that the problem of habituation to security warnings is much larger in scope than previously recognized because people may already be deeply habituated to a security warning that they have never seen before due to its similarities with other common notifications. ## Contributions Relative to RQ1

How does habituation to frequent non-security-related notifications generalize to infrequent security warnings? This study makes contributions relative to our first question about our theoretical and empirical understanding of generalization of habituation. First, this study makes a theoretical contribution by employing the concept of generalization of habituation. Although previous studies have effectively used habituation theory to explain how people habituate to repeated warnings over time, they only examined habituation to individual stimuli and did not contemplate habituation spanning novel stimuli. This is an important gap because generalization of habituation is “a critical aspect of habituation” (Rankin et al., 2009, p. 4) and “essential to the dual-process theory [of habituation]” (Groves & Thompson, 1970, p. 442). There are at least two reasons for this. First, observing generalization shows the full extent of the influence of habituation. As habituation grows stronger, it generalizes more to novel but similar stimuli (Thompson, 2009). Second, generalization can be used to differentiate habituation from sensory fatigue (Rankin et al., 2009). Without examining generalization, it is difficult to know if habituation or fatigue is at work. By contrast, showing that generalization of habituation ${ \mathrm { i } } { \mathrm { s } } ,$ in effect, followed by a recovery of response to a distinctively novel stimulus, provides strong evidence that the diminished response is caused by habituation and not fatigue.

Second, the theoretical property of generalization has important implications for security warning theory and practice. For example, prior research has shown that people give full attention to a warning the first time it is seen and that habituation gradually causes attention to decrease with later repetitions of the warning. According to the literature, to preserve the efficacy of warnings, the number of times a warning is displayed should be reduced to a minimum (Acer et al., 2017; Akhawe & Felt, 2013; Krol et al., 2012; Zeng et al., 2019). However, the identification of the mechanism of generalization revealed that this approach will not work in an environment with frequent and similar notifications, even if a warning has never been seen before. Thus, the problem of habituation is much more serious than previous research has suggested. A practical implication is that software developers should design security warnings such that they are distinct from common notifications and thus stand out when first displayed.

Third, prior research has empirically established that people habituate to individual warnings. However, past research has not examined whether habituation to a notification can generalize to a never-before-seen security warning. Although security scholars have long pointed to the problem of people’s automated reactions to notifications carrying over to security warnings (e.g., Böhme & Köpsell, 2010; Vance et al., 2018; West, 2008), empirical evidence for this phenomenon and how it occurs was absent.

We addressed this problem through two field studies (Experiments 1 and 3) and one fMRI laboratory experiment (Experiment 2), and show that habituation, in fact, can generalize from notifications to security warnings that have never been seen before (H1 supported). In Experiment 1, participants responded 30%-39% faster to a novel warning and were 1.95-2.6 times more likely to disregard it after habituating to a notification than when they received the warning without prior exposure to the notification. Likewise, these results are supported neurally in Experiment $^ { 2 , }$ which provides a more comprehensive view of generalization than is possible by measuring behavior alone (MacDougall-Shackleton, 2011; Marr, 1982). We also ruled out rival explanations of fatigue (Experiments 1 and 2) and cognitive engagement (Experiment 2). Furthermore, in a supplementary robustness test to Experiment 1, we demonstrated that people could consciously discriminate between performance notifications and security warnings when the task demands. These tests support the explanation of generalization.

Fourth, this research also contributes to UI design. A longstanding principle of UI design is consistency in appearance and function (Apple, 2025; Google, 2025; Krug, 2014; Microsoft, 2025). However, using the concept of generalization from habituation theory, we demonstrate that this widely accepted UI principle poses a threat to users by promoting generalization of habituation and desensitizing them to even rare security warnings. Furthermore, because adherence to this UI principle is widespread, so is the threat of generalization of habituation. Therefore, this research opens new avenues for research, pointing the way for researchers and practitioners to develop and test security warning designs that are resistant to generalization of habituation and reduce security warning disregard.

## Contributions Relative to RQ2

How can generalization to security warnings be reduced? This research also makes contributions relative to our second research question about how warnings can be designed to reduce the occurrence of generalization of habituation. First, repeatedly changing the appearance of a warning (i.e., a polymorphic warning) decreases habituation to that warning (Anderson et al., 2016a, 2016b; Vance et al., 2018). However, it remains unknown whether a polymorphic warning can prevent generalization of habituation from common notifications to a single presentation of a never-seen-before warning. Furthermore, because the conditions under which generalization occurs in the context of warnings were not previously understood, it was unknown which interventions would be most effective in reducing generalization.

By contrast, we developed two interventions based on the property of generalization from the dual-process theory of habituation and schema theory that specifically address the problem of generalization of habituation. Importantly, neither of these interventions is derivable from past work, as explained further below. These theory-driven interventions can guide software developers and designers in creating solutions that reduce generalization of habituation for individuals. In turn, helping individuals improve their security in this way can also improve the security of the organizations to which they belong, which rely on secure systems for their daily operations. Importantly, both interventions are effective in reducing generalization without imposing costs on users (Herley, 2009), such as mandatory delays or effort-intensive tasks (e.g., CAPTCHAs).

Second, the polymorphic warning design of past research manipulated the continued novelty of the visual appearance of security warnings over time. This is a different problem than ensuring that security warnings are resistant to generalization of habituation on their first appearance. For example, prior research has not stipulated any requirement for polymorphic warnings to be distinct from notifications; they only require polymorphic warnings to be sufficiently different from their last presentation to continue capturing attention. Likewise, past research has found no provision for the initial polymorphic warning presentation to be distinct from notifications.

By contrast, we focus on the visual distinctiveness of security warnings specifically in comparison to notifications upon the first exposure to a security warning. As we show in this study, because of generalization of habituation, security warnings must be visually distinct from notifications when first encountered. We demonstrate that visual distinctiveness can reduce generalization at the warning’s first appearance (Experiment 1, H2 supported).

Third, it is unknown from prior research whether non-visual interventions can reduce habituation to security warnings. These interventions are useful when visual interventions are not possible or desirable (e.g., due to a policy for UI consistency). By contrast, in this study, we drew on schema theory (Rumelhart, 1980) to hypothesize that making the mode of interaction of a warning distinct from the conventional “click-to-dismiss” paradigm will break the automatic, muscle-memory schema of how to physically respond to notifications. This forces the user to consciously attend to the warning so that warnings with a distinctive mode of interaction will exhibit less generalization. Our results show that changing the mode of interaction is also effective in reducing generalization, even when controlling for differences in visual similarity (Experiments 2 and 3; H3 supported). In Experiment 2, warnings with a slider or drag-and-drop mode of interaction exhibited substantially less generalized habituation, as measured by neural activation, compared to warnings with the standard “click-to-dismiss” mode. In Experiment 3, after exposing the participants to a series of notifications, we found that those who received a warning with the “click-to-dismiss” mode of interaction responded 38% faster and were 2-2.8 times more likely to disregard the warning than those who received the warning with the slider mode. Additionally, those who received the slider-mode warnings showed no indication of generalization of habituation from repeated notifications.

## Limitations and Future Research

Our research has certain limitations that also suggest future research opportunities. First, our experiments were conducted in a browser setting. While we anticipate that the results will apply to other devices and applications, where notifications, and thereby generalization, may be even more prevalent, further research is required to confirm this. Second, this study focused on the influence of habituation occurring at the neurobiological level on security warning disregard. However, we did not examine the extent to which higher-order processes—such as motivation, recall of training, and expertise, or conversely irritation—can influence or override the lower-order process of habituation. Future research should examine whether users’ knowledge and expertise can enable them to detect novel information and heed warnings despite the effects of habituation. Third, this study examined the traditional mousing and point-andclick UI paradigm; however, there are also other UI paradigms, such as audio and augmented and virtual reality. Future research should examine how our results extend to these and other UI paradigms. Fourth, although we illustrate that visual similarity influences the extent to which generalization of habituation occurs, we do so with four warnings and a single notification in Experiment 1. Future research could theorize about and measure the degree of similarity between warnings and notifications to determine the threshold at which generalization is likely to occur. One possible approach could be to test the degrees of visual similarity between notifications and warnings.

Another interesting area of future research would be examining how notifications across other devices (phones, smartwatches, etc.) throughout the day or within a certain period could influence generalization of habituation to security warnings. Following the experimentation model proposed by Vance et al. (2018), a longitudinal study examining generalization of habituation over time, either in a lab or a field setting, would likely provide interesting results.

## Conclusion

Generalization of habituation is a serious problem because it causes users to ignore important security warnings, even if the warning has never been seen before, simply because they have become inured to visually or modally similar non-securityrelated notifications. This can happen even when people consciously differentiate between notifications and warnings and discern that they belong to non-security and security contexts, respectively. This paper provides empirical evidence for this phenomenon. Through two online field experiments and an fMRI laboratory experiment, we demonstrated that the effect of generalization can be reduced by changing either the visual appearance or the mode of interaction of a security warning to distinguish it from common and benign notifications. These solutions can enable software developers to create visually and interactively distinct security warnings that circumvent the neurobiological effects of generalization and create space for more conscious deliberation, thus facilitating greater adherence to security warnings.
