# JUDGMENTAL BOT: CONVERSATIONAL AGENTS IN ONLINE MENTAL HEALTH SCREENING<sup>1</sup>

Ryan Raimi Information Systems Area, Naveen Jindal School of Management The University of Texas at Dallas, Richardson, TX, U.S.A {ryan.raimi@utdallas.edu}

Antino Kim Operations & Decision Technologies Department, Kelley School of Business Indiana University, Bloomington, IN, U.S.A {antino@iu.edu}

Sezgin Ayabakan Management Information Systems Department, Fox School of Business Temple University, Philadelphia, PA, U.S.A {ayabakan@temple.edu}

Alan R. Dennis Operations & Decision Technologies Department, Kelley School of Business Indiana University, Bloomington, IN, U.S.A {ardennis@iu.edu}

Only a fraction of people with mental health issues seek medical care, in part because of fear of judgment, so deploying text-based conversational agents (i.e., chatbots) for mental health screening is often viewed as a way to lower barriers to mental health care. We conducted four experiments and a qualitative study and, contrary to common assumptions, consistently found that participants perceived a text-based chatbot as more judgmental than a human mental health care professional, even though the interactions were identical. This greater judgmentalness reduced the willingness to use the service, disclose information, and follow the agent’s recommendations. Participants described judgmentalness as a rush to judgment without fully grasping the issues. The chatbot was perceived as more judgmental because it was less capable of deeply understanding the issues (e.g., emotionally and socially) and conveying a sense of being heard and validated. It has long been assumed that chatbots can address the real or imagined fear of being judged by others for stigmatized conditions like mental health. Our study shows that perceptions of judgmentalness are actually the opposite of what has been assumed and that these perceptions significantly influence patients’ acceptance of chatbots for mental health screening.

Keywords: Chatbot, conversational agent, judgmentalness, mental health, stigma, experiment, interview, grounded theory, mixed methods, theory development, theory testing, construct building, construct validation

## Introduction

The mental health crisis has seen a dramatic increase over the last few decades and is considered a silent epidemic of the 21st century (Morawska, 2019). By 2030, a major shortage of mental health resources and personnel is projected, making it important to identify other ways of delivering mental health services (Larson, 2021). One option is to use conversational agents controlled by artificial intelligence (AI), such as textbased chatbots (Dahiya, 2017). Chatbots are commonly used for commercial customer service processes (Følstad & Skjuve, 2019) and are increasingly being deployed in healthcare (Nadarzynski et al., 2019).

One application of chatbots is for mental health screening (i.e., assessing whether individuals should seek treatment), which requires fewer skills and less training than mental health treatment (Auerbach et al., 2007; Nash, 2023). The screening process is highly structured and usually involves users answering a set of questions (e.g., Patient Health Questionnaire-9—PHQ-9) to recommend a course of action (Abd-Alrazaq et al., 2019; Ghosh et al., 2018; Lee et al., 2017). Using chatbots for mental health screening is one way to meet the surge in demand for mental health services, especially for those who perceive mental health problems to be stigmatized (Stiefel, 2018). Users who associate high levels of stigma with mental health issues may be reluctant to discuss their concerns with a human agent because they may perceive humans to be judgmental about their conditions (Stiefel, 2018; Waskow, 1963). In contrast, a “judgment-free” chatbot (Aggarwal et al., 2023; Lucas et al., 2014) does not make moral judgments (Lucas et al., 2014; Skjuve et al., 2021); indeed, a survey by Oracle (2020) showed that many people would prefer talking about mental health issues with a chatbot rather than their manager.

We also theorized that a chatbot would be perceived to be less judgmental than a human mental health care professional; however, our findings contradicted this expectation. We began with two experiments (Study 1 comprised a main study and a robustness check) examining user reactions to a mental health screening service designed to help users decide whether or not to seek medical treatment. In both experiments, users perceived the chatbot to be more judgmental than a human mental health care professional, even though the interaction was identical. This perception of greater judgmentalness influenced subsequent actions: users were less willing to use the chatbot, disclose information to it, and follow its recommendations to seek treatment.

These results about judgmentalness run counter to prior theory and challenge our fundamental understanding of how users perceive chatbots. How can a chatbot be perceived as more judgmental when it has no personal opinions or moral standards? To better understand what it means to be judgmental in this context, we took a step back and conducted a qualitative interview study (Study 2). This study suggested that judgmentalness is better understood as “quick to make misinformed judgments” (Arday, 2018, p. 12), and provided three theoretical reasons to explain why users perceived chatbots to be more judgmental: They lacked real-life experiences, they were less able to develop a deep understanding of the issues facing the user (e.g., emotional and social meanings), and they did not convey to users a sense that they were being heard and validated.

We conducted two additional experiments (Study 3) to understand the relative importance of these three theoretical mechanisms. We again found that users perceived the chatbot to be more judgmental and found that two of the three theoretical mechanisms influenced judgmentalness: the lack of deep understanding and an inability to make users feel heard and validated. While a chatbot can provide information, it cannot truly understand human emotions or provide the same emotional support and validation as a human (Smith et al., 2015). Thus, a chatbot may be perceived as more likely to make quick, misinformed judgments when the situation requires a deep understanding of emotions and social meanings and validation of the user.

Our study makes three contributions. First, contrary to what has been assumed (but not tested) in past research, our experiments consistently showed that users perceived a chatbot to be more judgmental than a human mental health care professional and that these perceptions made them less willing to use it, disclose information to it, and follow its recommendations. Thus, the prevailing theory about chatbots (as well as conversational agents and AI more generally) needs to be revised, and future research needs to examine other contexts to identify the boundary conditions of our findings. Second, our interview study and two subsequent experiments provided the theoretical mechanisms underlying this effect. Chatbots are perceived as more judgmental because they lack a deep understanding of the user’s issues (especially the social and emotional meanings) and are unable to provide users with the same sense of being heard and validated. Thus, the prevailing theory about the theoretical mechanisms that affect judgmentalness needs to be revised to better capture how users view it. Finally, our experiments showed identical behavior by the human and chatbot; the chatbot performed at the same level of competence as the human. Thus, focusing solely on building better chatbots is necessary but not sufficient; the issues and their potential resolution lie in a better understanding of the human user.

## Prior Research and Theory

Chatbots are text-based conversational agents that interact using natural language (Dahiya, 2017). Chatbots are scalable (Rai et al., 2019) and can thus be quickly launched to meet a surge in demand when there is a shortage of qualified human agents. They can provide round-the-clock service at a low operational cost compared to human agents (Rai et al., 2019). Rule-based chatbots that use structured questionnaires are consistent in that they provide essentially the same results in response to the same inputs and can be easily revised in the face of rapidly changing information (AbuShawar & Atwell, 2016). They can be deployed on smartphones and laptops across a wide variety of applications (Rai et al., 2019). Chatbots are rapidly increasing in popularity, especially as firms begin to use chatbots powered by generative AI (Chang et al., 2022).

Chatbots are being deployed in healthcare for a variety of applications, and many healthcare organizations have begun deploying them for healthcare screening (Lattie et al., 2019). One critical area is mental health care screening, which has seen a dramatic increase in demand for services. The prevalence of depression and anxiety has increased by 50% over the past three decades (Stecher et al., 2024; Teepe et al., 2023). The COVID-19 pandemic exacerbated this situation, significantly increasing depression and anxiety (WHO, 2023). As a result, many healthcare providers are unable to meet the demand for services (Caron, 2021).

In this paper, we examine the use of chatbots for mental health screening, not mental health treatment. The screening agent is typically a healthcare worker not certified to provide treatment (Nash, 2023), such as a social worker, nurse, or other healthcare professional (Auerbach et al., 2007; Medline Plus, 2021). Screening strives to use a consistent process for assessing whether the user needs any medical treatment (e.g., using PHQ-9) and, when needed, referring the user to a healthcare provider for treatment (Dennis et al., 2020).

Many people believe that mental health issues carry a negative stigma (Eisenberg et al., 2009). Members of a stigmatized group are often treated poorly (Link & Phelan, 2001), including prejudice, stereotyping, and discrimination (Mojtabai & Olfson, 2020). Stigma reduces help-seeking behavior by individuals suffering from mental health issues (Clement et al., 2015). About half of all people with mental health disorders do not seek treatment (Mental Health America, 2022) due to a fear of being judged (Aggarwal et al., 2023; Dosovitsky & Bunge, 2021; Skjuve et al., 2021, 2022). Chatbots are assumed to be nonjudgmental (de Gennaro et al., 2020; Lucas et al., 2014), so they may offer important benefits to individuals reluctant to talk with a human agent.

## Judgmentalness

The fear of being judged by others is a major deterrent to seeking mental health treatment (Clement et al., 2015; Divin et al., 2018; Gulliver et al., 2010). A judgmental person is someone who is not understanding but instead is quick to criticize (Stiefel, 2018; Waskow, 1963). The most common antecedents of judgmentalness are the differences in world views, personal experiences, cultures, and values (Mathieu, 2019). These differences, rooted in values and experiences, can lead to ethnocentric superiority sentiments (Hall, 1976) and increase the perceptions of being judged. In a mental health setting, people with mental health disorders can experience negative judgments from others with different experiences and views (Tran et al., 2019).

People working for mental health screening services are trained to be nonjudgmental, understanding, and accepting (Gustafson et al., 2021; Milicevic et al., 2016; Rosenberg & Argentzell, 2018). Prior research shows that being nonjudgmental can improve the counseling experience for patients (Dimeff & Linehan, 2001; Dreyfus, 2013; Shaddock, 2000). If the agent exhibits a judgmental attitude, individuals are less likely to seek help (Gulliver et al., 2010). Individuals with mental health issues often avoid seeking treatment if they perceive the counselor to be judgmental about their condition (Daley et al., 2020; Stiefel, 2018).

If the agent is judgmental, individuals are less likely to disclose personal information (Clement et al., 2015; Gulliver et al., 2010). A nonjudgmental listener decreases a speaker’s self-expression concerns and enables the speaker to express their thoughts more clearly (Itzchakov et al., 2017; Kernis & Goldman, 2006). Therefore, being nonjudgmental can lead to a more insightful conversation (Dimeff & Linehan, 2001; Dreyfus, 2013; Shaddock, 2000) because people feel more comfortable sharing sensitive information without feeling ashamed or insecure (Lee et al., 2020; Stiefel, 2018). Prior research also shows that a patient’s compliance with a clinician’s recommendations depends on the therapeutic alliance between the two (Julius et al., 2009), which is contingent upon the clinician’s ability to listen, comprehend, and accept the patient’s perspective on their condition (Gabbard, 2014; Swanson et al., 1999).

In summary, the perceived judgmentalness of the agent (be it human or chatbot) decreases an individual’s willingness to seek help, disclose sensitive information necessary for screening, and follow the recommended next steps for treatment (Aggarwal et al., 2023; Lucas et al., 2014; Skjuve et al., 2022; Stiefel, 2018). Therefore, we hypothesize that:

H1: Perceived judgmentalness has a negative effect on the willingness to (a) use the screening service, (b) disclose personal information, and (c) follow the agent’s recommendations.

## Chatbots for Mental Health Care

Chatbots are well-suited for mental health screening because this process is highly structured and requires users to answer a set of questions in a predetermined sequence (Ghosh et al., 2018; Lee et al., 2017). Prior research shows that chatbots can triage patients in contexts such as the COVID-19 pandemic and healthcare readmissions and that they usually perform similarly to or better than humans (Ghosh et al., 2018; Lai et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2021). Many people do not seek treatment for mental health (Mental Health America, 2022) because they fear being judged (Chaix et al., 2019; Lucas et al., 2014).

Thus, offering a screening service by a chatbot may reduce the fear of being judged and encourage people to seek treatment (Joerin et al., 2019; Miner et al., 2019).

Judgmentalness: Social and cultural experiences give rise to different values (Hall, 1976), and these differences in fundamental values can trigger moral judgments that form quick automatic assessments and intuitions (Haidt, 2001; Tran et al., 2019). As chatbots lack cultural values, they should be less judgmental (Aggarwal et al., 2023; Lucas et al., 2014; Skjuve et al., 2022); thus, users are expected to trust them without worrying about being judged (de Gennaro et al., 2020; Lucas et al., 2014). Since users should be less likely to feel shame or embarrassment when they disclose information to a chatbot (vs. a human) because it is programmed to avoid moral judgments (Lee et al., 2020; Stiefel, 2018), they may be more willing to disclose sensitive content and discuss embarrassing topics with a chatbot because they should perceive them to be less judgmental (Grudin & Jacques, 2019; Ruan et al., 2019; Zamora, 2017).

To understand how prior research assessed the perceived judgmentalness of chatbots, we conducted a systematic literature review using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Moher et al., 2009). The search identified 61 papers examining chatbot judgmentalness, but no papers met the two criteria in Table 1; see Appendix A for details. These two criteria are crucial because whether a chatbot is “more judgmental” is incomplete without a clear and appropriate benchmark for comparison (i.e., more judgmental than who?). For instance, if we compare the chatbot to a human exhibiting judgmental behavior, the outcome is predictable, as users will intuitively view the less judgmental chatbot more favorably. Similarly, if we fail to control for the agents’ behavior— whether human or chatbot—and allow users to rely on their imagination, their responses may be shaped by personal experiences and expectations about how such agents may behave. In our research context, the most appropriate benchmark for a mental health screening chatbot is a human mental health care professional. Rather than measuring users’ expectations of how the agents might behave, we focus on assessing perceptions of actual behavior presented by both the chatbot and the human mental health care professional.

By contrast, most prior research has compared the chatbot to a friend or family member or an unspecified human, while virtually none have considered the professional healthcare context where humans and chatbots are bound by strong data privacy laws (e.g., HIPAA). The one paper that came the closest to our research context (Tong et al., 2024) did not meet the inclusion criteria because it asked users about the expectation of judgmentalness (“I feel that the physician/AI virtual doctor/AI-human hybrid would judge me because of my depression symptoms”) not the judgmentalness exhibited by the human healthcare provider or chatbot in a specific interaction. The key takeaway is that prior research shows that users expect chatbots to be less judgmental than the humans in their lives (e.g., friends, family members, and employers), but no prior empirical research has assessed the judgmentalness displayed by chatbots compared to the judgmentalness displayed by professional healthcare providers in the context of mental health care.

The belief that chatbots lack judgmentalness represents an important factor contributing to their popularity in the healthcare context (Parviainen & Rantala, 2022; Vijayarani & Balamurugan, 2019) and mental health in particular (de Almeida & da Silva, 2022; Dosovitsky & Bunge, 2021; Greer et al., 2019; Harmon, 2021; Martinengo et al., 2022; Shah et al., 2022). Much research has examined the use of chatbots as relational companions that provide emotional support, and such products (e.g., Replika) have garnered millions of users because they promise to be nonjudgmental. Studies suggest that such chatbots’ nonjudgmental nature may help build trust and facilitate relationship development (Medhi Thies et al., 2017; Ruan et al., 2019; Skjuve et al., 2021). Prior studies suggest that the nonjudgmental nature of chatbots is one of their most beneficial and sought-after characteristics (Grudin & Jacques, 2019; Ruan et al., 2019; Zamora, 2017). Thus, we theorize that chatbots will be perceived to be less judgmental than humans:

H2: Users perceive chatbots to be less judgmental than human agents.

Stigma: The perception of negative social judgment is one of the stigma-related barriers reported most frequently, and prior studies have underscored the fear of being judged and its close connection to stigma as major obstacles to seeking help for mental health issues (Clement et al., 2015; Divin et al., 2018). For example, Clement et al. (2015) found that stigma is a barrier to care for 21% to 23% of participants across shame, negative social judgment, and employment-related discrimination. Thus, individuals who view mental health issues as more stigmatized are more likely to perceive caregivers as judgmental, triggering treatment avoidance (Kurzban & Leary, 2001; Link & Phelan, 2001).

Chatbots may have an advantage over human agents in alleviating stigma-related problems surrounding mental health issues. Chatbots do not carry cultural norms and are therefore more likely to be perceived as nonjudgmental (Greer et al., 2019; Lovejoy, 2019; Martinengo et al., 2022). People who associate greater stigma with mental health issues would be less likely to feel embarrassment when they disclose their personal issues to a chatbot as compared to a human (Lee et al., 2020; Skjuve et al., 2021; Stiefel, 2018). Thus, people who feel greater stigma would likely perceive greater judgmentalness when interacting with a human than a chatbot (Aggarwal et al., 2023; Dosovitsky & Bunge, 2021; Lucas et al., 2014; Skjuve et al., 2021).

Therefore, we theorize that individuals who view mental health issues as highly stigmatized will perceive humans as more judgmental than chatbots:

H3: Stigma moderates the relationship in H2 such that increased stigma increases the perceived judgmentalness of a human agent relative to a chatbot.

## Study 1: Effects on Judgmentalness

The research framework is summarized in Figure 1. We theorized that a chatbot would be perceived as being less judgmental than a human mental health care professional, especially by those who perceive mental health issues to be stigmatized. This reduced judgmentalness would increase the user’s intention to use the screening service and their willingness to disclose information and follow its recommendations. There may also be other theoretical factors about the chatbot (both positive and negative) that could influence these outcomes. Although we theorized that judgmentalness is an important factor, we do not claim it is the only theoretical factor at work. Thus, we theorized partial mediation by judgmentalness and included the chatbot’s main effect in our empirical model.

## Method

Participants: Users of mental health screening services include those with and without a history of mental health conditions (Payne et al., 2003; Peppou et al., 2021). The rates of mental health issues among individuals using U.S. mental health screening services are similar to rates among the U.S. population (about 20% need some treatment, with 5% having potentially serious illnesses). Most users seeking mental health screening need no formal healthcare: 80% of users contacting U.S. mental health screening services are advised that they need no treatment, 4% receive a referral to treatment for mental illness, and the rest receive a recommendation for a brief treatment intervention.<sup>2</sup> Thus, a reasonable sampling frame is the U.S. population.

Accordingly, we recruited 300 participants from Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk) in compliance with standard protocols to ensure data quality (Steelman et al., 2014): We only recruited participants from the U.S. who had completed 1000 or more prior tasks (i.e., “HITs”) with a quality rating above 95%. MTurk states that “all users are validated and unique in order to protect the integrity of survey study results,”<sup>3</sup> for example, by providing valid and unique U.S. identity documents as required by the U.S. income tax law. Participants were paid \$2.00. Ten participants failed one or more of the five attention check questions and were removed, leaving 290 for analysis. We note that multiple studies in both JAMA and New England Journal of Medicine (among other medical journals) use MTurk to recruit participants (Creadore et al., 2021; Green et al., 2015; Mortensen & Hughes, 2018;

Solnick et al., 2020; Woloshin et al., 2022). Also, recent publications have concluded that MTurk users can be used in behavioral studies like ours (Lovett et al., 2018; Porter et al., 2019).

Our study was an online experiment with random assignment to treatment, which controlled for differences among participants. We note that the composition of our sample was fairly similar to those of earlier studies and national surveys for age, gender, income (Statista, 2019; Walters et al., 2018), and race (United States Census Bureau, 2019). For instance, in our final sample, half of the participants (49%) were female; 85% were White, 8% Black, 6% Asian, and 1% other. The mean age was 43.5, with a range of 25-76. The median category for household income was \$50K-\$75K. Our data showed that 55% of participants had previous personal experience with depression or anxiety, and 63% had a loved one with such prior experience, which aligns with U.S. population trends (Kessler et al., 2005, 2012). Accordingly, our analyses also controlled for differences in prior mental health experience. A power analysis using G\*Power (Faul et al., 2007) showed that the design had a power of 95% to find a medium-sized effect (f = 0.25) and 78% to find a small effect (f = 0.15).

Task: Participants were asked to assess a mental health screening service purportedly provided by Johns Hopkins Hospital (they were informed of the deception at the end of the study). Participants watched a 2.5-minute video vignette of a mental health screening session between an agent and a user with depression symptoms (see Appendix B). In the video, a user exhibiting depression symptoms interacted with an agent and received a medical referral recommendation. We developed the vignette based on a commonly used depression measurement scale (PHQ-9) (Kroenke et al., 2001) and publicly available mental health screening services.<sup>4</sup> PHQ-9 is a reliable and valid measure of depression severity and a useful clinical screening tool (Kroenke et al., 2001). The focus was on providing information, but it also included providing emotional support where appropriate. For example, when the user says, “Just feeling a little down lately, and that’s been going on for a bit too long,” the agent responds, “Oh, I’m so sorry to hear that, but I’m glad you decided to reach out.” A practicing psychiatrist with experience in mental health screening ensured that the vignette was representative of a good mental health screening session.

It is common to use vignettes to study human behavior and technology use (Cram et al., 2019; Shaw et al., 2003), and meta-analyses show that using vignettes results in the same research conclusions as studying actual behavior and technology use (Cram et al., 2019; Shaw et al., 2003). One major benefit of using vignettes is the higher level of control they offer. As participants see the identical event, with the only change being the desired manipulation (Dennis et al., 2012; Shaw et al., 2003), their perceptions are less contaminated by extraneous factors than in traditional experiments (Cummings & Dennis, 2018; Greenberg & Eskew, 1993). This is particularly important given the sensitive nature of the mental health context, where even subtle differences in wording can affect both the likelihood of individuals seeking help and the quality of support they receive (Volkow et al., 2021). However, the effect sizes in vignette studies are usually smaller than in studies where participants directly interact with the object of study (Mullen & Hu, 1989), making them conservative estimates of the real effect. Although healthcare organizations are deploying chatbots for mental health screening, some experts argue that current implementations have important limitations (Ward, 2021). A vignette design enables us to avoid the limitations of current technology and provides complete control between the two conditions.

Treatments: We randomly assigned participants to one of the two conditions: (1) human mental health care professional or (2) chatbot. We showed the same vignette in both conditions, but participants were informed that the agent in the video they were about to see was either a human mental health care professional or a chatbot (see Appendix B). We conducted ttests and $\chi ^ { 2 }$ tests to ensure that randomization worked properly and found no significant differences for perceived stigma, first or secondhand experience with depression or anxiety, or the (demographic) control variables (p < 0.05). See Appendix C2 for more details.

Measures: Our treatment variable, Chatbot, is an indicator variable with a value of 1 if the agent is a chatbot and 0 if human. We adopted prior measures (all 7-point Likert scales) for stigma (Eisenberg et al., 2009; Ward & Heidrich, 2009) and users’ willingness to use the service (Nicolaou & McKnight, 2011), disclose personal information (Anderson & Agarwal, 2011), and follow the agent’s recommendation (De Vreede, 1997). Since we could not find a well-established measure for judgmentalness, we selected eight items from Lemay’s 16 items (Lemay & Clark, 2008) for “perceived caring and regard,” with dictionary definitions that were identical (or very similar) in meaning to judgmental and demonstrating convergent validity. We also controlled for age, gender, race, and income, and two measures of past experience with mental health issues (first- and secondhand experiences through loved ones). All items are described in Appendix C1. All Cronbach’s alphas were higher than the desired 0.70 threshold, indicating adequate reliability.

The correlation matrix of all variables is in Appendix D1. The average variance extracted (AVE) for every measure was higher than 0.50, and composite reliability scores were higher than 0.70, indicating convergent validity (Hair, 2009; Nunnally, 1978). Furthermore, the square root of AVE for each measure was greater than the correlations between the measure and all other variables, indicating adequate discriminant validity (Fornell & Larcker, 1981). Stigma (the only continuous variable in the interaction term) was meancentered to avoid potential multicollinearity problems. In addition, all variance inflation factors were lower than the conservative recommended 5.0 threshold (O’Brien, 2007), suggesting that multicollinearity is not an issue in our study.

## Results

Table 2 shows the descriptive statistics for the three outcome variables and judgmentalness. To test our hypotheses, we followed the procedures of Hayes (2017) using the SPSS Hayes PROCESS macro (Model 8). All continuous independent variables were mean-centered.

Table 3 presents the results. Judgmentalness had significant negative effects on willingness to use $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , disclose information $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , and follow recommendations $( p <$ 0.001). H1a, H1b, and H1c are supported. We also observed that judgmentalness partially mediated the effect of agent type on willingness to use the service and fully mediated the effect of agent type on willingness to disclose personal information and follow the agent’s recommendations.

Contrary to what was hypothesized, the chatbot was perceived to be more judgmental than the human mental health care professional $( p = 0 . 0 0 7 )$ , and this increased as stigma increased (p = 0.004). Hence, H2 and H3 are not supported. The variance explained by the model predicting judgmentalness was 37%, which is what Cohen (2013) calls a large effect size. As shown in Table 2, the difference in means between the human and the chatbot for judgmentalness is what Cohen (2013) calls a small effect size (d = 0.29); the effect sizes for the outcome variables are also small. The interaction of chatbot and stigma had no significant effect on any outcomes (i.e., the direct effects of chatbot on dependent variables were not moderated by stigma). The variance explained for use, information disclosure, and following are 48%, 45%, and 47%, respectively; all large effect sizes.

## Discussion

We found no evidence to support our hypothesis that chatbots are perceived to be less judgmental than human healthcare providers. In fact, the results suggest an effect in the opposite direction. There were no differences in behavior between the human and the chatbot—all participants saw the same video—yet the chatbot was perceived as more judgmental. We also did not find any evidence that chatbots would be more helpful for those with greater stigma. Thus, users were less willing to use the chatbot services, disclose information to it, or follow its recommendations. We did not find any evidence that chatbots were more helpful to those with greater stigma, which may be because healthcare professionals were the basis of comparison, and they are less likely than laypeople to view mental issues as stigmatized.

Overall, our findings call into question our fundamental assumption that users perceive chatbots to be less judgmental than human healthcare providers and underscore the need to reconsider the theories underlying chatbots. They also have important implications for the deployment and use of chatbots for stigmatized conditions. The primary promise of chatbots for mental health stems from the assumption that people would find it easier to discuss stigmatized issues with a chatbot without the concern of being judged. However, our findings do not support this assertion.

Our results may prompt a possible connection to the concept of algorithm aversion; prior research shows that people are resistant to solutions provided by AI agents after seeing them make mistakes, even when their solutions are superior to those provided by a human. We searched for errors made by the agent in the treatments and found none. Thus, algorithm aversion triggered by mistakes does not fit our context.

A better explanation could be the chatbot’s failure to provide empathy and emotional support (Press, 2019), even though chatbots can be programmed to display empathy and support (Jhan et al., 2021). Chatbots with more advanced conversational skills have been shown to increase users’ perceptions of social presence and anthropomorphism (Schuetzler et al., 2020) and improve user satisfaction (Cheng & Pan, 2021). However, in contexts involving more nuanced issues (e.g., mental health), chatbots may not perform well (Abd-Alrazaq et al., 2019; Bradford & Ireland, 2021; Kelliher, 2021; Vaidyam et al., 2019) due to their inability to truly understand emotions and exhibit genuine empathy (Følstad et al., 2021; Luo et al., 2019).

Another possible explanation for our results may lie in the nature of the support provided by the agent. People who seek mental health treatment often desire two types of support: informational support and emotional support (Hobbs, 2009; Ki & Jang, 2018; Ko et al., 2013; Yorita et al., 2020). As an IT artifact, chatbots can often provide informational support as well as human agents. In our experiment, the information provided was the same in both chatbot and human mental health care professional treatments, so the actual quality of informational support was identical. The emotional support displayed was also the same for both (since the videos were identical). Our treatment was designed using best-practice recommendations and vetted by a psychiatrist to ensure it was a realistic portrayal of an online screening service. Throughout the video, the agent offers supportive comments without judging (e.g., “I’m sorry for how you have been feeling”); see Appendix B.

Although the displayed emotional support was constant across the treatments, the perceived emotional support may not have been the same. Comments designed to display emotional support coming from a human mental health care professional may indicate support and acceptance, but the exact same words coming from a non-human chatbot may ring hollow (Wakslak et al., 2024)—how can a computer truly feel sorry? Thus, one important question for the design of chatbots is whether there is value in displaying emotional support, given that computers cannot truly understand human emotions.

## Robustness Check

Given the unexpected results, we conducted a new study to ensure that our results were not unique to the sample, measurements, or vignette in Study 1 (see Appendix E). First, we recruited 1105 participants from Prolific Academic to ensure that our results were not sample specific. Second, we reassessed our measures of judgmentalness and stigma to ensure they had content validity, following the approach of Schmitz and Storey (2020). This resulted in new measures of judgmentalness and stigma (see Appendix F). Third, we designed a new vignette by varying the level of judgmentalness. Given the sensitivity of the mental health topic, we did not deliberately introduce judgmental comments. Instead, because research shows that a lack of emotional support can lead to higher judgmentalness (Hall-Renn, 2007; Ulmer et al., 2010; Weber, 2017), we removed the phrases designed to display emotional support to create a new vignette with a reduced amount of emotional support (see Appendix B). We assessed the two vignettes and found that perceived judgmentalness was higher for the new vignette (means: new = 1.85 vs. original = 1.62; t = 5.30, p < 0.001).

In both vignettes, our results once again indicated that the chatbot was perceived as more judgmental than the human mental health care professional. Our findings regarding judgmentalness aligned with the results in Table 3 and were consistent across both vignettes (see Appendix E for details).

As an additional robustness check, we also measured judgmentalness and stigma using the original measures from Study 1. We conducted analyses with all possible combinations of the original and new measures. The results for judgmentalness remained consistent with those in Table 2 across all cases, indicating that the findings hold regardless of the specific measures used.

In summary, we found the same results using different measures (including both the original and the new versions and all combinations thereof), a different vignette, and a different sample population, which provided further empirical support to our counterintuitive finding that chatbots are perceived to be more judgmental than humans, and stigma did not play a central role.

## Study 2: Understanding Judgmentalness

The unexpected results from Study 1 and its robustness check highlight the need to understand the theoretical mechanisms underlying users’ perceptions of chatbots’ judgmentalness. The lack of explanations in prior research further highlights the importance of exploratory study. Therefore, we adopted a mixed methods approach that combined quantitative and qualitative methods (Venkatesh et al., 2013). The mixed methods approach has been commonly used in prior studies because qualitative data can provide rich explanations of findings from initial quantitative analyses (Hackney et al., 2007; Piccoli & Ives, 2003) and help develop constructs and hypotheses that can be tested in subsequent quantitative studies (Grimsley & Meehan, 2007; Ho et al., 2003; Irma Becerra-Fernandez, 2001).

Qualitative research, such as interviews, plays a crucial role in complementing quantitative findings, particularly when results are unexpected (Hackney et al., 2007; Piccoli & Ives, 2003). Quantitative studies, such as our experiments, focus on statistical analysis of measured variables but may not fully capture the underlying reasons and the “why” behind the observed patterns. Qualitative methods are often used to build theory (Charmaz, 2014; Strauss & Corbin, 1998; Venkatesh et al., 2013) because they can help researchers understand how participants’ thoughts and feelings drive their behaviors and why certain results emerged (Hackney et al., 2007; Piccoli & Ives, 2003). The qualitative data adds a deeper explanation to better understand participants’ motivations, providing a holistic view that helps bridge the gap between numerical quantitative outcomes and human-driven perceptions and behaviors (Hackney et al., 2007; Piccoli & Ives, 2003). By using mixed methods, researchers can achieve a more comprehensive and nuanced understanding of human behavior, which is critical for theory development and practical application (Venkatesh et al., 2013).

The goal of Study 2 was to generate a list of potential theoretical factors that might explain why users perceive chatbots to be judgmental. Study 2 is a qualitative interview study designed to identify constructs and hypotheses, while Study 3 is a quantitative experiment designed to test these new hypotheses. Study 2 used grounded theory methods, as they enable theory to emerge from data without imposing preexisting expectations (Charmaz, 2014; Sarker et al., 2018; Urquhart et al., 2010).

## Methods

Over three days in July 2023, two authors conducted semistructured interviews with 41 participants at a large public university in the United States. This sample size is typical of prior qualitative studies (Sarker et al., 2013). Each interview took between 20 and 50 minutes (mean 30.5), and participants were paid \$25. The participants were 40% undergraduate students, 44% graduate students, and 15% faculty and staff members. About 59% were female; 35% were White, 8% Black, 40% Asian, and 15% other. The average age was 26, with a range of 18-54. About 29% had experience using online mental health screening services for depression and anxiety. About 51% had prior personal experience with depression or anxiety, and 93% had a loved one with prior experience. The range of ages and roles (i.e., student, faculty, and staff) indicates a representation of different voices, ensuring a broad consideration of the phenomenon (Rubin & Rubin, 2011).

Since the purpose of the study was to identify factors that may influence the perceived judgmentalness of chatbots, participants watched the video from Study 1 and then completed the interview. The interview began with general questions about the participant’s perception of the chatbot and the screening process and then focused on why the chatbot might or might not be perceived as judgmental. See Appendix G for example interview questions. We audio-recorded the interviews and used Microsoft Word to transcribe them. The interviewers took notes during the interviews and compared insights at the end of each day.

Two authors (one of the interviewers and one who did not do interviews) independently coded the transcripts using Dedoose 9.0.107 and wrote successive memos about the codes (Glaser & Holton, 2004). The coding and memos were revisited iteratively, following the principles of comparative methods and establishing analytical distinction (Charmaz, 2014). We followed the five steps of qualitative analysis for the grounded theory method (i.e., initial coding, focused coding, theoretical coding, clustering, and diagramming) proposed by Charmaz (2014). First, initial coding created the first-order codes that are close to the data. Second, focused coding created the second-order codes as categories of the first-order codes. Third, theoretical coding defined how the second-order codes related to each other. Fourth, clustering visualized the relationships between the second-order codes. Finally, diagramming integrated the relationships and created a theoretical model (Charmaz, 2014). The last three steps are interwoven with each other as they progress in accuracy and sophistication (Charmaz, 2014). Intercoder reliability (Cohen’s ??) was 0.92, showing excellent reliability (Cohen,

1960; McHugh, 2012). One of the authors coded the transcripts twice: once upon the completion of data collection and again about one year later, with 0.96 intracoder reliability.

## Results

The first- and second-order codes are reported in Table 4, with example quotes in Table 5. The seven second-order codes— the theoretical mechanisms—fall into three categories: (1) real-life experience of the agent, (2) understanding of information, and (3) external expression of validation.

The first category is real-life experience. The participants repeatedly pointed out that having real-life experience with the patient’s problems (i.e., anxiety and depression) is an important and integral part of the ability to make meaning of these experiences on a personal and human level. Having similar real-world experiences and drawing on those experiences to put the patient’s experiences in context strengthens the therapeutic alliance between the patient and the mental health provider (Hill & Knox, 2002; Unhjem et al., 2018). When healthcare providers lack real-life experience or fail to connect their own personal real-life experiences to the patient’s experiences, they can appear “quick to make misinformed judgments.”

The second category is understanding, which is the heart of “misinformed judgments.” One of the most recurring themes during the interviews was the difference between surface and deep understanding (Burton-Jones & Meso, 2008). Surface understanding is an understanding of the basic elements of a situation, akin to a syntactic understanding achievable by understanding the explicit knowledge about it (Chomsky, 1966, 2014; Lebovitz et al., 2021). Deep understanding requires an understanding of the relationships among the elements and the semantic meanings of these intertwined relationships across multiple dimensions (Burton-Jones & Meso, 2008; Chomsky, 1966, 2014; Lebovitz et al., 2021). Deep understanding requires tacit knowledge that is both articulable (e.g., street smarts) and inarticulable (e.g., emotions and situated social sense-making) (Busch, 2008). Surface understanding is necessary for diagnosis, and evidence shows that conversational agents can achieve this through explicit knowledge (Lebovitz et al., 2021). For example, explicit knowledge enables them to reach a surface understanding (i.e., diagnosis) of depression by using PHQ-9 as a human agent would. Deep understanding requires tacit knowledge to understand what this means at an emotional and social level (Busch, 2008). While surface understanding is necessary, other, more nuanced types of understanding are also important. For instance, the ability to truly understand the emotional impact of depression and anxiety and the ability to interpret the social meaning of depression and anxiety in interactions with others are also central to a deep understanding of a patient’s problems. Overall, a lack of deep understanding is central to judgmentalness.

The third potential theoretical mechanism was validation. Several participants emphasized the importance of being heard in the mental health context, a phenomenon closely related to and followed by the perception of being validated. As noted by a participant: “Perception of being heard is necessary to validating the patient’s problems.” Past research also confirms this and suggests that the perception of being heard and validated is important in the mental health context (Epstein et al., 2007; Pronk et al., 2022). Although chatbots can generate responses that increase the sense of being heard, when the source of these responses is revealed to be a machine (vs. human), people feel less heard and validated (Wakslak et al., 2024). When patients fail to believe they have been heard and validated, they can perceive a rush to a misinformed judgment.

The analysis indicated that these seven major theoretical constructs comprise three theoretical pathways that may influence users’ perceptions of chatbot judgmentalness (see Figure 2). One is having real-life experiences and connecting those to the patient’s experiences. The second is a deep understanding of the patient’s issues, influenced by a surface understanding driven by both explicit knowledge and tacit knowledge, which is partly an emotional understanding and partly a situated understanding of social meaning (Busch, 2008; Lebovitz et al., 2021). Finally, feeling heard, leading to feeling validated, was also identified as a factor (Epstein et al., 2007; Pronk et al., 2022).

## Discussion

The study showed that participants understood judgmentalness as not just having moralistic judgments (Skjuve et al., 2021; Stiefel, 2018) but also as being quick to reach a poor judgment (Arday, 2018). This study suggests three theoretical mechanisms that may explain why chatbots are perceived to be more judgmental than human mental health care professionals: They lack real-life experiences, they cannot have the same deep understanding of mental health issues, and they do not make users feel heard and validated in the same way as a human would. As a result, they were perceived as quickly rendering poor judgment. We also asked 41 participants about stigma, and all said that stigma would not inhibit their conversations with a mental health care professional, although it would with friends or co-workers.

In Study 3, we tested the theoretical model developed in Study 2. We designed two quantitative studies, an online experiment with participants from Cloud Research (Study 3a) and an inperson laboratory experiment with student subjects (Study 3b).

## Method

Participants. In Study 3a, we recruited 463 participants from Cloud Research (using their option for a sample representative of the U.S. population), who were paid \$2.50 for participating online; no participants had participated in the construct development studies above. We removed 50 participants who failed one or more of the six attention check questions, leaving 413 participants for analysis. In the final sample, about half of the participants were female (56%); 80% were White, 11% Black, 4% Asian, and 5% other. The mean age was 47.3, with a range of 18-81. The median category for household income was \$50K-\$75K. About 62% of participants had prior personal experience with depression or anxiety, and 71% had a loved one with prior experience. A power analysis showed that the study design had a power of 86% to find a small effect (f = 0.15).

In Study 3b, 387 undergraduate students at a large U.S. public university did the study in person at an on-campus computer lab and were compensated with extra credit. We removed 94 responses for failed attention checks, leaving 293 valid responses. About 58% of participants were female; 63% were White, 1% Black, 31% Asian, and 5% other. The mean age was 19.4, with a range of 18-26. The median family income was \$75K-\$100K. About 43% of participants had prior personal experience with depression or anxiety, and 73% had a loved one with prior experience. A power analysis showed that our design had a power of 73% to find a small effect $\left( f = 0 . 1 5 \right)$

Task: The task was the original vignette in Study 1.

Treatments: We used the same treatments as in Study 1 and randomly assigned participants to one of the two conditions: (1) chatbot agent or (2) human mental health care professional. We conducted t-tests and $\chi ^ { 2 }$ tests of perceived stigma, attitude towards $\mathrm { A I } ,$ personal innovativeness with information technology, trust in Hopkins $( \mathrm { i . e . }$ , Johns Hopkins Hospital), first- and secondhand experience with depression or anxiety, and the demographic control variables to ensure that randomization worked and found no significant differences in Study 3a and one in Study 3b $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ . See Appendix C4 and C5 for more details.

Measures: We first ensured the construct validity of our measures for the seven potential predictors of judgmentalness identified in Study 2. Following the approach of Schmitz and Storey (2020), we conducted a systematic construct-building exercise to develop measures for our theoretical constructs. We recruited 50 participants from Cloud Research to evaluate item clarity and relevance, using the same procedures as our previous construct-building exercise (see Appendices F3 and F4). Next, we conducted a preliminary study to assess convergent, discriminant, and predictive validity. Seventyseven Cloud Research participants evaluated a human mental health care professional and a chatbot in the same mental health screening scenario as Study 1, using the seven constructs. An exploratory factor analysis led to the removal of poorly loading items. The chatbot was rated significantly lower than the human on all seven factors $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . Based on these studies, we finalized the measurement items, which are provided in Appendix C1.

The dependent variables were willingness to use the service, disclose personal information, and follow the agent’s recommendation from Study 1. We used the revised measures from Study 1’s robustness check for judgmentalness and stigma. All the control variables used in Study 1 were used in Studies 3a and 3b. We added a measure of AI attitude (Grassini, 2023) as an additional control since the recent increase in public attention to AI may have influenced participants’ attitudes towards chatbots. The factor loadings and correlations are in Appendices D2, D3, and D4. We found no issues with reliability, convergent validity, discriminant validity, and multicollinearity.

## Discussion

Study 3 used two experiments (one online, one in-person) to test the model derived from the qualitative analysis in Study 2. Both studies replicated the original results of Study 1 and its robustness check, with participants reporting the chatbot to be more judgmental than a human mental health care professional, despite the chatbot exhibiting exactly the same behavior as the human mental health care professional. Two of three underlying theoretical mechanisms (understanding and validation) influenced judgmentalness in both experiments. In both studies, the chatbot was perceived to be less capable of surface understanding, emotional understanding, and social meaning understanding, which reduced deep understanding. This ties nicely to past research that suggests AI is less capable of understanding tacit knowledge (Lebovitz et al., 2021), which is important in mental health contexts (Busch, 2008). Having real-life experience was not a significant factor.

One aspect that may prove challenging for future research and practice is the perception of being heard and validated (Wakslak et al., 2024). Conveying a sense of being heard is a necessary element in perceptions of being validated, and, together, they act as a theoretical pathway influencing perceptions of being judgmental. The chatbot was seen by our participants as significantly less capable of exhibiting both of these skills, despite displaying the exact same behaviors. Thus, feeling heard and validated appears to be influenced at least as much by the user’s attitudes and beliefs towards chatbots as it is by the chatbot’s behavior.

Our participants recognized that the chatbot lacked real-life experience and thus could not connect its experience to the lived experiences of the patient. However, having real-life experience was not a significant factor influencing judgmentalness. This is perhaps good news for chatbot developers because conversational agents cannot gain the same real-life experience as humans.

## General Discussion

Across four online or in-person experiments with samples drawn from a student population, as well as three separate online survey pools representative of the U.S. population, participants consistently reported that they perceived the chatbot to be more judgmental than the human mental health care professional, despite the fact that it behaved identically to the human. Our findings call into question the fundamental assumption that users perceive chatbots to be less judgmental than humans and underscore the need to reconsider the theories underlying chatbots and AI more generally. They also have important implications for chatbot design, and deployment.

Thus, our research’s first important theoretical contribution is to rethink the theories underlying chatbots (and AI more generally). We have long assumed that being judgmental— being quick to form incorrect judgments, especially critical ones—is a human trait, a trait not shared by chatbots and other conversational agents. However, our participants ascribed greater judgmentalness to the chatbot because it was not human. Thus, being judgmental is not something we ascribe solely to humans. We studied one context and need more research in similar and different contexts to understand the boundary conditions.

Second, past research has theorized that chatbots should be perceived as less judgmental because they cannot make moral judgments (Skjuve et al., 2021, 2022). Chatbots do not make moral judgments because they are not programmed to do so—although it would be possible to design a highly moralistic chatbot that could make such judgments. This approach to judgmentalness lies in the belief that avoiding unkind words influences perceptions of judgmentalness. This may be true, but our studies avoided any display of moralistic judgment and were identical between the human and the chatbot. Thus, the differences in perceived judgmentalness were not driven by differences in moralistic judgment or other behaviors.

Therefore, our research’s second important theoretical contribution is that judgmentalness in this context is driven by factors other than moral judgments. Judgmentalness also involves being quick to form judgments without understanding the situation (Arday, 2018; Lea & Bradbery, 2020). This theoretical conception of judgmentalness shows that a chatbot is as capable of being as judgmental as a human. When a user describes experiences and symptoms to a chatbot, it can parse the text and produce the most relevant response according to the knowledge used to train it, giving a highly customized response that displays information and emotional support (at least superficially). But can it truly understand what it means to have depression or anxiety?

Technology lacks the tacit knowledge to deeply understand the issues at an emotional and social level. Thus, users perceive it as making judgments without a deep understanding. Under this theoretical interpretation, a chatbot may be perceived to be more judgmental because of what it cannot do, rather than what it does. This is in contrast to humans, who are perceived as judgmental because of what they do. Thus, in situations that call for a deep understanding of human experiences (e.g., mental health), chatbots are likely to be perceived as more judgmental than humans who act appropriately, even when they behave exactly as a human would.

Third, there were no behavioral differences between the human mental health care professional and the chatbot—all participants saw exactly the same behavior—yet the chatbot was perceived to be more judgmental. Thus, the differences in judgmentalness are not because the chatbot was less capable than the human; it was equally capable. The differences are due to the users’ perceptions of the chatbot. Thus, our research’s third important theoretical contribution is that judgmentalness is not influenced by behavior alone. The same behavior may be interpreted differently depending on whether it is displayed by a human or a chatbot. Thus, designing chatbots (and other AI agents) to behave in ways similar to humans is an important first but likely not sufficient step. Words from chatbots have different meanings than words from humans when emotions and social aspects are important.

Our findings also underscore the challenges associated with the practical deployment of chatbots. The results of Studies 2 and 3 indicate that the other factor influencing users to perceive that a chatbot is more judgmental than a human is that the chatbot does not make users feel heard and validated in the same way as a human would. The perception of being heard and validated is important in the mental health context (Epstein et al., 2007; Pronk et al., 2022) as well as other contexts. Participants felt significantly less heard and less validated after using the chatbot, despite having experienced the exact same behaviors as those displayed by the human mental health care professional. This is echoed by a recent study showing that although conversational agents can generate responses that increase the sense of being heard, when the source of these responses is revealed to be a computer (vs. a human), people tend to feel less heard and validated (Wakslak et al., 2024). Thus, feeling heard and validated is driven at least as much by the beliefs of the user as by the behavior of the chatbot. Changing user beliefs will be much more challenging than changing chatbot behavior.

Like all studies, our study has limitations. First, although our mixed method design attempted to mitigate the limitations of each individual method (Venkatesh et al., 2013), our experiments nevertheless suffered from artificiality and a lack of consequences, while our interview study suffered from a lack of control and potential researcher bias. Second, we recruited only adult participants from the United States, so our results are limited to a single sociopolitical, cultural, and healthcare context. Future research should investigate the generalizability of our results in various contexts, cultures, age groups, and countries. Third, other important factors (e.g., comorbidities such as diabetes, other mental health disorders, etc.) were controlled through random assignment to treatment but deserve consideration in future research. Fourth, we acknowledge the potential for bias as an inherent limitation of all qualitative research; however, we took every possible measure (e.g., triangulation of our findings through qualitative and quantitative approaches) to alleviate this concern. Finally, we focused only on chatbot applications for triaging potential patients, and future research can extend our work by examining the use of chatbots and other conversational agents for treating patients.

## Implications for Research

Our research offers six implications for future research. First, chatbots may be perceived to be more judgmental than humans, contrary to prior research assuming that users should feel no judgmentalness because chatbots are incapable of making moral judgments. Prior literature has accepted but never rigorously tested this assumption by using a clear human benchmark, measuring the chatbot’s judgmentalness, and evaluating the actual interaction of users and chatbots with regard to perceived judgmentalness. Our study is the first to do all of the above. The revised understanding of what it means to be judgmental (“quick to make misinformed judgments”; Arday, 2018, p. 12) means that instead of simply assuming that users perceive no judgmentalness, researchers need to carefully consider what is needed for AI to understand in a given context—and how to convey to users that it has this deep understanding. An important boundary condition to our findings is the role of emotional and social understanding. Chatbots may be particularly useful for contexts that do not require a deep understanding of human experiences (e.g., emotional or social understanding). Thus, it would be useful for future research to consider the judgmentalness of chatbots across contexts that differ in the need for emotional and social understanding.

Second, our study found two theoretical pathways that influenced perceptions of judgmentalness: Deep understanding (influenced by surface understanding, emotional understanding, and social meaning understanding) and feeling validated (influenced by feeling heard). Thus, improving the conversational agent’s abilities to better provide and display deep understanding and validation is a key step for future research. At the same time, it is important to note that our chatbot displayed identical behavior as a human mental health care professional, so user perceptions may be somewhat separate from the actual ability of the agent to deeply understand the patient and make them feel validated. Therefore, we also need more research to better understand how to influence human perceptions of chatbots and other AI tools.

A third theoretical implication is that the design goal of making chatbots appear as human as possible so that conversations seem more natural deserves some fundamental reconsideration. Our research shows that users interpret human and chatbot communication differently, even when they say the exact same words. Human mental health care professionals are trained to be supportive and accepting (Baer et al., 2004), and our research shows that programming a chatbot to display emotional support is also beneficial, although not as effective as when it is displayed by a human. Thus, more research is needed to better understand how the same words have different meanings when said by a human versus a computer. It may be that a best-practice conversation with a chatbot will look different than a best-practice conversation with a human agent. More research is needed to understand how different dialogue styles impact the effectiveness of chatbots.

Fourth, our research contributes to the computers are social actors (CASA) paradigm (Nass & Moon, 2000; Nass et al., 1994). We attribute human characteristics to computers and perceive them as social actors by relying on the social and cognitive cues we learn from our interactions with other humans (Nass & Moon, 2000). We extend this body of knowledge by suggesting that even when chatbots behave precisely the same as humans do (as in our studies), users may perceive the behaviors differently. This is particularly salient in our study, as social meaning understanding (which is a key attribute of “social actors”) is a major driver of users’ perceptions of chatbots. Hence, future research should examine the importance of social meanings in different contexts to better understand how chatbots are or are not perceived as social actors.

Fifth, individual differences between users are often important factors in technology use, and two important individual differences in the healthcare context are concerns about trust and privacy (Höglund et al., 2004; Kanter & Packel, 2023). We controlled for trust in the chatbot provider (i.e., Johns Hopkins Hospital) and beliefs about technology in general (i.e., PIIT) and AI in particular (i.e., AI attitude). Future research should investigate how trust (in technology and people) and privacy concerns (both general and specific) influence perceptions of judgmentalness and the use of chatbots because both influence how users interact with technology and the information they disclose (Dang et al., 2021; Lowry et al., 2011; McKnight et al., 2011).

Finally, one of our challenges was the lack of established measures for the key concept of judgmentalness and the other theoretical constructs. Hence, we conducted two construct building exercises to develop sound and reliable measures. Future research can use our validated measures of judgmentalness and other theoretical factors and build upon them.

## Implications for Practice

The most immediate practical implication of our findings is to be cautious about deploying chatbots for mental health screening. We know that chatbots do not morally judge the individuals they are designed to assist, but this is not the only element of judgmentalness that matters to users. Until chatbots are capable of developing a deeper understanding of mental health issues and giving users a stronger sense that they are being heard and validated, it may be more effective to deploy chatbots in areas that do not rely on these uniquely human qualities. The risk of using chatbots for mental health screening is that users may be less willing to use them, and if they do use them, they may be less willing to disclose information and follow their recommendations.

A second implication is that in a mental health screening context, deep understanding and the perception of being heard and validated are important. Understanding the physiological symptoms is necessary to provide sound medical advice, but this is not sufficient for a successful deployment. Developers of chatbots for mental health screening should identify ways to develop a more nuanced understanding of the social and emotional aspects of mental health issues and actively program them to display this understanding. Likewise, taking steps to display active listening and to validate the user’s experiences is key to their successful adoption and use. Further, these design implications also might apply to other stages in mental health care (e.g., treatment), as the scenarios used in our study had limited screening-specific factors.

A third implication is that we need to look beyond just improving the technology to address this greater judgmentalness; building a better chatbot is necessary, but not sufficient. In our studies, the behavior of the chatbot was identical to that of the human mental health care professional, so the differences were not due to lower performance by the chatbot. Although we encourage developers to improve their chatbots, this may not fully solve the problem. Healthcare providers need to address users’ fundamental beliefs about chatbots before users use them, disclose information to them, and decide whether or not to follow their advice. Developers can think of creative ways of influencing users’ beliefs (e.g., social and emotional understanding). For instance, instead of merely trying to emulate human behaviors, capitalizing on the unique capabilities of chatbots, like their access to vast amounts of data in real-time and and their ability to share other humans’ experiences with the user (e.g., their similar social and emotional struggles and their successful coping behaviors) may create a stronger sense of social and emotional understanding and validation. Designing such interventions is a significant design science undertaking, requiring an understanding of when and how to deliver these interactions appropriately.

A fourth implication is the need to monitor user perceptions about chatbots because users may have very different reactions than we expect. Based on prior theory (and a dearth of empirical studies), we assumed that chatbots would not be perceived as judgmental, but we were wrong. People’s attitudes towards new technologies can change over time as technologies mature, as they gain more experience with them, and as they hear about others’ positive or negative experiences with them. This suggests that if we fail to regularly monitor how users perceive their experiences with chatbots and other conversational agents, we may miss issues by making incorrect assumptions; we may fail to identify problems until it is too late to address them.

A final implication is that chatbots should be programmed to provide emotional support in mental health screening contexts. Even though users recognized that the emotional support was from a computer and was not perceived in the same way as emotional support from a human, users still valued it. There may be other contexts where emotional support is also valued (e.g., mental health treatment). Whether or not chatbots should merely emulate human-like empathy or lean into their own unique capabilities could be an interesting question to answer in future studies.

## Conclusion

One final note is in order. Our findings should not be taken as a blanket statement that chatbots and other conversational agents should not be used to provide mental health support. Our findings highlight where humans excel in the mental health context—particularly in providing deep understanding and genuine validation. Until AI reaches the level of deep human understanding and becomes capable of conveying a real sense of validation, this remains a unique and valuable strength that human healthcare professionals can offer. Our focus in this study was on comparing a well-trained chatbot and a welltrained human healthcare provider. Both displayed a supportive attitude and professional conduct, as vetted by an experienced psychiatrist. Our treatments illustrate how a proper mental health screening should be performed; however, this is not always the case in practice. Even before COVID-19, mental health issues were rising, and the pandemic has only worsened an already critical situation. Hence, there is a need to expand the capacity of mental health services, but qualified healthcare providers are in short supply. Can chatbots and other conversational agents help in such situations? Would people prefer a well-trained conversational agent controlled by AI or an inexperienced human performing under time pressure? It would be imprudent to make assumptions (in either direction) without thoroughly evaluating the underlying assumptions that influence users’ perceptions and behavior, even if those assumptions appear to be intuitive.

## PRISMA Literature Review Summary

We performed a systematic literature review in August 2024, searching the AIS™ eLibrary, ProQuest™, EBSCO™, and Emerald™ with the search terms “Chatbot” and “Judgmental” in the titles, abstracts, and introductions of the publications. We restricted our search to those in the English language peer-reviewed journals, books, conference papers, and proceedings. The search yielded 507 studies. We excluded seven duplicate records, which resulted in a total of 500 studies retrieved. We conducted a forward and backward citation search on these studies, which yielded 14 additional studies, for a total of 514 retrieved for examination. We excluded studies that did not include empirica data (450) or did not study text-based chatbots (3), leaving 61 studies. Of the 61 studies, none met the two criteria in Table 1.

## Details for Study 1’s Robustness Check

The goal of the robustness check was to ensure that our results were not unique to the sample, measurements, or vignette in Study 1. First, we reassessed our measures of judgmentalness and stigma to ensure they had content validity, following the approach of Schmitz and Storey (2020). For judgmentalness, we began with the eight items from Study 1 since there were no previously developed judgmentalness measures that we could borrow from prior literature. For stigma, we drew items for different aspects of stigma (internalized stigma, treatment stigma, and social stigma because these are the most important determinants of treatment-seeking in the mental health context) (Eisenberg et al., 2009; Ward & Heidrich, 2009); see Table F1. We recruited 37 participants from MTurk to assess the clarity and relevance of the items. Seven participants were removed due to failing one or more of the three attention check questions, leaving 30 valid responses for analysis. The participants were asked to rate the items on a scale of 1 to 4 along two dimensions of clarity and relevance; see Table F2. Items were dropped if more than 30% of the participants rated them less than or equal to 2 and/or had a mean of less than 3 on either scale. Accordingly, two of the judgmentalness items (i.e., empathy and patience) were dropped. All of the stigma items passed this test, but we removed the four lowestrated items to reduce the number of questions to include in next stage of our robustness checks. The correlation matrix of all variables is in Appendix D1.

We also tested a vignette displaying a different level of judgmentalness. We focused on emotional support—defined as an interaction in which people believe that they are cared for and that their feelings are understood (Cobb, 1976; Fuller & Larson, 1980)—because research shows that emotional support reduces perceptions of judgmentalness (Eshbaugh, 2011; Kim et al., 2013), particularly in stigmatized situations (Kimport, 2022). Similarly, a lack of emotional support and empathy can lead to higher perceived judgmentalness (Hall-Renn, 2007; Ulmer et al., 2010; Weber, 2017). Emotional support can be displayed by healthcare professionals in many ways, including acknowledging how the person feels, providing psychological support, showing empathy, and expressing feelings (Heaney & Israel, 2008; Trobst et al., 1994; Zhu et al., 2022). Therefore, to vary the level of judgmentalness of the agent in the vignette, we developed a new version in which the agent did not display any emotional support.

We recruited 1,194 participants from Prolific and used its option to recruit a sample representative of the U.S. population. Participants were paid \$2.00. We removed 89 participants who failed one or more attention check questions, leaving 1,105 participants for analysis. In our final sample, half of the participants (51%) were female; 78% were White, 9% Black, 4% Asian, and 4% other. The mean age was 44.9, with a range of 18-92. The median category for household income was \$50K-\$75K. About 66% of participants had prior personal experience with depression or anxiety, and 78% had a loved one who had a prior experience. Overall, we note that the composition of this larger sample— collected using Prolific’s feature to obtain a sample representative of the U.S. population—was quite similar to that in Study 1 with respect to age, gender, income, and race, providing confidence in our findings from Study 1.

We randomly assigned participants to one of the four conditions: (1) human agent, original vignette; (2) human agent, new vignette; (3) chatbot, original vignette; and (4) chatbot agent, new vignette. We used the same agent descriptions from Study 1. Through a one-way ANOVA (instead of t-tests since we had more than 3 groups), we found no significant differences for perceived stigma, personal innovativeness with information technology, trust in Hopkins, first-hand and second-hand experience with depression or anxiety, and all the (demographic) control variables to ensure that randomization worked (p < 0.05) (see Appendix C3).
