# HEALTH INFORMATION TECHNOLOGY REDEPLOYMENT FROM TARGETS TO ACQUIRERS IN THE HEALTHCARE INDUSTRY: THE ROLE OF CO-SPECIALIZED HUMAN CAPITAL IN VALUE CREATION<sup>1</sup>

Chengxin Cao Paul Chook Department of Information Systems and Statistics, Zicklin School of Business Baruch College, New York, NY, U.S.A. {chengxin.cao@baruch.cuny.edu}

Gautam Ray, Mani Subramani, and Alok Gupta Department of Information & Decision Sciences, Carlson School of Management University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, MN, U.S.A. {gautamr@umn.edu} {subra010@umn.edu} {alok@umn.edu}

In view of the increasing number of hospital mergers and acquisitions (M&As) and the significant role of healthcare services in the economy, a variety of recent studies have shed light on how health information technology (HIT) contributes to value creation from hospital M&As. However, little attention has been paid to value creation through the redeployment of the target hospital’s digital resources to the acquiring hospital and the conditions needed to create value from such resource redeployment. This paper highlights how post-M&A HIT redeployment from the target to the acquirer influences the quality of care of the acquirer. First, the results demonstrate the importance of retaining elements of the co-specialized human capital of the target hospital post-M&A as central to creating value from HIT redeployment. Second, HIT redeployment from the target to the acquirer is more valuable when the diagnostic context of the acquiring hospital is complementary to but also distinct from the diagnostic context of the target hospital. Third, the electronic health record system compatibility between the target and the acquirer also plays an essential role in facilitating value creation for the acquiring hospital from the transfer of knowledge-based capabilities from the target.

Keywords: Hospital M&A, HIT redeployment, adherence to recommended care, average length of stay, average inpatient cost, co-specialized human capital, diagnostic complementarity, EHR compatibility

## Introduction

National healthcare expenditure grew 7.5% to \$4.9 trillion in 2023 and accounted for 17.6% of the gross domestic product, making it one of the largest sectors of the US economy (CMS.gov, 2023). Hospital consolidations through mergers and acquisitions (M&As) are a widespread phenomenon across U.S. healthcare markets. Between 2016 and 2022, nearly 5% of hospitals were sold nationwide (Welch et al., 2022). M&As in this sector, although well-intentioned, have shown mixed influence on healthcare efficiency, prices, and clinical quality of care (Bailey, 2022). For instance, while the American Hospital Association’s analysis found that M&As were associated with a 3.3% reduction in operating expenses and improvements in care quality (May et al., 2021), hospital M&As were also shown to diminish competition, increase prices, or deteriorate patient outcomes in other contexts (Curto et al., 2022; Beaulieu et al., 2020; O’Hanlon et al., 2019). Given the size of the sector and the prevalence of M&As in this sector, it is important to understand how hospital M&As affect healthcare costs and the quality of care.

Hospitals engage in M&As for two broad reasons. Hospitals want to redeploy their existing knowledge and resources in new contexts or acquire knowledge and resources to fill resource gaps. The resource-based view suggests that redeployed resources are particularly valuable when they are deployed in contexts where resources are co-specialized (Nevo & Wade, 2010; Tafti et al., 2022). Health information technology (HIT) resources that are modular in nature can allow the reconfiguration and redeployment of resources by acquiring hospitals to target hospitals through M&As (Toppenberg et al., 2015). However, whether HIT can facilitate more effective integration and whether co-specialization and redeployment can enhance the performance of hospitals after an M&A remain underexplored. Du (2015) showed that acquiring hospitals with extensive and standardized digital resources redeployed their digital resources to target hospitals and that such resource redeployment created value by improving the performance of target hospitals post-M&A. Du (2015) highlighted the centrality of value creation through the reuse of acquirers’ digital resources in target hospitals, suggesting that the potential for redeployment of HIT resources in target hospitals is an important driver of M&A initiatives. In addition, a reduction in the prospects for the deployment of acquirer systems in target hospitals (e.g., when the target already has a system with similar functionality) has been shown to reduce the likelihood of the target hospital being acquired. Specifically, Somanchi et al. (2023) demonstrated that while the adoption of an electronic health record system (EHR) significantly increases a hospital’s likelihood of survival, it may make the hospital less desirable as an acquisition target because acquirers tend to prefer hospitals that do not already have a competing EHR in place so that they can more easily integrate their own.

While these studies have made important contributions, the focus in the literature on the digital redeployment of resources in M&As has largely been one-sided, completely ignoring the potential benefits of redeploying resources from target hospitals to acquiring hospitals after M&As. While the redeployment of resources to target firms can enhance target performance, given that developing new capabilities is time-consuming and risky (Ahuja & Katila, 2001), it is likely that the acquirer’s performance can also benefit from the novel resources and capabilities obtained from the target. Given that acquiring hospitals typically retain the majority of the combined entity after M&As, their post-M&A performance is an indispensable piece of the M&A value creation puzzle. As the core technology managing both administrative and clinical processes, HIT forms the foundation and repository of the hospital’s knowledge-based capabilities. Consequently, it is likely that acquiring hospitals can address gaps in their own capabilities by acquiring and redeploying target hospital HIT applications that contain knowledge that is complementary to the acquirer’s capabilities, allowing them to realize performance gains following an M&A.

The redeployment of the clinical tools used in the operating rooms from Dignity Health (the target hospital) to Catholic Health Initiatives (i.e., CHI, the acquiring hospital) is an illustrative example. After the \$29 billion takeover of Dignity Health by CHI, forming CommonSpirit Health—one of the largest non-profit health systems in the United States in 2019— Dignity Health redeployed its clinical technology to manage the peri-operative services for the combined entity across its 250+ operating rooms system-wide, generating significant efficiencies in operating room utilization and savings of over \$40 million in the first phase of the adoption of the tool enterprise-wide (LeanTaas, 2019; Siwichi, 2022).

Further, the implicit view underlying prior studies of HIT redeployment (Du, 2015; Somanchi et al., 2023) suggests that HIT, when redeployed to the desired location, creates value. This flies in the face of insights from the resource and knowledgebased view of the firm (Tafti et al., 2022), which suggest that the ability to derive value from redeploying resources is likely to hinge on the availability of complementary and organizationally embedded co-specialized assets (Gambardella et al., 2015; Powell & Dent-Micallef, 1997). Further, in knowledge-intensive contexts, the ability to create value from redeployment may hinge on access to knowledge embodied primarily within the human capital where the resources are being sourced from (Song et al., 2003). Although a few prior studies acknowledge the necessity of context-specific knowledge in redeployment, they typically view the context-specific knowledge required as being fully encapsulated and transferable with technology. This is illustrated, for example, in this quote from a manager from an acquiring hospital implementing systems in target hospitals (Tanriverdi & Du, 2011, p. 7):

When we pull all these new hospitals onto our [IT] systems, number one, you get the advantage of the intellectual property that went into the design of the IT systems. A lot of these community hospitals can’t afford to have people sit down and figure out what’s the best practice, what’s the best order set, what’s the medical efficacy of using one drug versus another. We leverage our clinical intellectual property that’s already been used to build this system.

Against this backdrop, this paper examines two key questions: (1) Does HIT redeployment from the target hospital to the acquiring hospital improve the acquirer’s quality of care? (2) How does such redeployment improve the acquirer’s quality of care? This paper makes four contributions to the literature on the role of IT in M&As in the healthcare industry. We show that M&A provides a means for acquiring hospitals to obtain digital resources from target hospitals (i.e., redeployment of digital resources from target to acquiring hospitals). Focusing on acquiring hospitals, the results indicate that the digital resources gained from target hospitals can help improve the quality of care of the acquirer. Second, we highlight the important role of the retention of co-specialized human capital in enabling HIT redeployment to acquiring hospitals. In particular, we show that a higher retention of the target hospital’s senior employees who are co-specialized with the target hospital’s digital assets enhances the impact of HIT redeployment on the acquirer’s quality of care. Third, we demonstrate that the redeployment of the target’s digital resources provides greater value to the acquirer when their diagnostic contexts are complementary rather than overlapping. Further, when the acquirer can learn from the target’s unique knowledge-based capabilities built from treating similarly more-complex cases and conditions, the acquirer’s post-M&A quality of care improved to a greater extent, compared to when both parties treated similarly lesscomplex cases. Finally, the compatibility of the target’s and acquirer’s EHRs plays a critical role in effectively facilitating the transfer of knowledge from the target to the acquirer.

## Literature Review

In this paper, we focus on the redeployment of IT-enabled knowledge-based capabilities from the target hospital to the acquiring hospital. This paper is related to three different streams of literature: (1) impact of HIT, (2) impact of IT on M&A outcomes, and (3) knowledge-based view of the firm.

## Impact of Health Information Technology

The information systems (IS) and health informatics literatures have long been interested in the impact of information technology on various hospital performance outcomes. In the healthcare domain, these medical technologies are typically referred to as HIT, defined as the application of information technology involving both computer hardware and software that deals with the storage, retrieval, sharing, and use of healthcare information, data, and knowledge for communication and decision-making (Thompson & Brailer, 2004). HIT contains both clinical and administrative components. Clinical IT is directly linked to patients’ names or unique personal identifiers to access their personal medical records; it also captures and stores the notes, records, and test results of the patients (Angst et al., 2012). In administrative IT, data is separated from the patient and is no longer used in decision-making about individual patients (Nenonen & Nylander, 2002). For instance, clinical IT contains radiology and cardiology applications, whereas administrative IT includes management reporting systems, transaction processing systems, administration systems, and registration systems. While HIT contains many stand-alone medical technologies that serve diverse functionalities, integrating these tools to communicate and interact with each other makes them interoperable so that they can support better decision-making.

Prior studies have examined the effects of HIT on both direct patient outcomes (e.g., mortality rate and readmission rate) (Bardhan et al., 2015) and care process quality (e.g., adherence to recommended care protocols, reduction of medical errors, technical protocols of care, and interpersonal care) (Angst et al., 2012). While patient outcomes directly measure results of care quality, prior studies suggest that direct patient outcomes can be too far removed from HIT variables, highlighting the necessity of identifying the processes and mechanisms through which HIT provides value to hospitals and patients (Pinsonneault et al., 2017). Most IS literature has focused on the impact of HIT on patient care outcomes (Bardhan et al., 2015; Menon & Kohli, 2013; Yeow & Goh, 2015). For instance, Bardhan et al. (2015) showed that cardiology IT is related to a reduction in readmission propensity and frequency, while administrative IT is related to a reduction in readmission frequency. The extant literature has particularly emphasized the positive impact of EHR modules, especially in cases that require complex information processing (Angst et al., 2011; Miller & Tucker, 2011; McCullough et al., 2016). An EHR is a multicomponent software system that manages patients’ electronic health records across providers and units to document and monitor health care delivery (Wowak et al., 2022). Angst et al. (2011) showed that a higher number of integrated HIT applications can decrease the cost and increase the quality of patient care. In McCullough et al.’s (2016) study, although EHR modules correlated with reduced mortality, the benefits were only experienced by patients with multiple conditions requiring cross-specialty care coordination and extensive clinical information.

Although many studies have shown positive impacts of HIT on patient care quality, this is not a foregone conclusion. A few studies have found insignificant impacts or even negative impacts (Kennebeck et al., 2012; Agha, 2014). In addition, a few IS studies have also examined process measures of patient care quality (Aron et al., 2011; Angst et al., 2012; Venkatesh et al., 2011). For example, Angst et al. (2012) used a structureprocess-outcome (SPO) framework and found that while cardiology IT had a positive impact on objective patient health status through improvements in the technical protocols of care, administrative IT adversely impacted interpersonal care processes. Venkatesh et al. (2011) showed that e-health system usage is positively related to care process quality variables (i.e., technical quality, communication, interpersonal interactions, and time spent) that are positively related to patient satisfaction. In this paper, we provide theoretical rationale and empirical support on the conditions under which the target-to-acquirer HIT redeployment exerts a positive impact on three measures of the acquirer’s post-M&A quality of care: adherence to recommended care, Case Mix Index (CMI)-adjusted average length of stay (ALS), and CMIadjusted average inpatient cost (average cost).

## Impact of IT on M&A Performance

Toppenberg et al.’s (2015) rich qualitative analysis of multiple acquisitions by CISCO suggests that a firm’s enterprise architecture is the source of capabilities for the transfer of knowledge and practices from acquiring firms to target firms. This work suggests that enterprise architecture and standardized business processes within acquiring firms can facilitate the transfer of business process knowledge from acquiring firms to target firms. Prior research also suggests that investments in IT enhance business flexibility, which is linked to the capability of firms to sense and respond to opportunities through appropriate M&As (Benitez et al., 2018). Many of these value-creating opportunities are likely to arise from the improvements in information quality and visibility for decision-making in complex and turbulent environments and in the coordination of decision-making in the combined firm (Tanriverdi & Uysal, 2011). However, the ability to derive these advantages requires the challenges arising from the complexity of reorganization and realignment of business processes to be addressed across the acquiring and target firms. IT investments are seen as enhancing the capability to integrate business operations post-M&A and derive the potential value unlocked by the M&A (Benitez et al., 2018).

Prior research also suggests that IT enhances the external coordination capabilities of firms, particularly in contexts where coordinating with partners involves transactionspecific investments (Cao et al., 2022a). This advantage enables firms to engage in information-based coordination and reduces the likelihood of both horizontal and vertical M&As. Cao et al. (2022a) also highlighted that enterprise systems enhance the internal coordination capabilities of firms, improving the likelihood of conglomerate acquisitions that will allow them to expand product offerings or enter new markets. In the context of large multi-business enterprises, prior research suggests that firms typically pay significant premiums (payments over the market value) for acquisitions (Díaz et al., 2009), and the extent of standardization of enterprise systems in acquiring firms enhances information sharing and managerial decision-making, leading to lowered premiums for acquisitions (Cao et al., 2022b). However, the extent of standardization of enterprise systems in target firms enables a superior private assessment of the target’s value by potential acquirers (compared to estimates of potential value based on publicly available information), which is reflected in higher acquisition premiums. Further, this work suggests that the enterprise system extensiveness of the acquirer enhances long-term value creation through the M&A by allowing the firm to realize potential synergies of the combined capabilities of the acquirer and target firms.

In the context of the hospital industry, Du (2015) described the role of IT in acquiring, integrating, and redeploying digital resources as being influenced by the level of IT standardization and IT extensiveness. IT standardization refers to the common IT tools and systems implemented to support organizational activities, whereas IT extensiveness refers to the functional and organizational scope of IT tools and systems to support business activities. Du (2015) showed that acquiring hospitals with extensive and standardized digital resources redeployed their digital resources to target hospitals, and highlighted how such HIT redeployment created value by improving the performance of target hospitals post-M&A. The work of Somanchi et al. (2023) provides a more nuanced understanding of the value of HIT in M&As. Their work suggests that acquirers either prefer target hospitals that have not adopted HIT (where acquirers can create value by implementing their systems in the target hospital) or target hospitals that have already implemented HIT from the same vendor as the acquiring hospital (where the common HIT would facilitate postmerger streamlining of processes and the creation of efficiency in the combined hospital system).

## Knowledge-Based View of the Firm

Under the knowledge-based view of the firm, firms are defined as generators, repositories, and integrators of knowledge (Grant, 1996; Hedlund, 1994; Kogut & Zander, 1992). The dynamic nature of a firm’s knowledge-based capability emphasizes its ability to generate innovations with successive generations of new technologies (Lei et al., 1996). While knowledge can organically grow within the organization, acquiring external knowledge through an M&A helps the acquirer avoid the time-consuming and risky processes of internal accumulation of knowledge (Dierickx & Cool, 1989; Steensma & Fairbank, 1999). In practice, many established firms choose to acquire small technology firms for their technological capabilities (Ahuja & Katila, 2001; Kale & Puranam, 2004), especially under industry conditions where rapid and frequent innovation is essential to the survival of the business, technology is highly complex, and specialized expertise is required.

The competitive advantage provided by knowledge-based capabilities lies in tacit knowledge. While explicit knowledge (i.e., knowledge that can be articulated, codified and shared) can be accessed through verbal communication and written documents, tacit knowledge is difficult to transfer from one organizational unit to another (Kogut & Zander, 1992). Such knowledge is typically “acquired” and embedded in individuals’ specialized skills and expertise (Grant, 1996). Utilizing the expertise residing in individuals requires integration capabilities to effectively combine such expertise to produce productive or innovative results. In this process, skilled individuals interact and work together; gradually, they develop interactions and relationships that integrate their specialized knowledge with the complementary skills of others. Hence, the value-generating knowledge of the organization is located beyond the individuals in complex social relationships, processes, and routines (Brown & Duguid, 1991; Reed & DeFillippi, 1990). Grant (1996, p. 380) suggests that the “critical source of competitive advantage is knowledge integration rather than the knowledge itself.” In other words, acquiring technology from the target firm does not always promise better performance for the acquirer; rather, the actual value creation hinges on the integration of knowledge-based capabilities of the target.

In an acquisition, by absorbing the target firm’s knowledge base into the acquirer’s, the acquirer expands its technical resource base, potentially enhancing outputs through economies of scale and scope (Henderson & Cockburn, 1993). However, acquisitions have also shown disruptive effects, reducing productivity, diverting managerial attention, and imposing transaction costs in the acquisition integration phase (Haspeslagh & Jemison, 1991; Hitt et al., 1996). M&A integration failures have repeatedly shown unexpected difficulty in post-merger integration between the acquiring and target firms due to the lack of common knowledge and agency problems (Henningsson et al., 2018).

The knowledge coordination and integration capacity of the acquiring and acquired units is defined in terms of their information processing activities to allocate tasks among units in a way that allows units to adjust their actions based on their anticipation of the other units’ actions (Tushman & Nadler, 1978). In other words, coordination and integration capacity is rooted in individual units sharing common knowledge to reach a state of agreement that allows each unit to act secure in the knowledge that the other units’ actions will be aligned with their own (Puranam et al., 2009). Increasing levels of interdependence among organizational units necessitate knowledge coordination and integration mechanisms with higher levels of coordination capacity (Gulati & Singh, 1998; Lawrence & Lorsch, 1967).

In summary, although there is a large body of literature linking HIT to hospital operations and patient care outcomes, these results may or may not apply in the M&A context due to the distinct HIT at two different entities, the inherent complexity of knowledge coordination and integration in this knowledgeintensive context, and the difficulty of external capability absorption during the restructuring period. Thus, our understanding of how HIT can be used to enhance hospital M&A performance is quite limited. Early work in IS (Du, 2015) studying the role of HIT in the post-M&A performance of the target hospital shows promising potential. Building upon Du (2015), this paper explores if and how HIT redeployment from target hospitals to acquiring hospitals improves acquirers’ post-M&A quality of care.

## Hypothesis Development

In this paper, we examine hospital quality of care from the perspective of the structure-process-outcome (SPO) framework. As a prevalent framework in the HIT literature, the SPO framework suggests that the resources utilized by providers (structure) enhance the processes involved in delivering care to patients (process), which ultimately leads to better patient outcomes (outcome). While structure variables include the physical, technological, and personnel resources in the care delivery system, the process variables emphasize the actions and interactions that are under the control of the providers, as well as the effectiveness of the clinical care and interpersonal care processes (Campbell et al., 2000; Pinsonneault et al., 2017). The outcome variables directly measure the effects of care on patients using their health status or evaluation. In this paper, we adopt Campbell et al.’s (2000) definition of quality of care for individual patients under the SPO framework, which is defined by two dimensions: accessibility and effectiveness—essentially, “whether individuals can access the health structures and processes of care which they need and whether the care received is effective” (p. 1614). Specifically, at the hospital level, we define accessibility as the affordability or the efficiency of the healthcare service provided, and effectiveness as the effectiveness of clinical care.

## Resource Acquisition and Hospital Quality of Care

Hospitals engage in M&As to redeploy their existing resources in new contexts or acquire resources to fill resource gaps. As the healthcare sector is characterized by constant innovation and technological complexity, with high reliance on knowledgebased resources residing in highly specialized experts, it is unrealistic for hospitals to develop all their capabilities organically. Much of the target hospital’s knowledge and capabilities reside in the administrative and clinical processes of the hospital. HIT, as the essential technology managing these processes, serves as the foundation and repository of the hospital’s knowledge-based capabilities. Therefore, hospitals lacking capabilities in specific areas may seek to acquire targets that have appropriate IT application capabilities. While growing IT resources and capabilities organically can be timeconsuming and costly, accessing such capabilities through acquiring targets with well-implemented IT systems can reduce the risk of implementation failure.

Hospital leaders indicate that significant benefits from a merger come from scale-based cost savings and clinical standardization, via redeploying standardized processes managed by digital technologies at the hospitals (Noether & May, 2017). Scalebased cost-saving benefits are widely reported in horizontal M&As (Rabier, 2017). Similarly, in the hospital context, acquiring hospital systems enjoy enhanced efficiency from redeploying either administrative or clinical applications. For instance, redeploying administrative applications like enterprise resource planning and materials management applications (thus shared between the acquirer and target) can provide cost savings through coordination of purchasing via better bargaining position and centralized warehousing and distribution. Redeploying clinical applications can also have similar effects. For example, redeploying order entry applications can help codify patient information and can remove inefficiencies by reducing rework and errors (Borzekowski, 2009).

Clinical standardization is a process whereby the acquirer and target share expertise to jointly develop and enforce standardized protocols at their hospitals, supported by the accurate and complete clinical and financial data embedded in the EHRs at the individual hospitals. The process of clinical standardization allows the acquirer to access and absorb the target’s technica know-how, without sacrificing the completeness of the target’s unique processes and their capabilities. Clinical standardization is often supported by sharing de-identified data among the involved parties, which provides valuable insights that help derive better treatment plans and answer questions on patient populations and clinical processes (HHS, 2012). Using deidentified data, providers can share information with other organizations to create better tools and treatment plans to improve patient care and outcomes. Redeploying administrative applications can enhance clinical standardization and improve care quality. For instance, the target’s quality management applications, if redeployed, can help the acquirer track and manage non-conformance and highlight opportunities to improve existing processes. Redeployment of clinical applications can also improve quality of care through better clinical standardization. As clinical HIT systems are involved in collecting patient data, diagnosis, and treatment, redeploying such HIT applications can help the acquirer collect patient data and identify their conditions quickly, and can improve compliance to customized standards based on the patient’s condition (Bates & Gawande, 2003). Consequently, redeploying either administrative HIT applications or clinical HIT applications can provide cost efficiency through the economy of scale from a larger and well-coordinated hospital system and can improve the effectiveness of clinical care through the redeployment of standardized clinical processes. Therefore, we propose that the redeployment of HIT from the target hospital can potentially fill gaps in the acquirer’s application portfolio to form an integrated HIT system that can improve cost efficiency and the effectiveness of clinical care:

H1: Greater HIT redeployment from the target hospital to the acquiring hospital improves the quality of care of the acquiring hospital.

## Role of Embedded Human Capital in Acquirer Quality of Care

Prior literature in post-M&A integration emphasizes the transfer and reconfiguration of knowledge as a key factor for a successful post-M&A integration (Capron et al., 1998). Despite the opportunities that M&As provide to recombine knowledge in novel ways to create value (e.g., Ahuja & Katila, 2001; Puranam & Srikanth, 2007), there is also danger of disrupting the tacit, socially complex routines that underlie this knowledge (Ranft & Lord, 2000, 2002). This is because tacit knowledge is based on accumulated experience and is embedded in the expertise, skills, and routines developed by organizational members over time (Winter, 1987). To this end, preserving employees from target hospitals after the M&A is essential to successful knowledge transfer. Ranft and Lord (2002) indicated that key employees from various functional areas are valuable in capability transfer. This study found that losing such employees was viewed as a main obstacle during the acquisition integration process, as these employees formed an integral part of a high-functioning group, and their collective know-how was considered the most valuable capability of the target organization. Similarly, Graebner et al. (2017) showed that leaders from acquired firms are essential to successful post-M&A knowledge transfer; due to their familiarity with their own firm’s knowledge base and technical capabilities, they are able to identify unexpected opportunities for resource redeployment to create value for the newly consolidated firm.

The nature of the healthcare sector suggests that the knowledgebased capabilities of the target hospital are embedded in the specialized skills of the physicians and providers and the established processes, routines, and relationships in the hospital. To utilize these knowledge-based capabilities requires integration capabilities to effectively combine such expertise to produce improved outcomes. As the tacit knowledge embedded in these capabilities is not easily codifiable and transferable, skilled individuals at both acquiring and target hospitals must communicate and interact in a seamless manner to gradually establish relationships, routines, and processes that integrate their specialized knowledge with the complementary skills and expertise of other individuals. This interaction highlights the necessity of incorporating the target hospital’s processes, along with its human capital, into the acquirer’s processes, beyond simply transferring the technological components. When the target’s system is successfully redeployed to the acquirer, the acquirer can leverage the knowledge base and specialists at the target hospital and improve the quality of care at the acquiring hospital.

In sum, the enhancement of the acquirer’s quality of care is the result of the transfer of complementary knowledge from the target to the acquirer, which accompanies HIT redeployment. Senior employees across functionalities, including but not limited to clinicians, engineers, marketers, and top management, embody the target’s intellectual capital and serve as the foundation of their capabilities. Therefore, knowledge transfer can be enhanced by the retention of senior employees (Ranft & Lord, 2000). We thus hypothesize:

H2: When senior employee retention at the target is higher post-M&A, HIT redeployment from the target hospital to the acquiring hospital improves the quality of care of the acquiring hospital.

## Role of Knowledge Complementarity and EHR Compatibility

The impact of target-to-acquirer HIT redeployment is likely to improve the acquirer’s quality of care via transfer of knowledge; however, the impact is likely to be stronger under the following two conditions: (1) when the target hospital possesses complementary knowledge and capabilities that can directly benefit the acquirer, and (2) when the acquirer’s HIT can effectively facilitate the transfer of knowledge and capabilities from the target.

The economic theory of complementarities states that for a set of resources to be complementary, the joint return gained from the set of resources should be greater than the sum of returns gained from the individual resources in isolation (Milgrom & Roberts, 1995). By definition, these complementary resources have to be interdependent and mutually supportive, but they should not be identical. Prior research suggests that in multibusiness firms, synergies arise from the complementarity of knowledge relatedness (Tanriverdi & Venkatraman, 2005). Along the same logic, we propose that to benefit from target-toacquirer HIT redeployment via knowledge transfer, the target’s knowledge repository needs to be complementary to but distinct from the acquirer’s knowledge reserve.

In the context of M&As, previous research highlights that resource complementarity, rather than resource similarity, is linked to better performance (Harrison et al., 1991). When resource profiles between the acquirer and candidate target are similar, despite the potential synergy from economies of scope and scale, such synergy is easily detectable by other potential acquirers (thus not private and uniquely valuable); hence the target’s price will be bid up to a point at which the potential synergy is eroded by the acquisition price—achieving little post-M&A abnormal return (Barney, 1988). In contrast, complementary resources between the acquirer and target present opportunities for uniquely valuable synergy due to the difficulty in detecting and duplicating such synergy from other bidders (Harrison et al., 2001). Thus, we propose that post-M&A, complementary knowledge resources between the acquirer and target are more likely to be specific to the hospitals involved, thus generating abnormal returns. This means that the acquirer is more likely to benefit from the transfer of the target’s knowledge when the target’s clinical capabilities are relevant and complementary to the acquirer’s (i.e., when the acquirer and target treat conditions and conduct procedures in the same major diagnostic categories and the conditions and procedures are not overlapping). In addition, when both parties deal with similar types of complex patient cases, the uncertainty and complexity faced in the process help to build up the hospitals’ organization-specific knowledge, compared to cases where both hospitals treat similar types of less complex cases, where routinized clinical guidelines are largely available. This suggests that the transfer of knowledge from the target hospital to the acquiring hospital is more likely to occur when the acquirer and target treat conditions in the same major diagnostic categories and when the treated conditions are complex, requiring substantial clinician discretion and expertise.

Similarly, knowledge transfer from the target to the acquirer is facilitated by the compatibility between the acquirer’s and target’s EHR. Common EHR vendors are necessary conditions for the two parties to share de-identified patient and treatment data seamlessly, as data captured by different vendors’ systems might not share the same data fields and format due to variations in data collection practices and the underlying data models used by each vendor. Such data sharing is helpful for the development of better tools and treatment plans to improve patient care and outcomes at the acquiring hospital. Furthermore, machine learning algorithms used in clinical decision support systems (CDSS) present opportunities for learning from the pooled clinical data in real time (Sutton et al., 2020). If acquiring and target hospitals share compatible EHR systems, they are better able to scale to more advanced AI/ML-based CDSS capabilities. Thus, high compatibility of the acquirer’s and target’s EHR applications supports the seamless access and usage of patient data from the target hospital, helping to derive valuable insights for realtime decision-making and facilitating successful knowledge transfer from the target to the acquirer.

Similarly, prior literature has shown that stand-alone medical technologies have limited effects on improving the operation and care quality of the hospital, and the integration of these technologies into an information and communication network shows more promising effects (Angst et al., 2011; Miller & Tucker, 2011; McCullough et al., 2016). Thus, we hypothesize:

H3b: When the compatibility between the target’s and the acquirer’s EHR is higher, HIT redeployment from the target hospital to the acquiring hospital has a more positive impact on the quality of care of the acquiring hospital.

## Data and Empirical Strategy

## Sample Construction

We extracted all hospital M&As in the U.S., reported by the HIMSS Analytics Database, from 2005 to 2017. For all the M&A deals reported, the acquirer was an individual hospital under a hospital system, while all the targets were individual hospitals. Our sample included acquiring hospitals that acquired targets in only one year between 2005 and 2017 to study the impact of target-to-acquirer HIT redeployment on the acquirer’s quality of care.<sup>2</sup> All specialty hospitals were dropped from the acquirer sample. Our datasets combined data from the following sources. First, the HIMSS Analytics Database reports hospital M&As (from the entity history records table) and hospital IT application information. Second, hospital financial and operational data was extracted from the Healthcare Cost Reporting Information System (HCRIS) Database and American Hospital Directory (AHD). Third, adherence to recommended care measures came from the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) Hospital Compare data. Fourth, hospital labor data came from Revelio Labs (reveliolabs.com).

The sample construction process is as follows. First, we extracted all hospitals reported in the HIMSS Database between 2005 and 2017 as potential acquirers. To account for the likelihood of conducting an M&A, we adopted Heckman twostep selection models (more details in the Estimation Models section). Thus, all hospitals available from the HIMSS Database served as the universe of hospitals that could potentially engage in M&A activities in the first-stage estimation of the Heckman selection model. We then merged in these hospitals’ M&A activities and other control variables for the Heckman first-stage estimation, resulting in 5,701 unique hospitals in the sample. Second, we constructed the sample for the second stage of the Heckman selection model, estimating the effect of redeployment on acquirer performance. The unit of analysis was the acquiring hospital year. We started by looking at all hospitals in the HIMSS Database that made an acquisition between 2005 and 2017. After merging in the dependent variables from the CMS Hospital Compare Database (adherence to recommended care information was available between 2005 and 2014) and AHD (ALS and average cost information was available from 2005 to 2017) and all the control variables, the final sample included 321-533 unique hospitals that conducted M&As within our sample period, depending on the dependent variable used. Table 1 describes the sample construction process. Within this sample, 326 unique targets were acquired. Often, the acquirer acquired multiple targets in the same year, ranging from 1 to 11.

## Measurements

## Dependent Variables

Following our definition of quality of care (Campbell et al., 2000), we focused on three dependent variables. Adherence to recommended care indicates the effectiveness of the clinical care process (Zhou et al., 2009; McCullough et al., 2016). It was measured by 19 process-of-care indicators<sup>3</sup> in the Hospital Compare Database. These indicators show how often or how quickly hospitals provided care that has been shown by research to get the best results for patients with certain conditions. These indicators were collected through surveying patients, with both a score (indicating the percentage of patients getting the recommended care) and the sample size reported. For instance, one of the survey questions was used to measure the percentage of heart attack patients given aspirin at discharge. To measure the adherence to recommended care of a hospital, we first merged all the indicators in a specific year into individual hospitals. The adherence to recommended care score for each hospital is the weighted average of the 19 indicators (the weight being the sample size of the survey question on that indicator).

CMI-adjusted average length of stay (ALS) indicates both the hospital’s efficiency and clinical care effectiveness. CMI reflects the complexity of procedures and the severity of complications of patients treated at a healthcare facility. ALS has been widely used in the HIT literature as a measure of efficiency and hospital care effectiveness (Glick et al., 2003; McDermott & Stock, 2007; Ayer et al., 2019). Prior research suggests that ALS is associated with cost and efficiency, as well as care effectiveness and speed in service delivery (Thomas et al., 1997); thus, it is viewed as an appropriate measure reflecting both efficiency and the effectiveness of clinical care at hospitals. CMI-adjusted average inpatient cost (average cost) measured the affordability of the healthcare services and thus the efficiency of a hospital’s operations overall (Dranove & Shanley, 1995; Du, 2015; Angst et al., 2011). ALS and average cost were calculated from Final Rule Medicare Inpatient Prospective Payment System claims data, obtained through the AHD.

## Independent Variables

Our main independent variable is target-to-acquirer HIT redeployment. Following prior literature, HIT includes both administrative and clinical components (Menon et al., 2009). Administrative HIT supports value-chain activities (examples include financial decision support, human resources, staff scheduling, and revenue cycle management, etc.), whereas clinical HIT comprises primary value-chain activities in a hospital (e.g., cardiology and picture archiving and communication system, emergency department information system, and laboratory systems, as well as EHR modules, such as clinical data repositories, clinical decision support systems, computerized practitioner order entry, and physician documentation). As in Du (2015), target-to-acquirer HIT redeployment is a dummy variable indicating whether newly added HIT (including either administrative or clinical applications) software products were installed in the acquiring hospitals two years after, compared to two years before the M&A—specifically, these newly added software products were not scheduled for installation in the acquirer before the acquisition took place, but they were in use by the target hospital before the M&A took place.

## Control Variables

We controlled for acquiring hospital characteristics, including for-profit status, teaching hospital status, age, and number of staffed beds and organizational similarity between the acquirer and the target.<sup>4</sup> We also controlled for the average distance in miles between the acquiring hospital and the target hospital (as prior studies have indicated that geographic proximity between the acquirer and target can affect post-M&A performance; Rabier, 2017) and HIT at both the acquiring and target hospitals, including the number of distinct HIT applications and the number of vendors used. In the first stage of the Heckman twostep model (see details in the next section), we followed prior literature and controlled for local conditions, including total number of hospitals collocated to the acquirer (within 0.3 miles); natural logarithm of the acquirer’s county population and per capita income (Dafny, 2009); hospital system’s for-profit status, age, proportion of academic hospitals, total number of staffed beds, and number of distinct HIT applications; and the number of vendors used by the acquiring hospital system (all data taken from the previous year).

## Empirical Strategy

## Estimation Models

The HIT used and its potential for redeployment may affect the likelihood of a potential acquirer conducting an M&A. In this case, the acquirers that did take over the targets tend to enjoy higher net benefits from M&A, compared to those that did not. This self-selection issue could bias the relationship between HIT redeployment and the acquirer’s post-M&A quality of care. In order to correct for this potential selfselection bias, we used a Heckman two-step selection model. To estimate the model, we needed to add at least one additional exogenous variable in the first stage that was not included in the second-stage model. Thus, we added the total number of hospitals collocated to the acquirer to the first stage estimation; as shown by Dafny (2009), the number of collocated hospitals increases the likelihood of the focal hospital entering into an acquisition.

To estimate the impact of HIT redeployment on the acquiring hospital’s quality of care, in the second stage, we adopted difference-in-difference (DID) models comparing the qualityof-care trajectory of acquiring hospitals that obtained digital resources from a target and those that did not. Specifically, we estimated:

## Senior Employee Retention: A Mechanism for Knowledge Transfer

We propose that the advancement of the acquirer’s quality of care is the result of the transfer of complementary knowledge from the target to the acquirer, accompanying the HIT redeployment. Senior employees, in particular, embody the acquired entity’s intellectual capital and serve as the foundation of the target’s capabilities; knowledge transfer can be significantly enhanced by the retention of senior employees. To test the proposed mechanism empirically, we measured senior employee retention of the target as a proxy for knowledge transfer. The labor data of the target hospitals came from Revelio Labs (www.reveliolabs.com). Revelio Labs captures and standardizes all public employment profiles from LinkedIn, presenting current and historical workforce composition and trends of any company. Specifically, Revelio Labs provides individual-level position data for organizations across industries. This database makes it possible to observe the position composition of any organization available on LinkedIn at a given time. We matched our target hospitals to the Revelio Labs positions using hospital names and location (city and state). Out of the 326 target hospitals in our sample, we were able to link 174 targets to the Revelio Labs labor data. Since Revelio Labs provides detailed information at the position-user level, we were able to observe the exact dates that an individual held a certain position in an organization and the detailed job title of the person, spanning administrative, marketing, engineering, sales, clinical, and finance/accounting positions.

Revelio labs also standardizes employment records and uses an ensemble model to label the seniority of a position, derived from factors such as individual’s current title, company, job history, age, and duration and seniority of previous employment.<sup>5</sup> The seniority score provided by Revelio Labs for each individual position has seven distinct levels (labeled from 1 to 7). We defined senior employees as positions in Categories 6 and 7 (according to Revelio Labs, Category 7 is at the senior executive level, e.g., CFO, COO, CEO, and Category 6 is at the executive level, e.g., managing director, Treasury; director of Engineering). For example, these senior employee titles include operations supervisor, administrative director, CEO, CFO, COO, chief administrative officer, chief human resources officer, chief information officer, chief legal officer, associate director clinical affairs, medical director, chairman of each medical department, chief medical/clinical officer, chief of surgery, chief nursing officer, chief physicist, and chief resident. In our final sample, there were 42,732 unique individuals employed by the target hospitals, with 1,003 individuals employed in these senior positions. These senior employees accounted for 2.4% of the overall observable employment.

Empirically, we adopted the same DID model and used the post-M&A senior retention rate (retention) of the target hospitals as a moderator. Senior retention rate was measured as the average yearly retention rate of senior positions at the target hospitals in Year 0 (i.e., the year the M&A occurred) to Year 2, after the M&A. Specifically, the yearly retention rate was measured as the percentage of the senior employees existing in the target hospital in December of the focal year among the senior employees that were present in the same target hospital in January of that year. We removed any cases where the senior employee left the target hospital and moved to the acquiring hospital for a new job from the calculation of the retention rate. This moderator reflects the extent to which targets managed to retain their senior talent after the M&A. We expect to observe higher acquirer quality of care when the target’s senior employees were retained, and lower quality of care when the retention of these skills was low post-M&A.

## Facilitator of Knowledge Transfer: Diagnostics Complementarity

If redeployment improves the acquirer’s quality of care through the transfer of knowledge, we would expect a more pronounced positive impact under the following two conditions: first, when the target hospital possesses complementary knowledge and capabilities that can directly benefit the acquirer; second, when the acquirer’s HIT can effectively facilitate the transfer of knowledge and capabilities. For the first condition, we explored four knowledge complementarity measures as moderators to reveal the impact of different types of clinical capability complementarity between the acquirer and the target: MDC (major diagnostic category) correlation, within-MDC DRG (diagnosis-related group) correlation, high-CMI DRG correlation, and low-CMI DRG correlation. MDCs comprise 25 mutually exclusive diagnosis areas, where each MDC corresponds to a single organ system or etiology and, in general, is associated with a particular medical specialty. When two hospitals treat similar MDCs, one party is more likely to possess medical knowledge that is valuable to the other party. DRG, on the other hand, is a more detailed classification of patients’ clinical diagnosis under MDC. DRGs present a patient classification scheme that provides a means of relating the type of patients a hospital treats (i.e., its case mix) to the costs incurred by the hospital. Within each MDC, patient conditions treated vary significantly in terms of the severity of illness, prognosis, treatment difficulty, need for intervention, and resource intensity (CMS.gov, 2019), measured by the CMI. For example, although both belong to MDC 5 (disease and disorder of the circulatory system), acute myocardial infarction discharged alive (MS DRG 280 281 282) is much less complicated to treat, compared to cardiac valve and other major cardiothoracic procedures with cardiac catheterization (MS DRG 216 2017 218).

The four knowledge complementarity measures are constructed as follows. MDC correlation indicates whether the acquirer and target treat conditions in the same MDCs. We constructed this measure as follows: (1) We calculated the percentage of discharges in each MDC in total discharge for both the acquiring hospital and the target hospital—this measure provided two 25-item vectors (one vector for the acquiring hospital’s MDC discharge percentages; one vector for the target hospital’s), and (2) we calculated the Pearson correlation coefficient between the two vectors. A high MDC correlation means that the acquirer and target treated patients in similar major diagnostic areas. We propose that when this correlation is high, the target tends to have more relevant knowledge for the acquirer to utilize (i.e., the acquirer and target are more likely to possess knowledge that is complementary to each other). For example, two hospitals that both treat many diseases and disorders of the respiratory system (MDC 4) would be more likely to leverage each other’s practices and treatment plans for such conditions, whereas another hospital that specializes in the nerve system (MDC 1) would be less likely to borrow and benefit from such knowledge.

Within-MDC DRG correlation, on the other hand, indicates whether, within the same MDC, the acquirer and target treat cases in the same DRGs. We propose that treating largely the same DRGs (thus high within-MDC DRG correlation) does not necessarily improve the acquirer’s quality of care. This is because the acquirer benefits most from knowledge transfer when the target’s knowledge is relevant but distinct (in other words complementary) from that of the acquirer. However, when within-MDC DRG correlation is high, the acquirer and target are likely to have experience treating the same DRGs in the MDC, reflecting a largely overlapping knowledge base. For this measure, we first calculated the percentage of discharges of each DRG in the MDC to which it belongs for both acquirer and target hospitals. Then we calculated the Pearson correlation coefficient of the two DRG-level vectors of the acquirer and target. While a high within-MDC DRG correlation means that within each of the MDCs the acquirer and target treated patients with largely the same conditions, we propose that this reflects high levels of knowledge similarity (in contrast to knowledge complementarity, measured by MDC correlation) between the acquirer and target.

Further, we calculated high-CMI DRG correlation and low-CMI DRG correlation. We used a CMI level of 1.5, which was the median CMI in our sample, as the cutoff for highversus low-CMI DRG. To construct the high-CMI DRG correlation, (1) we kept all high-CMI DRGs for each hospital; (2) for both the acquirer and target, we calculated the percentage of each DRG’s discharge in total discharge; and (3) we calculated the Pearson correlation coefficient of the acquirer’s and target’s high-CMI DRG percentage vectors obtained from (2). A large value of high-CMI DRG correlation indicates that the acquirer and target treated similarly complex cases. Low-CMI DRG correlation was constructed in a similar way, with only low-CMI DRGs.

We propose that when high-CMI DRG correlation is high, the acquiring hospital benefits more from the knowledge transfer from the target hospital. This is because in high-CMI cases, the clinicians have to deal with greater uncertainty and complexity, which requires more discretion, and are thus able to build more hospital-specific knowledge. In contrast, low-CMI cases are typically routinized according to publicly known guidelines and procedures. Hence, the acquirer is more likely to learn unique knowledge from the target when its corresponding high-CMI DRG correlation is high. Therefore, we postulate that HIT redeployment has a stronger positive impact on acquirer post-M&A quality of care when high-CMI DRG correlation is high, compared to when low-CMI DRG correlation is high.

## Facilitator of Knowledge Transfer: EHR Compatibility

We hypothesize that in order to utilize hospital-specific knowledge located at the target hospital, the acquirer needs to have a compatible EHR to effectively facilitate the transfer of knowledge from the target. EHR compatibility between the acquirer and target was operationalized as the number of EHR vendors added, using the following steps: (1) We listed the module-by-module EHR vendors used by the acquirer a year before the M&A. (2) Then, we listed the module-by-module EHR vendors used by the target a year before the M&A. (3) We calculated how many times we needed to “flip” the target’s EHR vendors to make it the same as the acquirer’s EHR vendors list.<sup>6</sup> In the same way, we also measured the compatibility of each of the five EHR modules separately—i.e., clinical data repository (CDR), clinical decision support system (CDSS), computerized physician order entry (CPOE), order entry (OE), and physician documentation (PD) vendors added.

EHR vendors added indicates how difficult it is for the acquirer to utilize the target hospital’s knowledge. A high compatibility of the acquirer’s and target’s EHR applications supports seamless access and usage of patient data from the target hospital and, as a result, facilitates successful knowledge transfer from the target to the acquirer (Somanchi et al., 2023). In line with this logic, we expect that when acquirer–target EHR compatibility is high (i.e., low levels of EHR vendors added), the positive impact of redeployment on acquirer post-M&A quality of care should be more pronounced. When EHR vendors added is low, it is more likely that the target is using the same vendors as the acquirer. In such cases, the knowledge accumulated at the target hospital is thus likely to be more easily accessible to the acquirer.

## Results

## Descriptive Statistics

The summary statistics are reported in Table 2. On average, 12% of the acquiring hospitals redeployed HIT from a target hospital. Overall, the acquiring and target hospitals used 50 and 53 unique HIT applications, contracting with between 11 and 12 vendors for these applications. HIT redeployment is negatively correlated with the acquirer’s adherence to recommended care and positively correlated with the acquirer’s ALS and average cost.

## The Impact of Redeployment on Acquirer Quality of Care

Table 3 reports the overall impact of redeployment on the acquirer’s quality of care. The first stage results show that the acquiring hospital system’s HIT resources increased its likelihood of engaging in an M&A. In addition, consistent with prior studies (Dafny, 2009), the number of collocated hospitals significantly increased the chance of a hospital system conducting M&A transactions. In the second stage, when no redeployment occurred, the M&A, on average, improved the adherence to recommended care $( \beta _ { p o s t } = 4 . 3 8 4 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ but had no significant impact on the ALS and average cost of the acquiring hospital. The interactions between post and HIT redeployment indicate the post-M&A quality of care effects on the acquirer from adopting the digital resources of a target hospital. The results show that HIT redeployment marginally increased the adherence to recommended care for the acquiring hospital post-M&A $( \beta _ { r e d e p l o y m n e t * p o s t } = 2 . 0 0 7 , p < 0 . 1 )$ ). However, although HIT redeployment showed positive impacts on the acquirer’s ALS and average cost, the effects were not statistically significant. Thus, H1 is partially supported (only on adherence to recommended care).

To test the validity of the DID setup, we examined the DID relative time model (Angrist & Pischke, 2009) to test if any pretreatment trend existed in the data. The year before the M&A (year t-1) was used as the baseline. The results in Table 4 show that none of the pre-treatment periods were statistically significant. The relative time model also reveals a significant positive impact of HIT redeployment on adherence to recommended care 3-4 years after the M&A. The relative time model shows no significant impact of HIT redeployment on the acquiring hospital’s ALS and average cost after the M&A.

## Explore the Mechanism: Senior Employee Retention and Transfer of Knowledge

As proposed in H2, HIT redeployment from the target to the acquirer improves the acquirer’s post-M&A quality of care through the transfer of knowledge-based capabilities. This effect suggests that the senior retention rate at the target hospital after the M&A should have implications for the acquiring hospital’s quality of care. Specifically, HIT redeployment should exert a comparatively more positive impact on the acquirer’s quality of care when the target’s post-M&A senior retention rate is high, compared to when the senior retention rate is low. Therefore, in the following analysis, we use the target hospital’s senior retention rate as a moderator for our baseline model—the quality-of-care implications of redeployment under high and low senior retention conditions. If the proposed mechanism is at work, we should observe a superior quality of care from HIT redeployment when the senior retention level after the M&A at the target hospital is high. On the other hand, when the senior retention level is low, we should observe lower quality of care from HIT redeployment at the acquiring hospital.

As in the original DID model, the coefficient of the interaction term between redeployment and post shows the overall effect of HIT redeployment. To illustrate the moderating effect of senior employee retention on a more detailed year-by-year basis, we examine senior employee retention as a moderator using the relative time model. Similar models are used in the following sections when testing the other two sets of moderators. The results in Table 5 support the above proposition. In the following models, we interact the relative year treatment variable with the target’s post-M&A senior retention rate. ## Addressing Endogeneity Concerns and Robustness Tests

A model that directly estimates the effect of redeployment on hospital quality of care could suffer from omitted variable bias. For instance, an acquirer with high IT management capabilities would be more willing to utilize a target’s digital resources (thus higher redeployment); meanwhile, such an acquirer would also be more likely to achieve better post-M&A quality of care as a result of its capability to exploit digital resources. Thus, we employed DID models with instrumental variables (IVs) as a robustness check for the models reported in the previous section. The IVs are the number of applications and the number of vendors used by the target before the M&A (i.e., preMA\_target\_app and preMA\_target\_vendor), and the average number of applications and the average number of vendors used by control hospitals before the M&A (i.e., preMA\_controlhospital\_app and preMA\_controlhospital\_vendor). Control hospitals were constructed as follows. For each acquiring hospital (i.e., treated hospital), we matched it to a set of candidate control hospitals that (1) were not involved in any M&A in the sample period, (2) were at least 200 miles away from the treated hospital, and (3) had the same academic status. Then, among the candidate control hospitals that met the above three criteria, for each acquirer (the treated hospital), we kept the top 10 candidate control hospitals that were most similar in size (number of beds) to the treated ones.

Valid IVs need to satisfy two conditions: (1) they must directly influence redeployment, and (2) they need to be exogenous (i.e., they cannot affect post-M&A acquirer quality of care other than through the redeployment variable). First, our IVs directly affect the explanatory variable. When target hospitals possess high levels of digital resources pre-M&A (i.e., higher preMA\_target\_app and preMA\_target\_vendor), HIT redeployment to the acquirer becomes more probable because it is more likely for the acquirer to find digital resources that can complement those the acquirer already uses. Regarding the second set of IVs (preMA\_controlhospital\_app and preMA\_controlhospital\_vendor), the higher level of digital resources at these comparable (in size and status) control hospitals would be likely to influence the HIT adoption decision of the acquirer (i.e., the treated hospital) through peer influence, encouraging the acquirer to increase its own digital footprint by redeploying digital resources from the target hospital (Angst et al., 2010). The first stage diagnostics show that these IVs are significantly correlated with the endogenous variables. The Cragg-Donald F statistics (ranging from 122.9 to 153.69) are significant and exceed the Stock and Yogo (2005) thresholds, showing that the IVs are not weak. In addition, the underidentification test also supports that the IVs are relevant (the p values of the Kleibergen-Paap rk LM statistics are all under 1%).

Second, these IVs also meet the exclusion restriction condition. It is unlikely that the pre-M&A digital resources of the target hospital affected the acquirer’s post-M&A quality of care directly, other than through the target-to-acquirer HIT redeployment, since before the M&A, the target and the acquirer were separate entities, and the target’s pre-M&A digital resources could have only influenced the acquirer’s quality of care through the redeployment of their resources to the acquirer. In the second set of IVs, we used control hospitals at least 200 miles away to make sure that the treated and control hospitals did not share the same local market—in such cases, technologies implemented at the control hospitals might have influenced the treated hospital’s quality of care directly through mechanisms other than influencing the treated hospital’s digital technology (for example, through local market competition). Results of the Hansen’s J test validated the overidentifying restriction. The Hansen’s J statistics were not significant; thus, we cannot reject the null hypothesis and conclude that the overidentifying restriction is valid.

To show the robustness of the model results reported in the previous section, we used control function models (Wooldridge, 2015; Ren et al., 2023) to estimate the main effects and models with the three key moderators, with the IVs listed above. In the control function procedure, in the first stage, the endogenous variables—redeployment and their interaction terms—were estimated against the four IVs and all the control variables using a linear panel model with the acquiring hospital and year fixed effects to obtain the estimated error term for each of the endogenous variables. In the second stage, we estimated the same models reported in the previous section with the estimated error terms controlled for in each regression. The results are reported in the Appendix. The models with correction for endogeneity had qualitatively similar results to the models reported in the previous section.

## Discussion and Conclusion

Despite the surge in M&As in the healthcare industry, results on how M&As impact quality of care remain mixed (Bailey, 2022). Hospitals operate in an environment emphasizing an increasing focus on the cost of providing medical care, viewed through the lens of the clinical care setting and the administrative costs of delivering services. However, prior work suggests that hospital consolidations either did not impact patient care quality or led to poorer outcomes (Beaulieu et al., 2020; O’Hanlon et al., 2019; Cerullo et al., 2022). Against this backdrop, this paper examines the role of an important factor— HIT redeployment—from targets to acquirers on the quality of care of the acquirer after M&As. Healthcare information technologies play a central role in influencing both the effectiveness of clinical care and cost efficiencies, and there is increasing evidence that M&As in general, and hospital M&As in particular, are driven by expectations of achieving advantageous digital resource combinations by combining the HIT resources of the target and acquirer into one entity (Du, 2015; Cao et al., 2022b; Somanchi et al., 2023).

While hospital M&As present an opportunity for acquirers to redeploy their HIT applications to target hospitals, this paper contributes to the literature by highlighting the value to acquirers from redeploying digital resources from target hospitals. It also highlights that the mechanism underlying such redeployment is the retention of senior personnel from the target hospital. As senior employees embody intellectual capital and serve as the foundation of the target’s capabilities, retaining such talent is vital to access the tacit and socially complex knowledge-based resources of the target. Further, the results indicate that the value of the knowledge embedded in the human capital of the target hospital for the acquiring hospital is limited to diagnostic complementarity in the task contexts between the target and the acquirer, as well as the compatibility of the EHR of the target and the acquirer, since this influences the knowledge transfer. Overall, our research suggests that HIT redeployment from the target hospital to the acquiring hospital improves the post-M&A adherence to recommended care on the part of the acquirer. However, the moderation analysis indicates that the acquirer’s ability to improve its quality of care from HIT redeployment from the target is contingent on acquiring a target that possesses complementary knowledge, which must be retained by the target hospital after the acquisition via retention of its senior employees and shared and transferred to the acquirer via compatible EHR.

The analysis of personnel retention explicates the mechanisms through which the redeployment of digital resources from targets to acquirers enhances outcomes. Senior employee retention, which allows the acquiring unit to access the knowledge and specialized skills of the target hospital, is important to facilitating digital redeployment. For instance, implementing systems to coordinate related processes for cancer treatment involves multiple iterations between clinical diagnosis by doctors, cytology screening of biopsies, confirmatory testing through PET and CT scans, and decisions about subsequent radiation therapies. This process requires complex workflows to be established among multiple units (physician groups, pathology labs, radiology, surgery, etc.) in order to share tests and results for diagnosis and decision-making regarding medication and treatment planning. It also requires these workflows to be interfaced with administrative systems to enable staffing, materials ordering, equipment scheduling, and patient billing. Further, the ability to seek input from individuals with experience in prior implementations (at the target hospital) and knowledge regarding potential problems and what is needed to anticipate and address these problems is likely to be critical to redeploy such systems into an acquiring hospital.

The analysis suggests that the impact of HIT redeployment on clinical care effectiveness and efficiency is higher when there is complementarity in the diagnostic context of the target and acquiring hospitals. For the acquirer to improve its quality of care via HIT redeployment from the target, the target needs to possess complementary rather than overlapping knowledgebased capabilities. Specifically, when the target and the acquirer focus on different major diagnostic categories, the acquirer can’t improve its quality of care using the knowledge-based capabilities of the target. However, when the acquirer and target treat conditions in the same major diagnostic areas, the acquirer can improve its quality of care. The improvement of quality of care occurs especially when the acquirer and target share complementary knowledge in handling complex cases and procedures—where there is ambiguity and where discretion is required—rather than when the target and acquirer focus on the treatment of less complex cases and procedures, where standardized protocols can provide the requisite knowledge.

Further, the impact of redeployment is higher if the compatibility between the EHRs of the target and acquiring hospitals is higher. Such compatibility enables the acquirer to access the knowledge-based capabilities available at the target hospital. Specifically, having compatible EHRs is essential for both parties to seamlessly share deidentified patient and treatment data. It also advances the capability of the acquirer to scale advanced AI/ML-based CDSS capabilities.

This research emphasizes the importance of the view of information systems in the healthcare industry as complex sociotechnical arrangements involving the intricate reconfiguration of organizational processes and resources for their functioning. The ability to advantageously redeploy resources from the target to the acquiring hospital is thus significantly enhanced by access to the experience and expertise of personnel from the target hospital. Recognizing the stickiness of the knowledge is critical in the M&A context, as retaining senior personnel ensures smoother system integration, mitigates potential challenges, and enhances the acquirer’s ability to leverage redeployed resources for improved clinical and administrative outcomes. Extending prior research (e.g., Somanchi et al., 2023; Du, 2015) that has contributed significant insights on the role of HIT in M&As, this work points to the human resource dimension as an important source to explain outcomes. Our work points to the importance of recognizing expertise embedded in individuals at a point when a dominant driver in the healthcare industry (as in other contexts) appears to be the promise of deriving significant cost efficiencies from AI and algorithmic approaches to delivering medical care. To the extent that novel AI systems, like the conventional information systems that preceded them, still require human expertise to configure and deploy them to create value, this study reaffirms the need to consider the human dimension involved in the adoption, routinization, and the scaling of information systems in organizational contexts.

The results have important implications for practice. This research indicates that acquirers experience a boost in their quality of care from accessing knowledge-based capabilities of the target hospital that are not possessed by the acquirer. The key to success is to retain senior personnel at the target hospital, particularly when the diagnostic context is complementary and the EHRs are compatible. In particular, acquirers should consider complementarity in diagnostic contexts and EHR compatibility when evaluating potential targets for acquisition, as the acquisition of such targets is more likely to improve the quality of care of the acquirer. In other words, acquirers should focus on targets that possess complementary rather than overlapping knowledge-based capabilities and on targets that possess compatible EHRs that facilitate rather than hinder the transfer of such knowledgebased capabilities. The results also highlight the importance of integration initiatives, as the benefits for the acquirer are not immediate and usually take a couple of years to manifest.

This research opens several avenues for future research. Case studies of the process of redeployment and acquirer-target integration in the healthcare context (such as the work of Tanriverdi & Du, 2011) could extend the understanding of the issues involved in resource sharing and transfer between acquirers and targets and of the considerations involved in personnel retention and retrenchment decisions. Such studies could also shed light on how the dynamics of interactions among acquirer and target personnel surrounding resource redeployments influence outcomes. Further, studies exploring resource redeployments from target units in the acquisition of other medical facilities, such as long-term care centers and ambulatory surgery centers, could also provide useful insights.

Researchers have approached the goals of deriving value in M&As in the healthcare setting in a unidimensional manner without distinguishing between the multiple potential goals of such initiatives (e.g., cost reduction, revenue enhancement, expansion of market footprint to acquire greater market power, achieving greater scale in specialized diagnostic areas, etc.). Each of these goals potentially involves distinct implementation strategies and thus differing approaches to resource reconfiguration and redeployment. Moreover, with private equity players increasingly becoming active in medical M&As (Cai & Song, 2023; Kannan et al., 2023), the nature of resource redeployments and their consequences for outcomes for M&As driven by financial considerations (as opposed to driven by operating synergies) is an important area for future research. Conventional healthcare organizations, even those operating with a for-profit motive, have inherently viewed their mission to be profitable as being secondary to delivering quality healthcare. Examining the nature of resource redeployments in M&As by private equity players in the medical industry is important in order to understand potentially advantageous strategies and to derive a greater understanding of potential downsides in M&As driven solely by financial motives.

Recent work points to the trend towards fewer but larger healthcare transactions in the industry (Bailey, 2022; Southwick, 2022). Large healthcare M&As can significantly change the competitive landscape, especially in local markets, and potentially lead to undesirable patient outcomes. Future research examining how the dynamics of resource redeployments (both from target to acquirer and from acquirer to target) differ in large versus small M&A transactions can highlight contingencies in how resource redeployments create value in the healthcare industry.

Finally, to address the potential endogeneity issue, we implemented a DID research design with instrumental variables and self-selection bias correction. The models were estimated with acquiring hospital fixed effects to eliminate bias caused by any unobserved time-invariant variable. These methodologies and the robustness analysis support a plausible causal mechanism. However, we acknowledge that unobserved time-varying factors may still have biased our estimates, a limitation of archival data analysis.
