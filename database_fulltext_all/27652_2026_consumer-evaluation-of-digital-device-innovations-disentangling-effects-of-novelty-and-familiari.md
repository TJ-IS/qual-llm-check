# CONSUMER EVALUATION OF DIGITAL DEVICE INNOVATIONS: DISENTANGLING EFFECTS OF NOVELTY AND FAMILIARITY<sup>1</sup>

Nila Zhang School of Management, Fudan University Shanghai, China {nilazhang@fudan.edu.cn}

Wai Fong Boh Nanyang Business School, Nanyang Technological University Singapore {AWFBoh@ntu.edu.sg}

Gunwoong Lee Korea University Business School Seoul, Korea {leegw@korea.ac.kr}

As digital devices increasingly integrate hardware and software features, firms must adopt innovation strategies that effectively balance novelty and familiarity to enhance consumer evaluation. While novel hardware components can introduce unique functionalities that attract consumers, excessive novelty may impede consumer acceptance. This study investigates how hardware component innovation strategies must navigate the delicate interplay between novelty and familiarity by examining two critical dimensions: the timing of hardware innovations (early vs. late) and the role of software-supported interaction (with vs. without related software support). By distinguishing between dominant design components familiar to consumers and non-dominant design components that are inherently unfamiliar, we uncover nuanced strategic insights. Our findings reveal that early introduction of dominant design innovations is crucial, and enhancing consumer interactions through software support significantly improves consumer satisfaction. Conversely, for nondominant component innovations, a later market introduction proves more advantageous. Notably, softwaresupported interactions are less effective for these non-dominant innovations, as such support may inadvertently accentuate their unfamiliarity. These findings provide strategic guidance for smartphone manufacturers to leverage software-supported interactions and optimize the timing of hardware innovations to achieve an optimal balance between novelty and familiarity.

Keywords: Digital devices, component innovation, novelty, familiarity, dominant design, consumer evaluation

## Introduction

Manufacturers of digital devices, such as smartphones, place significant emphasis on incorporating novel features and functionalities into their latest products to attract consumers interested in the most advanced technologies and to outdo their competitors. When asked about key factors that influence their purchase of a new smartphone, 61% of respondents cited innovative hardware components as a critical determinant (Teads, 2022). To outperform competitors, leading digital device companies, <sup>2</sup> such as Apple and Samsung, continuously introduce innovations into their latest product series.

Hardware components, the foundational elements that comprise a digital device, are pivotal to the core functionality of the product. Examples include microprocessors and randomaccess memory (RAM) chips, which provide processing and storage capabilities for personal computers (Adomavicius et al., 2008). Advances in hardware components of digital devices have significantly expanded their capabilities and improved performance (Klingebiel & Joseph, 2016). As highlighted by Lee and Berente (2013, p. 13), these “withincomponent/subsystem innovations are not necessarily minor.” Component innovations, such as the addition of new sensors or improvements to device cameras, can disrupt industries and greatly affect product performance (Tegarden et al., 1999).

Rapid technological advancements and the increasing availability of new hardware components have intensified the complexity firms face when designing next-generation digital devices to keep up with consumers’ growing demands (Lyytinen et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2022). Product innovations aim to introduce new features that provide enhanced value and capabilities (Danneels & Kleinschmidtb, 2001). However, consumer satisfaction largely depends on how they perceive the innovation’s value, which is influenced by their existing knowledge and past experiences (Ho et al., 2020; Zou et al., 2023). When innovations deviate significantly from consumers’ existing understanding, a disconnect can emerge between the expected and perceived value. To address this challenge, our study explores how innovators can balance novelty and familiarity in digital device innovation to effectively satisfy consumers. Specifically, we investigated two key hardware component innovation strategies: the timing of innovation introduction and the role of software in supporting consumer interactions with hardware innovations.

First, we examine how the timing of component innovations influences consumer evaluations. Early market innovations create a paradoxical dynamic: While they introduce technological advancements that potentially increase product utility, they also require significant cognitive recalibration from consumers (Klingebiel & Joseph, 2016). This cognitive challenge stems from the disruption of existing mental schemas, compelling consumers to adjust their technological expectations and understanding (Mandler, 1982). As a result, the timing of introducing innovation plays a critical role in shaping consumer perception.

We also investigate whether software support for consumer interactions with hardware component innovations influences consumer evaluations (Wang et al., 2022). While hardware components such as microchips and sensors provide the foundational capabilities, it is often the software interface that determines how consumers experience and derive value from these innovations (Rijsdijk & Hultink, 2009). When firms pair hardware innovations with software interfaces, these interfaces act as the primary lens through which consumers understand and interact with hardware features. Software interfaces can enhance accessibility and intuitiveness, making hardware components easier for consumers to recognize and adopt as incremental innovations. For highly novel hardware innovations, however, software interfaces may unintentionally highlight technical complexity, increasing the cognitive effort required to grasp the enhanced value proposition (Bu et al., 2022; Rahrovani et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2022). This strategic choice between hardware-only innovation and various levels of software integration remains a crucial yet understudied aspect of digital innovation that significantly impacts consumer satisfaction.

Our research aims to deepen our understanding of firms’ digital device innovation strategies by exploring the challenges consumers face in comprehending and evaluating novel digital innovations (Davis, 1989; Wang et al., 2022), contributing to the broader discourse on digital innovation in the consumer devices sector (Yoo et al., 2010). Researchers have primarily studied product innovations from the firm’s perspective and have not paid significant attention to the effect of consumer perceptions on innovation outcomes (Hauser et al., 2006; Yoo, 2010). Consumer perceptions of a product are influenced by the configuration of its attributes (Rindova & Petkova, 2007). Utilizing a consumer cognition perspective (Mandler, 1982), our study offers an alternative lens to explore and refine effective digital product innovation strategies. Without a deep understanding of how consumers react to product innovation designs, firms cannot achieve repeatable product success and develop superior product design strategies (Gu et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2022).

Component innovation plays a crucial role in digital device innovation, particularly as current digital devices increasingly integrate diverse hardware components and leverage synergies with associated software features (Wang et al., 2022; Yoo et al., 2010). However, existing information systems (IS) research has predominantly examined overall product innovativeness, overlooking the strategic contributions of individual components (Faulkner & Runde, 2019; Lee et al., 2020; Lyytinen et al., 2016). While some studies have explored complementary effects in bundled products, such as a game console paired with popular video games or a tablet PC with its accessories (Binken & Stremersch, 2009; Han et al., 2009), a significant research gap persists in understanding individual hardware component innovation strategies. Our study aims to address this gap by identifying effective product design strategies—specifically, the timing of innovation introduction and software-supported interaction—for innovative hardware components within a digital device. By analyzing how firms can strategically balance familiarity and novelty, we aim to provide nuanced insights into consumer assessment mechanisms and component-level innovation approaches.

We explore consumer evaluations of digital device innovation through online product reviews in the context of smartphone products. We recorded detailed hardware and software specifications for the 63 most popular smartphones offered by the 10 leading smartphone vendors from multiple data sources and collected 88,952 associated consumer reviews. We used textual analysis, including content and sentiment analysis, to (1) identify smartphone innovation design strategies and (2) assess consumer evaluations of smartphone innovations through review comments. The quantified data enabled an empirical investigation of the links between product innovation strategies and consumer satisfaction. Our findings provide valuable managerial insights for digital device firms and advance the digital innovation literature by connecting design strategies to consumer evaluations.

## Literature Review

To provide insights into how firms should innovate on digital devices while considering consumer reactions, we integrate two streams of literature: consumer reviews and product innovation strategies. This study examines how component innovation strategies of digital device companies influence consumer evaluations, with a particular focus on consumer reviews as a key metric of product reception. To understand how firms can innovate effectively, it is essential to consider not just the strategies they employ but also how consumers perceive and evaluate these innovations, as captured in their reviews. Traditional sales metrics, while important, present an incomplete picture of product success. Sales figures are significantly influenced by consumer perceptions shaped by marketing, pricing, and brand loyalty, and therefore do not fully capture post-use consumer evaluations (Ataman et al., 2010; Cheng et al., 2020). In contrast, consumer reviews offer a more comprehensive and qualitative perspective on consumer experiences with new digital device innovations (Saldanha et al., 2017). The significance of consumer reviews extends beyond immediate feedback. These reviews serve as a critical source of information that can profoundly impact a product’s longterm reputation. As the “voice of consumers,” online product reviews uncover qualitative factors that drive consumer satisfaction and adoption (Berger et al., 2020), reflecting what consumers truly value in a product (Chevalier & Mayzlin, 2006; Huang et al., 2017a).

Understanding consumer perceptions is particularly critical in the context of digital devices, which represent some of the most complex innovation ecosystems. Empirical evidence suggests that consumers may struggle to recognize the value of highly novel products (Rindova & Petkova, 2007), creating a substantial market challenge. Approximately 65%-77% of new products fail to achieve revenue and profit goals, primarily due to their inability to meet customer needs (Ramanujam & Tacke, 2016). This underscores the complexity of consumer innovation perception, particularly in rapidly evolving technological ecosystems (Nambisan et al., 2017; Rindova & Petkova, 2007). Given the complexity of digital devices, characterized by new combinations of digital and physical components (Yoo et al., 2010), firms must account for the interplay between hardware and software components in their innovation strategies. Consumer reviews, therefore, become an indispensable tool for refining these designs. Component innovations introduce unique challenges in this evaluation process, as consumers must not only understand individual technological improvements but also recognize their potential synergistic benefits (Christensen & Overdorf, 2000).

The existing literature on consumer reviews tends to examine the effects of marketing strategies rather than those of product innovation designs (Hauser et al., 2006; Yoo, 2010). Prior research has predominantly focused on contextual factors influencing reviews, such as reviewer expertise (Godes & Mayzlin, 2004; Hong et al., 2017), social networks of peers (Huang et al., 2017b), and reviewer identification (Aerts et al., 2017). Although some studies have explored product attributes, their analyses have been limited to general characteristics like price (Li & Hitt, 2010) and quality (Chevalier & Mayzlin, 2006), rather than specific innovation design elements. While existing literature has explored how augmented and peripheral products deliver additional functional and affective values to consumers (Baldwin & Clark, 1994; Ulwick, 2002), there remains a limited understanding of how specific component innovations within a product influence consumer evaluations and perceptions. Shifting the focus from marketing-driven outcomes to innovation-driven insights can reveal how product design shapes consumer evaluations of digital devices.

Prior studies on innovation strategies have typically focused on firms’ approaches (Danneels & Kleinschmidtb, 2001; Nambisan et al., 2017) but overlooked how consumers evaluate and comprehend novel products, underscoring the need to better understand consumer responses to product innovation designs. Yoo (2010) emphasized the importance of exploring firm strategies for digital innovation and integrating digital technologies with physical artifacts in device design, such as combining various hardware components with software features (Baldwin & Clark, 1994; Wang et al., 2022). To innovate successfully, firms strategically develop products that leverage complementary hardware components and effectively integrate software with hardware. (Schumpeter, 1934; Yoo et al., 2010)

As theories of digital product innovation cannot ignore the difficulties that consumers face in comprehending and evaluating the value of novel products, there is considerable scope to integrate these two important research streams (Davis, 1989; Wang et al., 2022), highlighting the importance of aligning product designs with consumer perceptions to achieve success. Since consumer perceptions of a product are influenced by the configuration of its attributes (Rindova & Petkova, 2007), firms cannot consistently achieve product success through superior product-design strategies without a comprehensive understanding of consumers’ reactions to product innovations (Gu et al., 2022).

## Theory and Hypotheses

## Consumer Evaluation of Product Innovation

Consumer evaluation refers to the process through which consumers assess products and form judgments. Consumers generate evaluations based on their cognitive perceptions of product value (i.e., usefulness) and confirmation of their expectations (Bhattacherjee, 2001; Lin et al., 2022). Many innovators prefer product improvement through novelty because products capable of generating “creative destruction” are believed to be more attractive to consumers (Schumpeter, 1942). As product innovations introduce superior performance and features previously unavailable in the market, they simultaneously raise consumer expectations (Danneels & Kleinschmidtb, 2001).

Accurately assessing a product’s actual value, however, relies heavily on a consumer’s familiarity with the product, such as existing knowledge and past experiences that serve as reference points (Ho et al., 2020; Zou et al., 2023). Innovations that make radical changes introduce many novel aspects, significantly departing from consumers’ existing understanding and frame of reference, which tends to raise consumers’ expectations of the innovation’s value (Hoeffler, 2003). Discordance between a high expected value driven by novelty and a low assessed value due to unfamiliarity can lead to dissatisfaction with the innovative product (Bhattacherjee, 2001). It is therefore crucial that innovators consider how consumers perceive and recognize the value of these novel products (Nambisan et al., 2017; Rindova & Petkova, 2007).

Adopting a cognitive perspective, Mandler (1982) proposed that the fit between a product’s attributes and the schemas, which are mental frameworks that organize knowledge and guide perception (i.e., the image or purpose of the product), affects consumers’ overall judgment of the product’s value. The greater the congruence between product attributes and consumers’ schemas, the greater the consumers’ familiarity with the attributes (Ma et al., 2015; Meyers-Levy & Tybout, 1989). However, incongruity poses a cognitive challenge, hindering consumers’ application of existing knowledge to evaluate new functionalities (Moreau et al., 2001). Highly novel products bring uncertainties and risks to consumers (Hoeffler, 2003), especially when they require changes in consumer thinking and behavior (Veryzer, 1998), as consumers are unable to gauge the potential value of unfamiliar innovations using their existing schemas (Rindova & Petkova, 2007). It is therefore important to understand how consumers discover new meanings and perceive the value of innovations (Nambisan et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2022).

## Different Types of Component Innovations

We tap into the theory of dominant design to propose two types of hardware component innovations. According to Anderson and Tushman (1990), during industry experimentation, various designs compete until a widely accepted set of core design concepts, known as the dominant design, emerges within the industry. A classic example is the QWERTY keyboard layout, which became the dominant design for typewriters and has persisted on computer keyboards. In the smartphone industry, Apple’s 2007 introduction of the iPhone led to a convergence around a dominant design (e.g., a multifunctional touchscreen device incorporating various key hardware components) (Klingebiel & Joseph, 2016). The smartphone market continues to evolve through components that enhance or add functionalities. Given that these components differ in perceived novelty and familiarity, we investigate two types of hardware innovations: dominant and non-dominant component innovations.

A component specification is considered to conform to a dominant design if it is broadly accepted and utilized in the industry (Tegarden et al., 1999). Dominant hardware innovations for digital devices are those that improve the features and performance of existing hardware components widely expected in digital devices. Dominant components are those that are widely expected to be included in any new digital device, as they are essential for enabling the basic functionalities of these products (Lee & Kang, 2016), such as the central processing unit (CPU), camera, and battery in the context of smartphones. In general, the hardware components that are upgraded are already familiar to consumers from earlier smartphone models on the market, making it easy for consumers to evaluate their value by comparing the upgraded versions to their understanding of existing products.

We also consider the extent to which smartphones incorporate radically new components that did not previously exist in digital devices, as companies continuously introduce new hardware components to remain competitive. We refer to these innovations as non-dominant component innovations, as they represent the introduction of new components to the market (Rahrovani et al., 2024). These innovations typically introduce unprecedented, novel functionalities that require consumers to adapt to novel usage patterns and may necessitate changes in their cognitive thinking (Veryzer, 1998). While consumers easily understand the importance of a fast CPU, for example, they may struggle to appreciate the value and usability of integrating a heart rate sensor into a smartphone. The unfamiliarity of the component makes it challenging for consumers to transfer their knowledge from using previous smartphone models or conventional heart rate monitors to heart rate components in smartphones.

## Component Innovation Strategies

Multiple strategies can be used to introduce innovative hardware components into new digital devices. In shaping consumer evaluations, we explore two strategies for component innovation that influence consumer perceptions of novelty and familiarity: the timing of component innovations and software-supported interactions.

Timing plays an important role in the introduction of innovations to the market (Giachetti & Mensah, 2023; Klingebiel et al., 2022) and can significantly impact how consumers make sense of component innovations in a digital device (Boulongne & Durand, 2021; Klingebiel & Joseph, 2016). Firms can adopt either early-mover or late-follower strategies. Early movers, like Apple with its initial introduction of facial recognition technology, often deliver novel advancements unfamiliar to consumers, enhancing perceived novelty but increasing complexity due to a lack of reference points. Late followers, such as Samsung with its subsequent adoption of facial recognition, benefit from consumer familiarity through exposure to earlier implementations, reducing perceived novelty but improving ease of understanding. Thus, early timing emphasizes novelty, while later timing prioritizes familiarity (Klingebiel & Joseph, 2016).

Digital devices are organized with a layered structure, including hardware components (representing physical logic) and software interfaces (representing digital logic) (Yoo et al., 2010). While software interfaces inherently mediate consumer interaction with hardware components by managing inputs and presenting outputs, firms can strategically choose different approaches: focusing solely on hardware component advancement, combining hardware innovation with existing software features, or developing new software features while innovating hardware components to shape how consumers perceive and experience the underlying hardware capabilities. For instance, a firm might choose to simply improve the resolution of the display of a device screen (hardware-only component innovation), improve the resolution of the display while adding adaptive brightness control (existing hardware component innovation with software support), or develop new motion sensors while launching a fitness tracking application (new hardware component innovation with software support). Direct interaction with software shapes consumers’ cognitive perception of the hardware and perceptions of product value, as their understanding is based on how hardware capabilities are presented and experienced through the software layer. The cognitive perception of hardware is influenced by interactions with associated software interfaces, bridging consumers’ experiences of the hardware components (Wang et al., 2022).

We examine both the timing of component innovations and software-supported interactions, as these are innovation strategies that affect consumers’ perceptions of novelty and familiarity when hardware component innovations are introduced into digital devices. We also differentiate between dominant and non-dominant component innovations, as consumers differ in their perceptions of novelty and familiarity with dominant components (familiar but less novel) versus non-dominant components (novel but less familiar). Hence, differentiating between these different types of component innovations is important as we explore how firms use various innovation strategies to balance the need for both novelty and familiarity. ## Timing of Innovation

The digital device market, such as that for smartphones, which are characterized by a high level of product technology and heterogeneity of features, is incredibly fast-moving. Early entrants that achieve significant consumer attention can set the standard for what attributes are considered important in the product (Carpenter & Nakamoto, 1994) and establish a distinct position to secure a competitive advantage (Rindova & Petkova, 2007). According to expectation-confirmation theory, consumer satisfaction evolves from forming, assessing, and confirming an initial expectation of product performance (Bhattacherjee, 2001). Consumers often draw upon their experiences and knowledge of existing or familiar functionalities to form these expectations and assess the value of a product (Lin et al., 2022; Zou et al., 2023). Unfamiliarity with novel innovations and the associated uncertainty are more likely to trigger incongruity between product attributes and consumer expectations, which can lead to a negative evaluation (Meyers-Levy & Tybout, 1989). Being a market leader in introducing innovative components is a doubleedged sword that provides significantly new added value while potentially confusing consumers due to novelty. The timing of component innovations thus has to differentiate between dominant and non-dominant component innovations.

Dominant component innovations involve incremental improvements to existing hardware components, making them straightforward for consumers to understand (Rindova & Petkova, 2007) so that they can readily form expectations and assess the associated improved performance (Lin et al., 2022; Olshavsky & Spreng, 1996). For these familiar dominant hardware components, such as improved screen resolution, innovations introduced early in the market are unlikely to confuse consumers. Their improved capabilities (e.g., a brighter and clearer screen) logically align with consumers’ existing schemes about how these dominant components operate and the benefits of upgrades. This clarity allows customers to easily understand the value propositions of “brighter,” “clearer,” or “higher resolution” without the need to develop entirely new mental models.

When dominant component innovations are introduced early in the market, consumer expectations about these innovations tend to be higher due to the first-mover advantages (Min et al., 2006). Early releases typically generate excitement, heightening expectations for these improvements (Lieberman & Montgomery, 1988). Consequently, being first to market with an enhanced hardware component can create a perception of superiority, shaping higher consumer expectations. When the enhanced performance of a dominant hardware component is readily perceivable, confirmation of consumer expectations about the value and usefulness of a product is likely to result in positive consumer evaluations. On the other hand, consumers often do not hold high expectations for dominant component innovations introduced late into the market, since many competing products may already include these improvements. Given the competitive nature of digital device markets, consumers are likely to be dissatisfied (or less satisfied) with the catch-up performance of a new device. Thus, we expect that dominant component innovations introduced late in the market will be perceived as uncompetitive and less appealing to consumers compared to those introduced early.

Similarly, non-dominant components that provide highly novel functionality can attract consumers and appeal to consumers’ needs for innovativeness (Rahrovani et al., 2024). Consumers are drawn to innovative products that introduce fresh, groundbreaking features, allowing them to explore novel experiences beyond what was previously attainable in the market (Hoeffler, 2003). Hence, consumers tend to have high expectations for such new non-dominant component innovations, as companies often emphasize the added value these features bring. Nevertheless, these novel components can be unfamiliar to consumers, making it difficult to assess their associated benefits and values without relevant prior knowledge of such radical new functionality (Mukherjee & Hoyer, 2001). Consumers must invest more time and effort to understand these innovations. As a result, non-dominant hardware innovations introduced early in the market may suffer from the lack of existing references for evaluation, diminishing the first-mover advantage and potentially leading to negative consumer evaluations.

Conversely, non-dominant innovations introduced to the market later may benefit from increased consumer familiarity, as expectations and evaluations can be formed based on observations from others who have previously interacted with products featuring these innovations, as information about such non-dominant innovations quickly spreads through online channels like social media and consumer reviews. Thus, we anticipate that consumers will find it easier to evaluate and appreciate non-dominant component innovations introduced late in the market, compared to those introduced early. ## Software-Supported Interactions

In the layered modular architecture of digital devices, the hardware layer provides the core capabilities of the device, while the software layer acts as an intermediary, translating the hardware functionalities into user-friendly interfaces. This layered approach abstracts the complex hardware operations, presenting them in a more intuitive manner for consumer interaction. The presence of software interfaces is pivotal in shaping consumer perceptions of products (Mayer et al., 2011; Wang et al., 2022). In fitness trackers, the fitness app interface enables consumers to interact with the heart rate monitor hardware by displaying heart rate data and exercise tracking. The process of making sense of and the acceptance of hardware innovations are closely linked to the direct interactions enabled by software interfaces (Rijsdijk & Hultink, 2009; Wang et al., 2022). Although software interaction can aid consumers in perceiving new functionality and comprehending the value derived from enhanced hardware components (Hoeffler, 2003; Kim et al., 2022), direct interactions can amplify the cognitive costs associated with learning about the new functionality of hardware innovations (Bu et al., 2022; Rahrovani et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2022), as consumers typically rely on simple heuristics to evaluate products (Kahneman, 2000).

Since software acts as the primary interface for consumers to interact with hardware components, the presence of a software interface is instrumental in helping consumers understand and appreciate the enhanced value of dominant hardware component innovations (Binken & Stremersch, 2009; Kim et al., 2022). By facilitating positive interactions, software support can heighten consumer understanding of innovation value, potentially increasing satisfaction with hardware components. However, the effectiveness of software-supported interactions varies across different types of hardware innovations. Some hardware components are more amenable to softwaresupported interactions than others. Camera technologies, for instance, can be readily enhanced through specialized apps, while central processing units (CPUs) present more complex interaction challenges. Manufacturers can strategically deploy software interfaces to optimize consumer engagement with both dominant and non-dominant hardware innovations, carefully considering the unique characteristics of each component <sup>3</sup> .

For dominant hardware components with which consumers are already familiar, the cognitive costs to learn about the new components are relatively low (Ma et al., 2015; Meyers-Levy & Tybout, 1989). We therefore expect that interactions with the software interface for dominant hardware components will boost consumers’ perceived usefulness of improved functionalities and contribute to a more positive consumer evaluation. For example, when the HTC One M9 first introduced a 20-megapixel camera into the market, its camera app interface enabled consumers to directly interact with and experience the improved image quality through features like photo capture and preview, highlighting the value of this hardware innovation.

In contrast, non-dominant component innovations introduce consumers to functionalities with uncertain utility, as these new features are unfamiliar (Hoeffler, 2003). Integrating a new software interface alongside these components is more likely to evoke incongruity between how the product is used and consumers’ cognitive schemas (Olshavsky & Spreng, 1996).

## Empirical Design

## Data Description

To validate our hypotheses, we collected data from multiple sources: (1) detailed hardware and software specifications from smartphone databases (GSMArena.com and

Phonearena.com) and (2) consumer ratings (1.0-5.0) and reviews from Amazon.com. The dataset included 63 smartphones from 10 leading smartphone vendors in the U.S. mobile phone market (i.e., Apple, BlackBerry, BLU, Google, HTC, Huawei, LG, Motorola, Nokia, and Samsung). <sup>4</sup> Because vendors may operate tiered product lines, with component innovations typically debuting in the flagship line before extending to lower-tier offerings, we analyzed only flagship smartphone lines (e.g., Samsung’s Galaxy S series and Apple’s iPhone series), updated annually with the latest components. This approach simplified analysis by excluding lower-tier products, where innovations are often minor or imitative. For verification, we examined hardware innovation patterns in Samsung’s Galaxy S series (detailed in Section 1 of the transparency materials <sup>5</sup> ) and confirmed that major innovations debuted in flagship lines, while mid- and low-end models featured only incremental updates.

We collected data on a total of 94,757 consumer reviews written between October 2010 and November 2019 from Amazon.com. We excluded 1,304 duplicate reviews from the same consumer for the same product, as well as reviews that only contained comments on the shopping experience and service without commenting on the product.<sup>6</sup> This resulted in 88,952 reviews for further analysis. We then randomly evaluated 1,000 of the remaining reviews to determine whether they reflected consumers’ smartphone evaluations and found that 97% of the reviews were relevant. Also, in robustness checks, we gathered 145,954 reviews from another data source (i.e., GSMArena.com) to exclude the potential single data source issue (see the Sampling Reviews From an Alternative Data Source section below). The findings are consistent between these two data sources. Table 3 lists the number of reviews and products for each smartphone vendor in our main dataset.

## Classification of Hardware Innovation Design Strategies

Next, we identified innovative smartphone hardware components and classified their innovation type based on our theoretical model using content analysis (Kolbe & Burnett, 1991). First, we compiled detailed specification information on the hardware components and software features of smartphones from 10 sampled vendors from GSMArena.com <sup>7</sup> and Phonearena.com.<sup>8</sup> In line with the theoretical model presented in Tables 1 and 2, we then categorized hardware component innovations into two types: dominant (D) and non-dominant (N). We further classified these hardware innovations based on their market entry timing (i.e., early [E] and late [L] innovations) and whether they included software-supported interactions (i.e., with related software component innovations [S] and without related software component innovations [O]). We then operationalized these component-level innovations in new smartphones based on (1) part of the dominant design (D vs. N), (2) adoption timing (E vs. L), and (3) software-supported interactions (S vs. O), as detailed in Appendix A.

## Dominant Hardware Component Innovations

To distinguish between dominant and non-dominant hardware components in a smartphone, we identified dominant components based on the benchmark modules provided by Phonearena.com, categorizing them into CPU, storage, graphics, network, battery, display, camera, audio, and connections. <sup>9</sup> Hardware components enhanced in new smartphones were classified as dominant (D) hardware innovations as per the benchmark modules. In contrast, newly introduced hardware components that did not fit into these categories were classified as non-dominant (N) innovations.

## Timing of Component Innovations

We further classified dominant and non-dominant design smartphone components into early and late innovations, based on the timing of the component innovation. Early innovations (E) are components that were introduced within a year of a smartphone’s launch, while late innovations (L) are components that had existed in the market for more than a year. The one-year time frame reflects the fixed schedules of smartphone launches, such as Samsung’s Galaxy S series (February-June) and Apple’s iPhone series (September). Innovations introduced within the first year can still represent planned enhancements made before public release.

## Software-Supported Interactions for Component Innovations

We then examined whether a hardware component had a directly related software interface provided by the smartphone manufacturer to support hardware interactions. For example, the “3D Touch” innovation introduced with the iPhone 6S was supported by the “Live Photos” software interface. In contrast, CPU enhancements that did not have a directly related software interface were considered not to have softwaresupported hardware components. Appendix Tables A1 to A3 illustrate the three classifications of hardware components for smartphones, respectively.

## Measurements

## Dependent Variables

We collected consumer evaluation data for each smartphone product in our sample to operationalize the dependent variable(s) in our research model. To assess the multifaceted consumer product appraisals, we used both the review rating scores (i.e., one to five stars) and the content of reviews gathered from Amazon.com. Numerical review scores were used to examine consumers’ overall satisfaction with smartphones, while textual review comments were scrutinized to identify opinions and sentiments regarding key aspects of smartphones using advanced text mining techniques.

Consumer numerical ratings (Rating scores): The consumer rating refers to the numerical review rating (ranging from 1.0 to 5.0) of our sampled smartphones on Amazon. We used numerical consumer review scores to measure overall consumer satisfaction with a smartphone product.

Consumers’ textual review comments (Sentiment scores): As numerical rating scores may not adequately capture nuanced differences in perceived product value among consumers, we mined textual consumer reviews to supplement the consumer ratings. Prior research has increasingly adopted text mining approaches to analyze the content of consumer reviews to address these nuances (Bai et al., 2020; Ghose et al., 2012; Zimmermann et al., 2018). Using natural language processing (NLP) techniques, we extracted product-related opinion sentences from consumer reviews and identified the sentiments they expressed. After removing irrelevant reviews, 78,736 reviews were used for the sentiment analysis. The text mining procedures are detailed in Appendix B. Appendix Table B1 presents the sampled reviews and their corresponding sentiment scores computed using a deep-learning model.

## Independent Variables

As previously described, the key independent variables were measured to show various combinations of dominant design (dominant [D] vs. non-dominant [N]), timing (early [E] vs. late [L]), and software-supported interactions (with software [S] vs. no software [O]) for hardware component innovations in a new smartphone.

## Control Variables

As controls, we included a set of review-, product-, and vendor-specific factors that could potentially influence consumers’ evaluations of smartphones. First, we controlled for review-specific attributes. Review\_timeliness indicates the log number of weeks between the launch date of a smartphone and the posting date of the consumer review. Review\_purchase shows whether a review was submitted by a consumer with a verified purchase to control for potential differences in reviews between consumers who had used a product and those who had not (Anderson & Simester, 2014; Chen et al., 2008). Review\_past is an indicator variable demonstrating whether a reviewer had submitted reviews prior to the review in question.

About 3% of the reviews in our dataset were contributed by the same consumers. Because consumers possessing prior knowledge (or experience) about smartphones are more inclined to draw comparisons with the focal product and existing alternatives (Rao & Monroe, 1988), we introduced Review\_knowledge as a binary variable indicating whether a reviewer demonstrated previous knowledge about smartphones. It is set to 1 if a review mentions past smartphone versions or another brand’s products for comparison purposes (e.g., “…iPhone 7’s battery very inferior compared to the iPhone 6 I previously owned…”), and 0 otherwise.

Second, we included product-level attributes (in addition to the hypothesized variables) that may influence consumer evaluations. Employing price data from GSMArena.com, which indexes smartphone prices from various sources on a daily basis, Product\_price was used to compare smartphone prices across time and countries (Wadhwa & Zhang, 2015; Zhao et al., 2013).<sup>10</sup> When instead considering the launch date price of a smartphone in the U.S. market in the model estimation, we obtained qualitatively consistent outcomes. Product\_interval indicates the log number of weeks elapsed from the launch of a predecessor model to the focal model to control for consumers’prolonged expectations regarding newer releases (Koca et al., 2021). Furthermore, we accounted for the availability of various product options unrelated to hardware component innovations that could have influenced consumers’ assessments, including the number of available colors (Product\_color; e.g., black, white, and red colors for the Google Nexus 5), sizes (Product\_size; e.g., iPhone X or iPhone X Plus), and storage capacities (Product\_storage; e.g., 34, 64, or 128 GB for the Huawei P10). Product\_material was included to capture whether there were changes in a smartphone’s exterior material compared to its past version (e.g., from a polycarbonate to a glass back cover in the transition from the Samsung Galaxy S5 to S6).

Third, we considered vendor-level attributes that could have influenced consumer perceptions of brand attributes associated with smartphones. Vendor\_marketShare is a binary variable for smartphone vendors among the top two global market share holders, who collectively represented approximately 40% of the market (e.g., Apple at 19.6% and Samsung at 18.9% in 2017). Because only lists of the top five vendors based on market share were available during the study period of 2011-2018, we recorded only the top two vendors’ market shares.<sup>11</sup> We also considered a firm’s technological orientation, which could have affected its product performance (Gatignon & Xuereb, 1997). We categorized smartphone vendors based on their expertise in software development (e.g., iOS, Android) and hardware manufacturing/design into three groups: (1) software-focused vendors employing proprietary operating systems (OS) (e.g., Google, Microsoft), (2) hardware-focused vendors solely producing and/or designing devices (e.g., Motorola, Samsung), and (3) integrated vendors incorporating both proprietary OS and hardware components in their products (e.g., Apple). Based on these categorizations, we generated two dummy variables: Vendor\_software (e.g., Google and Microsoft) and Vendor\_hardware (e.g., Samsung and Motorola). To validate this classification, we assessed vendor-level technological capabilities, including hardware, software, and core business areas. These time-consistent variables were excluded from the main analysis using vendor fixed effects but included in robustness checks with mixed-effects regressions. Our key research variables are presented in Table 4.

## Empirical Model

We investigated the associations between distinct innovation design strategies and overall consumer satisfaction with smartphones using Rating scores (and Sentiment scores for robustness checks) as the dependent variable, utilizing a fixedeffects linear regression model. The model included product innovation design predictors to examine the impact of different innovation strategies on consumer evaluations. The model specifications are as follows:

$$
E \left[ E v a l u a t i o n _ {i j k} \mid D o m i n a n t D e s i g n, T i m i n g, S o f w a r e S u p p o r t \right]
$$

$$
= \beta_ {0} + \underbrace {\beta_ {1} (E a r l y v s . L a t e | D o m i n a n t D e s g i n) _ {j k}} _ {\text {Timing | Dominant Design}}
$$

$$
+ \underbrace {\beta_ {2} (S W v s . N o S W | D o m i n a n t D e s i g n) _ {j k}} _ {\text { Software   Support } | \text { Dominant   Design }}

$$
+ \gamma (C o n t r o l s) _ {i j k} + R e l e a s e Y e a r _ {j} + \S_ {k} + \varepsilon_ {i j k},\tag{1}

where Evaluation<sub>ijk</sub> indicates a consumer i’s evaluation of a smartphone j offered by vendor k. The model identifies two innovation strategies, timing and software-supported interaction, and examines their impact on consumer evaluation for both dominant and non-dominant innovations. To test H1 and H2 on the timing of smartphone innovations, we included two product-level variables capturing the timing (early vs. late) of dominant and non-dominant hardware innovation (i.e., DE vs. DL and NE vs. NL), respectively. For H3 and H4 on software-supported interactions, we separated new component innovations into two sets of measures—new hardware with or without software-supported interactions—for hardware innovations conforming to dominant and non-dominant designs (i.e., DS vs. DO and NS vs. NO). A set of control variables $( C o n t r o l s _ { i j k } )$ was introduced, as previously described.

To account for unobserved product attributes affecting consumer evaluations, we included the smartphone’s release year (i.e., ReleaseYear<sub>j</sub>) instead of product-specific fixed effects, which omitted time-invariant product-level predictors (i.e., a smartphone’s innovation designs) in the model estimation. Consumer evaluations may be influenced by rival products and dominant designs from the same release year. Vendor-level fixed effects §<sub>k</sub> account for variations in consumer evaluations across smartphone vendors, and $\varepsilon _ { i j k }$ is an unobserved error term in the model.

We estimated the equation using ordinary least squares (OLS) with standard errors that are robust to product-level heteroscedasticity, as consumer reviews for the same product are unlikely to be independent. We further conducted a series of robustness tests to validate our empirical results (see the Robustness Checks section).

## Software-Supported Interactions for Hardware Component Innovations

Columns 3 and 4 of Table 5 show whether software-supported interactions for new component hardware innovations influenced consumer evaluation. The coefficient estimates reveal that introducing software-supported interactions with dominant hardware innovation (DS) increased consumer evaluation by 0.099 points, whereas adding dominant hardware innovations without software-supported interactions (DO) decreased consumer evaluation by 0.041 points. In other words, our results show that software-supported interactions for dominant hardware components enhance consumer appreciation of performance. Without such support, consumers may struggle to notice the expected improvements, leading to negative evaluations.

For non-dominant hardware component innovations, the results for hardware innovations with software interactions (NS) are negative and significant $( = - 0 . 0 4 5 ^ { * * } )$ , whereas the results for non-dominant hardware innovations without software interactions (NO) are positive and significant $( = 0 . 0 2 9 ^ { * } )$ . This confirms that software-supported interactions with non-dominant components (i.e., NS) can induce a higher level of unfamiliarity with novel hardware features for consumers, leading to unfavorable evaluations. The positive effect associated with non-dominant hardware innovations without software-supported interactions suggests that reducing consumers’ software-supported direct interactions with new hardware components that convey high unfamiliarity can be a strategic innovation approach for non-dominant component innovations. Thus, H3 and H4 are supported.

## Different Sampling Approaches

We evaluated our empirical findings using different datasets to assess whether the main results were limited to our sampled smartphone vendors and Amazon.com consumer reviews. Overall, we found consistent results across the models regardless of the datasets. The estimation outcomes and corresponding hypothesis testing results are summarized in Table 8.

## Sampling Vendors with Comparable Market Performance

It is possible that the inclusion of smartphone vendors with different market shares and performances could introduce bias when examining consumer product evaluations. To address this concern, we restricted our analysis to vendors with comparable market sizes and performance. We based our selection of a subsample on both a firm’s market share and the number of accumulated reviews for their products (as a proxy for sales performance on Amazon.com). For this subsample analysis, we excluded three vendors, BLU, HTC, and Nokia, due to their lower market shares and/or review accumulation. The remaining seven vendors were either in the top five vendors based on market share or attained an average of at least 500 reviews per smartphone device. Our analysis of this limited subset of the sample included 75,764 reviews (85% of the entire sample). The findings in Column 1 of Table 8 align with the main findings.

## Sampling Reviews with Relevant Product Evaluations

Despite our efforts to exclude reviews that focused solely on the shopping or service experience, rather than the device itself, it is possible that some irrelevant comments remained in our sample set. To address this concern, we employed a topic modeling approach, latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA), on our initial 88,952 sampled reviews to further identify such irrelevant reviews (details provided in Section 2 of the transparency materials). We identified a total of 12 topics based on coherence score and perplexity value (see Figure T3 in the transparency materials), two of which, “delivery” (5,229 reviews) and “purchase” (8,576 reviews), were considered irrelevant. The outcomes obtained after replicating our results while excluding reviews classified as “delivery” (Column 3) or “purchase” (Column 4) and excluding both topics (Column 5) are presented in Table 8. Across various combinations of subsampled reviews, the results consistently aligned with the main findings.

## Sampling Reviews from an Alternative Data Source

Our main analysis relies on Amazon.com as the primary data source for smartphone reviews. We supplemented our dataset with reviews from GSMArena.com, a renowned professional smartphone review website that specializes in offering an online forum for reviewing the technical details of smartphones. We gathered a total of 145,954 reviews for the ten sampled smartphones over the same research period from October 2010 to November 2019. Since GSMArena.com does not provide star rating scores for smartphones, we computed a sentiment score for each review comment to use as the dependent variable. Detailed data collection and preprocessing procedures are explained in Section 3 of the transparency materials. Estimation results using reviews from GSMArena.com are presented in Column 6 of Table 8. While some predictor estimates differ slightly in significance levels $( \mathrm { i . e . , } \beta _ { D L } = 0 . 0 0 2$ and $\beta _ { D O } = - 0 . 0 0 2 )$ and direction $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , \beta _ { N L } =$ $- 0 . 0 1 4 ^ { * * * }$ and $\beta _ { N O } = - 0 . 0 2 1 ^ { * * * } )$ compared to the main results, the overall outcomes support all four proposed hypotheses. The disparities stem from fundamental differences between GSMArena.com and Amazon.com. The former is an online community where consumers discuss their opinions on various smartphones, whereas Amazon.com is a consumer review site, where consumers are usually allowed to post their reviews after making a purchase and after using the product. Amazon.com is thus more aligned with our theoretical arguments, as we are examining how product component innovations influence the evaluations of consumers who have used the product. Nevertheless, validating our findings from data obtained from an alternative professional review site helps to ensure the external validity of our main results.

## Resampling Review Rating Scores

There is a concern that consumer ratings tend to skew toward extreme positive and negative scores, which may misrepresent overall evaluations (Chevalier & Mayzlin, 2006). In our dataset, 18.4% of reviews were one-star ratings and 56.3% were fivestar ratings. To address potential bias, we used three resampling methods to adjust the distribution of ratings and reduce the impact of extreme scores. Resampling procedures are detailed in Section 4 of the transparency materials. In brief, we addressed the impact of uneven rating scores in the sample by (1) randomly selecting only 22,056 five-star reviews (36.2% of the total reviews), equal to the combined number of two- to four-star reviews; (2) balancing distribution across three groups (one-star, five-star, and the rest), with approximately 15,000 reviews per group (33.3% each); and (3) evenly adjusting the proportion (20%) for each rating (one to five stars), resulting in about 5,000 reviews per rating score. Columns 1 to 3 of Table 9 report the estimation results for these three resampling approaches, respectively. Notably, our findings remain qualitatively consistent and support the proposed hypotheses across different resampling strategies for review scores. However, due to fewer observations in the resampled analyses, some coefficients became insignificant.

## Timely Consumer Evaluation

In the main analysis, we included all consumer reviews regardless of time since the smartphone launch. To validate our findings, we replicated the main analysis using reviews posted within 2 years of product launch (approximately 74% of consumer reviews), aligning with the average smartphone upgrade cycle of 24-25 months (Ng, 2019). The results in Column 4 of Table 9 largely reinforce the findings of the main analysis. Although H1 is weakly supported at the 10% significance level, the significant and positive impact of DE is more pronounced for positive evaluations, as compared to the insignificant impact of DL. Furthermore, Columns 5 and 6 replicate the analysis using reviews made within 1 year (44% of the sample) and 6 months (21%) post-launch, respectively. These findings also reveal more negative evaluations of early non-dominant design (NE) introductions compared to the main analysis. However, insignificant impacts for late dominant hardware components are likely due to the small sample size, not supporting H1 and H3.

## Alternative Model Specifications

## Vendor-Level Heterogeneity and Product Innovations

We evaluated whether the key findings are valid using a different model specification that accommodates vendor-level heterogeneity. As a consumer’s evaluation of a smartphone is generally influenced by a variety of factors at different levels (i.e., review-specific, product-level, and vendor-level attributes), we used a multilevel mixed-effects linear regression model (also known as a random intercept model) as an alternative model specification. We used product-level predictors to control for product-related innovation strategies<sup>12</sup> and vendor-specific random effects to control for unobserved vendor-specific covariates. Two additional timeinvariant vendor-level controls (Vendor\_hardware and Vendor\_software) were included. We ran mixed-effects models for both Rating and Sentiment consumer evaluation measures using a maximum likelihood estimator (MLE).<sup>13</sup> The estimates summarized in Columns 1 and 2 of Table 10 above confirm the findings from the main analyses.

## Non-Normality and Ordinal Nature of Rating Scores

Our OLS regression in the main analysis, which used consumer rating scores as the dependent variable, may not be suitable due to the non-normality and ordinal nature of the rating data. Therefore, we performed a multilevel (mixed-effects) ordered logistic regression on review scores ranging from 1 (lowest) to 5 (highest) to test for consistent results. This approach also accounted for the panel structure of our data, where multiple rating scores exist for the same smartphones over time.

The results, presented in Column 3 of Table 10, are consistent with those obtained from the OLS regression and continue to support all our proposed hypotheses. However, multilevel ordinal logistic regression did not effectively control for inherent consumer-, product-, and vendor-level attributes (i.e., the omission of fixed effects). For this reason, we chose not to adopt this model as our primary analytical approach.

## Instrumental Variables

It is possible that our main model specification suffers from endogeneity issues when measuring hardware component innovations with and without software interactions. These innovations may be correlated with the error term because the omitted variables confound both the independent and dependent variables. To address this, we employed two IVs: (1) hardware and software patent information using U.S. Patent data and (2) information on the innovation culture of smartphone vendors collected from Glassdoor.

A vendor’s patent information is closely related to its ability to offer component innovations in new products (Su et al., 2013; Zhou & Wu, 2010). Prior literature considers patents an important source of innovation (Arora et al., 2004; Gans et al., 2002), and forward and backward patent citations represent technological advancements made by a firm (Boh et al., 2014). We considered patent records as valid instruments since this information is not directly noticed by consumers. Detailed procedures observed in processing patent data are provided in Section 5 of the transparency materials. We replicated our main analysis, instrumenting for the key predictors tested in H1 and H2 (i.e., DE, DL, NE, and NL) using HW\_Dominant\_Patent<sub>jk</sub> and HW\_Non-dominant\_Patent<sub>jk</sub>, which indicate the number of hardware-related patents for dominant and non-dominant hardware innovations, respectively, when a vendor k introduces a new smartphone. SW\_Patent<sub>jk,</sub> SW\_Forward\_Citation<sub>jk,</sub> and SW\_Backward\_Citation<sub>jk</sub>, representing the number of software patents and forward and backward citations in the year when vendor k launches a new smartphone j, were used as IVs for the software-related predictors used in H3 and H4 concerning software interactions for new hardware innovations (i.e., DS, DO, NS, and NO).

The first-stage estimation results in Table T11 in the transparency materials reveal significant impacts of a vendor’s hardware- and software-related patent records on smartphone component innovation designs, indicating the appropriateness of our instrumental variables. Furthermore, the F-statistics in the first-stage estimations across all models far surpass the minimum threshold of 10.0 (Staiger et al., 1997), indicating that our IVs are not weak instruments (Kolko, 2012). Column 4 of Table T11 shows that the estimate of DL is no longer negative (as reported in the main analysis); however, our hypotheses continue to be supported because the significant positive effect of DE on consumer evaluation $( \beta _ { D E } = 0 . 0 9 6 ^ { * * * } )$ is significantly higher than the coefficient of $D L ~ ( \beta _ { D L } =$ $0 . 0 3 6 ^ { * * * } )$ , indicating that early dominant component innovations gained more favorable consumer evaluations compared to late dominant innovations. Although we had to use a smaller sample in the analysis due to the absence of patent records for a few vendors, the results confirm our hypotheses, proving that our key findings hold after controlling for endogeneity.

To more comprehensively address endogeneity concerns, we utilized alternative $\mathrm { I V s }$ for the endogenous regressors in the main model by measuring vendors’ organizational innovation culture using employee reviews on Glassdoor.com. Past literature highlights the importance of innovation culture in driving a firm’s product innovations (Berson et al., 2008; De Tienne & Mallette, 2012), and internal organizational culture is not directly observable to consumers. Our use of Glassdoor data aligns with previous studies’ examination of firms’ innovation abilities (Kim & Ra, 2022; Liu & Sun, 2021; Uyeno, 2020). ## Software Integration for Hardware Innovations

In the main analysis, we only considered hardware component innovations with or without software support. However, firms face strategic decisions regarding software integration when innovating hardware components: (1) integrating existing software interfaces, (2) pairing hardware improvements with new software support, or (3) providing no software support. To examine these strategic distinctions, we conducted additional analysis (detailed in Section 7 of the transparency materials). The results show that dominant component innovations receive more favorable evaluations when supported by software, whether existing or new, as this enhances the recognition of improved performance. Conversely, non-dominant innovations are better received without software support, as this reduces cognitive complexity and resistance. These findings align with our main results and underscore the importance of aligning software strategies with hardware innovation types.

In summary, our robustness checks, which employed different sampling approaches, dependent variables, and model specifications, support the validity and robustness of our empirical findings.

## Discussion and Conclusion

Organizations constantly compete to create successful and innovative designs blending hardware and software components to attract consumers and gain a competitive advantage. Our findings reveal how component innovation design strategies must balance consumers’ competing desires for novelty and familiarity in two key areas.

First, we demonstrate that introducing dominant component innovations late in the market reduces consumer satisfaction with digital devices, while the late introduction of nondominant component innovations is associated with higher satisfaction. Consumers can easily understand and evaluate early dominant hardware component innovations, as they represent incremental improvements to existing components. Consequently, consumers penalize firms for the late introduction of such innovations. For non-dominant component innovations, consumers often lack familiarity with such novel features. While consumers may hold high expectations for new hardware components, they may struggle to assess the value of new non-dominant hardware components, leading to expectation disconfirmation.

Second, our results show that software-supported interaction serves as an intermediary translating hardware capability into consumer-accessible functions. For dominant hardware component innovations, software-supported interaction helps consumers better recognize improvements through direct interaction. However, for non-dominant hardware component innovations, direct software interaction can trigger unfamiliarity and uncertainty about new functionality, reducing component evaluation. While new software initially raises expectations for hardware components, the subsequent disconfirmation reduces satisfaction.

Overall, based on the need to balance novelty and familiarity, we suggest the following strategies for digital device manufacturers: First, manufacturers should introduce known dominant component innovations early, while adopting a second-mover strategy for non-dominant component innovations. Additionally, software interaction design should be strategically considered for dominant component innovations, while a more measured approach for nondominant innovations is appropriate to avoid expectationreality gaps.

## Research Implications

We contribute to information systems research by testing a theoretical framework that explains how hardware and software components affect consumer evaluations of digital devices. As the digitization of products and services becomes more prevalent through advances in hardware and software, it is imperative that information systems research develop theories and practices that inform innovation strategies relating to hardware and software components (Yoo et al., 2012). Smartphones serve as a good example of how digitalization enables firms to integrate diverse resources to bring new meaning to products (Yoo et al., 2012). Our study enhances the understanding of innovation strategies for digital product vendors by emphasizing the approaches for introducing different hardware component innovations in creating digital devices.

We draw on literature highlighting the need for novelty and familiarity, integrating theories related to dominant design and consumer product reviews to provide insights into how demand-side perspectives can inform firms’ innovation strategies. We theorize how understanding consumers cognition perspectives may help digital device manufacturers factor in how consumers make sense of products that reflect various component innovation strategies. We empirically operationalize concepts illustrating the need to balance novelty and familiarity in the smartphone innovation context and demonstrate how a firm’s innovation strategy utilizing these concepts influences consumer evaluations.

The need for both novelty and familiarity points to the need to differentiate between dominant and non-dominant components and to understand how two key innovation strategies—timing and software-supported interactions— influence novelty and familiarity and affect consumer evaluations. Our findings serve as the foundation for a theoretical framework, provide guidance to firms interested in digitalizing their products, and advance research on consumer reviews, contributing to the limited research exploring component-level dominant designs and innovation strategies.

We contribute to the literature on consumer reviews by exploring how component innovation in digital devices impacts product evaluation, an area that is often overlooked. While digital component innovation is a key strategy for digital device manufacturers, its effect on consumer evaluations is not well understood. By linking innovation strategies to consumer evaluations, we help manufacturers optimize their component innovations. Our work bridges the literature on firm innovation and consumer responses, shifting the focus from product characteristics to component innovation. Prior literature has relied on surveys and laboratory experiments to examine innovation’s role in product adoption and satisfaction (Arts et al., 2011; Szymanski et al., 2007). Rijsdijk and Hultink (2009), for example, conducted a survey to examine consumer satisfaction with smart products, while Ma et al. (2015) studied consumers’ evaluation of core and peripheral innovations in controlled experiments. In contrast, our study empirically investigates consumer evaluation of innovation using a large-scale, real-world dataset. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to examine component-level innovations through the lens of dominant design theory, focusing on how novelty and familiarity affect consumer evaluation.

From a practical standpoint, our study introduces a new research angle by linking consumer-generated data, a source rarely leveraged in strategy-oriented research, to firm-level product innovation strategy. To validate the relevance and importance of this research angle, we conducted additional interviews with senior executives at a leading smartphone manufacturer. These interviews revealed that industry practitioners actively rely on social media and online review monitoring to inform product development decisions, often through dedicated Consumer Experience (CX) and Digital Experience (DX) divisions. This evidence confirms that the consumer perspective embedded in online reviews is not only analytically valuable but also closely reflects how leading firms shape their innovation strategies in practice. Building on this foundation, our research findings yield three strategic guidelines for digital product vendors pursuing component innovation: (1) balance the need for novelty and familiarity when developing novel products and leverage their review monitoring systems to specifically track consumer responses to both dominant and non-dominant component innovations separately, (2) introduce component innovations into the market as early as possible for dominant design components to meet customers’ baseline expectations while increasing software-supported interactions for such component innovations, and (3) consider the pitfalls associated with a lack of familiarity among consumers for non-dominant component design by introducing such components later in the market and decreasing software-supported interactions.

Our findings can be extended beyond digital products to other industries like automotive, home appliances, and industrial machinery, where component-driven innovation is key. Our research offers guidance to firms on how to balance novelty and familiarity when integrating dominant and non-dominant components into their product designs. We provide strategies for timing the introduction of innovative components and leveraging familiarity to facilitate consumer adoption of novel technologies.

## Limitations and Future Research

While this study provides valuable insights, it has limitations that suggest avenues for further research. First, we relied on consumer reviews from Amazon.com, which may not fully reflect broader sentiment. To address this, we replicated our analysis using reviews from GSMArena.com, the largest smartphone forum, and found consistent results. We also attempted to sample reviews from other sites like BestBuy.com and PhoneArena.com, but these sites offered insufficient or skewed data. For example, Amazon.com had 4,532 reviews for the iPhone 7, while BestBuy.com included only 41 reviews, and PhoneArena.com contained 13 reviews. Moreover, their score ratings were highly skewed to the maximum review scores with extremely small variations (i.e., 4.5 out of 5.0 on BestBuy.com and 9.0 out of 10.0 on PhoneArena.com). While we employed resampling approaches to address potential biases in online consumer reviews, we acknowledge an important limitation regarding unobserved consumer opinions. Despite using resampling to address potential biases, a key limitation remains the unobserved opinions of consumers who did not leave reviews. Future research could combine review data with other methods, like surveys or behavioral data, to better capture these missing perspectives.

Second, we used a sample of 63 smartphones from 10 firms to validate our hypotheses. Although the smartphone series used in our study were the most popular in each smartphone vendor’s product lines (e.g., Samsung’s Galaxy S Series), they may not represent all smartphone products in the market. Furthermore, although the 10 firms in our sample were quite diverse (e.g., Apple is a leading firm, whereas BLU is a small firm), we could not cover all smartphone vendors, especially new vendors with only one product in their smartphone product lines. However, as our sample accounted for approximately 90% of the smartphone market share, we believe it is representative of the products available during the research period.

Third, we measured consumer evaluation through online review ratings. While product innovation may impact sales, reviews offer unique insights into how innovations resonate with consumers. Unlike sales, which are influenced by factors like marketing or brand reputation (Ataman et al., 2010; Cheng et al., 2020), consumer reviews provide valuable insights into the specific components and features that impact consumers’ evaluations of a product (e.g., Liu & Karahanna, 2017). By focusing on reviews, our study uncovers how digital device innovations are perceived by consumers who have directly interacted with the products. Furthermore, the available sales history, including both the number of products sold and the corresponding dollar amounts, was limited during the research period, with only a few vendors providing specific figures.

Fourth, our analysis does not fully account for individual-level familiarity with smartphone component innovations, as product review platforms do not require consumers to disclose their expertise before posting reviews. Despite this, we addressed potential consumer evaluation differences by including review-level attributes from the comments. While this partially addressed heterogeneity in evaluations, future research could explore these dynamics further.

Fifth, our measurement of software support focused solely on the presence or absence of software interfaces in operating systems, overlooking the potential impact of feature quantity and quality. When firms introduce hardware innovations, they may bundle multiple software features that influence consumer evaluation of hardware capabilities. Additionally, the ecosystem of third-party developers represents another unexplored dimension, as firms can strategically open their hardware innovations through APIs. Future research could explore how both native software features and third-party software integration shape consumer responses to hardware innovations.

Finally, it was not possible to include all controls affecting a consumer’s judgment of digital products. We analyzed product- and vendor-level attributes but did not include external factors such as platform recommender systems. While it is not possible for a study to factor in all control variables, our focus on the innovation strategies that affect consumers’evaluations of smartphones precludes a clean field experimental design that may make the need for such controls less relevant. Future research could use such designs to examine more specific aspects of the innovation strategies relating to component innovations.

## Classification of Hardware Component Innovations

We classified hardware component innovations according to their (1) market dominance in design, (2) adoption timing, and (3) softwaresupported interactions in new smartphones.

## Dominant Hardware Component Innovations

There has been an established dominant smartphone design since the early 2010s, following the introduction of the first iPhone (Klingebiel & Joseph, 2016). To distinguish between dominant and non-dominant hardware components, we utilized benchmark modules from PhoneArena.com. Smartphone benchmark tests, widely used for hardware performance evaluation, include nine categories: CPU, storage, graphics, network, battery, display, camera, audio, and connections. Manufacturers often highlight improvements in these components when introducing new models. Accordingly, if a new smartphone improved these benchmark features compared to its predecessor, we recorded them as dominant (D) hardware innovations. New hardware components not included on this list were considered to be non-dominant (N) innovations. For example, the iPhone 6 (2014) improved four existing core components (CPU, GPU, storage, connection, network, display, and battery) and introduced two new ones (barometer and NFC sensors) compared to the iPhone 5S (2013). ## Software-Supported Interactions of Component Innovations

We then differentiated hardware component innovations based on whether they have software-support interactions. Multiple hardware components work together as a system to deliver features. Only some system functionalities directly interact with end consumers through software support, which enables consumers to tap into the functionalities embedded in hardware components. We measured the softwaresupported interactions of the hardware components by examining whether a hardware component had a directly related software interface. First, we gathered all the software features available in the OS that was officially launched with a smartphone. Data on software features were collected from GSMArena.com and the vendors’ official websites.<sup>16</sup> We then classified hardware components based on whether they had directly related software features. For example, the iPhone 6 introduced NFC, a set of short-range wireless technologies for communication between devices. This hardware component also included “Apple Pay,” a feature in iOS 8.0 that allows consumers to make payments in person through the app. On the other hand, although the Nokia Lumia 920 included an NFC component in 2012, there was no directly related software interface available at the time. ## Appendix B

## Text-Mining and Sentiment Analysis Procedures

## Opinion Sentence Identification

This appendix explains the text mining procedures used to extract product-related opinion sentences from consumer reviews and identify the sentiments expressed in these sentences. We first identified sentences reflecting consumer evaluations of smartphones. Following standard text mining practices (Büschken & Allenby, 2016; Lee & Bradlow, 2011), we divided each consumer review comment into individual sentences. Next, sentences that contained only irrelevant or less informative words or symbols (e.g., “Yes.”, “None.”, and “Thanks!!!”) were removed from the sample. We then identified sentences featuring key smartphone attributes, typically described using nouns or noun phrases (e.g., “camera,” “fingerprint,” “screen,” and “battery”).

Using a natural language processing (NLP) technique, called a part-of-speech (POS) tagger, we extracted nouns and noun phrases as candidate words that could characterize product features. We then applied a stemmer function to convert plural nouns into singular forms (e.g., from “photos” to “photo”), aggregated all the nouns used across the reviews, and counted their frequency of appearance. Only words that appeared in at least 1% of the review sentences were retained. While different consumers may have unique product-related stories and experiences, the words used to describe product features are generally similar for all consumers. Thus, sentences containing these commonly used nouns (and noun phrases) were considered opinion sentences.

Finally, sentences concerning something frequently mentioned but not directly related to the product’s attributes, such as the delivery, shopping experience, and service (e.g., “Amazon service,” “fast delivery,” and “good seller”), were also dropped. To verify our opinionsentence identification procedure, we randomly selected 1,000 sentences from the reserved 238,996 sentences and manually evaluated whether they reflected consumer opinions or sentiments regarding smartphones. Approximately 97% of the sentences were directly related to consumers’ evaluations of the sampled smartphones, confirming that the identified opinion sentences were representative of consumers overall evaluations.

## Content and Sentiment Analysis

After extracting consumers’ opinion sentences that evaluated smartphones, we conducted content and sentiment analyses to measure the extent to which consumers were positive or negative about smartphone products. First, we performed sentiment analysis on each consumer review sentence to capture the overall evaluation. After removing irrelevant sentences in the first step, we focused only on opinion sentences that evaluated smartphones in our textual analysis. We employed a deep-learning algorithm (Manning et al., 2014; Socher et al., 2013) for sentiment analysis. While most conventional NLP-based sentiment analyses heavily rely on the occurrences of polarity words (e.g., positive or negative words such as “good,” “bad,” and “amazing”) in a review comment, the deep-learning-based sentiment analyzer utilizes a recursive neural tensor network (RNTN) to train sentence structures and assess the meanings of noun phrases in review sentences to produce more credible and accurate sentiment analysis outcomes (Socher et al., 2013; Tang et al., 2014). The reported accuracy of this algorithm (greater than 85%) is significantly higher than that of other conventional sentiment analysis algorithms (Socher et al., 2013). We developed a Java program using the Deep-Learning Java package to compute a sentiment score (ranging from 1.0 [very negative] to 5.0 [very positive]) for each review sentence. The average sentiment score for all relevant sentences in a review comment represents the consumer’s overall sentiment about a smartphone.
