# AI-AUGMENTED CONTENT VALIDATION IN BEHAVIORAL RESEARCH: DEVELOPMENT AND EVALUATION OF THE RATER SYSTEM<sup>1</sup>

Jean-Charles Pillet TBS Business School Toulouse, FRANCE {jean-charles.pillet@tbs-education.fr}

Kai R. Larsen and David Dobolyi Leeds School of Business, University of Colorado Boulder Boulder, CO, U.S.A. {kai.larsen@colorado.edu}{david.dobolyi@colorado.edu}

Magno Queiroz College of Business, Florida Atlantic University Boca Raton, FL, U.S.A. {mqueiroz@fau.edu}

Abram Handler Leeds School of Business, University of Colorado Boulder Boulder, CO, U.S.A. {abram.handler@colorado.edu}

Jan Ketil Arnulf BI Norwegian Business School, Norwegian Defence University College Oslo, NORWAY {jan.k.arnulf@bi.no}

Rajeev Sharma Deakin Business School, Deakin University, Victoria, AUSTRALIA {r.sharma@deakin.edu.au}

Content validation is an essential aspect of the scale development process that ensures that measurement instruments capture their intended constructs. However, researchers rarely undertake this core step in behavioral research because it requires costly data collection and specialized expertise. We present RATER (replicable approach to expert ratings), a free web-based system (www.contval.org) that can help the broader research community (scientists, reviewers, students) gain quick and reliable insights into the content validity of measurement instruments. Guided by psychometric measurement theory, RATER evaluates whether a scale’s items correspond to their intended construct, remain distinct from other constructs, and adequately represent all aspects of the construct’s content domain. The system employs two unique artificial intelligence models, RATER<sub>C</sub> and RATER<sub>D</sub>, which leverage psychometric scales from 2,443 journal articles spanning eight disciplines and two state-of-the-art large language model architectures (i.e., BERT and GPT). A set of six complementary studies confirms the RATER system’s accuracy, reliability, and usefulness. We find that RATER can augment the scale development and validation process, increasing the validity of findings in behavioral research.

Keywords: Content validity, psychometrics, scale development, large language models (LLMs), machine learning, design science research, behavioral research, research rigor

## Introduction

As a critical step in instrument development in the social sciences, content validation has attracted and continues to garner interest from researchers across disciplines such as information systems (e.g., Schmitz & Storey, 2020), behavioral medicine (e.g., Almanasreh et al., 2019), education (e.g., Wang & Ahn, 2025), marketing (e.g., Haws et al., 2023), nursing (e.g., Zamanzadeh et al., 2015), management (e.g., Colquitt et al., 2019), and psychology (e.g., Spoto et al., 2025). Content validity assessment provides a formal procedure to guide the development of new instruments reflecting underlying constructs, the adaptations of existing instruments, and the ongoing reevaluation of existing instruments (Compeau et al., 2022; Heggestad et al., 2019; Larsen et al., 2023; Pillet et al., 2023). It has been argued that specifying how a construct’s definition relates to its items is as critical as the ensuing behavioral theory (Lambert & Newman, 2023). When a construct is operationalized correctly, the specified relationship between the items and their putative construct serves as a foundation for further theory development.

Content validity is evaluated through a threefold evaluation process: (1) establishing that each item within a measure accurately reflects its intended construct, (2) ensuring that items demonstrate exclusivity by avoiding domain overlap with unrelated constructs, and (3) verifying that the collective item set comprehensively represents the construct’s domain. The first criterion is assessed by evaluating the correspondence of each item to its intended construct, whereas the second emerges from its distinctiveness relative to other constructs (Colquitt et al., 2019; MacKenzie et al., 2011). The third criterion assesses representativeness based on the extent to which the item set collectively captures all the facets of the target content domain (Burton-Jones & Lee, 2017; Holden & Jackson, 1979; MacKenzie et al., 2011). All three evaluations are essential whether researchers are developing new constructs and instruments from scratch, adapting existing instruments to new contexts, or reevaluating the quality of published instruments.

Several manual processes for assessing content validity are available, including cognitive interviewing (Miller et al., 2014), focus groups (Vogt et al., 2004), card sorting (Moore & Benbasat, 1991), expert evaluations (Lawshe, 1975), and naive judge assessment (Anderson & Gerbing, 1991; Hinkin & Tracey, 1999). However, published studies rarely include content validity assessments. For example, appraisal estimates have ranged between 26% (Boudreau et al., 2001) and 19% in IS two decades later (Schmitz & Storey, 2020). Researchers in psychology have also observed a persistent absence of content validity assessments in empirical research (Cortina et al., 2020; Haynes et al., 1995; Heggestad et al., 2019). Inadequate attention to the content validity of measurement instruments is viewed as a significant barrier to scientific advancement (Aguinis & Vandenberg, 2014; Burton-Jones & Lee, 2017), and this is no minor issue. Insufficient attention to content validity has resulted in scholars questioning the link between measures and the conceptual domain of their constructs and unchecked proliferation of inadequate measures that can mislead empirical studies and hinder theoretical progress (e.g., Aguinis & Vandenberg, 2014; Heggestad et al., 2019; Van Knippenberg & Sitkin, 2013).

Researchers have speculated about the possible reasons for the lack of attention to content validation in the behavioral sciences. The cost, difficulty, and time needed to recruit human judges in the commonly employed content validation protocols are often prohibitive (Cortina et al., 2020). In addition, researchers may not know how to conduct such validations (Burton-Jones & Lee, 2017), and there remain lingering ambiguities in interpreting content validation procedures (Colquitt et al., 2019). The quality of current approaches also suffers from the cognitive limitations of human judges (Hoehle & Venkatesh, 2015), leading to low trust in the validity of the procedures in practice. Finally, content validity procedures are seldom replicable (Khodyakov et al., 2011), which may lead to a perception that they are not sufficiently rigorous and reliable. A content validation method that is affordable, fast, and replicable; that can guide the user through each step while employing best practices; and that does not rely solely on human judges could potentially address the problem of infrequent evaluation of content validity in scale development.

This paper presents and validates a novel approach to content validation that leverages recent advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) to assist researchers in developing new measurement instruments. The proposed approach is equally applicable to adapting existing instruments to new contexts and to evaluating the quality of existing instruments. In each of the above cases, focusing on the three content validation assessments—correspondence, distinctiveness, and representativeness—is important. Our method will particularly benefit scholars in fields such as information systems, organizational behavior, and the social sciences, all of which require systematic, theory-driven approaches to instrument design. Following Abbasi et al.’s (2024) prescription, we generalize beyond specific models to address the broader content validation task by making the models available on a generalizable platform. We also validate our method using Larsen et al.’s (2025) design validation approach.

We present the highest-performing content validity assessment models to date. Our proposed computational approach differs from traditional manual procedures in its ability to effectively analyze item content and provide iterative feedback on itemconstruct alignment. Empirical evidence from prior applications of language models in psychometrics and measurement supports the potential for such tools to advance both substantive and methodological work (Arnulf & Larsen, 2021; Evans et al., 2021; Gefen & Larsen, 2017; Hernandez & Nie, 2022; Rosenbusch et al., 2020).

Our AI-based system for content validation reduces manual effort, lowers costs, is replicable, and shortens content validity cycles. It is offered as a platform open to extension by other researchers, with validation and holdout data made readily available for this quickly expanding area of research. The RATER system, available at www.contval.org, provides access to the RATER (replicable approach to expert ratings) models, two fundamentally different models named RATER<sub>Classifier</sub> and RATER<sub>Distribution</sub> (RATER<sub>C</sub> and RATER<sub>D</sub> for short).<sup>2</sup> Using a large volume of dedicated training data, the RATER<sub>C</sub> model exhibits the highest level of performance in matching potential items against definitions while having fewer than 0.5B parameters, offering meaningful insights with minimal hardware requirements. Our team provides the use of this model to the community for free.

In contrast, RATER<sub>D</sub> leverages more recent advancements in generative AI to emulate the item rating procedure introduced by Hinkin and Tracey (1999)—henceforth referred to as H&T—via “synthetic” raters. Although it utilizes significantly more parameters (32B for our best open-weight model) and thus requires more computational resources, one advantage of this class of model is that substantially fewer training data are required to deliver competitive performance compared to RATER Moreover, this model uniquely offers the ability to quickly (and comparatively inexpensively) conduct more traditional content validity exercises and generate relevant metrics, such as those proposed by Colquitt et al. (2019). We support both open- and closed-weight models to maximize flexibility, with the latter simply requiring a user-provided API key to generate results.

Our commitment to transparent and replicable assessments not only enhances accessibility and reproducibility but also establishes our models as benchmark artifacts for future research (Larsen et al., 2020, 2025). The RATER system is designed to inspire users to adopt practices that elevate the quality and credibility of measurement instruments across diverse research domains. Expert evaluations highlight the system’s efficiency and clarity, and extensive testing of our LLM-based models confirms that they can complement traditional content validity procedures, yielding results comparable to those achieved by large teams of human raters. We provide comprehensive benchmark datasets for automatically predicting item/definition correspondence to further advance research in this field. We invite researchers to add models that outperform our own to the RATER system. Success in this shared initiative could strengthen the core of psychometric theory by enabling a convenient and costeffective method for content validity assessment, thus shifting the cost-benefit equation in favor of content validation.

## Conceptual Background

## Overview of Content Validation Procedures

Two approaches to assessing content validity procedures currently dominate the landscape: item sorting (Anderson & Gerbing, 1991) and item rating (Hinkin & Tracey, 1999). The item sorting approach provides judges with definition statements for multiple constructs and a randomized list of items that purportedly operationalize these constructs. Judges are asked to identify the construct that best matches each item, and interjudge agreement is then employed to determine the sets of items that best operationalize each construct. In the IS discipline, developing an instrument to measure the perceptions of adopting an information technology innovation is often cited as a methodological reference for this technique (Moore & Benbasat, 1991). The rationale underlying the rating approach is similar to that underpinning the item sorting approach. The key difference is that the latter captures judges’ responses as binary data. In contrast, the rating approach captures judges’ responses in the form of ordinal data, usually on a Likert-type scale.

Researchers can use the data generated through these procedures to create summary statistics that inform two critical aspects of content validity: definitional correspondence and definitional distinctiveness (Colquitt et al., 2019; Cortina et al., 2020). Definitional correspondence refers to the “degree to which a scale’s items correspond to the construct’s definition” (Colquitt et al., 2019, p. 1243). In contrast, definitional distinctiveness refers to “the degree to which a scale’s items correspond more to the focal construct’s definition than to the definitions of other” non-intended constructs (Colquitt et al., 2019, p. 1243). Both high correspondence and high distinctiveness are needed to justify the items included to operationalize constructs. Items that do not possess both properties are discarded or revised iteratively, and their content validity is assessed again after each revision. Items that demonstrate high definitional correspondence but low definitional distinctiveness produce contaminated measures (i.e., measures that capture aspects that are not part of the construct definition), while items that demonstrate high distinctiveness but low correspondence produce deficient measures (i.e., measure that do not capture central aspects of the construct definition—MacKenzie, 2003). Only items with high distinctiveness and high correspondence are retained. Researchers should review the rejected items, revise them, and repeat the content validation cycle until satisfactory results are reached (Schmitz & Storey, 2020).

In addition to correspondence and distinctiveness, content validity concerns the representativeness of an item set (Haynes et al., 1995; MacKenzie et al., 2011). Judgments about the representativeness of a scale concern the degree to which the items as a set collectively represent all the relevant aspects of the focal construct (Haynes et al., 1995; MacKenzie et al., 2011; Messick, 1995; Schriesheim et al., 1993). Representativeness relates to whether items that were not included should have been included in a scale (Cortina et al., 2020). Oversampling can be used to hedge against the risk that a scale lacks representativeness. However, it can lead to the inclusion of items beyond the boundaries of the focal construct’s domain (i.e., irrelevant items) and risks exhausting respondents with too many items. However, since no procedures have been proposed in the literature to formally assess representativeness, our approach is preliminary. We elaborate on future possibilities for more robust approaches to assessing representativeness in the Discussion section.

The contemporary procedures for assessing content validity described above represent three significant shifts from historical perspectives on content validity assessment. First, current content validation procedures emphasize the semantic tightening of the link between constructs and their observable items (Aguinis & Vandenberg, 2014). Second, judges “representative of the main study sample and population of interest” (Anderson & Gerbing, 1991, p. 734) are favored over domain experts due to the semantic nature of the content validity assessment task (Colquitt et al., 2019; Hinkin & Tracey, 1999). Third, the representativeness of instruments is becoming an increasingly salient issue in management and organizational psychology due to the preferred use of short scales (Cortina et al., 2020).<sup>3</sup>

In summary, the content validation process has evolved into a highly codified procedure involving dozens of naive judges assessing the semantic relationship between item-construct definition pairs. The activities involved in current content validation procedures are similar to those involved in the semantic parsing task, which converts text content into a logical form to extract meaning, a standard semantic computing task (Li et al., 2020). As described in a later section, we build on this similarity when constructing our AIbased system.

## Key Challenges Facing Current Content Validation Procedures

Despite some progress in improving content validation procedures, several issues remain unresolved. First, one recurrent shortcoming of these procedures is their inability to accommodate more than a few scales before the judges performing the task are cognitively overwhelmed. This is especially the case with the H&T procedure, which requires judges to perform granular numerical evaluations for each unique combination of items and construct definitions included in the evaluation set. To accommodate this constraint, less sophisticated content validation techniques may be used (e.g., Hoehle & Venkatesh, 2015) or the evaluation task may be broken up into smaller, more manageable parts (Colquitt et al., 2019). These workarounds illustrate the practical challenges that arise when implementing content validation procedures.

Second, access to the resources required to perform content validity assessment may pose a problem for researchers who must recruit hundreds of participants (e.g., Hoehle & Venkatesh, 2015) and spend as much as \$10,000 (e.g., Colquitt et al., 2019). Although researchers may reduce recruitment costs by using crowdsourcing platforms (Edgar et al., 2016; Goodman & Paolacci, 2017), the time and resources required for conducting a rigorous content validation exercise involving human judges are substantial. Resource inequalities produce imbalances and exacerbate global disparities in research participation.

Third, scales are increasingly being shortened to comply with contemporary data collection and analysis requirements because long surveys take more time to complete and tend to have more missing data and higher refusal rates than short surveys (Smith et al., 2000; Stanton et al., 2002). In addition, a data analysis framework (i.e., structural equation modeling) and mainstream criteria for evaluating scale performance (i.e., Cronbach’s alpha) strongly incentivize the use of short scales with highly redundant items over longer scales with diverse items (Baumgartner & Weijters, 2019; Cortina et al., 2020), thus threatening their representativeness (Heggestad et al., 2019). The extent of the threat to representativeness remains uncertain due to the incomplete state of current evaluative frameworks (Alley et al., 2024), and we are unaware of any codified approach to assessing the representativeness of a set of items.

Fourth, current approaches do not adequately accommodate the iterative nature of the measure development process. The measure development stage is highly iterative, with items being adjusted, revised, or amended as feedback is received (MacKenzie et al., 2011). The content validation process thus needs to apply a “rinse and repeat” approach to build trust and confidence in the resulting instrument (Schmitz & Storey, 2020, p. 40). Given the substantial resources required for content validation, researchers would benefit from cheap and fast techniques, especially since the item set may easily be expanded by automatically generating items from LLMs (e.g., Hommel et al., 2022).

Finally, current procedures require judges to make their assessments within the context of the set of items and construct definitions they are given. More specifically, the item-sorting approach requires judges to assess which of the given construct definitions an item best represents. Similarly, judges’ assessments following the rating approach capture how well an item represents a particular construct, relative to other constructs within the same study. As a result, the judgments arising from these approaches are influenced by the set of items and the constructs included in the exercise (Colquitt et al., 2019). Accordingly, interpreting correspondence and distinctiveness scores from those procedures is difficult, as the scores pertaining to an itemconstruct pair can change with the inclusion or exclusion of other constructs and items in the procedure. As far as we know, the susceptibility of extant content validation protocols to these variations has not yet been examined.

We argue that new approaches are needed to overcome these challenges and to help build robust constructs and instruments. The measurement field is ready to benefit from and to be transformed by emerging technologies (Salas et al., 2017). As semantic algorithms become more accessible and reliable, an exploration of how AI can enhance content validation by addressing the many challenges inherent in current procedures is timely. Specifically, AI models are not restricted by the limits of human cognition, do not require significant additional resources when the number of items and constructs to be evaluated increases, and do not require significant additional resources to execute the iterative cycles of refinement.

In Table 1, we identify the core components of the initial steps of the construct development process, summarize the key challenges facing the process, and indicate how researchers could leverage automated tools to address all six problems listed above. Within the table, our focus in this paper is on Step 3 and its requirements in terms of correspondence, distinctiveness, and representativeness. While not a requirement, a scientific understanding of the constructs as well as the content validity itself would be likely to improve if Steps 3a, 3b and 3c were implemented using two general approaches to AI instantiation: (1) the use of classifier models to generate probability scores that reflect how well items align with construct definitions (instantiated here as the RATER<sub>C</sub> model), and (2) the emulation of human raters by the creation of “synthetic raters” to assess item correspondence on a 7- point Likert scale, i.e., following the rating approach (Hinkin & Tracey, 1999), which is instantiated here as the RATER<sub>D</sub> model.

## Current Use of AI-Based Approaches in Psychometric Processes

Scale development is a complex and multistage process, and in the past, computational researchers have proposed methods to assist with different aspects of construct discovery (Larsen et al., 2008; Rosenbusch et al., 2020) and scale creation (Agogo & Hess, 2018; Hernandez & Nie, 2022). Although prior work has identified many issues and challenges associated with how researchers currently implement the first three steps in the process of MacKenzie et al. (2011), computational efforts to assist with scale development have not directly addressed the known challenges. These challenges include clarifying poorly defined concepts (Step 1), identifying redundant items (Step 2c), and ensuring the representativeness of an overall scale (Step 3c). For instance, computational efforts focused on the initial conceptualization stage (Step 1) have not attempted to help researchers identify and refine poor construct definitions; instead, prior work has focused on identifying overlapping constructs (Li et al., 2020; Rosenbusch et al., 2020) or on how models can help scholars to conceptualize constructs by organizing social media text (Agogo & Hess, 2018). These aims are important for scale development but do not help scholars create good definitions that only match the intended items.

Research on automatic item generation (Attali et al., 2022; Circi et al., 2023; Götz et al., 2024; Hommel, 2023) similarly helps scholars conceptualize ways to operationalize constructs. Such research therefore addresses the second step of the MacKenzie model (Step 2a). However, automatic item generation does not directly address the known problems with how researchers currently generate items; in fact, LLMgenerated items may exacerbate existing issues by introducing redundancies or producing low-quality items that fail to align with distinct definitions within an instrument. We thus regard computational work on automatic item generation as separate from but complementary to work measuring content validity. For instance, special-purpose LLMs designed for content validation might screen automatically generated items for inclusion or exclusion on a scale.

In the third step, shown in Table 1, researchers assess whether the items generated in the second step match the construct definitions from the first step. Early work on computational methods for Step 3 considered how to use general-purpose dialog-tuned transformer models such as Claude or ChatGPT for this specialized task (Beghetto et al., 2025; Ke & Ng, 2025).

There are at least six drawbacks to the use of black-box, proprietary, and general-purpose dialog models such as ChatGPT for content validation. First, these tools are not transparent; for instance, OpenAI has released few details on how its models are trained and does not allow researchers to inspect the model weights to understand the behavior of the model. This makes it difficult for researchers to understand why a particular model returns a particular answer, thus depriving scientists of the crucial ability to examine the causal drivers of model performance. Second, these tools are not reliable: OpenAI and Anthropic may adjust models or interfaces at any time based on commercial requirements. Third, proprietary models are not replicable, meaning that different researchers may obtain different outputs based on unknown and opaque factors such as model adjustments, personalization, or random seeds. Many common algorithms for text generation, such as top-k sampling (Fan et al., 2018), are inherently probabilistic, meaning they are designed to return different answers at different times. Fourth, the use of well-known models from commercial operators imposes financial costs on researchers. Prices may change in the future, and support for these models may disappear. Fifth, these tools do not provide support for scale-level measures such as representativeness, which are inherent to content validation. Finally, these general-purpose tools are not natively designed to support scale development and may perform less well than specialized models optimized for the content validation task.

The general-purpose BRASS bot system (Ke & Ng, 2025) addresses some of these limitations. The authors provide stable versions of BRASS for public use, meaning that replicability and reliability are ensured; however, BRASS is deployed on the poe.com platform, where researchers are required to pay for a premium account to use the best models. BRASS is also a general-purpose tool designed for all steps of scale development and is known to perform “inadequately” (Ke & Ng, 2025, p. 19) in the specific task of rating correspondence between items and definitions. It does not support scale-level measures.

To the best of our knowledge, only one computational method has a specific focus on content validation. Fyffe et al. (2024) proposed that researchers should train, tune, and evaluate custom-built transformer models designed for specific domains and offered an illustration of this process by developing a specialized model to classify “Big Five” personality traits (Costa & McCrae, 1992). The authors also released an artifact that was hard-coded to perform this fiveway classification task. In some settings, this approach may “improve the efficiency of scale development” (Fyffe et al., 2024, p. 289) by providing models that apply to scales within the Big Five domain; however, most behavioral researchers do not have the skills or resources needed to develop custom ML-models for arbitrary domains, and the semantics of personality constructs are known to be quite different from those of other constructs (Arnulf et al., 2014).

Given these limitations of existing work, we propose a platform for evaluating future models, as well as a domainindependent, open source, and no-cost system that can be applied reliably and transparently in academic scaledevelopment research. Such a system would offer models that can provide standardized measures, which can be applied reliably and would not require project-specific engineering from a research team. The platform will offer open-weight models, which will be free and may be more accurate than the alternatives, as well as closed-weight models via commercial APIs, including fine-tuned variants specifically tailored to our overarching task. Rather than requiring dedicated GPUs, these commercial models will simply require an end user to supply an API key to use the system, thus allowing them to generate results quickly and to pay only for their individual use.

## Development of AI Models and the RATER System

The AI models developed in this study use supervised learning to improve performance on the specific task of performing content validity assessments. In general, supervised learningbased AI models perform better than those based on unsupervised learning because they are optimized to perform tasks using bespoke training data. Even in the rapidly changing landscape brought about by recent advancements in generative pretrained transformer (GPT) large language models (LLMs), recent research continues to show that smaller BERT-style LLMs can outperform models that are several orders of magnitude more complex when fine-tuned with high-quality training data (Bucher & Martini, 2024).

Supervised machine learning involves developing models based on learning from a training dataset that provides instances of decisions within a specific context. Supervised AI models are employed for semantic assessment tasks in many fields—including in areas of psychology and psychometrics, and in screening for bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, depression, and dementia (Chandler et al., 2020; Dai et al., 2021)—and to assess attitudes (Arnulf et al., 2021; Kjell et al., 2021). Recent advancements in GPT-style LLMs have led to significant performance improvements in modeling tasks that have traditionally been challenging, such as depression detection (Qin et al., 2024), although more traditional BERTstyle transformers can outperform GPT-style models on some tasks (Zhong et al., 2023).

Since AI models are now mature enough to conduct complex semantic tasks, we therefore consider how they can assist researchers in assessing content validity. We thus evaluated a range of LLMs to develop two types of models, which differ in terms of their outcome: (1) probability classifiers, which consider the probability that the text of a particular item matches a given construct definition, and (2) distribution models, which simulate the ratings approach (Hinkin & Tracey, 1999) to content validation by creating a set of “synthetic” raters. These raters generate a distribution of scores based on how well the text of a particular item matches a given construct definition using an ordinal scale from 1 (extremely poor) to 7 (extremely good).

To develop the best probability classifier, we fine-tuned a variety of open-weight transformer models (Vaswani et al., 2017), including bidirectional encoder representations from transformers (BERT) and autoregressive variants. Specifically, with respect to the BERT family, we evaluated ALBERT (Lan et al., 2019), BERT (Devlin et al., 2019), DeBERTa and DeBERTa v3 (He et al., 2021), MobileBERT (Sun et al., 2020), and RoBERTa (Liu et al., 2019). For autoregressive models, we evaluated XLNet (Yang et al., 2019). Within each of these model types, we tested several variants based on model size (e.g., varying numbers of parameters, layers, etc.) to give a final benchmark set of 15 classifiers.

To identify the best distribution model, we fine-tuned both open- and closed-weight transformer-based models (Radford et al., 2018). The open-weight models considered here were Llama 3, 3.1, and 3.3 (Grattafiori et al., 2024), Phi-4 (Abdin et al., 2024), and Qwen2.5 (Yang et al., 2025),<sup>4</sup> and for each of these open-weight models, we considered a variety of different model sizes in order to assess the trade-off between overall model performance and computational demands (e.g., graphics processing unit [GPU] hardware requirements, runtime performance, and electricity consumption) for a total of 10 models. In contrast, for the closed-weight models, we selected three OpenAI models based on the availability of developer access to model fine-tuning; these included OpenAI’s GPT-3.5 Turbo, GPT-4o, and GPT-4o mini (OpenAI, 2024). Ultimately, we benchmarked a total of 13 distribution models.

## Fine-Tuning and Evaluation Data

To train and evaluate the best probability classifier, we followed the approach of Larsen and Bong (2016). Specifically, we gathered item definitions and corresponding item texts from across eight disciplines (behavioral medicine, education, information systems, management, marketing, nursing, psychology, and sociology) based on 2,443 journal articles. For each article, we took the cross-product of item texts against construct definitions, thereby producing intended and non-intended item text/construct definition pairs (i.e., an item paired with its actual definition was coded as an intended pair, whereas an item paired with a different definition from within the same source was coded as a non-intended pair). In total, the dataset consisted of 41,572 intended pairs and 245,854 non-intended pairs, giving 287,426 pairs in total.

We subsequently divided this dataset into training, validation, and test splits using a ratio of approximately 80/10/10. To avoid having items from the same journal article appearing across these three splits, we randomly sampled these proportions at the article level (i.e., as opposed to the item text/construct definition pair level). Ultimately, when counting at the row level, the final data split was 79%/9%/12% for the training, validation, and test sets, respectively, corresponding to 227,474 training instances, 26,648 validation instances, and 33,304 test instances (again, 287,426 in total, consistent with the value reported above).

Although this test set was ultimately used to benchmark all models (i.e., both the probability classifiers and distribution models), we needed an additional set of data to fine-tune the distribution models using rater responses collected from human respondents. We selected a set of definition/item pairs from existing published articles that met three key selection criteria: The authors were required to have (1) reported the definitions of constructs, (2) provided the texts for the item statements, and (3) reported the results of their factor analysis. We ultimately identified a total of eight articles using a rigorous selection process, as detailed in our transparency materials (https://osf.io/sfbgj). Four articles were drawn from the Academy of Management Journal—Zhang et al. (2020), Sherf et al. (2019), Yu and Zellmer-Bruhn (2018), and Colbert et al. (2016)—and four were drawn from the Journal of Applied Psychology: Roth et al. (2019), Bindl et al. (2019), Yu (2019), and Thompson and Bolino (2018).

From these articles, we collected 858 item-definition pairs, of which 159 were intended pairs and the remaining 699 were non-intended pairs. This imbalance was as expected, since for every intended pair, there are multiple non-intended pairs (i.e., an item forms non-intended pairs with all constructs in the study except the intended construct). To collect participant data, we subjected a subset of these 858 construct definition/item statement pairs to the H&T item rating exercise, following the guidelines of Colquitt et al. (2019). Their protocol requires each judge to evaluate the extent to which a given item statement matches three different construct definition statements, on a scale from 1 (the item does an extremely bad job of measuring the concept) to 7 (the item does an extremely good job of measuring the concept). Each item was paired against three definitions, when available, to yield correspondence scores: one was paired with the intended construct, whereas the other two were paired with nonintended constructs randomly selected from within the study. Limiting the construct set to three constructs aligns with the recommendation of Colquitt et al. (2019) to avoid taxing respondents and is commensurate with typical content validation efforts. Following this design, 467 item-definition statement pairs were subjected to the H&T procedure, and these resulting data were subsequently used to fine-tune our distribution models.

## Modeling Procedure

We conducted an extensive model evaluation to find the best possible classifier and distribution models. As noted earlier, we assessed 15 different classifier models, including both BERT and autoregressive variants. For each model, we utilized the training and validation datasets for the eight disciplines in a hyperparameter grid search to identify the best possible fine-tuning parameters using Hugging Face transformers (Wolf et al., 2019). We evaluated the same combination of 15 settings for each of the models, i.e., three learning rates (1e-5, 3e-5, 5e-5) multiplied by five training epochs (1, 2, 3, 5, 10). To optimize the training time and avoid out-of-memory errors, we utilized a fixed training batch size of 32 for smaller models and eight for larger models. Finally, we maximized ROC AUC as the training optimization metric using the validation set and saved the best model found across all training epoch checkpoints based on validation loss as the final model for each optimal hyperparameter combination.

With respect to the distribution models, we evaluated a total of 13, as noted earlier. The three closed-weight distribution models were fine-tuned using the OpenAI fine-tuning platform with automatic, recommended settings for the batch size, learning rate multiplier, and number of epochs. The 10 open-weight distribution models were fine-tuned using unsloth (Han et al., 2023) with five epochs and quantization employed to minimize hardware requirements (Lin et al., 2023). To achieve efficient inference with these models, we used vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023), and used fixed randomization seeds for all model training and inferencing to allow for reproducibility. The code employed for model training and evaluation is available in the transparency materials and via GitHub (https://github.com/davedgd/RATER).

## Model Evaluation

We report three key metrics to evaluate the performance of our models on the test data holdout: ROC AUC, macro F1, and expected calibration error (ECE). AUC provides a useful measure of the association between model prediction probabilities or ordinal outputs and ground truth labels (i.e., intended and non-intended item text/construct definition pairs). In contrast, macro F1 provides an overall measure of classification accuracy based on precision and recall. Given the imbalance across classes and our broader interest in predicting both matches and mismatches accurately, we employed the macro average of F1 as a second look at the performance of our models.<sup>5</sup> Finally, ECE provides a measure of a model’s calibration, namely the degree to which model probabilities are aligned with accurate decisions, which is relevant when gauging how directly individuals can interpret the scores of a particular model. We calculated the ECE for the distribution models using their observed ordinal scores, whereas, for the classifiers, we binned model confidence scores into seven buckets to make the results more comparable. The ECE measure was computed using net:cal (Küppers et al., 2020).

Table 2 presents the results of our benchmarking evaluation using the test set. The rows are sorted first by AUC and then by macro F1. We highlight three models based on their overall performance with respect to AUC: (1) the best probability classifier, i.e., deberta-v3-large; (2) the best open-weight distribution model, Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct; and (3) the best closed-weight distribution model, OpenAI GPT-4o. The performance of these models was generally similar, with differences of a few percentage points noted in AUC and macro F1 (e.g., the DeBERTaV3 model achieved the highest overall AUC and macro F1 scores; in comparison, the Qwen2.5 model had the lowest AUC of the three but was second best to GPT-4o in terms of macro F1). The ECE results for the classifier models consistently tended to be lower than those of the distribution models, which is likely due to the differences in how the models were trained and to architectural differences.

From a comparison of the results of the best classifier and distribution models, we see that the classifier models show clear performance advantages, with our best classifier models outperforming all distribution models across every evaluation metric. Moreover, the classifier models have several key advantages in terms of their computational efficiency: for example, the deberta-v3-large model, while relatively large by BERT-style model standards, has fewer than 0.5B parameters and can be efficiently inferenced using a CPU and less than 4GB of system memory. In comparison, the best open-weight distribution model, Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct, is over 64 times larger and requires a GPU with at least 24GB of video memory for efficient inferencing even with quantization, making this an infeasible choice for our goal of providing a free content validation model to all psychometric disciplines.<sup>6</sup>

Given the overall performance and efficiency of our top finetuned classifier model (i.e., deberta-v3-large), we used this model in follow-up experiments, as described in the next section detailing our empirical studies; we refer to it hereafter as RATER<sub>C</sub>. However, since classifier and distribution models each provide a unique perspective on the content validity process, we implemented the option to make use of both model types in our end-user system, as detailed in the next section, with the best overall fine-tuned distribution model (i.e., GPT-4o) referred to as RATER<sub>D</sub>. Finally, it is worth noting that the results of our benchmarking process are limited by the performance of the best LLMs available today. Since it is likely that better models will be developed in the future, we have thus developed a system that treats classifier and distribution models as modular components that can be interchanged based on hardware availability and/or updated based on future model developments.

## Evaluation of the RATER System

Having conducted a preliminary quantitative validation of the models based on constructs and instruments reported across eight disciplines, we conducted six summative criterion validity studies (Larsen et al., 2025) to evaluate the performance of our best and most economically viable AI model, RATER<sub>C</sub>, for the specific task of content validation.

## Study 1: Does RATER<sub>C</sub> Retain Its Efficacy in Management and Psychology?

The essence of content validity is that items should reflect the content domain of their intended construct rather than the content domains of non-intended constructs (Colquitt et al., 2019). Accordingly, the H&T procedure compares an item’s mean ratings on its intended construct against its mean ratings on other non-intended constructs; an item is considered to satisfythe requirements for establishing content validity when its mean rating on its intended construct is substantially higher than its mean ratings on other non-intended constructs. Similarly, Anderson and Gerbing’s (1991) procedure establishes content validity by comparing “the extent to which respondents assign an item to its posited construct more than to any other construct” (p. 734). Colquitt et al. (2019) followed a similar logic by comparing the average correspondence scores for intended construct pairs with the average correspondence scores for non-intended construct pairs. For any new content validation procedure to be considered valid, it should, at a minimum, meet the above test—that is, it should be able to distinguish between item-intended construct pairs and item-non-intended construct pairs. We therefore start by claiming that our RATER<sub>C</sub> algorithm meets this requirement, following Larsen et al.’s (2025) guidance for design science.

We tested this claim using the items and construct definitions reported in the studies in the validation sample. The use of RATER<sub>C</sub> resulted in classification scores (probabilities) for 159 intended pairs and 699 non-intended pairs that were previously used to fine-tune the RATER<sub>D</sub> model, giving a total of 858 item statement-construct definition pairs. These pairs came from articles that were published after the articles used to train the RATER<sub>C</sub> model, thereby increasing confidence that the model would also work for constructs and instruments that the model did not “come across” earlier during the training phase.<sup>7</sup>

The claim was assessed with a t-test in which the mean scores of item-intended definition pairs were compared against those of item-non-intended definition pairs. The mean score for intended pairs was 0.63 (SD = 0.43), whereas for non-intended pairs it was 0.097 (SD = 0.24). The difference between the two mean scores is significant (difference = 0.53, p < 0.001).

In addition to this first simple evaluation, we examined the extent to which the RATER<sub>C</sub> scores correctly identified items as belonging to their intended constructs, as identified by the authors of the studies in our validation sample. The ground truth in Study 1 was the set of item-intended construct pairs and the item-non-intended construct pairs theorized by the authors.

As a decision rule for the AI model predictions, we considered an item to be matched with the construct with which it shared the highest score. If both the AI model and the authors identified an item as belonging to the same construct, this was considered to be agreement; conversely, if the two identified an item as belonging to different constructs, it was considered to be disagreement. Study 1 provided an accuracy of 92%, indicating that RATER<sub>C</sub> correctly identified 92% of the itemconstruct pairs as intended or non-intended. Based on the classifier results presented in the confusion matrix reported in Table 4, the F1-score was 0.78 and Cohen’s kappa was 0.73, suggesting substantial agreement (McHugh, 2012).<sup>8</sup>

## Study 2: Is RATER<sub>C</sub> Consistent With Human Judges?

A key requirement of any new technological artifact is that its efficacy should be consistent with or better than that of the current best criterion approaches. The H&T rating procedure, which is currently considered state-of-the-art (Aguinis & Edwards, 2014; Colquitt et al., 2014, 2019; Cortina et al., 2020), requires judges to rate the extent of the overlap between an item and a series of construct definition statements using a Likert-style rating scale. In this process, all possible combinations of an item with a set of construct definition statements are evaluated to yield a unique correspondence rating for each item-definition pair. Although RATER<sub>C</sub> is based on different logic and training, it is designed to solve the same problem as the H&T rating procedure. The scores estimated by the AI model for definition-item overlap probability should be significant predictors of those generated by human judges, and we therefore tested whether RATER<sub>C</sub> met this requirement.

## Study 3: How Does RATER Compare With Other Tools?

We evaluated RATER<sub>C</sub> against BRASS Bot (Ke & Ng, 2025) using our 33,304 instance test set. BRASS does not have an API and cannot be used programmatically, and we therefore wrote custom scripts to include BRASS in our experiment. Even with this partial automation, the use of BRASS still required extensive manual data collection (e.g., copying and pasting information into and out of the chatbot interface). For this reason, we evaluated BRASS on a limited set of 3,303 item/definition pairs, sampled from our full test set.<sup>10</sup>

We used the BRASS/rate command for our analysis to return Likert-style scores that reflect how well an item matches its intended definition. For our experiment, we used the BRASS-Claude-2 implementation of BRASS available on the poe.com platform, which required signing up for a \$20 premium monthly subscription. We used BRASS-Claude-2 because the BRASS authors found that this model performed best for the content validation task (Ke & Ng, 2025, p. 18).

Throughout this work, we tested how well different computational models can use item text and definition text to make predictions about item-definition correspondence. This evaluation is appropriate because this information is available to human raters during the traditional H&T process. To facilitate a direct comparison of the results in Table 6, we set up the BRASS tool to use item definitions and item texts in making predictions. This test was successful, as it yielded evidence that RATER<sub>C</sub> has better efficacy on the task than the criterion artifact (BRASS), thus confirming criterion efficacy validity.

## Study 4: Can RATER<sub>C</sub> Scores Support Correspondence and Distinctiveness?

In content validity assessments, items are retained if they demonstrate “high” definitional correspondence and “high” definitional distinctiveness (Colquitt et al., 2019). However, prior work does not specify exactly what constitutes evidence of “high” correspondence and “high” distinctiveness. Following prior traditions in psychometrics research, we propose thresholds for “acceptable” levels of correspondence and distinctiveness. We follow the rationale that any threshold has to balance the probabilities of Type I (considering items as acceptable when they should not be acceptable) and Type II errors (rejecting items when they should not be rejected). As our training and test data are drawn from relatively highquality sources, it could be argued that a high proportion of the items are a priori acceptable from a content validity perspective. Hence, a low threshold corresponding to some number below the mean should be acceptable. On the other hand, there is empirical evidence that very few of the instruments, even in high-quality journals, are subjected to rigorous content validity assessment. Given this observation, it could be argued that only a low proportion of the items are a priori acceptable; hence, a high threshold corresponding to some number above the mean should be acceptable.

As a result, for illustrative purposes, we employ mean values as thresholds to aid researchers in their decision-making when using the RATER tool. However, a detailed empirical assessment of acceptable thresholds is beyond the scope of this paper. We expect community consensus on appropriate thresholds to support decision-making to emerge over time.

We used the large holdout set employed for earlier benchmarking to identify our illustrative thresholds. We reran our analysis to compare intended and non-intended RATER<sub>C</sub> scores using this dataset and found that the mean score for items and intended construct definitions was 0.620. We also found that the mean score for unintended construct definitions was 0.061. We therefore propose that high correspondence between an item and its definition produces a RATER<sub>C</sub> score of 0.620 or higher. We also propose that high distinctiveness between an item and its intended definition produces a score of 0.062 or lower for all non-intended definitions. We refer to this in the following as the “62 heuristic.”

We propose the 62 heuristic as guidance for researchers, rather than a hard rule. Further, we would like to stress that the threshold scores are model-specific and that they might evolve with further empirical research. We also stress that the suggested thresholds derive from our illustration above using mean values; they would change if some other rule were employed to determine the threshold—for instance, 1 standard deviation above or below the mean. Keeping that in mind, the web version of RATER provides users with the option to set their preferred thresholds for flagging acceptable versus nonacceptable items.

## Study 5: Can RATER<sub>C</sub> Be Extended to Address Representativeness?

The first two content validity criteria focus on correspondence and distinctiveness, whereas the third criterion requires that a scale’s items collectively represent the content domain of the construct, that is, it has representativeness, defined as the extent to which the set of items fully represents the construct’s content domain. Kerlinger (1973, p. 458) addressed the issue of representativeness for the case of multi-dimensional constructs by positing that a scale’s items should represent each construct “approximately equally” and be “proportional to the facets of the targeted construct” (Haynes et al., 1995, p. 239). We draw on Kerlinger’s arguments and MacKenzie et al.’s (2011) illustration of assessing the representativeness of the multidimensional construct of “trustworthiness,” which includes the subdimensions of benevolence, integrity, and ability.<sup>11</sup> To represent trustworthiness, a scale must contain items that correspond to each such subdimension of trustworthiness.

## Study 6: How Do Users Evaluate the RATER System?

As called for in previous studies on the relevance of research in the IS domain (Rosemann & Vessey, 2008), we conducted an applicability check of our content validation system. An initial pilot test was done with faculty colleagues and doctoral students, leading to many changes to the system with the aim of increasing accessibility. The final check summarized here was conducted with seven participants from external institutions across Europe and the U.S. To ensure ecological validity per Larsen et al. (2025), the participants were selected for their high levels of expertise in construct validation and scale development. None were financially rewarded for participation.

One senior member of our team conducted a one-hour, stepby-step training session with all participants, using previously published scales to demonstrate the tool’s capabilities. Participants were then given the task of using the system for their scales. They were given access to our system at www.contval.org for at least one week before filling out our response survey.

The response survey was constructed according to Venkatesh et al. (2003), using a 7-point Likert scale to measure the predictors of system ease of use (μ = 6.6; σ = 1.2), usefulness $( \mu = 5 . 7 ; \sigma = 1 . 2 )$ , and intention to use the system (μ = 5.3; σ = 1.4), as is common in applicability checks (e.g., Li et al., 2020). The quantitative responses indicated that our expert participant panel perceived the tool as easy to use and useful for practice, with high levels of intention to use the system, although intention was generally lower than the high scores on ease of use and usefulness would suggest.

The qualitative responses shed light on this evaluation. As highlighted in our literature review, content validation is rarely performed, even among scholars with a routine interest in construct validation. In our panel, only two out of seven participants systematically engaged in content validation prior to data collection. After participating in our study, one tester remarked: “It gave me an eye opener regarding the importance of employing a well-established construct definition.”

The participants did not share a consistent view of why content validation is rarely done. Most commented on the expense and the cumbersome nature of content validation before data collection, leading to post hoc statistical procedures. Others pointed to a lack of shared norms and standards for content validation. This lack of shared norms was also visible in the respondents’ comments on the output of our system. All of them agreed with the statement “Would you use the system in your future research if it was connected to a published methods paper?” One rater embraced the option of published norms in this way: “Yes, I would use RATER in my future research if it was connected to a published methods paper, because RATER provides specific numeric values that guide the application of survey items for constructs, ensuring confidence and reliability in their use.”

Notable reservations among the participants suggested that a lack of understanding of the underlying semantic analysis could hinder widespread use: “I understand the estimated loadings, but I am uncertain of the underlying analysis, [i.e.,] what causes loadings to give the value reported.” There is little knowledge about the quantitative output that AI systems might provide before data collection, which gave rise to some pertinent questions from the panel, for example, “How does the metric of the scores relate to the psychometric indices we are used to, and which guidelines should be applied to assess the reliability of outputs?” These questions may have arisen from our focus on explaining the RATER system rather than sharing its performance characteristics as evaluated in this section.

Overall, the applicability check provided valuable insights into the potential and limitations of the proposed tool, indicating that the tool itself is stable and easy to use. Concomitantly, it drew attention to the need for improved content validation in the field, as well as more research on how AI tools can be of use in establishing metrics in this area. We expect that as researchers increasingly use AI agents for many psychometric tasks, the performance and replicability of fine-tuned models, such as our RATER models, will become clearer, regardless of whether users understand the underlying mathematics and design decisions of large language models. This applicability check directly led to further improvements in the user interface and our introduction of heuristics for cutoffs (for example, the “62 heuristic” for the RATER<sub>C</sub> model).

## Further Examination of the Trust Scale Example

The previous subsection reports on what was achieved and shared by an outside research team with access to the RATER system. What follows are our own follow-up activities and recommendations. Going back to the IT procedural fairness scale, we examined items associated with IT procedural fairness and noticed that each item mentioned some variation of “process and communicate,” although the definition only involved procedures. Since RATER<sub>C</sub> is free and fast, a researcher may wish to write and evaluate new items in such settings.

We removed “and communicate” from the items, which led to high RATER<sub>C</sub> scores that were well above the 62 heuristic. However, this edit left the items with wide-ranging and unclear content: for example, “most IT processes information in a way…” is such a general statement that respondents’ answers are likely to “bleed” into other constructs, creating semantic predictability in the results (Gefen & Larsen, 2017). We would instead encourage researchers to look at the theoretical work on IT procedural fairness, which suggests that it may be a multidimensional construct. Following this guidance, we wrote several new items, some of which were confirmed by RATER<sub>C</sub> as measuring the subdimensions of IT procedural fairness, such as “I believe that Information Technology makes decisions fairly, without showing favoritism or bias,” which was written for Leventhal’s (1980) bias suppression subdimension.

In this example, we observed one important difference between the two approaches. On average, RATER<sub>C</sub> gives high scores to items with their intended definition and low scores to items with a non-intended definition (with an average score of 0.60 on intended pairs versus 0.03 for unintended pairs). The H&T procedure assigns high scores to items with their intended definition but also assigns high scores to those with a nonintended definition (average scores of 0.77 and 0.50, respectively). RATER<sub>C</sub> assigns values to items with their intended definitions that are 20 times higher than those assigned to non-intended definitions, whereas the H&T procedure assigns values that are only 1.53 times higher; this suggests that, in contrast to H&T scores, RATER<sub>C</sub> provides accurate scores over the whole range, from zero to one, a finding that may be related to RATER ’s low expected calibration error, as shown in Table 2.

To gain a sense of whether the divergence between the results from RATER and H&T reflected the limitations of the RATER model in this setting, we asked five expert researchers to rank the five items in order of correspondence to the trust in IT as a belief construct. This section illustrates how researchers can harness RATER in assessing the content validity of new, adapted, or existing scales. Rather than replacing conventional techniques (e.g., the H&T procedure, which was used in this scenario), RATER offers complementary insights concerning the correspondence, distinctiveness, and representativeness of scales’ items. The flexibility of the system allows researchers to make swift, iterative changes to the conceptual definitions or the items. While the application of RATER in the case of new scale development is intuitive and straightforward, this case illustrates that RATER can be employed to assess content validity in a wide range of alternative measurement development scenarios as well, e.g., evaluating adaptations of existing scales or reevaluating existing scales against published and new construct definitions (Compeau et al., 2022; Heggestad et al., 2019; Larsen et al., 2023; Pillet et al., 2023). Going forward, we expect that tools such as RATER will be employed to assess content validity not only in the case of developing new instruments but also in other cases whenever there is a need to evaluate content validity.

## Discussion

This work proposes a new computational technique for assessing the content validity of psychometric scales. Content validity provides a systematic procedure for ensuring that survey instruments measure the intended, unobservable constructs. However, this core process linking psychometric theory to measurement practice has so far received little attention from a large body of literature on computational tools for scale development. At present, researchers lack an open source, replicable, reliable, domain-independent, specialized, and no-cost computational tool to help assess the content validity of a scale.

Our work addresses this need by motivating, contextualizing, and evaluating RATER<sub>C</sub> and RATER<sub>D</sub>, two new transformerbased approaches for automatically assessing content validity. We find that RATER’s output is consistent with existing manual practices, is useful for researchers using and validating scales, and is better at assessing content validity than more general NLP systems.

We have also deployed RATER via a public web interface at www.contval.org to enable future use by the broad research community (scientists, reviewers, students). Moving forward, this free public resource will reduce the cost of conducting content validity assessments, support rapid iteration during the early stages of scale development, and provide a standardized and easy-to-use implementation of known best practices in creating instruments. For these reasons, the RATER models may help survey researchers across behavioral disciplines to develop high-quality scales that correctly measure their intended constructs.

## Contributions to the Literature on NLP for Scale Development

Our work makes five contributions to the literature on NLP for scale development. First, we introduce the RATER<sub>C</sub> and RATER<sub>D</sub> models, which offer computational improvements on prior work. Most importantly, RATER models are domain independent and can be run without specialized tuning on any domain. This represents an important advantage over existing computational techniques for assessing content validity, which require the application of specialized machine learning methods such as fine-tuning for specific domains (Fyffe et al., 2024). This property may prove important for adoption, as we do not expect that psychometric researchers, who sometimes skip content validity assessment altogether (Boudreau et al., 2001; Schmitz & Storey, 2020), will invest in building custom machine learning models to develop better scales. In contrast,

RATER can be applied off the shelf to any instrument, which might help researchers employ content validation in their work or help reviewers gain insights about the content validity of the measures used in a study. Our work also improves on an existing general-purpose system for scale development (Ke & Ng, 2025) because it can be run without token or message limits, at no financial cost to the researcher and without payments to a commercial platform. Although RATER is compatible with commercial APIs that require payment, it can also be run for free without usage limitations. In contrast, BRASS Bot (Ke & Ng, 2025) is deployed on the poe.com platform, which currently requires payment (e.g., for access to well-known models or to send more than 100 messages daily).

Second, we present a systematic, large-scale, and data-driven approach to evaluating any computational model for predicting human judgments of item/definition correspondence. We have analyzed 2,443 journal articles spanning eight disciplines to create a dataset encompassing 227,474 training instances, 26,648 validation instances, and 33,304 test instances to enable a comprehensive evaluation. Our evaluation data are available to benchmark future models that predict item/definition correspondence. Our large-scale evaluation approach overcomes a key limitation of traditional manual content validation methods, which have typically restricted comparisons to a narrow subset of definitions (i.e., the intended construct and two orbiting constructs). Consequently, our methodology could significantly enhance the rigor and reliability of content validity assessments.

Third, we provide the first computational approach to measuring the representativeness of an item set in the limited case of multidimensional constructs. Representativeness is central to content validity (Kerlinger, 1973; MacKenzie et al., 2011), but to the best of our knowledge, this core property has been ignored in existing computational research in this area. In this paper, we follow the canonical work of MacKenzie et al. (2011), who focus on the representativeness of multidimensional constructs. We discuss possible extensions in the section dealing with future research below.

Fourth, we conducted an applicability check assessment (Rosemann & Vessey, 2008), demonstrating that computational approaches to content validity are ready for application in real research settings. Applicability checks are frequently employed in design science research to demonstrate that new computational tools are relevant and valuable in practice.

Finally, RATER is open source, transparent, replicable, reliable, and follows documented methodological principles from psychometric research. As we describe in our literature review, this represents an improvement over commercial blackbox commercial NLP systems such as ChatGPT (Beghetto et al., 2025) for scientific research.

## Implications for Behavioral Research

We offer a public web interface at www.contval.org, which provides access to RATER and can be extended to other possible models in a modular fashion for predicting item/definition correspondence. This has several implications for behavioral research using psychometric scales.

First, our tool can provide early quantitative feedback to scale development researchers before they conduct expensive human subject experiments. This form of low-cost feedback can help researchers create better measurement instruments and is much better aligned with the iterative nature of content validation than the current H&T protocol. Researchers must invest in formal content validity assessment to learn whether items and definitions exhibit high correspondence and distinctiveness (Colquitt et al., 2019). Automated, rapid, and low-cost input from RATER early in the scale development process can help researchers quickly revise scales based on early feedback; this process could lead to high-quality measurement instruments, which are central to conducting sound empirical research (Cortina et al., 2020; Podsakoff et al., 2016). During our applicability check, participants explained that tools like RATER might help them improve items and definitions before engaging in the main data collection.

Second, our tool provides easy, automatic, and standardized access to best practices in content validation. This could promote the adoption and awareness of known best practices by providing fast and low-cost feedback on items’ correspondence, distinctiveness, and representativeness across a scale. At present, researchers sometimes skip the content validation process (Boudreau et al., 2001; Schmitz & Storey, 2020) or neglect established steps such as assessing the distinctiveness (Cortina et al., 2020; Schmitz & Storey, 2020) or representativeness of a scale (Cortina et al., 2020). Providing automatic and standardized tools should help researchers who do not have the resources to conduct a content validity assessment or who lack the background knowledge to assess content validity in their work.

Third, our work improves the transparency, replicability, and reliability of content validity assessment. We offer opensource tools based on transparent methodological principles that return consistent input-output pairs. These properties can help make content validation more transparent, controllable, and replicable. These qualities are essential in scale development (Heggestad et al., 2019) and management research (Aguinis et al., 2018) but are often overlooked in contemporary research (Flake & Fried, 2020).

## Limitations and Future Work

Our work has several limitations, which could be addressed in future research. The first limitation lies in the susceptibility of our model to surface linguistic features, such as complex grammatical and syntactic structures. This may be an important limitation given that considerable variations in the writing quality of survey items and conceptual definitions persist even among published studies that have gone through several rounds of refinement (Hardy & Ford, 2014; Wacker, 2004). To assess the influence of wording quality on model performance, we concatenated definitions with items to compute Flesch Reading Ease scores, which are a function of the number of words per sentence and the number of syllables per word (Flesch, 1948) for each of the 33,304 item/definition instances in the test data. The results showed that Flesch Reading Ease scores significantly predicted model correctness, χ2 (1) = 77.94, p < 0.001, and we observed the expected pattern, in which the model was less likely to be accurate when readability was lower (approaching 85%) vs. higher (exceeding 93%). In response to this limitation, we extended the RATER system to add a warning to each item row when the Flesch Reading Ease score for the combined item/definition pair was 25 or lower, but with an adjustable threshold. This approach operationalizes MacKenzie et al.’s (2011) suggestion that researchers should ensure a high level of ease of readability before subjecting construct and item statements to content validation.

A second limitation pertains to the operationalization of the representativeness of psychometric scales. Our computational approach to assessing representativeness follows existing practices in the IS literature, which focus on multidimensional constructs (MacKenzie et al., 2011). Future research needs to focus on computational approaches to assessing representativeness for unidimensional scales. This is an open theoretical challenge that needs to go beyond addressing some issues that have been identified, such as identifying interitem redundancies, desirable conceptual redundancies, and grammatical or linguistic redundancies (DeVellis, 2017). Extensions of work that build on this conceptualization are promising (Alley et al., 2024). Further conceptualizations for assessing the representativeness of unidimensional scales that consider the capabilities of emerging LLMs, such as connecting latent semantic meanings of items to the content domain space that a construct is supposed to represent, are worth exploring. Such conceptualizations would tie back to traditional views on the role of domain sampling in generating items to ensure the representativeness of scales.

Finally, our work assumes that institutions such as companies and research labs will continue to release new open-weight language models that improve over time (e.g., in terms of inference speed, model size, and/or model capability). Given its modular design, other models for scale development or general-purpose use could be easily integrated into our system.

## Conclusion

This research developed RATER<sub>C</sub> and RATER<sub>D</sub>, AI-based models for assessing the content validity of psychometric scales. The models were developed to address known limitations in the current protocols for content validity assessment, including issues surrounding effort required, reproducibility, time, and cost. The series of evaluations reported above confirm that RATER can validly assess the correspondence, distinctiveness, and representativeness of scales, three key assessments involved in content validation. RATER offers researchers an additional tool (in addition to the current tools and resources) to develop high-quality instruments for measuring constructs.

RATER has been made available for free public use (www.contval.org). Since this free and easy-to-use tool addresses many of the barriers to conducting content validity assessments, we expect that the frequency of conducting and reporting content validity assessments will increase in the future, including among journal editors and reviewers who will be able to swiftly gain a sense of the content validity of a research instrument. Concomitantly, the quality of measurement instruments employed in future research is also likely to improve.

The development and validation of RATER shows the promise of using AI-based tools created specifically to support research activities. While RATER addresses only a subset of tasks involved in measure development, it demonstrates the need for future research to develop AIbased tools to support other tasks in the construct and instrument development process.
