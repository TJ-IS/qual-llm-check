# REGULATING AI: LESSONS FROM SCIENTIFIC COMPUTING<sup>1</sup>

Jonathan Wareham, Angelo Kenneth Romasanta, and Laia Pujol Priego\*

Ramon Llull University, ESADE Business School, Barcelona, SPAIN

{jonathan.wareham@esade.edu} {angelokenneth.romasanta@esade.edu}

David Osimo

The Lisbon Council, Brussels, BELGIUM

{david.osimo@lisboncouncil.net}

Motivated by the perceived importance of explainability to AI regulation, this paper examines the complex relationship between theory, as a proxy for explainability, and computation in scientific practice. Drawing from both historical and contemporary examples of scientific computing, we make three key arguments: First, we show that theory-computation relationships exist in diverse configurations, ranging from theory-foregrounded and theory-backgrounded to more nuanced arrangements where theory is selectively integrated. Second, scientific fields strategically manage the inherent opacity of some computational methods by injecting theory at critical junctures, showing that complete “explainability” is not always a prerequisite for scientific progress or utility. Third, we argue that these lessons from scientific computing have important implications for AI regulation anchored in explainable AI (XAI). We suggest that general approaches to AI oversight should be complemented with a context-specific focus on how theory and computation interact in different domains. Implications for AI regulatory regimes and Information Systems (IS) research are discussed.

Keywords: Regulation, Explainable AI (XAI), Science, Scientific Computing

## Introduction

Debates over the regulation of artificial intelligence (AI) often converge on the basic premise that AI systems must be explainable<sup>2</sup> to be effectively governed. Science has a great deal to say about explainability. Yet, as scientific disciplines have grown increasingly dependent on computers, the assorted history of how computation has interacted with theory—as its primary expression of explainability—offers some unexpected yet compelling insights useful for AI policy. Appreciating this, however, requires an understanding of how science works.

There is a common misconception that natural scientists gather data about the phenomena they study. The misconception arises from the fact that “data” do not exist in nature. Energy, however, does exist—abundantly. Thus, what empirical researchers in the sciences do is capture signals that convey forms of energy. Such energies can be endogenous (e.g., light from stars) or, alternatively, if the subject emits insufficient energy to generate a useful signal, the scientist can guide exogenous energy sources (e.g., X-rays, electrons) to probe the phenomenon with the expectation of a signal in return. However, what that “signal” comes to signify depends on numerous factors: the nature of the energy (acoustic, magnetic, photonic); the medium the signal passes through (air, water, solid material); how the energy interacts with the object (absorbed, reflected, diffracted); the instruments and techniques used to capture the signal (microphones, calorimeters, scintillators); and, most importantly, the theoretical apparatus within which the research is situated. The combination of these elements determines how the raw inputs of science—signals—are collected, cleaned, converted, computed, and, finally, constructed into data. This higher-order practice of assembling and interpreting scientific evidence is not a mere afterthought: It constitutes the bulk of scientific labor and is what makes science so challenging. To help with this heavy lifting, the processing and interpretation of signals are now completed with a sophisticated array of computational tools, so much so that computation is now a fundamental pillar of the scientific enterprise.<sup>3</sup>

Computation’s increasingly central role in modern science was acknowledged in 2024, with the Nobel Prizes in both Physics and Chemistry being awarded for AI applications. Although the Nobel Prize has historically honored theoretical contributions or empirical techniques<sup>4</sup> that advance the understanding of how natural systems function, it had never been awarded for an exclusively computational technique. More remarkably, both 2024 accolades are essentially void of any theoretical ontology. Given the preeminent status of theory throughout most scientific history, these unprecedented honors provoke uncomfortable questions: How do we evaluate scientific breakthroughs based on computational techniques without clear causal models (Krenn et al., 2022)? Do they constitute real scientific understanding (Karniadakis et al., 2021)?

These questions extend far beyond scientific discovery, directly confronting decision-makers and regulators across domains where public safety and environmental protection are critical (Ebert-Uphoff & Hilburn, 2023; Haupt et al., 2021; Bracco et al., 2024). What if climate scientists use atheoretical models to dictate shipping patterns, air traffic, or pollution guidelines? What happens if doctors use AI models to recommend or perform treatments with scant understanding of the underlying mechanisms? Should we, as citizens, accept their consequences? Can regulators still trust them? Does explainable science need explainable AI (XSc = XAI)? Yet apprehensions of theory-free computational techniques now extend well beyond scientifically-intensive regulatory regimes: The inexplicability of many AI systems has provoked fears of unexplainable behaviors (Lebovitz et al., 2021; Berente et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2021); unforeseen consequences (Bubeck et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2022); detrimental bias (Graziani et al. 2023; Nishant et al., 2024); misalignment with ethics and public welfare (Bauer et al., 2023; Dwivedi et al., 2023; Meske et al., 2022); and geopolitical or existential threats (Bostrom, 2017; Bostrom et al., 2020). This suggests that AI regulatory debates must grapple with both extant regulatory regimes embracing AI, as well as nascent AI phenomena without regulatory precedence.

Emergent concerns of inexplicable AI have prompted divergent regulatory responses. The European Union has embraced a precautionary stance with its comprehensive AI Act. The Act asserts that understanding the inner workings of an AI system is essential for safety, with safeguards designed proportionally with perceived risks. Explainable AI (XAI) has, in turn, become the North Star for many of its regulatory initiatives (European Commission, 2024; NIST, 2024). In contrast, in early 2025, the United States began to focus more on an outcome approach, addressing potential harms that may surface once models are deployed (The White House, 2025).

The regulatory aspiration of full AI explainability is understandable, given the risks. But it tends to present explainability as a dichotomy: either showcased in a well-lit display cabinet, concrete and scrutable, or ensconced in a black box, vaporized into a deep neural net. Such oversimplifications, however, miss the nuanced relationship between understanding, prediction, and trust that has long existed in domains that rely heavily on computational methods.

Fortunately, scientific history offers valuable insights for managing this complexity. While scientific computation<sup>5</sup> is now used in almost all aspects of scientific work, contrary to expectations, the relationship between the scientific enterprise and computing has not always been comfortable or uncontentious. Surprisingly, in the early days of computation, theory-minded members of some scientific communities viewed the computer as a glorified slide rule, a rudimentary numerical hack that had no place alongside the aesthetic refinement of formal mathematics and theory (Hammersley & Morton, 1954). Intriguingly, this equivocal relationship between theory and computation continues today (Mitchell, 2023; Mitchell & Krakauer, 2023). Nevertheless, science has learned to work with atheoretical computational methods, developing sophisticated practices for managing partially understood processes. Building on this experience, we make our central argument: the theory-no theory dichotomy evident in AI discourse is an immense simplification that is inadequate for guiding policy discussions. The relationship between theory and scientific computing assumes numerous configurations depending on purpose and context. A more sophisticated understanding of the theory-computation relationship can inform debates on AI regulation in both incumbent and nascent regimes.

The convergence of science, regulation, and computation provides critical insights for both extant regulatory regimes embracing AI and broader debates on nascent AI. First, AI regulation should not be treated as a distinct challenge, but rather should be integrated into broader regulatory thought. This shifts the conversation from how to regulate AI as an isolated phenomenon to the broader scope of how all regulatory processes are best designed as AI evolves and permeates them. Second, we argue that regulatory processes, like science, are neither homogeneous nor monofunctional. Regulation is often multistage or hierarchically structured. Hence, for effective regulation, it is important to consider both when theoretical convergence is essential and, likewise, when it is acceptable for AI to diverge from theoretical grounding for more tentative or generative results. Third, this compositional view shifts the regulatory focus from the entire system to specific components, defining control points with purposeful theoretical corroboration that allows for a tailored governance strategy appropriate for the risk, character, and interactions across subprocesses. Together, these insights suggest that effective AI governance requires accommodating the diverse ways theory and computation interact across different domains, application phases, and risks.

To elaborate this argument, this Issues & Opinions piece explores several interconnected themes: the evolving role of computing in science, theory as a proxy of explainability, and the implications for AI regulation. We begin by tracing the assorted history of scientific computation within the post-WWII physics landscape, focusing on the emergence of Monte Carlo simulation (Galison, 2011, 2016). We then analyze recent examples from high-energy physics, computational chemistry, structural biology, and climate science to illustrate distinct modalities of computation-theory interaction. We use insights from these experiences to inform how to balance innovation with appropriate oversight in both established domains adopting AI and emerging areas of AI governance. We conclude by examining implications for information systems research in understanding and governing sociotechnical AI systems.

## Computation in Science: The Early Debates

Scientific inquiry has been shaped by two major technological transformations in the past century. The first was the embrace of big machines and sophisticated instruments, exemplified by the cyclotron developed in the 1930s at the University of California, Berkeley (Hiltzik, 2015). The second major evolution was the advent of computing, whose early developments were driven by the military needs of WWII.

Famous examples include Bletchley Park, where Alan Turing and colleagues conducted cryptography (Grey & Sturdy, 2010), and the ENIAC computer (Haigh et al., 2018), which was designed to calculate artillery trajectories.

A pivotal shift foreshadowing today’s AI challenges was the postwar development of Monte Carlo techniques by John von Neumann, Stanislaw Ulam, Nicholas Metropolis, and others,<sup>6</sup> who were seeking to better understand the physics of a hydrogen bomb. They needed to model the diffusion of neutrons through plutonium to calculate the probability of a nuclear fusion reaction (Galison, 2011). The Monte Carlo simulation, a reference to the random outcomes of roulette wheels, uses pseudorandom number generation to simulate the inherent stochasticity of energy propagating through fissile material. Neutrons could scatter from a nucleus, be absorbed by a nucleus, or cause a nucleus to undergo fission, emitting an unknown quantity of neutrons. As none of these probabilities were calculable via traditional techniques, von Neumann and colleagues refined the Monte Carlo methods for machine computation. They produced the first simulations of nuclear fusion using over a million IBM punch cards with the ENIAC computer at the University of Pennsylvania. Importantly, this new technique was not simply a faster way of solving existing calculations; it represented a fundamentally different way of doing science. The growth of computer simulations in the 1950s defied the traditional scientific dichotomy of theorist versus experimentalist, giving rise to terms such as “experimental mathematics” and “theoretical experiments” (Galison, 2011), referring to a mode of numerical, almost mechanized analysis of physical systems alien to the norms of both empiricists and theoreticians.

Unsurprisingly, this approach was met with considerable skepticism, considered vastly subordinate to the refined world of equations that dominated the Einstein-Dirac apparatus of theoretical physicists (Hammersley & Morton, 1954). It threatened to replace “real,” theoretical science with “low-brow” tasks of computation and numerical approximations (Kowarski, 1972a). Furthermore, legitimate concerns arose regarding replicability and inscrutability, qualities that were otherwise clearly accessible in disciplined mathematical deduction. The computer programs were often too large to be publishable, and the peculiarities of computing environments limited the replicability of simulations (Galison, 2011). Theorists regarded the computer as a pedestrian tool and even discouraged its use whenever possible (Hammersley & Morton, 1954). A deeper metaphysical debate emerged about how computers functioned; that is, what was the most appropriate language to describe “scientific truth”? Can brute computation substitute for the Platonic truth described by elegant differential equations?

Eventually, however, some of Monte Carlo’s staunchest critics recognized that despite (and because of) its atheoretical approach, its simulations could become a new source of insight (Tukey, 1972), particularly in fields rife with stochastic processes, such as chemistry or engineering. These scientists eventually embraced computation as an approach that was equal, not subordinate, to purely mathematical theorizing (Galison, 2016). As this adoption grew, many scientists began to assert that “stochasticism” (Galison, 2011, p. 146) was the optimal language to describe the numerous random processes that occur in nature (Kowarski, 1972b). Pundits of this new computational apparatus viewed elaborate mathematics as formalisms that obfuscate a more direct epistemic engagement with nature’s stochastic character (Galison, 2011).

Crucially, Monte Carlo’s value began to be appreciated not just in terms of its predictions but in the subtle hints it provided that stimulated new ways of viewing a problem. It encouraged, or even forced, scientists to rethink, reimagine, and reassemble problematizations. Paradoxically, liberation from extant theory became an asset: Not only did Monte Carlo enable more useful representations and predictions of stochastic processes, but it eventually nourished novel theory development. Computation slowly solidified its status to become a rival, more useful language to describe randomness; an alternative scientific vocabulary with its own ontological value (Galison, 2011).

Although the history of Monte Carlo simulations presages the concerns of today’s AI by some 70 years (Birhane et al., 2023), like AI, these simulations were suspiciously viewed as inexplicable and highly sensitive to their deployed architectures (Galison, 2016). Skeptics were also uncomfortable with the pseudorandom nature of these black-box simulations, deriding their lack of any clear causal logic or “world models” (Mitchell, 2023). Nonetheless, the scientific community learned to work with its atheoretical nature to address previously intractable problems and ultimately fuse it with scientific understanding.

## Three Current Tales of Scientific Computing

Scientific instruments are traditionally assumed to agnostically detect or measure concepts, but not to embody or define them (van Helden & Hankins, 1994). However, many historians of science consider this perspective naive (Daston & Galison, 2021; Galison, 2008; García-Sancho et al., 2014). The ensemble of detection, imaging, and computational instruments does not simply record phenomena, but actively shapes them, conditioning signals to be integrated into larger theoretical explanations of the phenomena examined. Hence, theory, instrumentation, and computation become tightly coupled in the machinery of data, evidence, and scientific knowledge production (Hacking, 1992). The 2024 Nobel Prizes clearly demonstrate this special status of computation in science.

When referring to theory, we consider it broadly as the applied conceptual apparatus that explains the functioning of certain aspects of the natural world by describing the regularities inherent in objects and events. Although a comprehensive definition is well beyond the scope of this essay, we note that theoretical explanations are a product of many things, including human imagination and mathematical deduction, observation, and experiment. For our purposes, it is important to recognize that science uses theory as a language to understand and predict the behavior of the systems it studies— sometimes referred to as “world models” in AI literature (Mitchell, 2023). In simple terms, theory is about explaining things with transparent, principle-based, or causal logic that is codifiable, scrutable, refutable, and ultimately useful.

In the following, we present three cases showing various configurations between theory and computation. Theory can be foregrounded, thereby defining how data are generated and interpreted. Conversely, theory can be backgrounded, absent in the algorithmic logic and only called upon to interpret the findings. Finally, the relationship between theory and computation can also be more interwoven and multidirectional (Karniadakis et al., 2021; Lavin et al., 2022).

## Theory in the Foreground: CERN

Our first illustration focuses on the Large Hadron Collider (LHC) at the European Organization for Nuclear Research (CERN), widely recognized as the world’s most powerful infrastructure for high-energy physics research. CERN’s application of scientific computing has been driven by efforts to refute and validate the dominant theory—the Standard Model. To test this theory’s predictions, physicists developed ATLAS,<sup>7</sup> the largest detector ever constructed for a particle collider, measuring 46 meters in length and 25 meters in diameter. The ATLAS and CMS detectors<sup>8</sup> were designed to record over a billion particle collisions per second. On July 4, 2012, ATLAS and CMS announced that they had independently detected a new particle consistent with the predicted attributes of the Higgs boson (Brumfiel, 2012), an elementary particle that gives mass to everything in the natural world (Azhari et al., 2020). Corroborating the Higgs boson posed a major challenge since it cannot be observed directly: It is highly unstable and decays into other particles almost immediately (Brumfiel, 2012),<sup>9</sup> rendering it impossible to detect by any straightforward method. To accommodate this challenge, theoretical physicists predicted the possible decay modes and the probability of each mode occurring. Experimentalists then detect these decay products and match their occurrence with theoreticians’ predictions.

Computation, fully guided by theory, plays a crucial role in this process. Complex algorithms, developed to simulate millions of particle collisions and their possible decay pathways, offer physicists a benchmark for comparing data from LHC’s detectors with theoretical predictions. Theory dictates what to look for, where and how to look for it, and how to interpret it. Without theory embodied in the extensive computation, the massive data generated at the LHC are void of any evidential value or ontological meaning. In this configuration, theory permeates every aspect of the computational workflow.

## Theory in the Background: Power Storage

Our second illustration comes from the application of generative computational methods in materials synthesis, which has resulted in the discovery of new battery materials with improved energy storage capacity, linearity, and longevity (Attia et al., 2020). A landmark example in this area is work from Stanford University, MIT, and Toyota Research Institute, predicting the attributes of thousands of new materials for lithium-ion battery cathodes.<sup>10</sup> This collaboration predicted new types of solid-state battery electrolytes, promising to revolutionize electric vehicles by offering increased energy storage and safety (Attia et al., 2020).

Traditionally, the discovery of new materials has involved time-consuming and resource-intensive trial-and-error approaches. Recent computational models have enabled scientists to quickly generate novel, hypothetical materials with desired properties based on extensive training data about the composition, structure, and attributes of known materials (Carrete et al., 2014). To develop these tools, scientists first define the desired attributes of the materials (e.g., higher storage capacity, greater stability, faster charging). Then, based on databases from literature and laboratory measurements on existing materials (Jain et al., 2013), scientists can develop computational models that extract relationships between a material’s composition and its properties (Schmidt et al., 2019). These models can then generate new hypothetical structures based on the characteristics sought. In the final step, scientists need to verify the feasibility of synthesizing the materials and later determine experimentally whether the materials truly conform to the predicted properties (Finegan et al., 2021).

In this example, the role of theory is rather implicit, as computation does not start with a formal theory of how different elements combine to form a material with certain attributes. Instead, the model learns patterns from data about existing materials and then uses these patterns to predict new materials.<sup>11</sup> In this configuration, theory is not embedded within computation itself; it becomes relevant primarily for scientists to validate the outputs from computation.

## Theory on Demand: AlphaFold

Our third example is provided by DeepMind, a subsidiary of Alphabet (Google). In 2018, DeepMind introduced AlphaFold to the protein science field as a tool to predict protein structures with an unprecedented degree of accuracy (Jumper et al., 2021). As the building blocks of life, proteins fulfill various functions, including forming the structural components of cells, killing foreign particles, and transporting necessary molecules throughout the body. As the 3D shape of a protein ultimately dictates its function, specifying these structures is critical to understanding biology and discovering new pharmaceuticals.<sup>12</sup> Determining their structures empirically, however, is challenging, taking years even for just one protein. Experimental techniques such as X-ray crystallography, nuclear magnetic resonance, and cryogenic electron microscopy can only work for some proteins under certain conditions.<sup>13</sup> While physics-based computational methods offer an alternative, they have struggled with accuracy due to the astronomical number of possible molecular bonds amino acids can form in 3D space.<sup>14</sup> It was thus a huge breakthrough when AlphaFold demonstrated its ability to predict more than 200 million protein structures with high levels of accuracy.<sup>15</sup>

## When Theory Is Inserted Punctually

While AlphaFold succeeds in the relevant metrics for structure prediction,<sup>16</sup> it offers no insight into how proteins fold in nature! The deep learning model’s mechanism is theory-free until the final phase, where the predicted structure is examined for violations against known laws of chemistry and physics. Researchers in computational science refer to this as physicsinformed AI (Karniadakis et al., 2021), where the term broadly references any theoretical framework to constrain the model without defining the algorithmic mechanism (Lavin et al., 2022). In fact, when a team of researchers attempted to modify AlphaFold by introducing theoretical constraints (e.g., legitimate chemical bonds) earlier in the model, they found that it led to worse performance in its predictions (Ahdritz et al., 2022; Tan & Zhang, 2023).

## When Computation Informs Theory

In the same manner that theory can unidirectionally “inform” specific points, AI can reciprocate, such that the intermediate and final outcomes of atheoretical computation can enrich theory. This bidirectional transaction between theory and computation is referred to as physics-infused AI (Karniadakis et al., 2021; Lavin et al., 2022). For instance, scientists are particularly interested in protein dynamics—the ways proteins move and change shape to perform their functions as carriers or pumps. Membrane proteins that transport molecules inside and outside of cell walls by changing shapes are of significant medical interest. However, such dynamics are largely impossible to detect when a protein is in its static, crystallized form. Surprisingly, AlphaFold can help predict these dynamics, indicating regions of flexibility on a protein surface that facilitate conformational changes (Guo et al., 2022). In these instances, AlphaFold has been repurposed to facilitate theoretical insights that were unexpected byproducts of its main purpose of global structure prediction.

## When Computation Informs Empiricism

Traditional experimental methods for structure elucidation are notoriously difficult, yet surprisingly, AlphaFold has proven useful for them. In X-ray crystallography, the most widely used experimental method, a biologist attempting to identify the structure of a protein (e.g., from a coronavirus) would attempt to generate many samples of that protein in crystal form. On the extremely lucky chance that the protein crystallizes, this sample can then be sent to a synchrotron radiation facility where it can be exposed to powerful X-ray beams. The X-ray emitted photons diffract from the electrons of the protein’s atoms, producing a 2D scattered image of dots called a diffraction pattern. By combining thousands of diffraction patterns captured from various angles, analysts can then eventually derive the atomic structure. However, as the diffraction patterns produce notable phase interference, solving this “phase problem” is prickly and time-consuming.

With AlphaFold, however, scientists can wield it to generate basic models of the protein (i.e., a homologue). By using this predicted structure as a starting model to resolve phase interference, scientists can go back and forth, cycling between AlphaFold predictions and empirical validation. This workflow accelerates the process and makes the final output of higher quality (Read et al., 2023). The value of AlphaFold in this instance is neither final structure predictions nor the replacement of empirical validation. Rather, it is its application of intermediate outputs to solve substantial empirical challenges and accelerate traditional research.

## Configurations of Theory-Computing Relationships

These three cases across science illustrate the various roles theory plays with respect to computation. At the LHC, theory is foregrounded, permeating everything: The experimental design, instrumentation, data, and computation are all fully defined by theory. Independent of theory, computational predictions and data have no semantic value. In the power storage example above, theory is far more detached and backgrounded. Generative AI is trained on known materials to propose novel structures. Here, theory is absent from the algorithm, backgrounded, and only considered in the subsequent interpretation and validation of the results. AlphaFold uses theory more discriminately—only summoned at the final stage to ensure predictions comply with known chemical laws. Adapting extant literature (Karniadakis et al., 2021; Lavin et al., 2022), we call this unidirectional insertion of theory “theory-informed.” Adapting AlphaFold into workflows has led to several unexpected outcomes. AlphaFold predictions, regardless of their accuracy, have led to theoretical enrichment beyond the initial purview of single protein structure prediction. Extending the literature (Karniadakis et al., 2021; Lavin et al., 2022), we call this bidirectional relationship “theory-infused.” Additionally, the intermediate outcomes of the AlphaFold algorithm have accelerated traditional empirical research. ## Configurations Evolve

These configurations are not static but can rapidly evolve in response to theoretical evolution, technological improvements, higher-quality data, and other factors. Climate science provides a compelling illustration of this evolution. Historically, climate modeling could be characterized as theory-foregrounded. The seminal work in physics-based climate modeling was Richardson’s (1922) Weather Prediction by Numerical Process, which proposed what was then a new way to forecast the weather using differential equations. The first large-scale computerized calculations were conducted by a group led by von Neumann and Charney in the 1950s on the ENIAC. The 2D model required 24 hours of computing to produce a 24-hour forecast with mixed accuracy (Carbon Brief, 2018). For most of climate science history, physics-based models have held primacy due to their interpretability and strength in long-term projections, leading to greater legitimacy with policymakers and regulators.

However, physics-based climate models have limitations. They can be computationally demanding, as resource requirements increase geometrically with accuracy needs. Additionally, physics-based models do not easily generalize from one geographic region to another. Consequently, from 2010 onwards, climate scientists began to explore how theory-free machine learning<sup>17</sup> (ML) can address the limitations in physics models (Haupt et al., 2021; Lewis et al., 2024). Of note, climate science has some of the richest, most highly curated data sets in existence since the founding of the World Meteorological Organization in the 1950s, making it ideal for ML.

Initially, ML was used in a theory-informed capacity. For example, it was used to rapidly parameterize physics-based models for new regions or accommodate extreme events such as hurricanes or volcanic eruptions (Molina et al., 2023). It has been used for real-time refinement of predictions, given the large computational demands of physics-based models.

More remarkably, climate scientists have recently begun to use ML to extract physical laws (i.e. equations) directly from data (Bracco et al., 2024). This theory-infused approach scours data to unmask poorly understood physics (Seyyedi et al., 2024). Some predictions suggest that physics-free ML techniques will soon surpass physics-based models due to lower computational costs, greater reliability, and better representations of lower-scale climate processes. Given the primacy of physical models in the discipline, this transition is considered nothing less than profound, if not existentially unsettling to the profession. Yet as of today, growing acceptance in the discipline supports the view that theory-based and theory-free computation are highly complementary (Bracco et al., 2024; Ebert-Uphoff & Hilburn, 2023).

The profound impact of climate science in numerous regulatory spaces, such as environmental protection, energy infrastructure planning, public health, urban planning, agriculture, air traffic, shipping, and automation (NOAA, 2020), reinforces the need for nuanced AI oversight, as experts gain an increasingly refined understanding of the relative strengths and risks of these hybrid approaches. Yet what specific principles should guide a regulatory approach that accommodates a context-appropriate mix of theory-based and theory-agnostic computation?

## Regulatory Responses

## Incumbent Regulation

Despite the frequent framing of AI as a relatively nascent and independent phenomenon, many specialized domains already espouse regulatory frameworks that incorporate empirical evidence with theory-based and theory-free computations. Medical regulatory bodies in both the United States (FDA) and European Union (EMA) have adopted pragmatic guidelines for where and how AI models can be submitted as evidence in drug development, dependent on perceived risk levels (EMA, 2024; FDA, 2025). When AI-based evidence is submitted, general technical documentation on data training, validation and bias elimination, and model development and architecture are required. Notably, they do not categorically exclude atheoretical AI models, allowing their use when more explicit transparent models are unsatisfactory and the benefits can be substantiated.

Due to the relatively early phase of adoption, both FDA and EMA guidelines are currently high-level, accommodating the broad range of potential applications and risk levels across drug development processes (Niazi, 2023).<sup>18</sup> Regulators recognize that the appropriate scope of AI-based evidence will vary across specific regulatory phases, disease severities, and treatment modalities. For example, where upstream processes such as target identification and molecule research can utilize theoretically divergent and generative models, downstream processes that directly impact public safety, such as toxicity and efficacy evaluation, require theoretically convergent models. In other terms, the evaluation of AI-based evidence in medical regulations will be granular, specific to regulatory subprocesses and disease categories.

Similarly, the American Society of Mechanical Engineers (ASME), which focuses on medical devices such as implants, heart valves, blood pumps, and diagnostic equipment, pioneered the field of AI evaluation against real-world physics models (ASME, 2018). They offer guidelines for accepting theory-free models, but require validation by physics-based models with documentation of the governing equations, inputs, ranges, uncertainties, training data, and comparator test conditions, as well as appraisal with empirical results (ASME, 2018). The ASME V&V40 standard is a pragmatic and flexible framework oriented toward breaking computational evidence into the most important subprocesses and assuring validity at purposeful control points. Across the ASME, FDA, and EMA, there is a recognition that AI use will increase in both quantity and sophistication, and regulatory regimes should reflect the nascency of the phenomenon with constant reassessment (Aldieri et al., 2023; Niazi, 2023; Viceconti et al., 2021).

## Nascent AI Regulation

Most regulatory frameworks struggle to reconcile competing priorities, such as mitigating potential harms while not excessively constraining innovation or economic prosperity. For general-purpose AI, the United States and the European Union are taking divergent approaches (Delcker, 2025). On one end, the European Union’s AI Act, adopted in 2024 with a timeline for full implementation in 2031 (European Commission, 2024), emphasizes ex ante requisites for explainability, with strong guardrails for systems applied in critical domains affecting citizens’ fundamental rights, such as biometrics, critical infrastructure, employment, law enforcement, and migration (Council of the European Union, 2020). One focal concern is that the lack of transparency could lead to a loss of human control: “human oversight and transparency are an essential element in ensuring that AI systems are in conformity with the relevant legislation” (Council of the European Union, 2020, p. 8). Hence, the EU AI Act envisages an extensive set of preemptive measures, including adequate risk assessment and mitigation; quality of the datasets; logging to ensure traceability; adequate information to the user; appropriate human oversight; and high levels of robustness, security, and accuracy. While it does not explicitly require AI systems to be built on a formal theoretical foundation, the requirements push developers to articulate how their models work ex ante, well before the AI solution is publicly deployed. Appropriately, the EU AI Act is based on a hierarchical philosophy of proportional regulation from proportional harm, distinguishing harm risk across unacceptable, high, limited, minimal, and systemic levels (Council of the European Union, 2020).

On the other end, the United States’ treatment of AI is more fragmented and ex post. The Trump administration was quick to revoke previous regulatory executive orders, taking a far more liberalized stance to minimize oversight and promote innovation. The U.S. approach can be broadly described as outcomes-focused, addressing the potential harms of these models through extant federal and state legal frameworks and sector-specific regulations (Gracias, 2024). Consequently, the U.S. federal administration currently shows little interest in requirements that illuminate the models’ internal workings. Rather, the regulatory ethos is grounded in legal liability, responding to any harms as they emerge.

Each strategy has relative strengths. The EU strategy, with its focus on preemptive evaluation, is likely best suited for singleuse deployments that are relatively static, narrowly scoped, and often implemented in public administration where monitoring is feasible. The US emphasis on ex post liability is arguably a less prudent but likely more pragmatic approach towards the multiuse nature of generative AI models, particularly in the private sector, where the boundary conditions of use evolve rapidly.

## Discussion

The central argument of this essay is that the theory-no theory dichotomy permeating much policy debate, as well as the associated principle of explainability, is overly simplified. Theory-intensive and theory-free computation are often combined in numerous, complementary configurations. A deeper appreciation of this subtlety can be beneficial to AI policy debates where XAI is a fundamental ideal. Accordingly, we structure our discussion around three core themes: (1) the heterogeneous configurations of theory-computation relationships, (2) how science manages and embraces the lack of explainability, and (3) the implications of these practices for AI regulations.

## Different Fields, Different Goals, Different Configurations

In scientific practice, the relationship between theory and computation is far from binary. It spans a continuum where computational methods can be entirely theory-driven, largely atheoretical, or anywhere in between. At CERN, refinement and corroboration of a mature theoretical framework, the Standard Model, dictates all experimental design and data interpretation. In contrast, battery science employs generative AI models that rely on pattern recognition to expand the boundaries of the problem space. Meanwhile, AlphaFold exemplifies a hybrid configuration where its deep learning architecture operates largely without theoretical guidance until the final stages. Its liberation from extant theory not only enables more accurate predictions but also generates theoryinfused and empirics-infused outcomes that, unexpectedly, inform both theory development and experimental workflows (Read et al., 2023).

Scientific historians have long noted that theories often have their greatest utility when they are partial and incomplete, as this is where question-generating and question-answering cycles are most generative (Canguilhem, 1988; Rheinberger, 1997). What may be unique to recent advances in scientific computation is the relative speed, as faster cycles of divergence and convergence amplify the normal flux of generative and confirmatory science. As atheoretical models become more prominent in this dynamic, they prompt us to reconsider the assumption that complete transparency is always essential. Unexpected perspectives, methods, and other computational byproducts of atheoretical computing can be both epistemologically useful and ontologically legitimate. Rather than dismissing them outright, our examples show that ontologically agnostic algorithms in AI deserve more nuanced consideration.

## Managing the Inexplicable

A “scientific understanding” of a phenomenon can take many forms. Each discipline espouses different norms or epistemic virtues (Cetina, 2009), that dictate what counts as legitimate scientific knowledge. This can range from the aesthetics of mathematical equations to the richness of verbal representations, to predictive accuracy, and to the malleability or repurposability of a theoretical apparatus. Legitimate scientific knowledge continually evolves, and new tools and methods demonstrate their virtue as a field grows, morphs, and matures (Daston & Galison, 2021). Hence, the canvas of science is strewn with ontologically centric and ontologically indifferent techniques, rendering an imperfect-yet-useful bricolage of evidence around which consensus might eventually form (Galison, 2008). In many respects, such processes are germane to all human understanding— incomplete, fragmented, and nurtured through sensible prodding of the unknown (Hickman, 2007). As with medicine, epidemiology, or climate science, notions of understandability may be partial and incomplete; not all theories have qualitative characteristics that speak to human intuition, any more than complex programming architectures do (de Regt & Dieks, 2005; Lavin et al., 2022).

To understand why different scientific domains successfully integrate opaque computational methods, it is useful to distinguish between explainable AI (XAI) and what we term explainable science (XSc). XAI focuses on the ability of an algorithm’s developers to articulate its decision-making logic, with or without theory. Stated differently, AI can be explainable yet lack any correspondence to a recognized theory. XSc concerns broader transparency and reproducibility norms as scientific understanding accrues through theoretical, evidential, or computational insights. The crucial insight here is that the goal is not to render the entire computational process scrutable, but to ensure that the overall scientific workflow remains trustworthy. This does not mean abandoning theoretical rigor. Rather, it involves safeguards that are carefully negotiated and calibrated as the scientific process evolves. As we have described, in exploratory settings (e.g., materials discovery), theoretically agnostic AI could be tolerated or even useful when it is augmented with sufficient theoretical safeguards; that is, where theory is inserted at strategic points to anchor theory-agnostic outputs to nurture the best scientific understanding (Mainzer, 2007).

## Insights for AI Regulation

While our preceding reflection may imply that we appeal for a more liberal policy toward AI regulation, this is not the case. What we do argue is that the issue is more subtle than often construed. A policy premised on a naive, idealistic notion of XAI can be tempered by acknowledging that all models have strengths and weaknesses, and a model’s degree of inscrutability constitutes just one dimension of a more complex risk-benefit calculus. The assumption that explicability equals understanding is often an idealized ambition at best. Even science frequently diverges from such naive aspirations, given that opaque mechanisms and scant evidence of how outcomes are derived leaves them partially unintelligible even to scientists themselves. Rather than viewing this as a failure, science has developed sophisticated practices for managing and validating outputs from incompletely understood (computational) processes. Hence, policy can learn from how science has pragmatically managed diverse theory-computation configurations. In Table 2, we summarize how these configurations could lead to more targeted and effective regulatory pathways that move beyond one-size-fits-all approaches, specifying the scope of risks, appropriate governance mechanisms, and suitable application contexts.

This approach helps resolve a central tension in current regulatory debates: the balance between ex ante and ex post governance, embraced by the EU and the US, respectively. While many discriminant AI models are static, single-use deployments, and thus more amenable to ex ante evaluation of their design, newer applications are increasingly based on generative foundational models that are innately dynamic, multi-use, and frequently retrained and redeployed. These forms of deep neural networks can often be excessively complex, adaptive, and self-learning, making ex ante, onceand-done, explainable-by-design governance verge on the impractical (Bennetot et al., 2025; NIST, 2024; Bubeck et al., 2023). In these cases, AI is perhaps more appropriately conceived as a malleable service whose delivery and use evolve in tandem with the application space (Lavin et al., 2022; OECD, 2021). Even rigorous and mature approval regimes, such as those in medicine, have extensive postapproval safety monitoring in place, acknowledging that it is impossible to anticipate all risks in advance. As AI has a related set of transparency and safety challenges, we argue that ex ante and ex post regulatory mechanisms need to be balanced and coordinated. This further suggests a need for pragmatic techniques for ex post compliance that go beyond mere reporting, yet combine purposeful control points, theoretical corroboration, and use case-specific auditing (Bennetot et al., 2025).

Furthermore, our configurations provide a vocabulary to manage AI in different operational contexts, from discovery settings to high-stakes consumer-facing applications. An AI producing a fluke prediction in a lab is less harmful than a flawed medical diagnosis: The former represents an exploratory process where unexpected outcomes can be an asset; in the latter, predictability is critical. To illustrate, consider an AI diagnostic tool in oncology. This single system can be analyzed as a composition of multiple configurations. It may combine a theory-foregrounded component for checking known drug contraindications (requiring ex ante validation) and a theory-backgrounded component for identifying novel patient-data correlations (requiring ex post monitoring). This compositional view shifts the regulatory focus from the entire system to specific components and their critical control points, allowing for a tailored governance strategy appropriate for the risk, character, and interactions across subprocesses.

Our foray through science, computing, and explainability brings to the surface some core topics that could enrich both AI policy and IS scholarship if made more explicit. Our framework provides a clear avenue for IS researchers, particularly in consumer-facing or service industries, where such hybrid systems are emerging without mature regulatory traditions. First, IS researchers can study how theoretical knowledge can complement theory-agnostic AI in consumer-facing or service industries—sociotechnical systems where regulatory guardrails are less common. Drawing on the field’s strength in technology governance, IS research could formulate implementable levers for post-implementation compliance to establish what constitutes “sufficient” theoretical understanding in unregulated domains being transformed by AI: When is theoretical convergence critical? How can purposeful control points and auditing regimes be enacted? Relatedly, when is it acceptable for AI to diverge from established theoretical paradigms for more exploratory or generative results? And how can such processes be combined in more complex, iterative cycles such as those in multistage or hierarchically structured regulatory processes? Finally, contexts where a lack of explainability could be a strategic asset—areas where systems can remain deliberately unconstrained by theory as a placeholder to permit the more generative exploration of a problem space—need further exploration. Metaphorically, this shifts research foci away from drawing accurate “maps,” to exploring “territories,” where the complex and incomprehensible is allowed to discover and construct its own theoretical value and ontological legitimacy.

## Conclusion

Science, computing, and theory have evolved through an assorted and subtle relationship that has been both contentious and beneficial throughout their history. The liaison between computation and scientific understanding is more nuanced and complex than often portrayed, and such diversity can inform regulatory debates in surprising ways. The atheoretical and inscrutable aspects of many AI deployments are not exclusively liabilities—novel modalities of exploring and simulating the universe, liberated from theoretical priors, offer numerous opportunities that should be acknowledged. Policy discussions on AI can benefit from applying the nuance and refinement represented throughout history to the current state of the art of scientific computing.

## About the Authors

Jonathan Wareham is a professor/catedrático of information systems at ESADE Business & Law Schools, Ramon Llull University, where he previously served as dean (faculty & research) and vice dean (research). His current research focuses on scientific computing, scientific policy, and how scientific technologies are influencing the business world. Wareham’s research has been published in journals and proceedings such as Organization Science, Decision Sciences, MIS Quarterly, Decision Support Systems, IEEE Transactions on Engineering Management, IEEE Computer, Communications of the ACM, and others. He holds a position as senior fellow at the Lisbon Council and serves as coeditor in-chief of CERN Innovation Journal.

Angelo Kenneth Romasanta is an assistant professor at Esade Business School. His research examines how scientific communities make sense of, adopt, and create value from frontier technologies like AI and data infrastructures. He has been actively involved in ATTRACT, a €55 million European initiative to spin off technologies from leading research institutes, including CERN, EMBL, and ESO. Informed by his background as a chemist, his work aims to bridge science with business to maximize the societal impact of emerging technologies.

Laia Pujol Priego was an assistant professor at ESADE Business & Law Schools, Ramon Llull University. She was also a research fellow at The Lisbon Council, a Brussels-based think tank. Laia’s research program focuses on digital innovation, in particular, the management implications of developing and commercializing emerging and science-based technologies, data commons infrastructures, and data practices. Her research has appeared in MIS Quarterly, Technovation, Government Information Quarterly, Industry and Innovation, Issues of Science and Technology, and Innovation: Organization & Management. She sadly passed away in 2023.

David Osimo is executive director and director of research at Lisbon Council. Mr. Osimo oversees the organisation’s strategic direction, leads the research activity, expands its impact, and ensures long-term financial sustainability. He leads the workstream on areas as diverse as the data economy, digital government, and the future of work, as well as pioneering novel collaborative methods for policy research. He previously served as a scientific officer in the European Commission Joint Research Centre and as a co-founder of two independent startups. He holds degrees from the universities of Milan and Cardiff.
