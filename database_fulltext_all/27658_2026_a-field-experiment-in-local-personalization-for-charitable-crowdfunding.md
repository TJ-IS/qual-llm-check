# A FIELD EXPERIMENT IN LOCAL PERSONALIZATION FOR CHARITABLE CROWDFUNDING<sup>1</sup>

Lauren Rhue Robert H. Smith School of Business, University of Maryland College Park, MD, U.S.A. {lrhue@umd.edu}

Atiya Avery Harbert College of Business, Auburn University Auburn, AL, U.S.A. {atiya.avery@auburn.edu}

Jessica Clark Robert H. Smith School of Business, University of Maryland College Park, MD, U.S.A. {jmclark@umd.edu}

Charitable crowdfunding platforms use personalized outreach to engage donors and increase contributions, often leveraging cognitive heuristics such as home bias (an inclination towards local projects) and social influence (an inclination towards personal connections). While both heuristics can shape giving behavior, they may not always align, and prior literature offers limited insight into how they interact or which dominates in practice. We address this gap through a large-scale randomized field experiment with nearly 160,000 donors on a major charitable crowdfunding platform. In this experiment, donors who had previously supported non-local projects were randomly assigned to receive one of two types of personalized emails: (1) local personalization, which highlighted projects in the donor’s billing location, or (2) default personalization, which highlighted projects from the same community as the donor’s most recently supported project. Results show that local personalization increased engagement and donations overall, even among donors without prior evidence of home bias, suggesting that geographic cues can activate latent heuristics. However, among donors with a history of giving via social influence, local personalization increased engagement but not the likelihood of a donation. Moreover, local personalization disproportionately directed funds toward affluent communities demographically similar to the donor’s own, reinforcing a rich-get-richer dynamic. These findings highlight the behavioral power and equity risks of simple personalization strategies, offering insights for platform design and the responsible use of heuristics in charitable crowdfunding.

Keywords: Crowdfunding, charitable crowdfunding, field experiment, geographic preferences, home bias, social influence, donation-based crowdfunding, personalization, rich-get-richer

## Introduction

Charitable crowdfunding platforms allow individuals to directly contribute to fundraisers addressing pressing social needs, such as classroom materials, health emergencies, or natural disaster relief (Burtch & Chan, 2019; Burtch et al., 2014). These platforms have grown rapidly (Burtch & Chan,

2019; Burtch et al., 2014), channeling billions of dollars in donations over the last two decades (Chan, 2017; Osili et al., 2021). Unlike other forms of crowdfunding (Mollick, 2014), charitable platforms operate with an explicit prosocial mission to facilitate giving that advances the public good (Burtch et al., 2014). Donors browse available projects and determine which, if any, to support (Belleflamme et al., 2014; Mollick, 2014; Huang et al., 2019; Lambrecht & Tucker, 2013).

Because donors have discretion over where their money goes, platforms’ prosocial missions can conflict with actual donation patterns. Donors may disproportionately support certain areas or types of projects, creating a misalignment between individual giving preferences and broader societal need (Xiao & Yue, 2021).

To navigate the large number of potential donation opportunities, donors often rely on heuristics: cognitive shortcuts that simplify decision-making (Kahneman, 2011). In the context of charitable crowdfunding, two common heuristics are home bias and social influence. Home bias refers to the tendency to support geographically local recipients (Lin & Viswanathan, 2016), while social influence refers to the tendency to support projects recommended by social contacts (e.g., Hur et al., 2023). These heuristics guide attention and action by evoking feelings of familiarity, obligation, or trust, without requiring systematic evaluation of all available options. While each heuristic has been individually studied, less is known about how they interact or compare. Prior research often assumes that social influence and home bias reinforce one another (Agrawal et al., 2015), but this alignment is not guaranteed. Donors may support geographically distant friends or fund local strangers. It remains unclear which heuristic is more influential in driving engagement and donations, especially when they point to different choices or are absent from a donor’s prior behavior.

This gap is relevant for platforms aiming to optimize donor outreach. Charitable crowdfunding platforms often personalize communications with potential donors to increase engagement, typically by inferring interests from past behavior. Yet when donors show no clear history of acting based on home bias or social influence, it is unclear which strategy is more effective or whether either applies. For example, in our study, most donors had not previously exhibited home bias in their giving. To guide its default personalization strategy, the platform used the common assumption that past donation patterns predict future preferences (Samuelson, 1948). Yet donors may still respond to a personalization approach designed to trigger the home bias heuristic, even in the absence of clear prior heuristic use by donors. The platform must decide how to approach its personalization, raising the question of which approach will most effectively motivate giving. Platforms must also balance the potential for a personalization approach designed to activate home bias to produce a “rich-get-richer” dynamic, channeling donations toward already advantaged areas and creating tension with the platform’s mission to help those in need.

We addressed the aforementioned gap by exploring three questions relevant to charitable crowdfunding platforms and a local personalization approach. First, how effective is local personalization for donors who have not previously supported local projects? Second, how does the effectiveness of local personalization differ for donors whose prior giving was due to social connections? Third, what are the broader implications of local personalization for donation patterns and the platform’s long-term charitable mission? To explore these questions, we conducted a large-scale randomized field experiment in partnership with the educational crowdfunding platform DonorsChoose. Nearly 160,000 prior donors were randomly assigned to receive personalized emails based either on their home location or the location of their previous donation. This design allowed us to isolate the effects of local personalization, particularly among donors without prior evidence of home bias.

Our findings show that local personalization significantly increased both donor engagement and donation rates, even among donors without prior evidence of home bias. However, we also found that such personalization was substantially less effective for donors who had previously responded to social connections. In fact, donors with prior social influence were less likely to donate under either personalization strategy. Finally, we found that local personalization tended to concentrate funding in more affluent areas, highlighting a trade-off between short-term engagement and long-term social equity.

This study contributes to the information systems literature on digital platforms, charitable crowdfunding, and personalization by offering new insight into how heuristics shape online giving. We show that home bias can be effectively activated through local personalization, increasing engagement and donations, but that this approach may be less effective for donors previously influenced by social ties. These findings illuminate how donors navigate giving decisions in charitable crowdfunding and suggest a natural segmentation of the donor base. In addition, we demonstrate how local personalization can unintentionally exacerbate geographic disparities in funding, reinforcing a tension between short-term donor engagement goals and long-term mission-driven outcomes.

The next section describes the theoretical background for this study. The following sections discuss the data, empirical analysis, and results, before concluding with a discussion of implications and limitations.

## Related Literature

Charitable crowdfunding platforms enable donors to support individuals and communities in need by contributing small amounts of money online (Burtch & Chan, 2019; Mollick, 2014). Unlike commercial crowdfunding models that offer tangible rewards, charitable crowdfunding platforms operate with prosocial goals, facilitating donations without a financial return to the donor (Agrawal et al., 2015; Bapna & Umyarov, 2015; Lin et al., 2013). Donors on these platforms derive non-economic value from giving, motivated by moral, emotional, or identity-based concerns (Agrawal et al., 2015; Bapna & Umyarov, 2015; Gerber & Hui, 2013; Lin et al., 2013; Liu et al., 2015). Over time, charitable crowdfunding has grown into a significant source of financial support for a wide variety of pro-social causes (Burtch & Chan, 2019).

In choosing which campaigns to support, donors browse through a range of available projects and assess them along several dimensions. These include attributes such as the number of updates and fundraising goal (Mollick, 2014), the language and sentiment used in project descriptions (Althoff et al., 2014; Mitra & Gilbert, 2014; Yuan et al., 2016), and the facial expressions and appearances in project images (Duarte et al., 2012; J. Kim & Park, 2018; Rhue & Robert, 2018). However, donors often lack complete information about project quality and face cognitive limits in processing the large volume of options that are available to them (Burtch et al., 2014; Geva et al., 2019; K. Kim & Viswanathan, 2019). As a result, systematic evaluation of all available projects is frequently infeasible.

To navigate this complexity, donors often rely on heuristics, which are mental shortcuts that simplify decision-making by using associative or emotional cues rather than deliberate, analytical evaluation (Kahneman, 2011; Dale, 2015). Rooted in dual-process theories of cognition, heuristics reflect the fast, intuitive System 1 mode of thinking rather than the slow, deliberative System 2 (Kahneman, 2011). Two key heuristics relevant to the charitable crowdfunding context are home bias and social influence. Home bias refers to donors’ tendency to support geographically local recipients, driven by identity, emotional resonance, or a sense of obligation to one’s community (Burtch et al., 2014; Lin & Viswanathan, 2016). Social influence refers to the tendency to support projects created, recommended, or shared by people with whom the donor has a social connection, leveraging trust as a heuristic cue (Hur et al., 2023; Hong et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2015).

Both heuristics relate to the broader concept of homophily, the tendency to affiliate with similar others (Kossinets & Watts, 2006). Geographic proximity, shared background, and social ties all serve as signals of similarity that can increase trust and reduce uncertainty, particularly in settings like crowdfunding, where donors cannot directly verify project quality (Burtch et al., 2014; Kim & Viswanathan, 2019). While past research has demonstrated the individual effects of home bias and social influence, few studies have examined how they interact, particularly when they offer competing cues. For example, a donor may have previously given to a project shared by a distant friend (social influence without home bias) or donated to a local stranger (home bias without social influence). It remains unclear whether one heuristic has a stronger influence in such cases or how they affect donor behavior when misaligned.

Moreover, prior studies have not fully explored how these heuristics can be strategically leveraged through personalization, despite their widespread use on charitable crowdfunding platforms. Personalization refers to tailoring content and recommendations for individual users (Zhang, 2011; Khan et al., 2009) based on behavioral or demographic data, such as past donation history or geographic location, to promote relevance and responsiveness (Zhang, 2011; Xiao & Yue, 2021). These strategies aim to increase engagement, defined as user interactions with platform content (e.g., opening or clicking on emails), and ultimately donations, defined as monetary contributions to specific campaigns. For example, marketing messages may promote local projects based on the donor’s location or highlight campaigns similar to those previously supported (Xiao & Yue, 2021). Personalization has been shown to increase attention and engagement (Ansari & Mela, 2003; Munz et al., 2020), particularly when it aligns with users’ preferences or identities (Lambrecht & Tucker, 2013; Munz et al., 2020; Urban et al., 2014; Bleier & Eisenbeiss, 2015). Yet it is unclear whether a personalization approach based on home bias remains effective for donors who have not previously exhibited such tendencies.

In addition, personalization may have unintended consequences. Platforms that emphasize location-based attributes risk reinforcing inequitable patterns of giving, as donors from wealthier areas tend to give more to charitable causes overall (Igra et al., 2021). As a result, personalization strategies based on home bias could inadvertently concentrate donations in already advantaged communities, exacerbating geographic disparities (Touré-Tillery & Fishbach, 2017). While heuristics help donors cope with information overload, they may also amplify structural disparities if used without consideration of the broader consequences.

In summary, prior research has documented the roles of home bias and social influence in charitable giving and shown how personalization can shape donor behavior. However, most existing studies have analyzed these heuristics in isolation, inferred their effects from observational data, and given limited attention to their role in platform outreach strategies. As such, there remains a gap in understanding how these heuristics interact, whether they can be strategically activated through personalization, and what the broader implications are for donation equity and platform goals.

## Hypothesis Development

Personalization strategies on crowdfunding platforms often rely on donors’ past behaviors to predict future giving preferences. Yet prior donations may not always reflect stable preferences; they can instead be shaped by temporary circumstances, platform design constraints, or social influence. To disentangle these dynamics, we contrast the effects of local personalization, which activates home bias and location-based heuristics, with the influence of prior social connections, which operate through reliance on personal relationships rather than project characteristics.

## Local Personalization

Personalization strategies on charitable crowdfunding platforms often rely on donors’ past behavior to predict future preferences (Xiao et al., 2021; Zhang, 2011; Zhang et al., 2019; Ho & Lim, 2018; Laussel & Resende, 2022; Tam & Ho, 2006). Following a revealed preference logic (Samuelson, 1948) would indicate that donors who previously supported non-local projects will continue to do so (Song et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2024). However, this assumption does not always hold (Lambrecht & Tucker, 2013). Past behavior may reflect temporary circumstances such as a social connection to a specific fundraiser rather than a stable preference for nonlocal giving. In addition, platform constraints may distort observed preferences. On all-or-nothing crowdfunding platforms, the limited availability of local projects may lead donors to support non-local causes even if they would prefer to give locally (Cherepanov et al., 2013).

Local personalization, which we define as targeting donors with projects from their current geographic area, may activate a home bias heuristic, prompting support for local causes even among donors with prior non-local giving (Lin & Viswanathan, 2016). This heuristic can be triggered by emotional identification with the local community (Chapman et al., 2022; James & Zagefka, 2017), perceived obligation to one’s neighbors (Kessler & Milkman, 2018), or a sense of moral responsibility (Yang et al., 2020). As a result, local personalization may feel more personally relevant and trustworthy, thereby increasing both engagement, defined as interactions with the email, and donations. We therefore hypothesize:

H1a: Local personalization increases engagement among donors who previously supported non-local projects.

H1b: Local personalization increases donation likelihood among donors who previously supported non-local projects.

It is less clear whether local personalization will be effective for donors whose prior giving was driven by social influence; that is, donors who supported projects recommended directly by a referring contact. Social influence operates as a separate heuristic, wherein donors rely on endorsements from people they trust. The core driver of the decision is the relationship with the recommender, not the project’s location (Hur et al., 2023). While social influence and home bias can align (e.g., a friend recommends a local project), they often diverge, especially as digital networks span geographic boundaries (Bailey et al., 2018). A donor who previously gave to a distant friend’s campaign may have been motivated by trust in that individual, not by an affinity for the project or its location (Agrawal et al., 2015). In such cases, the emotional and cognitive triggers behind social influence may not transfer to geographically targeted outreach. As a result, local personalization may still capture the donor’s attention, leading to higher engagement, but may not translate into donations among those previously influenced by social connections. We hypothesize:

H2a: Local personalization increases engagement for all donors with prior non-local donations, including those previously influenced by social connections.

H2b: Local personalization increases donation likelihood only among donors without prior evidence of social influence.

## Consequences

While local personalization can increase engagement and donation rates, it may also create unintended consequences. Although charitable crowdfunding platforms often seek to direct resources to the most underserved communities (Mota et al., 2020), most donors live in more affluent areas (Igra et al., 2021). Activating the home bias heuristic through local personalization may inadvertently concentrate donations in already advantaged communities, creating a misalignment between short-term engagement goals and the platform’s broader mission. This dynamic reflects the Matthew effect (Merton, 1968), or the “rich get richer” phenomenon, in which those with more resources tend to receive even more. Because donors are more likely to support fundraisers that reflect the characteristics of their own communities (Kossinets & Watts, 2006), local personalization may unintentionally amplify existing disparities. We hypothesize:

H3: Local personalization leads to a rich-get-richer effect in donation patterns, concentrating funding in more affluent areas.

## Study Context

We conducted a field experiment in collaboration with DonorsChoose, a charitable crowdfunding platform that enables U.S. public school teachers to request funding for K-12 classroom projects. Since its founding in 2000, DonorsChoose has raised over \$1.5 billion to support more than 89,000 public schools. <sup>2</sup> As of the time of writing, 88% of U.S. public schools have had at least one project posted on the platform. Teachers use the site to post requests for classroom resources, specifying their fundraising goals, the deadline, and other project details. Projects are removed once their funding goal is met or the deadline is reached. Figure 1 provides an example of a project page on the platform. Supporting educational projects in underresourced schools has been shown to reduce achievement gaps between low-income and high-income students (Keppler et al., 2022), so donations on this platform can have meaningful consequences for educational equity.

## Experimental Design

Like many digital platforms, DonorsChoose uses personalized email campaigns to engage donors and encourage repeat contributions. We leveraged this infrastructure to conduct a large-scale randomized field experiment in October 2021 with nearly 160,000 prior donors to test how local personalization affects donor behavior. Personalized emails were sent according to randomly assigned conditions, and we tracked donor behavior for one week following email delivery.

The experimental conditions were randomly assigned from one of two relevant donor locations: (1) their billing zip code, which is their billing location and presumably represents their home location, and (2) the recently supported zip code, which corresponds to the site of their most recent donation. The control group received a personalized email featuring projects near their recently supported zip code, DonorsChoose’s standard outreach strategy at the time of the experiment. The treatment group received a personalized email featuring projects near their billing zip code, aiming to activate home bias. Although there were more than 200,000 donors in the anonymized database, our main analysis focuses on the subset of nearly 160,000 donors whose most recent donation was non-local; that is, their billing and recently supported zip codes differed. For each condition, donors were directed to a landing page displaying projects from the assigned geographic area.<sup>3</sup> The randomly assigned location appeared consistently in the email’s subject line, pre-header, body text, and landing page link, ensuring full compliance with the experiment regardless of whether the recipient opened the email. All other content was held constant across groups. Table 1 provides a sample illustrating the treatment and control conditions for a hypothetical donor with a billing address in Boston, MA, and a previous donation in Rochester, NY. This design isolated the effect of local personalization on donor behavior.

## Measures

We used anonymized, donor-level data provided by the platform to construct the key variables listed in Table 2. Each donor was associated with a unique ID, billing zip code, recently supported zip code, and behavioral indicators for whether they opened, clicked, or donated via the email. The donor-level measures were used to calculate aggregate engagement (open rate and click rate) and donation (donation rate and donation amount) metrics. Among donors, the average open rate was approximately 28%, the click rate was 5%, and the donation rate was 0.2%, which are in line with industry benchmarks for email marketing.<sup>4</sup> To capture the tendency to give due to geographic proximity, we calculated the distance measure, the geodesic distance between the billing and recently supported zip codes. A smaller value for distance indicates giving closer to the donor’s home. Prior social influence was inferred using a referral indicator for whether a donor’s first donation came through a teacher referral link.

We also enriched the dataset with zip code-level demographic attributes using U.S. Census data<sup>5</sup> and the ZipCodeR package in R (Rozzi, 2021). For each relevant zip code (billing, recently supported, donation), we included aggregate metrics: median household income, median home value, total population, and racial composition. We identified the billing city as the largest major city near the billing zip code and the recently supported city as the largest major city near the recently supported zip code. To preserve anonymity, the analysis dataset did not include individual-level demographic data.

## Randomization

To verify that the experimental randomization was successful, we conducted a balance check comparing the treatment and control groups on key pre-treatment covariates. As shown in Table 3, there were no statistically significant differences between the groups on social influence (referral—0.651 vs. 0.648, p-value = 0.2697) and on distance (Distance—457439 vs. 457932, p-value = 0.9186), demonstrating that individual donors had a similar propensity for social influence and home bias across treatment and control. Treatment and control groups were also statistically indistinguishable on avg. income in billing zip, avg. income in supported zip, billing population, recent population, billing BIPOC<sup>6</sup> 80%+, and recent BIPOC 80%+, indicating that treated and control donors live in similar areas and donate to similar areas. We thus conclude that the randomization was valid.

## Effects of Local Personalization on Engagement and Donations (H1a and H1b)

Consistent with H1a and H1b, local personalization significantly increased both donor engagement and giving behavior. Table 4 reports the mean response rates by experimental condition. Donors in the local personalization condition had significantly higher open rates (0.282 vs. 0.277, $p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , click rates (0.0542 vs. 0.0436, p < 0.001, increase of 24%), and donation rates (0.00285 vs. 0.00209, $p < 0 . 0 0 1$ increase of 36%). These findings suggest that local personalization activated the home bias heuristic, even among donors whose prior giving did not reflect home bias, leading to improved engagement (opens and clicks) and donation rates.

While the billing and recently supported zip codes differed by design, some donors may have still lived near their previously supported project. ## Heterogeneous Effects by Social Influence (H2a and H2b)

We next investigated whether the impact of local personalization differed according to donors’ prior donation behavior. Specifically, we examined whether open rates, click rates, and donation rates varied based on whether their first donation was made through a teacher referral link, which served as a proxy for social influence. This analysis was used to test H2a and H2b.

Table 6 shows engagement and donation rates separately for referred and non-referred donors. Among referred donors, local personalization significantly increased click rates (0.047 vs. $0 . 0 3 9 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ but had no significant effect on open rates or donation rates. In contrast, among non-referred donors, local personalization significantly increased open rates (0.300 vs 0.292, p < 0.05), click rates (0.067 vs. 0.052, p < 0.001), and donation rates (0.005 vs. 0.004, p < 0.01). Notably, referred donors were less responsive overall than non-referred donors across both conditions, with lower open, click, and donation rates regardless of personalization strategy. For example, within the local personalization condition, referred donors had substantially lower open rates (0.300 vs. 0.272), click rates (0.067 vs. 0.047), and donation rates (0.005 vs. 0.0015) than non-referred donors. Similar patterns held for the control group for the open rate (0.292 vs. 0.269), click rate (0.052 vs. 0.039), and donation rate (0.004 vs. 0.0012).

These results suggest that while local personalization improved engagement across all donors (supporting H2a in part), it only increased donation likelihood among those without prior evidence of social influence (supporting H2b). Triggering home bias was an effective mechanism for increasing both engagement and donations, but this effect was specific to donors who did not initially give through social connections. Donors with prior social influence may require more relationship-driven cues to motivate giving, while nonreferred donors may be more susceptible to the local personalization strategy.

## Consequences of Local Personalization (H3)

We assessed the income and demographic characteristics of donation recipients across experiment conditions. To examine whether local personalization caused donors to support wealthier areas, we restricted the dataset to the 395 donors who gave through the experimental email. For each of these donors, we used the donation zip code to obtain the median household income of the donation recipient’s area. To further explore these indirect effects, we analyzed the demographic and socioeconomic similarity between donation recipients and each of the donor’s two relevant locations. For each of the three zip codes, we constructed normalized community attribute vectors using income, population, racial demographics, and geographic characteristics. We then computed two similarity measures: cosine similarity and Euclidean distance. Cosine similarity reflects the directional alignment between two community attribute vectors A and B: $\begin{array} { r } { c o s i n e ( \mathbf { A } , \mathbf { B } ) = \frac { \mathbf { A } * \mathbf { B } } { | | \mathbf { A } | | | \mathbf { B } | | } . } \end{array}$ Euclidean distance for the two community attribute vectors A and B captures the overall magnitude of difference: $\begin{array} { r } { d ( \mathbf { A } , \mathbf { B } ) = \sqrt { \sum _ { i } ( A _ { i } - B _ { i } ) ^ { 2 } } } \end{array}$ . Similarity measures were calculated between each donor’s donation attribute vector and the attribute vectors for each condition.

Columns with odd indices represent similarities based on the donor’s billing zip code, while columns with even indices represent similarities based on the zip code of the donor’s most recently supported project. First, we notice that donors gave to locations that were significantly more similar to the location in the donor’s experimental condition. This pattern held for the full sample of donations (Columns 1, 2, 5, and 6) and for the subset of donations sent to “other” areas (Columns 3, 4, 7, and 8). This result highlights that donations in the local personalization more closely resembled their local community, and donations in the default personalization resembled their recently supported community. These results suggest that donors exposed to local personalization were more likely to support communities demographically similar to their own, even when not giving directly to their own zip code. This is consistent with homophily-based mechanisms that extend the effect of home bias beyond geographic proximity.

Taken together, these analyses support H3: Local personalization contributes to a rich-get-richer effect, both by directly increasing donations to donors’ own (often affluent) communities and by shifting donations toward demographically similar areas. This effect was especially pronounced among more affluent donors, who tended to support wealthier schools. While local personalization improved engagement and donation rates, these benefits may come at the cost of reinforcing geographic and socioeconomic inequalities over time.

## Discussion

This study builds on the charitable crowdfunding literature by using a randomized experiment with nearly 160,000 donors to examine how local personalization in email outreach influences donor engagement and giving behavior. By focusing on donors whose prior giving showed no evidence of home bias, we tested whether local personalization activates the home bias heuristic. We also examined how these effects vary by prior exposure to social influence and assessed whether such personalization reinforces disparities in resource distribution.

Our experiment yields three main insights. First, local personalization significantly increased both engagement and donations (H1a, H1b), even among donors who previously supported non-local projects. This finding indicates that proximity cues can activate home bias, even in the absence of prior local giving behavior. Second, the effect of local personalization varied according to donors’ history of social influence (H2a, H2b). Donors who were previously influenced by referrals showed increased engagement but no significant change in giving, while non-referred donors were responsive across both engagement and donation measures. This suggests opportunities for segmentation based on heuristic susceptibility. Third, local personalization potentially exacerbated existing inequalities by increasing donations to both donors’ local, affluent communities and to other communities demographically similar to theirs (H3). This reveals a trade-off between increasing short-term engagement and advancing long-term equity.

## Theoretical Implications

This work contributes to the information systems literature on heuristics and personalization in digital environments. While prior research has established the importance of home bias and social influence in isolation or assumed they operate jointly (Bapna & Ganco, 2023; Burtch et al., 2014; Kim & Viswanathan, 2019; Lin et al., 2013; Rhue & Clark, 2022), we provide comparative evidence of their distinct and interactive effects. Our results suggest a hierarchy of heuristics: Social influence rooted in interpersonal trust appears to exert a stronger effect than geographic proximity. At the same time, donors without strong social cues are more susceptible to proximitybased framing. Our results are consistent with prior work showing that social influence provides a more precise evaluative tool than geographic similarity (Hur et al., 2023).

We also contribute to growing research at the intersection of personalization on platforms and social equity (Burtch & Chan, 2019; Rhue & Clark, 2022). While personalization is typically framed as a strategy to improve relevance and engagement, we show that heuristic-based targeting can generate unintended distributive consequences, resulting in a rich-get-richer effect. Notably, this skewness in giving was not only toward donors’ own communities, but also toward demographically similar communities and schools, suggesting that local personalization also potentially activates homophily. Our experiment isolated these effects in a relatively simple setting, offering clear evidence that even minimal forms of personalization can shape the allocation of social resources in inequitable ways.

## Practical Implications

For platform designers and practitioners, our results highlight both the power and potential risks of local personalization. Even in the absence of local giving history, local personalization can increase engagement, challenging the dominant assumption in personalization that past behavior is the best predictor of future behavior (Xiao et al., 2021; Zhang, 2011; Zhang et al., 2019). In our setting, a static attribute of home location outperformed behavioral targeting (via donation history), echoing prior findings from targeted advertising (Lambrecht & Tucker, 2013).

However, the same strategy that boosts engagement may also exacerbate inequality. Local personalization can steer donations toward wealthier and demographically similar communities, potentially undermining the prosocial missions of charitable platforms. To mitigate these effects, platforms might consider rotating or combining multiple personalization schemes; for example, blending geographic targeting with social influence, cultural similarity, or other heuristics shown to affect donor behavior (Burtch et al., 2014; Munz et al.,

2020). Recent research also suggests that alternating across multiple targeting rule strategies can increase equity over time (Gao et al., 2023). While our experiment contained static rather than algorithmic recommendations, our findings may inform more dynamic personalization or recommendation systems, helping platforms balance engagement with fairness across campaigns and cohorts.

## Limitations

This study has several limitations that offer opportunities for future research. First, we inferred heuristic use from behavioral responses rather than from self-reported reasoning. Although this enables large-scale causal inference, follow-up studies could incorporate surveys or interviews to better understand donor perceptions and motivations. Second, other heuristics, such as name-based similarity (Munz et al., 2020), could be activated to increase donor engagement. Future work could explore more nuanced interactions among heuristics as the basis for personalized outreach. Third, our demographic data was anonymized and aggregated at the zip code level, limiting our ability to analyze effects by race, age, or other individual-level demographics. Future studies with more granular data could deepen insights into how personalization interacts with identity. Fourth, while we show that local personalization can reinforce inequality, we did not directly evaluate mitigation strategies. Future research could test alternative approaches, such as dynamically varied messaging to assess whether platforms can improve prosocial outcomes without sacrificing engagement. Finally, although our focus was on home bias, other heuristics likely contribute to differences in donation flows. Exploring how encouraging more deliberative thinking, rather than relying on cognitive shortcuts, affects giving could offer another avenue for reducing inequality.

## Conclusion

This study provides causal evidence that local personalization meaningfully boosts donor engagement on charitable crowdfunding platforms, even among donors with no prior indication of home bias. At the same time, it reveals how such strategies can inadvertently amplify socioeconomic and geographic disparities. By uncovering how two key heuristics, home bias and social influence, interact with platform outreach, we shed light on the behavioral and societal consequences of personalization. As digital platforms are playing an increasingly important role in directing resources, their design choices carry both technical and moral weight. Balancing effectiveness and equity is thus not merely a design challenge but a fundamental question of platform responsibility.

## About the Authors

Lauren Rhue is an assistant professor of information systems at the Robert H. Smith School of Business at the University of Maryland. Her research has been published in MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Journal of the Association of Information Systems, Journal of Management, Production and Operations Management, Health Policy and Technology, and Social Networks, as well as in law review articles in American Law Review and Journal of Science and Technology Law. She received her Ph.D. in information systems from the New York University’s Stern School of Business, and graduated from Stanford University with a B.S. in management science and engineering.

Atiya Avery is an assistant professor of information systems management at the Harbert College of Business at Auburn University. Her research has been published in Journal of Medical Internet Research, Communications of the Association of Information Systems, Information & Computer Security, and Journal of Information Technology Case and Application Research. Her research focuses on advancing the academic discourse in minimizing the harms and increasing the benefits of technology to directly shape policies and industry best practices. She received her Ph.D. in management information systems from the College of Business at the University of Illinois Chicago and graduated from Georgia State University with an M.S. in managerial sciences and a B.B.A. in risk management and insurance.

Jessica Clark is an assistant professor of information systems at the Robert H. Smith School of Business at the University of Maryland. Her research focuses on closing the gap between cutting-edge machine learning algorithm development and the needs of end users, such as researchers and industry practitioners. She also studies the negative social impacts of the large-scale interactions that are facilitated by online platforms and the ways of mitigating such effects. Her research has been published in venues such as MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Production and Operations Management, Health Policy and Technology, and Data Mining and Knowledge Discovery. She received a Ph.D. in information systems from the New York University Stern School of Business and a B.S. in mathematical sciences from Worcester Polytechnic Institute.
