# EXPLORING ONLINE HELP-SEEKING TENDENCIES: THE INFLUENCE OF EXPERIENCE TYPE AND HELP PROVIDER TYPE<sup>1</sup>

Shaobo (Kevin) Li Division of Information Systems & Management Engineering, College of Business, Southern University of Science and Technology, Shenzhen, CHINA {lisb3@sustech.edu.cn}

Xun (Irene) Huang Department of Global Business and Marketing, Lee Shau Kee School of Business and Administration Hong Kong Metropolitan University, Hong Kong, CHINA {ixhuang@hkmu.edu.hk}

Le Wang Department of Information Systems, College of Business City University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong, CHINA {Le.Wang@cityu.edu.hk}

Yuwei Jiang Department of Management and Marketing, Faculty of Business Hong Kong Polytechnic University, Hong Kong, CHINA {yuwei.jiang@polyu.edu.hk}

Xin (Robert) Luo Department of Marketing, Information and Decision Sciences, Anderson School of Management University of New Mexico, Albuquerque, NM, U.S.A. {xinluo@unm.edu}

Businesses are increasingly providing online assistance through help features to enhance the user experience in the digital era. Understanding factors influencing users’ tendencies to seek online help is crucial for optimizing resources and improving support and the overall user experience. Drawing on utilitarian- and hedonic-motivation systems theory, this paper examines how the type of experience and the type of help provider impact users’ online help-seeking behavior. Across three studies—including secondary data analysis, an online experiment, and an observational study of actual user behavior—we found that users are more (vs. less) inclined to seek help when encountering difficulties in utilitarian (vs. hedonic) experiences. This pattern was driven by users’ greater focus on achieving specific outcomes in utilitarian contexts, in contrast to their emphasis on experiential enjoyment in hedonic contexts. Importantly, users showed a stronger preference for seeking assistance from human agents over service robots when facing challenges in utilitarian experiences. Nevertheless, during hedonic experiences, no notable difference between humans and robots emerged in users’ inclination to seek help. These findings highlight the importance of considering the type of experience and the type of help provider when planning online support services, contributing valuable insights to the literature on hedonic vs. utilitarian motivation systems, users’ online help-seeking behavior, and user experience.

Keywords: Online support, online help-seeking tendencies, human customer service representative, service robot, utilitarian experience, hedonic experience, process-outcome focus

## Introduction

In the landscape of information systems (IS) research, the profound impact of the digital economy and platform economy on users’ behavior and the dynamics of customercompany interactions has garnered significant scholarly attention (Mao et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2021; Zhu et al., 2021). Customer relationship management strategies underscore the imperative for companies to adapt their support systems to align with the evolving needs of users in the digital era (Elshan et al., 2023; Packard et al., 2018; Ye & Kankanhalli, 2018). This necessitates a nuanced understanding of users’ help-seeking behaviors, a crucial aspect within the IS landscape (Venkatesh et al., 2003). This knowledge is pivotal for IS practitioners seeking to allocate resources efficiently and improve user support services.

To address user challenges, companies have implemented a range of IS-enabled support channels. For example, Uber’s 24/7 call center and Bank of America’s chatbot “Erica” illustrate how firms leverage IS innovations to provide timely and efficient assistance (Keenan, 2021). While these systems offer clear benefits, maintaining an effective support infrastructure incurs substantial costs, a challenge welldocumented in the IS literature (Kumar & Telang, 2011; Scherer et al., 2015; Shugan, 2005; Wetzel et al., 2014). Thus, companies must carefully manage help-seeking behavior, ensuring that support is effective without being overly resource intensive. Prompt assistance can foster positive brand impressions and customer loyalty (Palmatier et al., 2009), whereas delayed or excessive support may backfire, leading to dissatisfaction and higher operational costs (Ku et al., 2013; Liu et al., 2019; Mick & Fournier, 1998).

When users encounter challenges, they may choose to seek help through the support button or attempt to resolve issues independently. Understanding when and why users seek or avoid help is essential for optimizing customer relationship management strategies (Packard et al., 2018; Winer, 2001). Previous research has shown that users do not always opt for external assistance, even when assistance is available and potentially beneficial (Kim et al., 2016; Lee, 1997; Subramani et al., 2021). Our paper builds on and extends these findings. Kim et al. (2016) focused exclusively on gaming, a typical hedonic experience. While our findings align with their theorizing, we broaden the scope by exploring a range of experience types. Additionally, we diverge from Subramani et al. (2021), who examined the role of information availability by introducing a novel mechanism: the nature of the user’s experiences.

We investigated how two key factors—experience type (utilitarian vs. hedonic) and help provider type (human agent vs. service robot)—jointly shape users’ online help-seeking behaviors. Drawing on prior research (Lowry et al., 2013), we define utilitarian experience (e.g., using a learning app) as goaloriented and instrumental, driven by the practical utility of achieving a task (Batra & Ahtola, 1991; Chen et al., 2017; Strahilevitz & Myers, 1998). In contrast, hedonic experiences (e.g., playing a video game) are rooted in emotional enjoyment and sensory pleasure (Hirschman & Holbrook, 1982; Lowry et al., 2013). Grounded in the theory of utilitarian and hedonic motivational systems (UMS/HMS) (Lowry et al., 2013), we propose that utilitarian experiences activate an outcomefocused mindset, making users more inclined to seek help when problems arise. Conversely, hedonic experiences trigger a process-oriented mindset, reducing the perceived need for external assistance. Importantly, we emphasize that these distinctions reflect the primary motivation and focus of the user’s engagement with a specific task, rather than the general category of the product or service. While a product may be broadly utilitarian or hedonic in design, the nature of a user’s experience is shaped by their task-specific goals. For example, interacting with an app to complete a work assignment reflects a utilitarian experience, whereas using the same app to unwind or enjoy leisure content would reflect a hedonic one. Our investigation centers on how these task-specific experiential orientations influence subsequent help-seeking behavior in online contexts.

We further distinguish between experience types, which refer to the objective nature of an activity, and motivational systems, which denote the psychological processes it activates. This distinction allowed us to examine how contextual features of the user experiences influence help-seeking decisions, offering both theoretical insights and practical implications for digital support design. Users engaged in utilitarian experiences are typically goal-oriented and solution-focused. They perceive problems as barriers to goal completion and are thus more likely to seek efficient help, especially from human agents. In contrast, users in hedonic experiences tend to tolerate disruptions, viewing them as part of the process. As a result, they are less motivated to seek immediate assistance. Based on these differing mindsets, we hypothesize that users’ tolerance for difficulties—and thus their propensity to seek help—varies systematically across experience types. Moreover, we propose that the help provider type moderates this relationship. For utilitarian experience, users prefer human support over service robots, perceiving humans as more competent in resolving personalized or urgent issues. For hedonic experiences, users are generally less inclined to seek help, as maintaining enjoyment is prioritized over problem resolution.

The current research makes important theoretical and practical contributions. First, we enrich the literature on online helpseeking by introducing experience type as a novel factor. While previous studies have examined help seeker characteristics (e.g., Best et al., 2014; Chen & Zahedi, 2016; Kim et al., 2018) and help provider features (e.g., Kim et al., 2016; Mousavi et al., 2020), our work sheds light on how different user experiences shape support behavior. Additionally, we explore how the provider type interacts with the experience type to influence help-seeking. This integrated approach deepens our understanding of user-support dynamics in digital contexts.

Second, our study advances the UMS/HMS theoretical framework within IS research (e.g., Goodhue & Thompson, 1995; Lowry et al., 2013; Lowry et al., 2015; Venkatesh et al., 2003; Zhang et al., 2019). By examining how utilitarian and hedonic motivations influence help-seeking behavior, we respond to calls for more research on HMS in IS (Lowry et al., 2013). Our findings suggest that users’ tolerance for issues and preferred support modalities differ based on experience type, offering a more nuanced understanding of motivational dynamics in digital service interactions.

Third, we contribute to the growing literature on user preferences between human agents and service robots (Adam et al., 2022; Holthöwer & van Doorn, 2023; Garvey et al., 2023). Prior studies have explored “algorithm preference” and “algorithm aversion” in pre-purchase contexts. For example, robots tend to be preferred in situations involving embarrassment (Holthöwer & van Doorn, 2023) or the delivery of bad news (Garvey et al., 2023), while humans are generally favored for subjective tasks requiring trust and personalization (Castelo et al., 2019; Longoni & Cian, 2022; Longoni et al., 2019). Our research extends this line of work to the post-purchase usage phase, showing that in utilitarian settings, users prefer human help due to its perceived effectiveness—an insight with important implications for service design.

Finally, our findings offer actionable guidance for practitioners seeking to optimize digital support systems. Companies can use our insights to tailor support strategies based on experience type. For utilitarian products, firms should prioritize human agents to ensure efficient, personalized assistance. For hedonic offerings, users are less likely to seek help and tend to show no strong provider preference, making service robots a cost-effective alternative. While previous studies have focused on specific contexts such as gaming (Kim et al., 2016) and information availability (Subramani et al., 2021), our study provides a broader framework grounded in fundamental experience distinctions. This is particularly useful because: (1) most products and services fall somewhere on the utilitarian-hedonic spectrum, (2) experience types are easier to manipulate than individual traits, and (3) the post-purchase phase—crucial for customer retention—remains understudied in IS literature.

## Literature Review

## Users’ Online Help-Seeking Behaviors

Help-seeking is defined as the act of requesting assistance, information, advice, resources, or support from others to achieve a goal or resolve a problem (Nadler et al., 2003). Scholars in psychology, medicine, education, sociology, and public policy disciplines have studied the domain of helpseeking (e.g., Bui, 2003; Hunt & Eisenberg, 2010; Kim et al., 2018; McKinlay, 1973). These studies have shown that individuals are more likely to seek advice when they feel anxious (Brooks et al., 2015), when the problem is complicated (Schrah et al., 2006), or when the cost of seeking advice is low (Sniezek & Buckley, 1995). A separate line of research has focused on investigating the antecedents of users’ help-seeking behavior. Within this research stream, it has been found that users tend to seek more help before making decisions (e.g., purchase decisions) when they exhibit higher involvement, possess a stronger attitude toward the experience, and have greater time availability (Beatty & Smith, 1987). Conversely, users may be hesitant to seek help or advice when they feel anxious about interacting with help providers, such as financial advisors (Gerrans & Hershey, 2017).

Alongside traditional offline avenues, users are progressively turning to online platforms, forums, and AI-powered service robots as alternative sources of assistance. For example, an emerging trend is evident in the online pursuit of mental health support, mainly driven by the enhanced accessibility and perceived anonymity provided by these platforms (Naslund et al., 2016). Likewise, online forums play a crucial role as valuable resources for information exchange and emotional support, underscoring the community’s significance in facilitating help-seeking behavior (Biyani et al., 2014). Furthermore, in the realm of online help-seeking, conversational AI agents play a pivotal role in augmenting user engagement by fostering human-like interactional competencies in these agents, specifically cognitive, relational, and emotional skills (Chandra et al., 2022).

Previous research has highlighted various factors influencing individuals’ propensities to engage in online help-seeking. When confronted with online security threats, soliciting assistance and guidance proves to be an effective coping mechanism, as evidenced by a positive link between users’ perceived security threats and their inclination to seek help (Chen & Zahedi, 2016). Similarly, adolescents with heightened privacy concerns are more inclined to seek help with privacy settings on social networking sites (Chen et al., 2016a). On the flip side, when alternative avenues are accessible, individuals are less inclined to seek help online. For instance, an analysis of longitudinal data on repository usage and technical support calls in an engineering company revealed that enhanced access to codified knowledge resulted in a reduction in requests for technical support (Subramani et al., 2021).

## Online Helpers: Human Agents vs. Service Robots

Amid technological advancements, the role of human agents in service delivery remains a focal point in IS research. Studies have shown that users generally perceive service quality more positively when interacting with human agents, who bring a valuable human touch and interpersonal communication to IS service provision (Han et al., 2022; Schanke et al., 2021). In particular, human agents with high emotional intelligence are better equipped to understand and meet users’ emotional needs, leading to improved service outcomes and greater user satisfaction (Lee et al., 2013; Luong et al., 2021). Additionally, a hybrid approach that combines human expertise with automated systems can enhance both service efficiency and user satisfaction, highlighting the importance of balancing both human intervention and automation in IS services (Adam et al., 2022).

On the other hand, the adoption of service robots, especially AI-driven chatbots, in help-seeking environments has rapidly increased. The adoption of chatbots for routine inquiries across industries has led to quick responses, cost savings, and high user convenience (Chen et al., 2023; Dolata et al., 2023; Ge et al., 2021; Luo et al., 2019). However, preferences vary depending on the task. For objective tasks, where there is a clear right answer, users often exhibit “algorithm appreciation,” favoring algorithmic advice over human input due to the cognitive trust placed in algorithms for objective decisions like financial calculations (Castelo et al., 2019; Logg et al., 2019). In contrast, for subjective tasks that lack definitive answers—such as those involving personal tastes or hedonic choices—users tend to trust human recommendations more, reflecting concerns that AI may not fully capture unique individual characteristics (Castelo et al., 2019; Longoni & Cian, 2022; Granulo et al., 2021).

Studies have highlighted that enhancing the perceived emotional and human-like qualities of algorithms can increase chatbot usage in tasks. Personalization, based on user preferences and past interactions, fosters familiarity and trust, leading to prolonged engagement and higher satisfaction with chatbots (Chandra et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2022; Dennis et al., 2023). The design of chatbots—such as the degree of anthropomorphism, conversational style, and mode of interaction—further influences their acceptance (Roy & Naidoo, 2021; Van den Broeck et al., 2019). Han et al. (2022)

explored whether AI agents should express positive emotions during customer service interactions and the impact of such emotional expressions on user experience and service outcomes. Their study underscores the increasing importance of emotional cues in digital interactions.

Moreover, the characteristics of both helpers and help seekers significantly influence online help-seeking behavior. While incorporating human-like attributes in service robots is generally beneficial, it can sometimes lead to reduced helpseeking, particularly in gaming environments, where an anthropomorphized help provider might decrease users’ enjoyment and willingness to seek help (Kim et al., 2016; Kim et al., 2018). These findings underscore the importance of carefully designing virtual help providers in specific contexts.

Previous studies in this area have primarily concentrated on the characteristics of help seekers (Best et al., 2014; Chen & Zahedi, 2016; Kim et al., 2018) and help providers (Kim et al., 2016; Mousavi et al., 2020), exploring how these factors influence online help-seeking behaviors. Much of this research paradigm has been contextualized within specific settings such as online clothing retail (Schanke et al., 2021) and gaming (Kim et al., 2016). However, what remains absent from prior research is an exploration of how different types of experiences (for exceptions, see Granulo et al., 2021; Longoni & Cian, 2022) might influence users’ online help-seeking behavior and their preferences for robot or human agents as help providers. Unlike individual differences, which are challenging for practitioners to manipulate, product characteristics hold stronger practical implications for influencing people’s online help-seeking tendencies. Each product can be categorized as relatively more utilitarian or hedonic, and practitioners can emphasize either aspect based on the resources available to the human agents. Additionally, unlike previous research focusing on pre-purchase helpseeking behavior, such as seeking recommendations (Granulo et al., 2021; Longoni & Cian, 2022), our study centers on the post-purchase usage phase, examining how users seek help during their engagement with a product or service. This distinction is critical, as post-purchase help involves different psychological dynamics. In such cases, users face actual (not hypothetical) usage challenges, develop psychological ownership, and aim to enhance realized rather than anticipated value. These differences call for new theoretical insights into the drivers of help-seeking behavior.

To address this research gap, we propose that users’ tendencies to seek help online can be influenced by the type of experience they are engaging in—specifically, whether it is a utilitarian or hedonic experience—and the type of helper they receive help from after clicking the help button, specifically, a human agent or a service robot.

## Utilitarian vs. Hedonic Experiences

IS research has traditionally emphasized UMS, which is rooted in users’ task-oriented approach to technology for fulfilling specific functional needs. Utilitarian experiences are characterized by considerations of perceived ease of use and usefulness, aligning with the technology acceptance model and focusing on efficiency, productivity, and task-technology fit (Davis, 1989; Goodhue & Thompson, 1995; Lowry et al., 2015; Venkatesh et al., 2003). However, the substantial growth in HMS sales, outperforming UMS sales for over a decade, has underscored the need for more research in this area (Lowry et al., 2013). Hedonic experiences, as conceptualized by van der Heijden (2004), involve users deriving pleasure and enjoyment from technology interactions, with enjoyment, playfulness, and social/emotional fulfillment driving user engagement and satisfaction (Lowry et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2019). Lowry et al. (2013) contributed to this field with the hedonic-motivation system adoption model, emphasizing the intrinsic motivation and significance of enjoyment and playfulness in system adoption, thus providing a framework for understanding the serious consideration of these factors in the adoption process.

Extant research has identified various antecedents of preferences for utilitarian versus hedonic experiences. For example, individuals tend to find it easier to justify choosing utilitarian offerings versus hedonic ones. As a result, they are more likely to opt for a utilitarian experience when they feel a need to justify their choice (Okada, 2005). On the other hand, hedonic experiences, compared to utilitarian experiences, can induce feelings of guilt (Kivetz & Simonson, 2002). Thus, individuals are more likely to choose the hedonic alternative in a consumption context that is less likely to trigger anticipatory guilt (e.g., when choosing for others; Lu et al., 2016). Additionally, when experiencing a lack of control, individuals may turn to utilitarian purchases as a way to restore feelings of control, as utilitarian products are generally perceived to be problem-solving tools (Chen et al., 2017). Adding to this stream of literature, the current research examines a consequence rather than an antecedent of utilitarian versus hedonic experience, namely, users’ online help-seeking tendencies.

It is worth noting that utilitarian and hedonic experiences used in the current research differ from the distinction between objective and subjective tasks used in Castelo et al. (2019). While objective and subjective tasks are defined by the presence or absence of an objectively correct answer, utilitarian and hedonic experiences are characterized by their focus: utilitarian experiences emphasize practical problemsolving and efficiency, whereas hedonic experiences prioritize enjoyment and the experiential process.

## Hypothesis Development

As previously discussed, we propose that users are more inclined to seek help from a human agent rather than a service robot during a utilitarian experience versus a hedonic experience. This tendency stems from differences in users focus on outcomes versus processes across these contexts. Specifically, users in utilitarian settings are more outcomefocused, prioritizing task completion and problem resolution, whereas users in hedonic contexts are more process-focused, seeking enjoyment from the experience itself (Escalas & Luce, 2004; Yang et al., 2012; Zhao et al., 2007).

Utilitarian experiences are typically driven by the motivation to accomplish specific goals efficiently (Lowry et al., 2013; McLean & Wilson, 2016; Whitley et al., 2018). For instance, when purchasing a computer printer, users prioritize smooth, reliable printing. Faced with obstacles in such contexts, users are more likely to seek help that can restore functionality and enable them to resume task completion (Bridges & Florsheim, 2008; Rintamäki et al., 2006). Help-seeking in these situations is instrumental and efficiency-driven.

In contrast, hedonic experiences prioritize psychological enjoyment, where the process itself is central to value creation (Hirschman & Holbrook, 1982; Lowry et al., 2013). For instance, when users engage in activities such as playing a video game or watching a movie, they often value the sense of immersion and emotional gratification. The use of a game console, for example, is typically motivated by the desire to experience excitement and pleasure during gameplay (Murray & Bellman, 2011). Unlike utilitarian experiences, where solving problems or achieving goals defines the core value, hedonic experiences are driven by motives of entertainment, exploration, and emotional satisfaction (Rintamäki et al., 2006). At their highest level, hedonic experiences are characterized by deep emotional fulfillment, where users become fully immersed in the activity, momentarily detaching from external concerns (Alba & Williams, 2013).

It is important to note that this distinction reflects a relative rather than absolute difference in outcome orientation. Even within hedonic contexts, where enjoyment and personal gratification are paramount, users may still exhibit some outcome focus, especially in competitive settings or when pursuing personal achievement goals. Thus, hedonic activities are not devoid of outcome concerns but are instead relatively less outcome-focused compared to utilitarian activities.

These differences in the focus on process versus outcome likely influence how users respond when they encounter difficulties. In utilitarian experiences, the emphasis on goal attainment means that obstacles tend to trigger help-seeking behavior, as users seek to quickly restore functionality and continue toward their objectives. By contrast, in hedonic experiences, where value stems from the process itself, challenges are often perceived as part of the experiential journey rather than disruptions. As such, users may be less inclined to seek help immediately and may even derive meaning or enjoyment from overcoming such challenges. This idea is supported by previous research in experiential consumption. For example, Cutler et al. (2014) found that difficulties encountered during hiking were often later remembered as integral and meaningful components of the experience. Similarly, Loewenstein (1999) observed that pain and discomfort are often inherent in leisure pursuits like mountaineering, contributing to their emotional depth. Related studies in climbing, gaming, sports, and the arts have shown that challenges enhance enjoyment, particularly when individuals feel capable of navigating them (Abuhamdeh & Csikszentmihalyi, 2012). Scott et al. (2017) further noted that temporary hardship during leisure activities can allow individuals to reconnect with their corporeality and escape from everyday routines. Based on these observations, we propose that in contexts prioritizing emotional fulfillment, users will be less likely to seek help when encountering difficulties during hedonic experiences versus utilitarian experiences. These distinctions in outcome versus process focus help explain why help-seeking behavior may differ across utilitarian and hedonic experiences. Based on this reasoning, we propose the following hypotheses:

H1: Users are more (vs. less) inclined to seek help when encountering difficulties in utilitarian (vs. hedonic) experience.

H2: This effect is mediated by users’ heightened focus on outcome (vs. process) in utilitarian (vs. hedonic) experience.

Not all help providers are equally skilled at addressing personalized or complex issues, and service robots often fall short in this regard. While service robots excel at routine, standardized tasks with high efficiency and accuracy (Flynn, 2016; Ge et al., 2021), they tend to struggle when faced with ambiguous problems, emotionally charged situations, or the need for personalized responses (Alton, 2016; Luo et al., 2021). In contrast, human agents have unique strengths in problem framing, emotional regulation, and adaptive problem-solving— capabilities that are especially valuable in complex utilitarian contexts (Toister, 2016; Morgan, 2017). For example, when dealing with issues such as billing disputes or service breakdowns, users often require nuanced, context-sensitive support that current AI systems are not yet equipped to deliver.

Utilitarian experiences are typically outcome-focused and may involve encountering obstacles to achieving a specific goal. In such cases, users tend to value assistance that is both cognitively and emotionally responsive. Human agents are generally better at offering this type of personalized and flexible support (Toister, 2016; Longoni & Cian, 2022), especially when users are experiencing frustration or distress. While service robots are increasingly capable of simulating emotional responses, they often lack genuine emotional understanding or the ability to regulate interpersonal dynamics in real time. As Korteling et al. (2021) note, simulated empathy may not be sufficient in emotionally demanding situations, as users perceive a lack of deep emotional engagement and authenticity. This limitation makes robots less suitable for handling the complex, emotioanlly charged help-seeking situations that arise in many utilitarian experiences.

Conversely, in hedonic experiences, the process itself is central to the user’s enjoyment. Seeking help in such contexts can interrupt the flow of the experience and may even reduce its hedonic value. As a result, users tend to avoid help-seeking altogether. When help is required, the source—human or robot—is less consequential, as the emphasis is not on resolving a specific outcome but on maintaining the experiential flow. In such cases, the provider’s capabilities are secondary to the user’s desire to remain immersed in the activity.

Our study focuses on the post-purchase usage stage, where users encounter difficulties during product or service consumption. This contrasts with the pre-purchase stage, where users are primarily concerned with evaluating alternatives, managing uncertainty, and minimizing investment risks. In the post-purchase context, help-seeking is driven by execution goals—users aim to solve usage problems and derive value from something they have already committed to. This shift activates distinct psychological mechanisms: sunk costs increase motivation to persist, reduce avoidance, and foster psychological ownership. As a result, usage problems are perceived as threats to realized value, making help-seeking more pressing and personally meaningful.

This emphasis on the post-purchase stage represents a conceptual shift from most existing research on algorithm aversion and human-versus-AI preferences, which tends to emphasize pre-purchase recommendation scenarios (e.g., Castelo et al., 2019; Longoni & Cian, 2022). While pre-purchase decisions are shaped by concerns about fairness, personalization, or competence, post-purchase help-seeking is more directly influenced by users’ desire to resolve existing problems (in utilitarian settings) or preserve immersion and enjoyment (in hedonic settings). By emphasizing this stage-specific motivation, our work complements and extends prior literature. We highlight how the temporal dynamics of consumption—moving from anticipation to actual usage—alter user preferences for human versus AI assistance. It also challenges the assumption that help provider preferences formed during decision-making naturally carry over to usage contexts.

In summary, we propose that the preference for human help providers arises primarily in utilitarian experiences that involve complex problems, where the adaptability and emotional support provided by humans are particularly valued. In contrast, users are less likely to seek help during hedonic experiences. When they do, they exhibit no strong preference between human and robotic providers. Accordingly, we hypothesize:

H3a: When users encounter difficulties during utilitarian experiences, they are more inclined to seek assistance from a human agent rather than a service robot.

H3b: When users encounter difficulties during hedonic experiences, they don’t show a clear preference for either type of service provider.

## Overview of Studies

The current research consists of three studies: a secondhand dataset analysis, an online experiment, and an observational study of users’ actual behavior. Together, these studies examine users’ help-seeking behavior across different contexts.

Study 1 documents the basic effect by analyzing real-world help-seeking behaviors on Quora.com. Study 2 replicated these findings through controlled experiments, demonstrating the mediating role of the outcome vs. process focus and the moderating role of service provider type. Following prior research on utilitarian versus hedonic experience (e.g., Whitley et al., 2018), we applied distinct framing (utilitarian vs. hedonic) to the same experience to minimize potential confounds from contrasting scenarios. Study 3 investigated real-time helpseeking behavior on a mobile app, manipulating the app’s framing to be either utilitarian or hedonic, and examined the downstream effects on users’ app evaluations.

Target sample sizes were conservatively selected based on prior research on utilitarian versus hedonic experience (e.g., Chen et al., 2017) and help-seeking behavior (e.g., Kim et al., 2018). Power analyses using G\*Power confirmed that the sample sizes were sufficient to detect moderate effects (Faul et al., 2009). With approximately 100 participants per cell, we aimed for 80% power to detect a medium effect size of Cohen’s d = 0.40 with α = 0.05 (two-tailed; Gervais et al., 2015). Importantly, all studies were preregistered, and all data, manipulations, and measures have been fully reported. Detailed information on the stimuli and measures is available in Appendix A.

## Study 1: Secondhand Data Analysis

This study aimed to provide real-world evidence that users are less likely to seek help during hedonic (vs. utilitarian) experiences. Specifically, we compared the number of questions users posted on a popular question-and-answer platform, Quora, about their use of hedonic versus utilitarian mobile applications (apps). We hypothesized that users would seek help less frequently (i.e., post fewer questions) when encountering issues with hedonic (vs. utilitarian) apps.

## Method

App selection: We identified the top 30 apps in each of the 23 categories listed in the Apple App Store as of January 7, 2020. After accounting for overlap across categories, the final dataset consisted of 684 unique apps.

Utilitarian-hedonic index (Uti-Hed): To classify the apps along the utilitarian-hedonic spectrum, we conducted an online survey with 469 U.S. adult participants (Mage = 37.71; 52.5% female) recruited via Amazon’s Mechanical Turk (MTurk). Participants were introduced to definitions of utilitarian apps (“apps primarily designed for practical problem-solving”) and hedonic apps (“apps primarily designed for fun and excitement”), adapted from Alba and Williams (2013) and Dhar and Wertenbroch (2000). Each participant evaluated 50 randomly selected apps from the 684 focal apps on a 7-point scale (1 = very utilitarian, 7 = very hedonic). Each app was rated by at least 28 participants, and the average ratings were used to construct the Uti-Hed index.

Help-seeking behavior: To measure help-seeking behavior, we used web crawlers to collect the number of questions posted on Quora related to each app during the period from January 8, 2020, to March 8, 2020. Quora, a widely used knowledge-sharing platform (Botelho, 2018; Johnson et al., 2015), served as the data source. Using app names as keywords, we identified 6,646 relevant questions. After manually reviewing these questions, we excluded those unrelated to app usage (e.g., general discussions, promotional content), resulting in 5,905 valid questions. The number of these questions served as a proxy for help-seeking behavior.

Control variables: To account for potential confounds, we included several control variables in our analysis: app popularity based on download numbers (ranking), user ratings from the App Store (rating), the number of days since the app’s initial release (age), fixed effects for app categories to address heterogeneity across categories (category dummies), and perceived complexity of apps, measured by ratings from 563 MTurk participants on a 7-point scale (complexity). ## Discussion

Using real-world data, Study 1 demonstrates a significant relationship between experience type (hedonic vs. utilitarian) and users’ help-seeking behavior. As hypothesized, users were less likely to seek help (i.e., post questions on Quora) when encountering issues with hedonic apps compared to utilitarian apps.

A potential concern is whether the individuals asking questions on Quora were actual users of the apps, as some questions may reflect pre-usage considerations (e.g., “Should I buy this app?”) rather than problems encountered during usage. To address this, we randomly sampled 5% of the total dataset (333 questions) and had two research assistants independently classify whether each question related to app usage (e.g., whether they concerned specific features, functions, updates, or user interfaces). Discrepancies were resolved through discussion. The results show that 288 questions (86.5%) were determined to be about app usage, suggesting that the majority of questions originated from actual users.

A key limitation of this archival study lies in the operationalization of the experience type. While categorizing apps based on their dominant utilitarian or hedonic orientation using the Uti-Hed index provides a systematic and scalable method for large-scale analysis, this approach serves as a proxy for the user’s specific experience at the time of helpseeking. In practice, individual interactions may deviate from the app’s dominant classification—users might engage in utilitarian tasks within hedonic apps, or vice versa. A more precise method would involve classifying the underlying task motivation in each of the 5,905 help-seeking queries. However, the heterogeneity and scale of the user-generated content made this infeasible. Future research could address this limitation by employing smaller, targeted datasets or applying advanced natural language processing techniques to classify help-seeking behavior at the task level.

As with all archival studies, potential uncontrolled confounds warrant consideration. For instance, the frequency of technical issues or major updates during the data collection period could influence the volume of questions posted—apps undergoing frequent changes or experiencing more bugs may naturally elicit more help-seeking. Additionally, pricing structure (e.g., free vs. paid apps) could influence user motivation to seek help, with paying users potentially having stronger expectations for problem resolution. While our analyses controlled for key covariates such as app popularity, user ratings, app age, and category, future studies with access to richer metadata—including app maintenance history and pricing models—could more comprehensively account for these factors.

## Study 2: The Moderated Mediation Effect

The main goal of Study 2 was to examine the mediation role of outcome (vs. process) focus and the moderating role of the help provider type (human agent vs. service robot). Specifically, Study 2 aimed to determine whether the effect of utilitarian (vs. hedonic) experience on help-seeking tendencies is mediated by the focus on outcomes (vs. processes) and moderated by the help provider type. Moreover, this study sought to rule out several potential alternative explanations, including the perceived efficiency of the help provider, users’ perceptions of task complexity, the perceived adversity of the difficulty, the perceived subjectivity of the task, and the perceived importance of the consumption goal.

## Method

A total of 400 participants (M<sub>age</sub> = 39.44; 48.0% female) took part in this preregistered study<sup>2</sup> via Prolific in exchange for a small payment. The study employed a 2 (experience type: utilitarian vs. hedonic) × 2 (help provider: human vs. robot) between-subjects design.

Participants were instructed to imagine trying a new headset at the start of the survey. In the utilitarian condition, they were asked to imagine purchasing an office headset to make their online course more convenient. While trying to understand and use the headset’s new features, they encountered challenges in adjusting the settings to optimize audio quality for online learning. In the hedonic condition, participants imagined acquiring an entertainment headset designed to enhance their music and gaming experience. As they attempted to familiarize themselves with the headset’s new features for better enjoyment, they encountered difficulties in adjusting the settings for optimal audio quality for music and gaming. In both conditions, participants were told that the headset company had a dedicated website with a help button for assistance with any problems encountered.

In the human agent condition, participants were informed that clicking the help button would start a chat with a human customer service representative from the headset company. They were then asked to indicate their intended response using a scale from 1 (definitely figure it out by myself) to 7 (definitely click the help button). In the service robot condition, participants were told that clicking the help button would initiate a chat with the headset company’s AI-powered chatbot. They were also asked to indicate their intended response on the same scale.

Afterwards, participants read definitions of outcome focus and process focus, and indicated their focus using a three-item scale adapted from Zhao et al. (2007): (1) “In the previous scenario in which you imagined using an office (entertainment) headset, do you place more importance on the process or the outcome?” (2) “In the previous scenario in which you imagined using an office (entertainment) headset, which aspect is more important for you: the process or the outcome?” (3) “In the previous scenario in which you imagined using an office (entertainment) headset, to what extent do you perceive yourself as leaning more towards a process-focused orientation as opposed to an outcome-focused one?” Participants rated these items on a scale from 1 (definitely the process) to 7 (definitely the outcome). We averaged the scores to create an index of focus (α = 0.62), with higher scores indicating a stronger outcome focus.

Finally, we measured five potential alternative mechanisms to ensure the validity of our findings and account for confounding factors. First, perceived efficiency of the help provider was assessed to rule out the possibility that participants preferred human agents over robots due to efficiency rather than the outcome vs. process focus. Second, the perceived complexity of difficulty was measured, as greater task complexity could influence help-seeking tendencies, especially in utilitarian contexts. Third, perceived adversity of difficulty was included to test whether participants viewed difficulties in utilitarian contexts as more negative or frustrating, which could increase help-seeking. Fourth, the perceived subjectivity of the task was measured because hedonic tasks, such as music or gaming, may be seen as more subjective, influencing the need to seek help. Fifth, the importance of consumption goals was assessed, as users in utilitarian contexts may value goal achievement more, potentially leading to higher rates of help-seeking. By measuring these alternative mechanisms, we ensured that the observed effects were driven by the outcome focus vs. process focus rather than these other factors. Detailed stimuli and measures of these alternative explanations can be found in Appendix A. Pretests for the manipulation check can be found in Appendix B.

## Discussion

The findings of Study 2 provide support for the mediation role of the outcome (vs. process) focus and the moderating role of the help provider type (human agent vs. service robot) in helpseeking behavior. Specifically, the study shows that utilitarian (vs. hedonic) experiences influence help-seeking tendencies through the focus on outcome (vs. process), with this relationship being moderated by the help provider type. In utilitarian contexts, where the goal is achieving specific outcomes, users prefer support from human agents over service robots. This preference is due to robots’ limitations in handling personalized inquiries, as they are more effective for routine tasks but may struggle to adapt to individual user needs. In contrast, human agents are better equipped to address personalized issues, making them more appealing to users seeking efficient problem resolution. On the other hand, in hedonic contexts, where users prioritize the enjoyment of the process, they are less likely to seek help. As a result, the help provider type—whether human or robot—becomes less important, as the focus shifts to the overall experience rather than achieving a specific outcome. The results of this study also rule out potential alternative explanations, including perceived efficiency, perceived complexity of difficulty, perceived adversity of difficulty, perceived subjectivity of difficulty, and the importance of consumption goals.

## Study 3: Help-Seeking Behavior on Mobile Apps

Study 3 aimed to achieve two key objectives: first, to replicate the mediation role of the outcome (vs. process) focus and the moderating role of the help provider type (human agent vs. service robot) in the field setting by observing users’ actual behavior when clicking the help-seeking button on a mobile app, and, second, to explore a downstream consequence of this behavior by examining users’ evaluations of the app. To achieve this, we developed a custom mobile app for the study, framing it either as an online driving-training app (utilitarian experience) or a driving-gaming app (hedonic experience), with assistance provided either by a human agent or a service robot. We anticipated that participants would be less likely to seek help when using the hedonic-framed app compared to the utilitarian-framed one. Moreover, we hypothesized that users encountering challenges during utilitarian experiences would be more likely to seek help from a human agent rather than a robot. In contrast, during hedonic experiences, we predicted that users would not show a clear preference for either type of help provider. We also proposed that the focus on outcome versus process would moderate this effect. Finally, we expected that the presence of a “help-seeking” button for a human agent, compared to one for a service robot, would enhance users’ evaluation of the mobile app, but only when the app was framed as utilitarian.

## Method

A total of 400 participants $( M _ { \mathrm { a g e } } = 2 7 . 1 8 ;$ ; 57.5% female) participated in this preregistered study<sup>3</sup> via Credamo for a small payment. The study employed a 2 (experience type: utilitarian vs. hedonic) × 2 (help provider: human vs. robot) between-subjects design.

A mobile app featuring simulated car driving scenarios was developed for this study. Participants were instructed at the beginning that the purpose was either to engage in an onlinedriving training (utilitarian condition) or an online-driving game (hedonic condition). Users navigated through scenarios requiring them to answer 10 multiple-choice questions related to car driving. In the utilitarian condition, participants were introduced to an online driving training app focused on “driving training and skill enhancement.” In contrast, the hedonic condition introduced participants to a driving game app, described as centered around “driving games and leisure entertainment.” All participants were informed of a help button in the app interface to access assistance if needed. In the human agent condition, participants were told that they could interact with a human customer service representative by clicking the help button. In the service robot condition, they were told that they could interact with an AIpowered chatbot by clicking the help button.

In all four conditions, participants encountered 10 drivingrelated questions in the app, with each question displayed on a separate page. They could click the help-seeking button to chat with a human agent or a service robot to receive a hint to help them answer the question. Hints provided were identical across conditions to control for content variation. No rewards or incentives were offered for correct answers. The frequency of button clicks was recorded as an index of participants’ helpseeking tendencies. After completing the survey, participants rated the app on a 1 (highly negative) to 7 (highly positive) scale. Additionally, after reading the definitions of outcome focus and process focus, participants indicated their focus on a single-item scale ranging from 1 (definitely the process) to 7 (definitely the outcome) in response to the prompt: “When using this APP, do you place more importance on the process or the outcome?” (see Appendix A for the detailed stimuli and measures).

## Discussion

Study 3 provides robust support for earlier findings by leveraging users’ actual behaviors to demonstrate that individuals are more likely to seek help in utilitarian contexts and show a strong preference for human assistance over robotic support in these scenarios. This tendency is significantly less pronounced in hedonic contexts. Furthermore, the study reveals substantial downstream effects of these behaviors, highlighting that users value human assistance more than robotic help during utilitarian experiences, as evidenced by more favorable app evaluations when human help is available. Last, to rule out potential alternative explanations discussed in Study 2, we carried out an additional post-test (see Appendix B) to check whether these same alternative explanations could account for the results. The findings were consistent with Study 2 and support the idea that our main results in Study 3 were not driven by those alternative factors.

By directly measuring both help-seeking behaviors and subsequent app evaluations, this study not only strengthens the existing theoretical framework but also provides new practical insights for service design on digital platforms. The observed differences in help-seeking behavior across various experiential contexts and types of assistance offer important guidance for optimizing user support mechanisms in mobile apps, aiming to better address diverse user needs and enhance user experiences.

## General Discussion

Our findings, derived from the three studies—including secondhand data analysis, an online experiment, and an observational study on real user behaviors—offer valuable insights into users’ online help-seeking behaviors. Specifically, we explored the impact of the experience type and the help provider type. We reveal that users are more likely to seek assistance when faced with challenges in a utilitarian experience, as opposed to a hedonic one. Furthermore, our results show a clear preference for humans over robots when users encounter difficulties in utilitarian contexts. However, this preference does not emerge when users experience challenges in hedonic contexts.

Study 1 analyzed help-seeking behavior on Quora, showing that users were more likely to seek help by posting questions in the online forum for utilitarian apps as compared to hedonic apps. Study 2 extended this research in a controlled setting, revealing that users in utilitarian contexts were more likely to seek help and preferred human assistance when help was needed, while no clear preference for help provider type emerged in hedonic contexts. Additionally, a moderated mediation model demonstrated that the effect of utilitarian (vs. hedonic) experience on help-seeking tendencies was driven by an outcome (vs. process) focus and moderated by the help provider type. This study also ruled out potential alternative explanations, including perceived efficiency, task complexity, adversity, subjectivity, and the importance of consumption goals. Finally, Study 3, conducted in a custom mobile app for driving training or gaming, replicated these findings by observing actual user behavior. It found that users were more likely to seek help in utilitarian contexts, and those who received human assistance evaluated the app more positively than those interacting with service robots, particularly in utilitarian experiences. Together, these studies highlight the role of experience type, help provider type, and focus type in shaping help-seeking behavior.

## Theoretical Contributions

This research makes several theoretical contributions. First, our findings extend previous research on understanding the antecedents of online help-seeking behavior. Prior studies have identified various factors influencing individuals’ inclination to seek help online (e.g., Chen & Zahedi, 2016; Chen et al., 2016; Subramani et al., 2021). Research in this field has primarily focused on examining the characteristics of help seekers (e.g., Best et al., 2014; Chen & Zahedi, 2016; Kim et al., 2018) and help providers (e.g., Kim et al., 2016; Mousavi et al., 2020) that could impact users’ tendency to seek help online. Kim et al. (2016), for example, focused exclusively on gaming, a typical hedonic experience. Our paper extends this work by systematically examining how different types of experiences— utilitarian vs. hedonic—affect help-seeking tendencies. While our findings align with their theorizing, we broaden the scope by exploring a variety of experience types. Additionally, we differentiated our research from Subramani et al. (2021) by examining a different mechanism. Whereas Subramani et al. (2021) focused on the availability of information from alternative sources, our study investigated how the nature of the experience itself influences help-seeking behavior. Moreover, prior research has not addressed how experience types may influence consumers’ preferences for human agents vs. service robots as help providers. Unlike individual differences, which are difficult for practitioners to influence, product characteristics, such as whether a product is more utilitarian or hedonic, have stronger practical implications for shaping online help-seeking tendencies. By categorizing products along this spectrum, practitioners can strategically emphasize either utilitarian or hedonic aspects, depending on the resources available to the human agent.

Second, our findings contribute to the literature on user preferences for service robots vs. human agents (e.g., Adam et al., 2022; Han et al., 2022; Holthöwer & van Doorn, 2023; Garvey et al., 2023; Schanke et al., 2021) by highlighting a key but underexplored distinction: the post-purchase usage stage. Prior research has identified conditions under which users prefer algorithms (algorithm preference) versus humans (algorithm aversion). For example, users have been shown to be more receptive to service robots in contexts involving embarrassment or the delivery of bad news, where robots can reduce discomfort (Holthöwer & van Doorn, 2023; Garvey et al., 2023). In contrast, algorithm aversion often arises in subjective, emotionally laden tasks (Castelo et al., 2019; Longoni & Cian, 2022). Extending this work, we demonstrate that in post-purchase contexts—especially in utilitarian experiences—users prefer human agents over robots, which in turn enhances brand attitudes. Crucially, while prior studies focus on pre-purchase decision-making, our study shows that preferences observed in decision contexts do not carry over to usage contexts in a straightforward manner. For instance, although algorithm aversion has been well-documented in subjective decisions, we found that this tendency is attenuated during hedonic usage experiences. This suggests that psychological ownership and active engagement may reshape users’ openness to robotic assistance. These insights have important implications for the design of customer relationship management systems aimed at supporting product usage and customer retention, rather than merely initial adoption.

Third, our findings contribute to the evolution of UMS and HMS theories (e.g., Goodhue & Thompson, 1995; Lowry et al., 2013; Lowry et al., 2015; Venkatesh et al., 2003; Zhang et al., 2019). By delving into users’ online help-seeking behaviors in both utilitarian and hedonic experiences, the study acknowledges the duality inherent in these motivations, providing nuanced insights into user behaviors and motivations. This alignment with the call for more research on HMS systems in the broader IS landscape (Lowry et al., 2013) underscores the study’s relevance. Our research also introduces a dynamic interplay between these motivation systems, suggesting that users display varying tolerance levels for issues during integrated experiences. Moreover, previous studies in this domain have primarily focused on identifying antecedents that influence users’ preferences between utilitarian and hedonic experiences (e.g., Dhar & Wertenbroch, 2000; Okada, 2005). Only recently have scholars started examining the outcomes of utilitarian versus hedonic experiences (e.g., Botti & McGill, 2011; Whitley et al., 2018). Our study also contributes to this expanding body of literature by revealing a novel consequence of utilitarian (vs. hedonic) experience: an increase (vs. decrease) in help-seeking behavior among users.

Lastly, our findings indicate that the observed effect is driven by a process focus versus an outcome focus, which is triggered by different types of experiences. This aligns with prior research, which shows that utilitarian experiences are often motivated by users’ desire to efficiently accomplish tasks or achieve specific goals, whereas hedonic experiences are driven by the desire to experience psychological enjoyment (Dhar & Wertenbroch, 2000; Hirschman & Holbrook, 1982; Whitley et al., 2018). In addition to these findings, we also observed that utilitarian (vs. hedonic) experiences lead users to prioritize the outcome (vs. process), which consequently provides a higher (vs. lower) motivation to complete tasks efficiently. Thus, our study contributes to research on the effects of a process focus versus an outcome focus. While previous research has mainly focused on the consequences of activating an outcome (vs. process) focus (e.g., Yang et al., 2012; Zhao et al., 2007), our work extends this line of research by demonstrating a novel situational antecedent of users outcome versus process focus (i.e., experience type) and a novel consequence of individuals’ outcome versus process focus (i.e., online help-seeking behavior).

## Managerial Implications

Our findings have several pertinent managerial implications. Online service support has become crucial in shaping user experiences. However, companies may lack a clear understanding of the resources required to establish helpproviding channels, whether types of experiences might influence resource allocation for users’ service support during the post-consumption stage, and how this allocation might occur. Additionally, while service robots provide a costeffective means of assistance, the balancing act between human agents and these technologies warrants careful consideration. Hence, the insights garnered from our present research can empower companies with a contemporary understanding regarding the importance of tailoring precise levels of assistance to users, considering the experience type, help provider type, and cost. This understanding can guide them in optimizing resource allocation and elevating user support, which can thereby enhance user satisfaction and bolster loyalty. For instance, as employing human agents to assist users’ inquiries typically incurs greater costs than using service robots, companies must strategically allocate human resources to optimize outcomes. For example, on e-commerce platforms, consumers tend to be more inclined to seek help by clicking the help-seeking button on webpages featuring utilitarian products than on webpages showcasing hedonic products. Therefore, e-commerce companies can use the prevalence of utilitarian products as a basis for determining the appropriate allocation of human agents to serve customers.

Given the significance of online service support, companies should prioritize establishing effective help channels to enhance users’ online experience. Our findings indicate that companies offering utilitarian experiences should offer multiple channels to provide timely help to their users, as utilitarian users tend to have a higher need for help. Delivering timely assistance for utilitarian experiences and services can also enhance users evaluations of the brand. Importantly, our studies highlight that not all assistance providers have equal efficacy in resolving users’ issues. Companies should especially consider allocating human agents for users facing complex problems related to utilitarian tasks or personalized concerns. However, the decision to deploy human agents vs. service robots requires a careful evaluation of cost-effectiveness. For instance, human support, while more versatile and capable of handling complex inquiries, incurs higher ongoing costs, including salaries and training. In contrast, service robots entail an upfront investment in technology but offer scalability and lower marginal costs over time. Companies should conduct a detailed cost-benefit analysis to determine the optimal mix of human and robotic resources. This analysis should consider not only direct costs but also the impact on customer satisfaction and retention, which directly influence long-term revenue. In essence, a meticulous resource allocation strategy is necessary for companies seeking to offer optimal help resources to their users. This allocation should be attuned to the utilitarian-hedonic nature of the experience in question, the help provider type (human agent or service robot) involved, and the cost-benefit analysis. By strategically aligning their help-provision approach with these considerations, companies can significantly enhance user experiences and bolster overall user satisfaction.

Lastly, our findings suggest that companies have the ability to frame an experience as either hedonic or utilitarian simply by using different descriptions. When a company lacks sufficient resources to provide timely help to users, it may be beneficial to frame the experience hedonically. By doing so, the company can encourage users to solve problems independently instead of relying on the company for assistance. This approach can help companies save on the cost of post-purchase service. On the other hand, if a company has already established effective help-seeking channels that provide a competitive advantage, framing the experience as utilitarian could be a favorable option. This approach can elevate users’ evaluation of the company and reinforce the perception of reliable user support. In conclusion, companies can strategically leverage framing techniques to align their offerings with the level of online support they can provide, thereby optimizing resource allocation and enhancing the overall user experience.

## Limitations and Future Research Directions

The findings of our studies offer intriguing opportunities for future research. First, alternative explanations for our findings should be considered. For instance, participants might perceive utilitarian consumption as more important than hedonic consumption, thereby influencing their likelihood of seeking help in these contexts. To address this, we conducted separate studies across all contexts in Studies 2 and 3 (detailed in Appendix B). The results confirmed no significant difference in the perceived importance between utilitarian and hedonic experiences, thus ruling out this potential explanation. Future research could also explore other alternative explanations. For example, researchers could investigate differences in emotional engagement and the need for control between utilitarian and hedonic experiences. It is also possible that users engaging in utilitarian experiences might feel a stronger sense of urgency, making them more inclined to seek help.

Second, a compelling question arises regarding whether users engaging in hedonic experiences might exhibit a higher intention to seek help if their outcome focus were heightened. Prior research has demonstrated that users become more concerned about the negative consequences of their behaviors (e.g., failure) in a group context as opposed to an individual context (Briley & Wyer, 2002). Building on these previous findings, we predict that there may be contexts in which users engaging in hedonic experiences may prioritize the outcome and strive to avoid negative consequences, particularly in a group context (e.g., playing an online video game with teammates). Consequently, users’ outcome focus could be triggered in such group settings, leading to a greater intention to seek help when faced with difficulties during hedonic experiences (e.g., encountering a challenging task in an online video game). Therefore, it is plausible that the context (group vs. individual) may moderate the effect of the experience type on users’ help-seeking tendencies. Moreover, while our study demonstrates that outcome focus drives help-seeking tendencies in utilitarian contexts, we did not directly measure motivation as part of this mechanism. Future research could explore the role of motivation more explicitly to better understand how it interacts with the outcome focus to influence help-seeking behaviors. For instance, future studies could explore whether individuals in utilitarian contexts demonstrate stronger intrinsic or extrinsic motivation to complete tasks efficiently and how these motivational factors influence their reliance on various sources of help. Such work could provide deeper insights into the psychological drivers of help-seeking behavior and further validate the framework proposed in this research.

Third, in our studies, we focused on users’ help-seeking behavior as a downstream consequence of engaging in utilitarian (vs. hedonic) experiences. However, there are other potential consequences worth exploring in future research. One important direction is to delve into users’ emotional responses when they encounter difficulties. For instance, users engaging in hedonic experiences might assume that problems arising from such experiences are relatively easy to solve, causing them to fear potential embarrassment when seeking help. On the other hand, users might underestimate the difficulty level of the problems they are facing during hedonic experiences. Exploring these possibilities can provide valuable insights into the complex dynamics of users’ helpseeking behaviors. These avenues for investigation represent promising areas for future research.

Fourth, while our research thoroughly examines how the type of help and the type of help provider influcences help-seeking behaviors, we did not specifically explore the impact of problem types or the timing of problem emergence. Future research could investigate how different types of problems— ranging from technical issues to usability questions—impact the choice between human and robotic assistance. Additionally, the timing of the problem’s emergence—e.g., whether it is during critical usage moments or routine operations, or whether is at the initial stage or the advanced stage—may also play a crucial role in determining the type of help sought. Understanding these dynamics could provide deeper insights into the design of more effective user support systems tailored to specific user experiences and situational contexts. Furthermore, although we maintained consistent difficulty levels across utilitarian and hedonic conditions, variations in difficulty might lead to nonlinear changes in users’ help-seeking tendencies. If the difficulty becomes too high, users’ motivation to engage in certain behaviors could decrease. Thus, future research could explore how varying difficulty levels impact users’ motivation to seek help.

Lastly, while this research provides a robust framework for understanding how utilitarian and hedonic experiences influence help-seeking through outcome-focused versus process-focused motivations, certain contexts may blur these distinctions. For example, hedonic experiences, which are typically process-focused, may shift toward an outcome focus in social contexts like competitive multiplayer gaming, where competition or collaboration emphasizes achieving external goals. This highlights a limitation of our binary classification, as situational factors may alter motivational orientations. Future research could explore how social dynamics, such as teamwork or rivalry, impact these shifts within hedonic experiences and investigate whether repeated exposure reinforces or diminishes such changes. Addressing these nuances could extend our framework and enrich the understanding of how motivations evolve in dynamic contexts.

This could relate to productivity improvements and overall satisfaction with sound quality and performance after completing tasks.

## Method

A total of 200 participant $( M _ { \mathrm { a g e } } = 3 9 . 6 3 ; 4 9 . 0 \%$ female) took part in this study via Prolific for a small payment. The study employed a 2 (experience type: utilitarian vs. hedonic) × 2 (help provider: human vs. robot) between-subjects design. Participants were instructed to imagine trying a new headset at the start of the survey.

In the utilitarian condition, they were asked to imagine purchasing an office headset to make their online course more convenient. While trying to understand and use the headset’s new features, they encountered challenges in adjusting the settings to optimize audio quality for online learning. In the hedonic condition, participants imagined acquiring an entertainment headset designed to enhance their music and gaming experience. As they attempted to familiarize themselves with the headset’s new features for better enjoyment, they encountered difficulties in adjusting the settings for optimal audio quality for music and gaming. In both conditions, participants were told the headset company had a dedicated website with a help button for assistance with any problems encountered.

In the human help provider condition, participants were informed that clicking the help button would start a chat with the headset company’s service staff. In contrast, in the robot help provider condition, participants were told that clicking the help button would initiate a chat with the headset company’s AI-powered service robot.

First, as a manipulation check, participants rated the perceived nature of the experience on a scale from 1 (very utilitarian) to 6 (very hedonic) after reading definitions of hedonic and utilitarian experience. Then, participants were asked: “Who was described as providing help in the scenario above?” (1 = human agent; 2 = AI agent).
