# AUTISTIC TRAITS, THEORY OF MIND, AND IMPLICATIONS FOR IT PROFESSIONALS’ EMOTIONAL LABOR AND MENTAL WELL-BEING<sup>1</sup>

Ronnie Jia G. Brint Ryan College of Business, University of North Texas Denton, TX, U.S.A. {RonnieJia@gmail.com}

Cynthia K. Riemenschneider Hankamer School of Business, Baylor University Waco, TX, U.S.A. {C\_Riemenschneider@baylor.edu}

Heather H. Jia College of Business, Illinois State University Normal, IL, U.S.A. {HHJia@ilstu.edu}

Departing from the situation-centric approach common in emotional labor research, we adopt a trait-based perspective to examine how autistic traits shape IT professionals’ experiences of social demands and mental health. Across three quantitative studies—two with IT professionals and one with college students preparing for IT careers—we found that autistic traits are consistently central to IT identities. IT professionals as a group are likely underdiagnosed and, on average, exhibit elevated autistic traits, which, in turn, can heighten their emotional labor and erode mental well-being. Compared with the general population, IT professionals, on average, report substantially higher levels of anxiety and depression, with prevalence rates more than 2.3 times the norm, underscoring the potential mental health costs of sustained emotional labor. These findings suggest that emotional labor is not merely situational but anchored in dispositional factors that are widespread within the IT profession. Results from the Reading the Mind in the Eyes Test further show that theory-of-mind processing is more laborious for IT students than for business management students, thus providing a sociocognitive explanation for many IT professionals’ social challenges. Taken together, these findings advance a disposition-centric paradigm that expands the nomological net of emotional labor and calls for a reexamination of long-standing assumptions about IT work, IT worker identities, and the broader discourse on neurodiversity in technical professions. This work challenges scholars and practitioners alike to embrace autistic traits as an integral and foundational element of IT professional identity and to reimagine IT management as managing neurodiversity.

Keywords: Autism, autistic traits, neurodiversity, theory of mind, emotional labor, well-being, mental health, empathizing-systemizing theory, Reading the Mind in the Eyes Test, AQ

## Introduction

Information technology (IT) professionals refer to individuals responsible for staying current with advancements in hardware and software, developing technology solutions to business problems, and/or supporting business clients’ technology use (Riemenschneider & Armstrong, 2021). One of the many challenges confronted by IT professionals is the need to perform emotional labor in workplace interactions (e.g., Rutner et al., 2008, 2011, 2015; Rutner & Riemenschneider, 2015; Giddens & Riemenschneider, 2020). Emotional labor is defined as the mental effort and internal energy expended to display appropriate emotions and suppress undesirable ones in the workplace (Ashforth & Humphrey, 1993). “Emotional labor may facilitate task effectiveness by regulating interaction and obviating interpersonal problems” (Ashforth & Humphrey, 1993, p. 89), but it could also undermine job satisfaction (Hülsheger & Schewe, 2011; Diefendorff et al., 2011) and heighten work exhaustion (Rutner et al., 2008) and job stress (see Gabriel et al., 2023 for a review), which are urgent and persistent issues in the IT workplace (Riemenschneider & Armstrong, 2021). Thus, emotional labor provides a useful lens with which to study these challenges and explore their impact on IT professionals’ mental health.

Prior work suggests that expectations of appropriate emotional displays may vary by job role—while developers typically have limited direct contact with business clients, technical support personnel frequently interact with clients to troubleshoot problems and are thus more likely to encounter dissatisfied users and perform emotional labor (Rutner et al., 2015). Because IT professionals routinely span boundaries— interacting with internal functional units as well as external vendors and consultants—and bridge technological and business domains (Riemenschneider & Armstrong, 2021), a deeper understanding of IT professionals’ emotional labor is increasingly important.

Existing research has primarily taken a situation-centric approach, successfully identifying workplace situational factors (e.g., organizational mandates for employees’ emotional demeanor) as key drivers of emotional labor among IT professionals. However, these situation-centric models fall short in accounting for dispositional variations that may shape how emotional labor is experienced. This gap is particularly relevant given descriptions of IT professionals as “unsocial” or “uninterested in communication” (Guzman & Stanton, 2009, p. 181), which may reflect underlying autistic traits (Jia et al., 2022). Defined as characteristics associated with the autism spectrum condition that may be present in varying degrees in individuals regardless of their diagnostic status, autistic traits may influence how IT professionals experience social demands. Thus, a promising direction for advancing our understanding of IT professionals’ emotional labor is to adopt a trait-based approach to examine the role of individual characteristics, such as autistic traits. By extending beyond the situation-centric approach, this trait-based explanation is a conceptual advance that broadens the applicability of the emotional labor construct from boundary-spanning roles to anyone who needs to navigate social demands in the workplace—virtually all IT professionals, including those who rarely interact with clients but nevertheless collaborate with IT colleagues (e.g., developers, Rutner et al., 2015).

Existing work on emotional labor has also successfully demonstrated its various organizational implications (e.g., turnover, productivity). However, to account for its multitude of impacts, we must also examine employees’ personal outcomes, such as mental well-being, which is defined as a combination of eudemonic (optimum functioning) and hedonic (positive affect) well-being (Ryan & Deci, 2001). Likely the most important individual outcome, employee well-being has been characterized as the grand challenge of emotional labor research (Gabriel et al., 2023) and the “ultimate criterion for organizational sciences” (Tay et al., 2023, p. 1141).

Therefore, we seek to address these explanatory shortfalls through a conceptual advance on both its antecedent (i.e., autistic traits) and outcome (i.e., well-being) sides. We ask: Do IT professionals’ autistic traits heighten their emotional labor and undermine their mental well-being? Answering this question expands the nomological net of the emotional labor construct and responds to the call to address the grand challenge of employee well-being in emotional labor research (Gabriel et al., 2023).

Since autism is concomitant with several mental health conditions (e.g., Bernardin et al., 2021; Linden et al., 2023), the importance of investigating IT professionals’ mental well-being may be accentuated if they have elevated autistic traits. Thus, we ask: Do IT professionals exhibit higher autistic traits than the general population? Answering this question can contribute to both emotional labor research and a deeper understanding of IT professionals’ psychological profile and their social difficulties, offering a new perspective to the age-old question, “Are IS people different?” (Ferratt & Short, 1986). If they are, then the obvious next question is why?

To unpack this difference and explore its possible underlying mechanisms, we ask: Why might social communication be more laborious for IT professionals? To answer these research questions, we conduct three quantitative studies—two with IT professionals and one with students—each addressing a distinct question.

The overarching theoretical framework guiding this research is the empathizing-systemizing (E-S) theory, which posits that individual differences in personality, cognition, and social behavior can be explained by one’s capacity for empathizing (i.e., understanding people) and systemizing (i.e., understanding logical systems) (Baron-Cohen, 2002). While systemizing supports the analysis of structured, rule-based systems, it offers limited utility in navigating dynamic interpersonal interactions, which require empathizing (Baron-Cohen, 2002)—and especially theory of mind (Premack & Woodruff, 1978), which refers to one’s capacity to attribute mental states (e.g., beliefs, desires, intentions) to others in order to interpret and predict their behavior (Baron-Cohen et al., 1985; Baron-Cohen & Hammer, 1997). Thus, E-S theory provides a valuable framework for juxtaposing the strengths and weaknesses observed in many IT professionals. Figure 1 presents the overarching E-S theory and the complementary theoretical frameworks underpinning each study—vocational interest theory in Study 1, theory of mind in Study 2, and cognitive dissonance, conservation of resources, and allostatic load theories in Study 3.

As summarized in Figure 1, to effectively present this multi-study, multi-theory investigation, the three research questions (RQ) are examined in the following order: We begin by establishing the phenomenon (RQ1: Do IT professionals exhibit elevated autistic traits?) and then examine the underlying mechanism, such as theory of mind capacity (RQ2: Why might social communication be more laborious to IT professionals?), and finally explore the implications of autistic traits for IT professionals’ emotional labor and mental well-being (RQ3: How do autistic traits shape workplace experiences and outcomes?).

## Theoretical Development

While E-S theory serves as the overarching theory (Figure 1), several complementary theories are introduced to further enhance hypothesis development for the specific aims of each study. We begin with prior research on neurodiversity and autistic traits.

## Neurodiversity and Autistic Traits

Neurodiversity, a concept that has recently been introduced into IS research (Loiacono & Ren, 2018; Jia et al., 2022), refers to the natural neurological differences in the human genome regarding attention, learning, mood, sociability, and other mental functions (Armstrong, 2011). Although the term was originally coined to counter the deficit-laden overtones in the clinical diagnosis of “autistic spectrum disorder” (Griffiths, 2017), it has, over time, been broadened to include six other neurodevelopmental conditions, such as attentiondeficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and dyslexia (Loiacono & Ren, 2018). Recent IS research has argued for a more expansive view of neurodiversity (Turel & Bechara, 2024). This work centers on autism and autistic traits, which are introduced next.

As detailed in Table 1, autism spectrum condition, or autism, is a neurodevelopmental condition that is characterized by (1) pervasive deficits in reciprocal social communication and interaction, and (2) restricted, repetitive patterns of behavior, interests, or activities, which also form the two essential diagnostic criteria according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) (APA, 2013).

Though defined by its dual diagnostic criteria, autism is associated with a constellation of autistic traits, which refer to characteristics associated with autism that may be present to varying degrees in individuals, regardless of their diagnostic status. In other words, despite its binary clinical diagnosis (positive/negative), autism is a spectrum condition, and autistic traits are continuously distributed throughout the whole population, with most people at subclinical levels (Constantino & Todd, 2003; Ruzich et al., 2015a). Indeed, reflecting this view is the popularity of selfreport autistic trait measures. For instance, the Autism Spectrum Quotient (AQ-50) (Baron-Cohen et al., 2001b), a widely used screening instrument in clinical settings and a measure of autistic traits in the general population (Happé & Frith, 2020), assesses five core traits (i.e., social skills, communication, attention to detail, attention switching, and imagination). This research considers both (binary) autism and (continuous) autistic traits across its three studies.

In contrast to the traditional pathological view, the neurodiversity lens takes a strength-based approach to autism, acknowledging both its advantages and disadvantages (Table 1). We next use E-S theory to examine these strengths and weaknesses, such as emotional labor.

## Empathizing-Systemizing (E-S) Theory

E-S theory posits that the mind has a social dimension (empathizing) and a nonsocial dimension (systemizing) and that one’s ability to understand people (empathizing) and logical systems (systemizing) accounts for individual differences in various personality, cognitive, and social factors (Baron-Cohen, 2002). As summarized in Table 1, although systemizing helps one understand and predict the law-governed inanimate world, it is of limited use for predicting and responding to moment-by-moment changes in another person’s emotion and behavior, which requires empathizing (Baron-Cohen, 2002). Thus, E-S theory predicts that those high in autistic traits exhibit impaired empathizing along with hyper-systemizing (e.g., Baron-Cohen, 2009, 2020; Baron-Cohen et al., 2001b, 2007; Baron-Cohen & Wheelwright, 2004). We next discuss the implications of high systemizing and low empathizing.

## Systemizing

As systemizing allows one to predict and control the behavior of a system (Baron-Cohen, 2002), recent IS research has found a linkage between autistic traits and intrinsic interest in technology (Jia et al., 2022), which may lead to the pursuit of a technical education and a career in technical fields. Empirical E-S research has shown that those in science, technology, engineering, and math (STEM) fields exhibit higher autistic traits than those in the humanities and social sciences (Baron-Cohen et al., 2001b, 2007; Kidron et al., 2018). Conversely, individuals with high autistic traits exhibit a strong drive to systemize (Baron-Cohen, 2006, 2008; Baron-Cohen et al., 2003; Wheelwright et al., 2006) and high aptitudes in systemizing fields, such as STEM (Baron-Cohen, 2002).

Among the various systemizing fields, computers would seem to be “an ideal interest” for those with high autistic traits because you not only “do not have to talk to or socialise with them, but … they are logical, consistent and not prone to moods” (Attwood, 1997, p. 94). IS researchers have also observed that some special talents associated with autism are a great fit for certain IT jobs, such as software testing, cybersecurity, and other work demanding excellent concentration and visual thought (Loiacono & Ren, 2018; Wareham & Sonne, 2008). Since autism is highly genetic (heritability > 80%; Bai et al., 2019), it is not surprising that this “geek syndrome” is “surging” in Silicon Valley children (Silberman, 2001), which has led to a “disproportionately high demand for autism services” in Santa Clara County, California (Silberman, 2015), the cradle of the U.S. technology industry.

Other research has also found that computer hackers exhibit high autistic traits along with poor social communication skills, obsessional interests, and intense task focus (e.g., Baron-Cohen et al., 1999; Hunter, 2009; Klawe, 2001; Seigfried-Spellar et al., 2015). One cybersecurity expert observed that “almost all of the hackers that he had met had shown classical autistic traits” (Patel, 2012, p. 4). In sum, cumulative evidence from E-S theory literature and other autism research suggests that individuals with high autistic traits are likely to be attracted to technology and the IT field.

Complementing E-S theory is Holland’s theory of vocational interests. In early work, vocational interests were conceptualized as “expressions of personality” (Holland, 1966, p. 278), and occupational types were labeled “personality types” (Holland, 1973). The theory of vocational interests (Holland, 1966, 1973) postulates that individuals, when given the choice, will strive toward vocations that allow them to express their personality and that this match between personal tendencies and occupational requirements promises effective adjustment and eventually career success, making the career choice personally rewarding (Dilchert, 2007). In other words, individuals seek, derive satisfaction from, and remain in work environments where good person-job congruence exists (Holland, 1985). As a result, one of the most consistent findings in vocational psychology is that people are differentially attracted to careers as a function of their own personality (Dilchert, 2007). Thus, to the extent that autistic traits are associated with an interest in technology (Jia et al., 2022), the theory of vocational interests predicts that these traits will likely lead to an education and career in technology.

At the convergence of E-S theory and vocational interest theory, we argue that people are not randomly assigned to the IT profession; when given the choice, they self-select into and out of the IT profession based on a host of individual characteristics, such as autistic traits. Attraction to a profession, selection by it, and attrition from it yield particular kinds of persons in a profession (Schneider, 1987). For IT, it is likely those with higher autistic traits. For instance, IT professionals have been characterized in ways that align with low empathizing, high systemizing, and symptoms specified in the DSM-5 diagnostic criteria for autism, such as being less warm and less outgoing (Moore, 1991); less social and single-minded in technical pursuits (Guzman & Stanton, 2009); or analytical, logical, predictable, inflexible, and resistant to change (Wynekoop & Walz, 1998). These attributes are not exclusive to men in IT but also extend to many women in IT, who may describe themselves as “mathematical,” “logical,” and “less social than other women” (Trauth, 2002, p. 110). It appears that those with high autistic traits, regardless of gender, may self-select into IT work and education.

H1: Compared to the general population, IT professionals exhibit higher levels of autistic traits.

It is important to note that while we posit that IT professionals as a group exhibit elevated autistic traits, there is likely considerable heterogeneity within the group, mirroring the normal distribution of autistic traits observed in the general population (Happé & Frith, 2020).

Autistic traits are examined as a continuous variable in H1 (as well as in H4), where a quantitative difference is hypothesized. However, they can also be assessed in qualitative terms if (continuous) autistic trait scores exceed a clinical threshold (dichotomous), indicating probable diagnosis. In this case, we can examine whether IT professionals are also qualitatively different.

H2a: Compared to the general population, a greater proportion of IT professionals exhibit clinically significant levels of autistic traits, indicating probable diagnosis.<sup>2</sup>

However, this does not mean that a greater proportion of IT professionals have been diagnosed. Since universal autism screening for all U.S. children was not recommended by the American Academy of Pediatrics until 2007 (Johnson & Myers, 2007), the current IT workforce in the U.S. is likely underdiagnosed. However, given its high heritability (> 80%; Bai et al., 2019), the condition is likely more prevalent in IT professionals’ immediate families (Sasson et al., 2013a), which can provide corroborating evidence for the underdiagnosis in the IT workforce.

H2b: Autism is currently underdiagnosed among IT professionals.

H2c: Autism diagnoses are more prevalent in IT professionals’ immediate families than in the general population.

## Empathizing

Derived from the Greek forms en (“in”) and pathein (“to feel” or “to suffer”)—i.e., feeling from the inside (Cerniglia et al., 2019)—empathy has been defined as one’s ability to infer and share another’s emotional experiences (Gallese, 2003). A deficit in empathizing might account for the social difficulties in autism (Baron-Cohen et al., 2002), as it can heighten emotional labor and mental health challenges and affect sociocognitive functioning.

Theory of mind: As a primary trait governing individual differences in social ability (Baron-Cohen, 2009), empathizing underpins the capacity to accurately identify others’ emotions (i.e., cognitive empathy) and to vicariously experience and respond to others’ emotional states (i.e., affective or emotional empathy) (Baron-Cohen, 2002; Baron-Cohen & Wheelwright, 2004). Between the two aspects of empathizing, cognitive empathy, often referred to as theory of mind (Table 1), is a prerequisite since an ineffective mind reader cannot formulate a socially appropriate affective response to another’s mental state (Baron-Cohen, 2002).

Theory of mind (ToM) (Premack & Woodruff, 1978), also known as “mentalizing” (Frith et al., 1991) or “mind reading” (Baron-Cohen et al., 1997), refers to the capacity to understand others by ascribing mental states (e.g., beliefs, desires, intentions, pretense, imagination) to them (Baron-Cohen et al., 1985; Baron-Cohen & Hammer, 1997). As a component of social cognition (Adolphs, 2002), ToM is the major way in which individuals make sense of or predict social behavior (Baron-Cohen & Hammer, 1997) and is a cornerstone of human adaptation to complex and effective social institutions (Senju, 2012). Since the concept was first introduced (Premack & Woodruff, 1978), ToM has been central in various behavioral, cognitive, and neuroscience fields (Senju, 2012). Although ToM was initially proposed as a theory of autism that explains the social communication difficulties in autistic people (Baron-Cohen, 1990; Baron-Cohen et al., 1985), ADHD was later also found to be related to ToM challenges (Korkmaz, 2011). We next present sociocognitive and neurocognitive evidence linking autism with ToM deficits.

In sociocognitive research, while most typically developing children can pass first-order belief tests (i.e., inferring someone’s beliefs) by four years of age and second-order belief tests (i.e., “John thinks that Mary thinks x”) by five or six years, almost all autistic children have difficulty with these ToM tests at those age levels (Baron-Cohen & Hammer, 1997). These difficulties persist into adolescence and adulthood, affecting pragmatic language and social reciprocity (Livingston et al., 2019). Advanced ToM tests have been developed to detect more subtle differences in adults’ capacity to “tune in” to another’s mental state (Baron-Cohen et al., 2001a). One such test is the Reading the Mind in the Eyes Test, or the Eyes Test (Baron-Cohen et al., 1997, 2001a), which involves looking at photographs of the eye region of the faces and inferring the mental states of the persons in the photographs. The test shows that autistic adults with normal or above-average intelligence nevertheless have lower accuracy than controls, evidencing subtle impairments in mind reading and social cognition in high-functioning adults on the autistic continuum (Baron-Cohen et al., 1997, 2001a). Eye-tracking research also shows that even those who successfully pass standard ToM tests when explicitly prompted do not spontaneously or intuitively anticipate others’ actions (Senju et al., 2009). Instead, many rely on deliberate and effortful compensatory processing, which is mentally exhausting (Hull et al., 2019) yet still inefficient for spontaneously inferring others’ mental states in dynamic social settings.

In neurocognitive research, a neurological basis has been identified for ToM deficits in autism. A meta-analysis of functional MRI studies showed that autistic people exhibit reduced activity in key ToM-related regions, including the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), the posterior superior temporal sulcus (pSTS), the precuneus, and the amygdala (Schurz et al., 2021). In contrast, affective empathy is related to the inferior frontal gyrus (IFG), thus identifying distinct neuroanatomical bases for cognitive (ToM) and affective empathy (Shamay-Tsoory et al., 2009).

ToM deficits can undermine social functioning (Wieckowski et al., 2020) as mind-blindness leads to an inability to impute beliefs to others and to predict their behavior (Baron-Cohen, 1990). Research has shown that lower ToM capability is associated with poorer social and conversational abilities (Peterson et al., 2016), such as violations of conversational norms (e.g., providing too much, too little, or irrelevant information, Alkire et al., 2023) and greater difficulty in initiating and maintaining interpersonal relationships and achieving mutual understanding (Niedenthal & Brauer, 2012). See Table 1 for more details about ToM.

The framing of autism as a spectrum condition, with associated traits and social, communicative, and broader cognitive variation continuously distributed throughout the population (Ruzich et al., 2015a; Sasson et al., 2013b), suggests that ToM deficits observed in the more severe end of the spectrum may also be observable in milder forms in the general population (Greene et al., 2020). To the extent that those in computing fields exhibit high autistic traits (Baron-Cohen et al., 2001b), they are also likely to have varying degrees of weakness in ToM, which can affect their ability to attribute mental states of their business clients.

In the IS literature, some initial evidence of ToM challenges among IT professionals, even at the executive levels, has been documented, though not recognized as a sociocognitive deficit. For instance, a case study (Pearlson et al., 2015) describes the experience of a newly promoted CIO, whose apparent ToM difficulties nearly led to his dismissal after failing to see the perspective of the business executives and likely missing numerous behavioral cues for months. It was only after a direct and stern warning by the CEO that the CIO realized that the business executives wanted him to not dwell on technology and instead “communicate with them in words they understood” (Pearlson et al., 2015, p. 165). This is a classic case of an otherwise highly intelligent (hyper-systemizing) IT person lacking the empathic capability to spontaneously and intuitively “tune in” to business clients’ mental states. Thus, strategic alignment of IT to business objectives can be viewed as a challenge rooted in IT executives’ theory of mind capacities.

## H3a: IT professionals have lower accuracy in theory of mind tasks than their business clients.

In addition to accuracy, it has been noted that response time (RT) or “speed of processing … may be a fruitful way to explore individual difference on [a ToM] task” (Baron-Cohen et al., 2001a, p. 247). Defined as the elapsed time between the onset of a stimulus and one’s response to the stimulus (Luce, 1991), RT is positively related to task complexity or cognitive load (e.g., Turcotte & Oddson, 2022). Jensen (2006) notes that “the larger the task’s information load, the greater is the total processing time” (p. 161). Indeed, research using the Eyes Test has found that autistic people are not only less accurate but also slower (Li et al., 2022; Sucksmith et al., 2013). In the general population, higher autistic traits also lead to longer RT, though not necessarily lower accuracy (Miu et al., 2012). For both autistic individuals and those with high autistic traits, the longer RT results from an inability to attribute mental states spontaneously or intuitively (Senju et al., 2009) and the associated use of deliberate, effortful reasoning as a compensatory strategy (Livingston et al., 2019), which requires more time than effortless automatic processing. Thus, to the extent that IT professionals exhibit higher autistic traits, they will find ToM tasks more cognitively demanding, leading to longer RT.

## H3b: IT professionals have longer RT in theory of mind tasks than their business clients.

Emotional labor: Hochschild (1983, p. 7) describes emotional labor as “the management of feeling to create a publicly observable facial and bodily display.” Emotional labor can involve either surface acting or deep acting (Hochschild, 1983). Surface acting refers to displaying emotions that align with organizational norms but contradict one’s true feelings (Rutner & Riemenschneider, 2015). Research has shown that surface acting is a significant predictor of employee exhaustion, while deep acting (i.e., modifying one's feelings to match the expected emotional display) has either a weak or nonsignificant relationship with work exhaustion (e.g., Hülsheger & Schewe, 2011; Rutner et al., 2011). Therefore, this research focuses on surface acting.

As discussed earlier, existing IS research on emotional labor has typically focused on emotion display rules at the IT workplace as its primary driver (e.g., Rutner et al., 2008, 2011, 2015; Rutner & Riemenschneider, 2015). However, social psychology research has long established that human behavior reflects the interplay of individual characteristics and situational factors (Lewin, 1936). Thus, a situationcentric approach may overlook the role played by individual traits in view of the various business-IT communication problems (e.g., Hornik et al., 2003; Wynekoop & Walz, 1998). For example, IT professionals have been found to be “uninterested in communication” and “unsocial” (Guzman & Stanton, 2009, p. 181). It has been noted that such social difficulties and the resulting coping strategies can lead to stigmatization and attempts to conceal their “stigmatizing mark” (i.e., social ineptness) to smooth and manage tension while also trying to minimize business interactions (Moore & Love, 2004). However, to the extent that IT professionals are “uninterested in communication,” their social difficulties are unlikely to be limited to client interactions. Rather, such difficulties likely extend to interactions with IT coworkers. In other words, social interactions—whether boundary spanning or internal—can be emotionally laborious for those “unsocial” ones. For example, Whitten (1995) reports a case where communication failures between developers and a system integration team undermined the latter team’s effectiveness and morale.

What is the possible origin of such characteristics in IT professionals? These earlier IS studies become less puzzling when viewed through the lens of neurodiversity, as one essential feature of autism is social communication difficulties. Furthermore, it has been widely noted that autistic people often use various strategies and behaviors to cope with social interactions and that autistic traits are related to camouflaging attempts (e.g., Hull et al., 2017), which have been similarly observed in IT professionals (e.g., Moore & Love, 2004). Cook et al. (2021, p. 2) notes:

Non-autistic people similarly use social behaviour to create desirable social impressions and facilitate positive outcomes in interactions with others (i.e., impression management or self-presentation behaviours …). However, … autistic people are more likely to experience a mismatch between their natural way of being and the demands of the social environment … camouflaging represents an attempt to manage this mismatch between a person’s autistic way of being and the non-autistic social environment.

Thus, the neurodiversity lens acknowledges the possibility of individual neurocognitive differences (e.g., a weakness in empathizing) as a driver of emotional labor, especially in IT work settings without explicit emotion display rules. More specifically, E-S theory and the neurodiversity lens provide a trait-based explanation by conceptualizing an IT professional’s emotional labor as a mismatch between one’s weakness in empathizing and the socially oriented workplace (despite the alignment between their strength in systemizing and technical work).

Existing IS models of emotional labor, rooted in situationcentric conceptualizations from organizational research, typically carry the implicit assumption that IT professionals resemble human service workers and lack an innate inclination toward social aversion and avoidance. The neurodiversity lens, with its inherent focus on individual traits, relaxes this assumption, and IT professionals with higher autistic traits are posited to experience greater emotional labor.

H4: Autistic traits are positively related to IT professionals’ emotional labor.

This study focuses on an autistic trait particularly relevant to one’s response to social demands: social communication challenge, or social challenge.<sup>3</sup> Defined as one’s level of impairment in verbal and nonverbal social interactions and communication (Jia et al., 2022), it captures the social dimension of the dual DSM-5 diagnostic criteria of autism (Table 1). After examining antecedents of emotional labor, we next discuss its implications.

Work exhaustion and turnover intention: When employees exhibit emotional demeanor expected in customer interactions, they publicly display certain emotions while suppressing others in order to meet higher-order performance goals despite the potential detrimental effects on their psychological health (Hochschild, 1983). One such adverse outcome is work exhaustion, which refers to the depletion of the mental and emotional energy required to fulfill job demands (Moore, 2000) and is a core dimension of burnout (Maslach & Jackson, 1981). How then does emotional labor influence work exhaustion?

At least two theories can explain this mechanism. Applying cognitive dissonance theory (Festinger, 1957), Hochschild (1983) argued that suppressing one’s genuine emotions and adopting the required demeanor creates inner tension or dissonance, and that such incongruence with oneself can, over time, cause psychological problems. Thus, the dissonance paradigm views adverse outcomes like work exhaustion as a result of inner tension and incongruence.

Conservation of resources (COR) theory (Hobfoll et al., 2018) provides another explanation. Emotional labor requires considerable mental effort: employees must continuously monitor their actual and desired emotions and invest sustained effort to amplify, suppress, or fake emotional expressions, which drains mental resources, heightens strain, and diminishes well-being (Grandey, 2003; Hülsheger & Schewe, 2011). Thus, in the COR paradigm, exhaustion results from an effortful regulatory process that depletes one’s mental resources (Holman et al., 2008).

In sum, both the dissonance and resource depletion paradigms associate emotional labor with adverse psychological outcomes. The link between emotional labor and work exhaustion is well established in organizational research, with meta-analyses reporting correlations of 0.374 (Hülsheger & Schewe, 2011) and 0.32 (Kammeyer-Mueller et al., 2013). As a “meso-term stress reaction” (Dormann & van de Ven, 2014), emotional exhaustion develops within a few months to years (Taris et al., 2005). Longitudinal studies have reported lagged effects of emotional labor on exhaustion for periods ranging from 4 to 24 months (see review in Winkler et al., 2024).

H5: Emotional labor is positively related to IT professionals’ work exhaustion.

One of the adverse implications of work exhaustion is turnover intention. As in H5, COR theory provides a framework for understanding this relationship. Because individuals strive to acquire, maintain, and safeguard their resources, COR predicts that when persistent exhaustion depletes these resources, employees may opt to leave their positions to escape excessive demands and prevent further resource loss (Moore, 2000). Thus, turnover serves as a coping mechanism for exhausted, resource-depleted employees. Since Moore’s (2000) initial work linking IT professionals’ work exhaustion to turnover intention, many IS studies have supported this relationship (e.g., Ahuja et al., 2007; Joseph et al., 2007; Zaza et al., 2023).

H6: Work exhaustion is positively related to IT professionals’ turnover intention.

Beyond economic and material indicators of well-being, there has been growing interest in one’s mental well-being, which refers to a combination of eudemonic (optimum functioning) and hedonic (positive affect) well-being, such as “feeling good and functioning well” (Ryan & Deci, 2001). In the IS literature, in addition to technology users’ well-being (Turel & Bechara, 2024), IT professionals’ well-being has also been an issue of concern in research on turnover intention and burnout (e.g., Ahuja et al., 2007; Armstrong et al., 2015; Joseph et al., 2007).

In organizational research, work exhaustion has been consistently found to undermine both work-related and general well-being (see Hülsheger & Schewe, 2011, and Winkler et al., 2024, for reviews). This linkage between exhaustion and wellbeing suggests that exhaustion is likely a mediator between emotional labor and well-being.

To better understand this mechanism, we move beyond studies using subjective measures to examine research on physiological impacts. According to the allostatic load model (McEwen, 1998), one’s stress response involves activation of allostatic (i.e., neural, neuroendocrine, neuroendocrineimmune) mechanisms, and prolonged or repeated activation results in a heavy allostatic load, increasing health risks such as hypertension (Winkler et al., 2024). Consistent with this model, experimental subjects instructed to suppress their genuine emotions in customer interactions exhibit higher heart rates (Rohrmann et al., 2011) and blood pressure (Hopp et al., 2010) than those instructed to freely express their true feelings. Beyond these immediate effects of emotional labor, longitudinal research has also found a lagged effect on blood pressure over three years (Winkler et al., 2024). Thus, aligning with Hochschild’s (1983) contention that emotional labor can, over time, cause health problems, physiological evidence provides support for its direct impact on well-being.

In view of evidence for both direct and mediated relationships, exhaustion might partially mediate emotional labor and wellbeing. Thus, in conjunction with H5 (emotional labor → work exhaustion), work exhaustion is hypothesized in H7 as a partial mediator between emotional labor and well-being. Since work exhaustion involves the gradual depletion of the mental and emotional energy required to meet job demands (Moore, 2000) and typically develops over several months to years (Taris et al., 2005), its impact on mental well-being— which encompasses positive affect and optimal functioning (Ryan & Deci, 2001)—likely follows a similar timeline, consistent with physiological research showing a multi-year lagged effect (Winkler et al., 2024).

H7: Work exhaustion is negatively related to IT professionals’ mental well-being.

Well-being and mental health challenges: Implied in the above hypotheses is a mediated relationship between autistic traits and well-being (i.e., ↑autistic traits → ↑emotional labor → ↑work exhaustion → ↓mental well-being). However, prior work suggests the possibility of a direct linkage (i.e., partial mediation) because mental well-being is negatively correlated with mental health challenges (Keyes, 2005), which are concomitant with autism (e.g., Bernardin et al., 2021). More specifically, nearly three out of every four autistic individuals experience severe stress, anxiety, or depression (Linden et al., 2023). Since mental well-being is a combination of both eudemonic (optimum functioning) and hedonic (positive affect) well-being (Ryan & Deci, 2001), mental health challenges encountered by autistic people likely affect their well-being.

There exist at least three mechanisms for the increased risk of ill-being and mental health challenges. The sociocognitive mechanism, directly related to E-S theory, stems from the weakness in empathizing (Table 1). A reduced capability to intuitively recognize others’ mental states, understand implicit social rules, and navigate interpersonal dynamics cannot be remedied by strengths in systemizing. Consequently, lower empathic capacities can hinder social interactions and lead to social rejection or marginalization (Mazurek, 2014), thus undermining self-esteem, life satisfaction, and overall wellbeing (Livingston et al., 2019; Syharat et al., 2023).

The behavioral mechanism, rooted in the social compensation hypothesis (Livingston & Happé, 2017), involves the internalization of (neurotypical) social norms (Syharat et al., 2023) and the associated attempts to compensate for or camouflage one’s sociocognitive difficulties and autistic persona in social interactions (Hull et al., 2019). However, these behavioral strategies require considerable mental energy (Cook et al., 2021) and create inner tension with one’s authentic self, increasing the risks of ill-being through processes consistent with the resource depletion and dissonance paradigms discussed earlier (H5).

The physiological mechanism, grounded in the allostatic load model (McEwen, 1998), explains how the above sociocognitive and behavioral mechanisms physiologically affect well-being. Due to their lower empathic capabilities and use of masking strategies, daily social and workplace interactions can be stressful to those with high autistic traits and repeatedly trigger stress responses, leading to prolonged allostatic activation and increased health risks (Winkler et al., 2024). In view of the above sociocognitive, behavioral, and physiological mechanisms, IT professionals with higher autistic traits are expected to report lower well-being. However, it is crucial to recognize that ill-being is not an intrinsic feature of high autistic traits—all three mechanisms are activated in social environments.

H8a: Autistic traits are negatively related to IT professionals’ mental well-being.

Since mental well-being and mental health are correlated (Keyes, 2005), a corollary of H8a is a positive relationship between autistic traits and mental health challenges. Because there exist clinical cut-points and normative data for mental health conditions, we next compare the mental health challenges experienced by IT professionals to the population norm. In view of the sociocognitive, behavioral, and physiological mechanisms discussed above, to the extent that IT professionals exhibit higher autistic traits (Baron-Cohen et al., 2001b) and these traits are associated with greater mental health challenges (a corollary of H8a), IT professionals are expected to report more severe symptoms of anxiety and depression—two mental health conditions described in detail in Table 2. This results not only from the dispositional factors (i.e., autistic traits) discussed here but also from occupational stressors (i.e., perceived workload, time pressure) in the IT profession (Riemenschneider & Armstrong, 2021). A confluence of these intrinsic and situational factors can heighten IT professionals’ mental health risk in ways not seen in many other fields. However, as noted earlier, since these factors are only activated in certain social and work settings, mental health challenges are not an intrinsic characteristic of IT professionals.

## Study 1

To establish the phenomenon that IT professionals not only exhibit higher autistic traits than the general population quantitatively (H1) but are also more likely to meet the clinical cut-point for probable diagnoses qualitatively (H2a), we conducted Study 1 with IT professionals in a multi-billiondollar privately held enterprise operating in several industries in the U.S. Midwest.

## Method: Study 1

The participating organization had 90 IT employees working in various technical roles. All were invited to participate. A total of 32 IT professionals completed the online survey, though four of them did not fill out demographic questions at the end of the questionnaire (Table 3).

Autistic traits were measured using the 50-item Autism Spectrum Quotient (AQ) (Baron-Cohen et al., 2001b). It has a scoring range of 0-50 and comes with normative data as well as a clinical cut point—a score of 26 or higher indicates that one might meet the diagnostic threshold. At this cutoff, its sensitivity (true positive) and specificity (true negative) ratios are such that 83% of cases are correctly classified (Woodbury-Smith et al., 2005). As noted earlier, however, the AQ instrument is not a diagnostic tool.

An invisible page timer was embedded in each survey page in all three studies, which provided evidence for participant attentiveness (i.e., averaging at least 2.0 seconds per item). Details about the use of the page timer are reported elsewhere (Jia et al., 2026).

## Results: Study 1

The average AQ-50 score across the 32 respondents was 21.56 (SD = 6.72, Cronbach’s α = 0.77). Among the 28 IT professionals who answered demographic questions, four selfidentified as autistic (AQ-50 scores: 22, 25, 25, and 33), though none had been formally diagnosed (Table 3).

Based on the mean AQ-50 scores in the general population $( M _ { m e n } = 1 7 . 8 9 , M _ { w o m e n } = 1 4 . 8 8$ , Ruzich et al., 2015b) and the gender composition in this sample (67.9% men), a genderweighted test statistic of 16.93 was computed to control for gender in the one-sample t-test. The results show that the IT sample mean of 21.56 was significantly higher than the test statistic $( t = 3 . 9 0 , p < 0 . 0 0 1$ , medium effect size: Cohen’s d = 0.69, Hedges’ g = 0.67), thus supporting H1.

In addition to the above quantitative difference, the results revealed a qualitative distinction, as nine respondents (28.1%) met the cut-off (≥ 26), indicating probable diagnoses. This proportion is significantly higher than the general population (3.05%, z = 8.25, p < 0.001). In the most conservative case of all nonrespondents (58) scoring below the cut-off, the prevalence rate (9 out of 90) would still be significantly higher than the norm (z = 3.42, p < 0.001), thus supporting H2a.

## Discussion: Study 1

As hypothesized, the IT professionals scored quantitatively higher on the AQ-50 than the norm. Qualitatively, compared to the general population, a higher percentage of participants met the clinical cutoff. While the sample size precludes any claims of generalizability, what is remarkable about this finding is the unremarkable nature of the participating company—it is neither in a technology-intensive industry (e.g., banking, insurance) nor is it located in a technology hub like Silicon Valley, where half of IT professionals are said to be autistic (Smith, 2011). However, it bears repeating that while IT professionals, on average, exhibit elevated autistic traits, there is substantial variation within the group. Indeed, the majority of this sample (71.9%) did not meet the AQ-50 cutoff, underscoring the heterogeneity of autistic traits within the IT workforce.

Despite the small sample size, these quantitative and qualitative differences align with prior autism research documenting elevated autistic traits among those in computing (e.g., Baron-Cohen et al., 2001b). As both a continuous scale in the general population and a validated clinical screening tool (83% accuracy), the AQ shows promise for IS neurodiversity research. Since external validity cannot be reasonably established in a single study, let alone this inaugural one, Study 1 emphasized internal validity—that is, ensuring the soundness of the finding that 10%\~28.1% of the IT technical staff in this U.S. Midwestern company exhibited clinically significant autistic traits suggestive of probable diagnoses.

## Study 2

To explore the sociocognitive basis of IT professionals’ social challenges, Study 2 assessed the ToM capacities of IT and business management students—proxies of future IT professionals and their business counterparts.

## Method: Study 2

## Participants

A total of 235 undergraduate students enrolled in an IT course and a business course at a university in the U.S. Midwest performed a theory of mind task for extra course credit. All participants were juniors or seniors. After removing those majoring in other fields (13), the sample consisted of 222 students, including 143 IT majors from four computingrelated degree programs (Table 4) and 79 business management majors as neurotypical controls.

## Theory of Mind Task

Participants performed the Reading the Mind in the Eyes Test, Revised (Eyes Test) (Baron-Cohen et al., 2001a), which consists of 36 photographs of the eye region of faces displaying various complex mental states. As an “advanced theory of mind test,” it can “detect subtle individual differences in social sensitivity” (Baron-Cohen et al., 2001a, p. 241). Though some have raised concerns about the test (e.g., Higgins et al., 2023), others have provided supporting evidence (see Vellante et al., 2013, and Kim et al., 2024, for reviews). As “one of psychology’s most widely used tests” (Kim et al., 2024), it has been recommended by the U.S. National Institute of Mental Health as “a current best option” for assessing “Mental/Emotional Perspective Taking” (NAMHC Workgroup, 2016).

Each photograph (Figure 3), along with four adjectives describing the mental state, including one correct answer, was presented on a separate page. Participants were instructed to click on the adjective that correctly inferred the mental state in each photograph as quickly as possible. However, no time limit was imposed; participants could take as long as they

needed. A page timer recorded participants’ RT for each photograph. Participants had a practice trial before the ToM task. No feedback was given during the task as to their performance. Besides the Eyes Test, participants also answered demographic questions and an autistic trait scale (i.e., social communication challenge) from the AQ-9 (Jia et al., 2019), which exhibits excellent psychometric properties (Belcher et al., 2023; Waldren et al., 2025).

## Results: Study 2

## Mean Difference Tests

To control for gender when assessing the influence of the IT major on ToM performance (H3a/b), we first conducted a two-way ANOVA test. The results in Table 6 indicate that gender had no significant effect (interaction or main) on autistic trait or ToM accuracy, RT, or $R T _ { B o x - C o x ; }$ only the major was significantly related to autistic trait, RT, and $R T _ { B o x - C o x }$

After ruling out the gender effect, t-tests were performed to assess the mean difference between the two majors. As shown in Table 7, the IT students exhibited higher autistic traits (i.e., social challenge) than controls with a medium effect size, thus replicating results from the employee survey in Study 1 (H1). In terms of ToM performance, the results show that IT majors’ accuracy (0.704) was not significantly lower than that of the management students (0.710). Thus, H3a was not supported based on t-test results. However, the IT students’ RT was significantly longer $( R T _ { I T } = 7 . 2 9 \mathrm { s } ,$ $R T _ { M G T } = 5 . 2 3 \mathrm { s } , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ with a large effect size (Δ = 1.26). The t-test using the normalized $R T _ { B o x - C o x }$ yielded a similar large effect size (d = 1.23), thus supporting H3b based on ttest results.

## Robustness Check

Since the Eyes Test assesses the “language of the eyes,” and people in different cultures may not speak the same language with their eyes (Adams et al., 2010), we conducted a robustness check on the possible cultural effects on Eyes Test performance. (Research has shown that the effect is cultural, but not racial; Martin et al., 2021). After removing participants born outside the U.S. (Table 4: 21, 9.5%), the results remained substantively the same: gender had no significant interaction or main effect on autistic trait (p = 0.253 and p = 0.928, respectively), accuracy (p = 0.377 and p = 0.193, respectively), or RT (p = 0.736 and p = 0.276, respectively) while there was a significant impact of academic major on autistic trait (p = 0.007) and RT (p < 0.001), and a nonsignificant effect on accuracy (p = 0.369).

## Median Test

Because the RT distributions for the two groups were significantly different (two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov test statistic = 3.002, two-sided asymptotic $p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , median tests were also conducted to confirm the robustness of the results. The results from a two-sample Mann-Whitney U test (test statistic = 8673.00, two-tailed asymptotic $p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ indicate that the median IT student’s RT (6.51s) was significantly longer than that of the median management student (4.93s). These results remained substantively the same when non-U.S.-born participants were excluded. H3b was thus supported in the median test.

## Discussion: Study 2

Study 2 sought to explain IT professionals’ social difficulties and found one explanation in their lower ToM capacities. In keeping with prior sociocognitive and neurocognitive evidence in autism research, the IT students were found to underperform in the Eyes Test—they had significantly longer RT while achieving slightly (though not significantly) lower accuracy. As RT is positively related to cognitive load (Jensen, 2006), the large effect size in RT indicates that the ToM task was more effortful for the IT students, which offers a possible explanation for the social difficulties encountered by many IT professionals. The effortful processing in mind reading, literally greater emotional labor, requires “cognitive resources not otherwise used by neurotypical individuals for mentalizing,” which can be highly taxing and can lead to exhaustion and, over time, diminished mental health (Livingston et al., 2019, p. 103). The IT majors’ parity in accuracy may have been a result of such effortful compensatory processing.

Consistent with substantial heterogeneity in ToM capacities observed among autistic individuals (e.g., Humphreys et al., 2007), the significantly higher standard deviation of RT among IT students relative to the control group (Table 7) suggests a greater dispersion of ToM capacities among IT majors. Thus, a subset of IT students may be “stumbling at the first step of empathy” (Clark et al., 2008), as ineffective mind reading would hinder the ability to formulate appropriate affective responses to others’ mental states (Baron-Cohen, 2002).

It is important to realize that the Eyes Test utilizes static photographs; however, we still observed a large effect size in RT. In dynamic social settings that require rapid and spontaneous processing, a seemingly benign weakness could become much more amplified. Indeed, it has been argued that in everyday social interactions, where facial “expressions tend to be subtle, complex, and brief,” “any … impairment, even if mild, is likely a direct contributor to social problems” (Wieckowski et al., 2020, p. 1608).

It is also important to consider that the IT students’ higher RT may be underestimated in a computer-based test since “RT itself necessarily involves some degree of motor response as well as cognitive processing” (Jensen, 2006, p. 245). IT students frequently interact with computers and use a mouse, and many are also avid players of video games, which can enhance handeye coordination (Latham et al., 2013) and manual dexterity (Alsafi et al., 2017). Consequently, they may have a built-in advantage over management students in computer-based tests, where an advantage in fine motor skills could obscure ToM difficulties. The IT students nevertheless still had longer RT.

Although other IS research has shown a gender difference in certain domains (e.g., Serenko & Turel, 2021), the gender effect was, again, nonsignificant in Study 2 (as in Study 1). This result is consistent with prior research showing no difference in Eyes Test accuracy between diagnosed males and females (Baron-Cohen et al., 2015; Lai et al., 2012), despite a significant gender difference in autistic traits in the general population (Ruzich et al., 2015b). Similarly, an IS study has shown that gender is not a differentiating factor for IT interest (Jia et al., 2022). These results align with Holland’s vocational interest theory and support the notion that women who self-select into IT education and careers likely exhibit levels of autistic traits that are comparable to those of men in IT. Thus, the significant gender difference in autistic traits observed in the general population may not be present between men and women in IT. Importantly, when gender and academic major were jointly considered in this study, it was major—but not gender—that emerged as a significant differentiator of autistic traits and ToM capacities. Since there also exists evidence in support of the gender effect (e.g., Serenko & Turel, 2021), future work should explore whether these results are context dependent and whether gender effects disappear after controlling for autistic traits.

## Study 3

To explore the implications of autistic traits for IT professionals’ emotional labor and mental well-being, we conducted Study 3 with a sample of IT professionals.

## Method: Study 3

An online survey was distributed to approximately 900 IT professionals in two U.S. companies in 2022, including a Fortune 500 manufacturer and a multibillion-dollar business in the consumer services industry. A total of 292 responses were received. After removing those submitting incomplete responses and those employed in nontechnical roles (e.g., clerical, secretarial), the sample consisted of 249 IT professionals in a variety of job roles (Table 3).

In addition to demographic questions, the survey included a set of existing scales (Appendix A). Similar to Study 2, social communication challenge was measured using a scale from the AQ-9 (Jia et al., 2019). Emotional labor was assessed by a measure of surface acting (Grandey, 2003; Thomas et al., 2018) since deep acting has a nonsignificant or weak relationship with work exhaustion (e.g., Rutner et al., 2011). We also administered scales for work exhaustion (Chen & Karahanna, 2018), turnover intention (Moore, 2000; Rutner et al., 2008), mental well-being (Short Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Wellbeing Scale) (Stewart-Brown et al., 2009), and clinical scales for anxiety (Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale [GAD-7], Spitzer et al., 2006) and depression (Patient Health Questionnaire [PHQ-9], Kroenke et al., 2001).

## Results: Study 3

Table 8 presents descriptive statistics, scale reliabilities, and construct correlations. Composite reliabilities and Cronbach’s α all exceeded 0.70. Convergent and discriminant validity were demonstrated in the factor loading matrix (Appendix B), where each item had the highest loading on the intended factors and lower loadings on the other factors, with low cross-loadings (Hair et al., 2017). No off-diagonal correlation in Table 8 exceeded the square root of the AVE on the diagonal, further evidencing discriminant validity (Fornell & Larcker, 1981).

LISREL9.3, a covariance-based SEM tool, was used to test the research model. The results in Figure 5 show that the model exhibited satisfactory fit. Since the addition of a marker variable did not improve model fit $( \varDelta \chi ^ { 2 } = 7 1 . 0 8 , \varDelta d f = 7 3$ n.s.), common method bias (CMB) is unlikely to be a major concern. The presence of many low correlations among the latent constructs (Table 8) further suggests that the results are unlikely to be attributable to CMB.

As shown in Figure 5, emotional labor is significantly related to work exhaustion $( p \ < \ 0 . 0 0 5 )$ , supporting H5. Their correlation (r = 0.37 in Table 8) is in keeping with prior metaanalytic studies $( r = 0 . 3 7 4$ , Hülsheger & Schewe, 2011, p. 374; $r ~ = ~ 0 . 3 2$ , Kammeyer-Mueller et al., 2013). Work exhaustion, in turn, has a significant positive relationship with turnover intention $( p < 0 . 0 0 5 )$ and a significant negative relationship with mental well-being $( p ~ < ~ 0 . 0 0 5 )$ , thus supporting H6 and H7, respectively. Social challenge is positively related to emotional labor $( p \ < \ 0 . 0 0 5 )$ , thus supporting H4. As hypothesized in H8a, social challenge $( p <$ 0.005) is negatively related to well-being.

Figure 5 also shows several additional significant paths. Social challenge has a direct negative relationship with turnover intention $( p \ < \ 0 . 0 5 )$ , and emotional labor is negatively related to well-being $( p < 0 . 0 0 5 )$ . Table 9 presents the total and indirect effects.

To examine whether the IT professionals exceeded population norms in anxiety (H8b) and depression (H8c), we used 2022 benchmark data (Terlizzi & Zablotsky, 2024), matching the year of our survey. The results in Table 10 show that, for both anxiety and depression, significantly fewer IT professionals were in the “none or minimal” severity category while significantly more were in clinically significant (“moderate” $\mathrm { o r } \ \mathrm { ^ { * } s e v e r e ^ { 3 3 } } )$ categories, thus supporting H8b and H8c. Table 11 illustrates how Study 3 results compare to those of other research using different measures.

To assess whether the IT professionals were underdiagnosed with autism (0.80%, H2b) or had a higher prevalence in their immediate families (6.02%, H2c) than the population norm (3.05%, Zablotsky et al., 2023), z-tests for two proportions were conducted. The results indicate support for both H2b (z = -2.06, p = 0.020) and H2c $( z = 2 . 7 3 , p = 0 . 0 0 3 )$ ). The prevalence of autism in the respondents’ immediate families (6.02%) represented approximately 2.3 times higher odds (95% CI: 1.33\~3.86) than the U.S. prevalence rate (3.05%).

As shown in Table 12, the IT professionals (both men and women) in Study 3 exhibited significantly higher levels of autistic traits related to social communication challenge than the IT students in Study 2 (overall t = 4.74, men: t = 3.22, women: t = 3.63, all p < 0.001). However, MTurk participants in a separate study reported even higher autistic traits than the IT professionals (overall t = -3.23, p < 0.001; men t = -3.32, p < 0.001; women: t = -1.95, p = 0.055, two-tailed).

## Discussion: Study 3

The neurodiversity lens, with its inherent emphasis on individual differences, represents a new approach to emotional labor that complements the traditional emotional labor research conducted in the context of human service professions, where boundary-spanning service employees “put on a show” as mandated by organizational rules but otherwise may not have social communication difficulties or an innate inclination toward social aversion and avoidance. Through this lens, social communication challenge predicts emotional labor, providing evidence for an internal mechanism for emotional labor and lending support to a traitbased approach in this area of IS research.

Replicating prior work, we found that emotional labor heightens work exhaustion and turnover intention. Several relationships were also shown to be partially mediated. For example, consistent with our expectations, social challenges can suppress turnover intentions because of the social demands of the job search process and the subsequent adjustment to a new workplace. While prior turnover research has often focused on job characteristics (e.g., workload, autonomy, Ahuja et al., 2007), this study offers a trait-based explanation.

Autistic traits were posited to undermine IT professionals’ mental well-being through several mechanisms. The results show that while social challenges had an indirect effect on well-being through sociocognitive and behavioral mechanisms (via mediators such as emotional labor and work exhaustion), it also exerted a direct physiological impact on well-being, in keeping with the allostatic load model. These results complement the focus on occupational stressors in existing IS research (e.g., Guzman et al., 2008; Riemenschneider & Armstrong, 2021) and demonstrate the wide-ranging impacts of autistic traits and emotional labor for IT professionals, from job attitudes (e.g., turnover intention) to personal outcomes (e.g., well-being).

Although other IS research has shown gender differences in certain domains (e.g., Serenko & Turel, 2021), there is also evidence (e.g., Jia et al., 2022) suggesting that females who self-select into IT education and careers may not be substantively different from their male counterparts. As shown in the low correlations between gender and criterion variables (Table 8), women in this sample did not experience emotional labor, work exhaustion, turnover intention, and well-being at different rates than men.

Aligning with the hypothesized sociocognitive (E-S theory), behavioral (social compensation hypothesis), and physiological (allostatic load) mechanisms, the IT professionals, on average, reported significantly higher mental health symptoms than the norm, possibly resulting from the dual impacts of trait and occupational factors. However, it is important to note that the cross-sectional data in this study cannot establish causality. The proportions of IT professionals who met the cut-points for probable anxiety (39, 15.67%) and depression (44, 17.67%) both represented about 2.3 times the prevalence rates in the general population (Table 10). One caveat to note is that the survey did not ask whether the respondents were taking any medication to manage their anxiety or depression symptoms. The proportions of IT professionals meeting cutoffs would be underestimated if any respondents with elevated anxiety or depression scored below the thresholds because of their medication. Following recent

IS research on technology users’ mental health (Turel & Bechara, 2024), this work represents an initial effort to use clinical measures to examine IT professionals’ mental health, and the results are telling.

When it comes to the prevalence of autism, IT professionals in this sample were likely significantly underdiagnosed since most respondents (88.2%) were over 35 years of age and were thus unlikely to have been screened as children. However, we found that their immediate family members were significantly more likely to have been diagnosed with autism than the norm.

Study 3 participants held various IT job roles. Analyzing them as a group does not imply that all IT professionals exhibit high autistic traits. In fact, systematic differences across IT job roles have long been noted (e.g., programmers vs. analysts, Wynekoop & Walz, 1998). However, such within-group variation does not detract from the between-group differences distinguishing IT professionals from their clients. For example, one study found “IS personnel in all positions to be more analytical and logical than the general population” (Wynekoop & Walz, 1998, p. 69), which highlights an autistic trait in IT professionals at the collective level (i.e., high systemizing). Indeed, if within-IT differences were to outweigh business-IT differences, there would be little reason to ask whether IT people are different (Ferratt & Short, 1986) or to study IT occupational culture (Guzman & Stanton, 2009).

## General Discussion and Meta Inferences

This research draws on three quantitative studies to empirically test a trait-based approach to IT professionals’ emotional labor and to offer a sociocognitive explanation for their social difficulties. In Study 1, a full-length autism screening instrument demonstrated quantitative and qualitative differences in IT professionals’ autistic traits compared to normative data. In Study 2, using IT students as proxies, we established that ToM deficits are a sociocognitive mechanism that can explain IT professionals’ social difficulties. In Study 3, data from a large sample of IT professionals revealed the implications of autistic traits: heightened emotional labor and reduced mental well-being, among other outcomes. IT professionals in the sample were likely underdiagnosed for autism and reported more severe mental health challenges than the norm. Cumulative evidence has led to an evolution of understanding of neurodiversity in the IT profession. ## Implications for Research

This multi-study, multi-theory investigation makes several contributions to research. It advances our understanding of emotional labor and neurodiversity in the IT community, culminating in a sociocognitive account of IT professionals’ social difficulties. From a trait‑based perspective, emotional labor extends beyond boundary‑spanning roles to encompass all IT professionals high in certain characteristics, fundamentally reframing the phenomenon as not only role‑bound but also dispositional. Consistent with predictions from E-S theory, this research demonstrates that elevated autistic traits and ToM weakness broadly shape how IT professionals respond to workplace social demands. This conceptual shift not only expands the nomological net of emotional labor but also introduces a disposition-centric paradigm that calls for a reexamination of long-standing assumptions about the nature of IT work and IT worker identities and psychological profiles.

As these dispositional factors clarify prior descriptions of IT professionals (e.g., “lack of social skills,” Guzman & Stanton, 2009, p. 181), they offer another perspective on employee turnover and burnout. Rather than attributing these outcomes solely to environmental, occupational, and technological factors, this work underscores the importance of bringing IT professionals and their dispositions to the forefront. Indeed, “the people make the place” (Schneider, 1987).

This work may also inform other areas of IS research. To the extent that IT professionals have a weakness in ToM capability and facial processing, this has significant implications for daily business-IT communication as well as strategic alignment (Reich & Benbasat, 1990). To the extent that IT managers resemble rank-and-file IT professionals more than business managers (Moore, 1991), any ToM weakness observed in IT students and frontline employees is also likely present in IT managers, as exemplified by the CIO discussed above, who missed numerous cues from business executives (Pearlson et al., 2015). Additionally, one distinctive characteristic of the IT profession is the requirement to understand both IT and the business domain of one’s employment (Riemenschneider & Armstrong, 2021). It is thus useful to examine business-IT communication, alignment, and IT occupational characteristics through the lens of ToM and neurodiversity.

This work also has implications for IS gender research. While prior studies have shown gender differences in certain domains (e.g., Serenko & Turel, 2021), gender effects are consistently nonsignificant in this research, in keeping with autism literature findings. These diverging results highlight the importance of considering the role of autistic traits in future IS gender work.

## Implications for Practice

## Neurodiversity in the IT Community

IT professionals, on average, exhibit high autistic traits, which influence various aspects of their work and life experience. In many ways, E-S theory perfectly captures the bifurcated experience of many IT professionals. On the one hand, they tend to possess excellent problem-solving capabilities, a deep understanding of complex systems and processes, and strong technical skills (i.e., hyper-systemizing), providing them with joy, satisfaction, and a sense of purpose. On the other hand, they often face daily challenges in social interactions with clients, coworkers, and family members (i.e., hypo-empathizing) and feel compelled to perform emotional labor and camouflaging, all of which heighten their risk of burnout and ill-being. The current IT workforce is likely underdiagnosed for autism. The focus on diagnosed job applicants and employees in corporate neurodiversity programs overlooks many others, such as those who were not screened in childhood and those exhibiting elevated though subclinical levels of autistic traits.

## ToM Capacity, Social Skill Training, and Business-IT Relationship

While the characterization of a “lack of social skills” (Guzman & Stanton, 2009) may create an impression that such skills can be acquired in ways like learning a programming language, this research suggests that such deficits will be exceedingly challenging to overcome because one’s ToM capability has a significant genetic component—research on twins has demonstrated a 28% heritability in Eyes Test scores (Warrier et al., 2018). Genetics partially explain why intensive treatment programs designed to increase ToM abilities in autistic people have yielded mixed results (Fletcher-Watson et al., 2014; Wieckowski et al., 2020).

Such evidence also casts doubt on the effectiveness of shortterm behavioral interventions aimed at teaching IT professionals social or “soft” skills. A large U.S. technology firm trains new hires to make eye contact (Purtill, 2018). While this offers additional (anecdotal) evidence of elevated autistic traits in IT professionals—given that reduced eye contact is a hallmark of autism and other neurodevelopmental conditions (APA, 2022)—it also reveals a limited understanding of neurodiversity. An averted gaze often stems from the fact that eye contact can be “painfully stressful” and overwhelming for autistic individuals, and reading another’s eyes may be “confusing” due to ToM difficulties (Baron-Cohen, 2020, p. 141). Thus, such training does not address the underlying neurocognitive causes (Livingston et al., 2019) for the absence of such socially expected (neurotypical) behavior. Focusing on outward behavior may even prove counterproductive, increasing pressure to “put on a show” and deepening the sense of stigma. In fact, camouflaging is precisely the kind of coping mechanism that some autistic people have adopted—they give the appearance of eye contact by looking at the interaction partner’s forehead, nose, or mouth (Cook et al., 2022; Livingston et al., 2019). After all, engaging in (or mimicking) eye contact is only the first step; the real challenge lies in reading another’s mind in the eyes, which is a weakness shared by many IT professionals. Thus, rather than attempting to ask technical people to improve their social skills (i.e., become better at performing emotional labor or camouflaging), it may be more empowering—and therapeutic—to understand the negative impacts of stigmatization on their well-being and selfesteem (Moore & Love, 2004), promote awareness of neurodiversity, and foster self-acceptance and self-compassion.

While emotional labor has been shown to heighten IT professionals’ exhaustion and ill-being, performing such labor is arguably a “core feature” of the IT job rather than “an extraneous inconvenience,” as an anonymous reviewer insightfully noted. To effectively support and enable business strategies, IT professionals must perform such labor and correctly interpret business clients’ mental states. Obviously, those with higher ToM capacities are better equipped for roles with significant social demands. The great dispersion of ToM capabilities among IT students highlights the importance of ensuring person-job fit at the IT workplace. Traditional tools for assessing social and leadership skills (e.g., self-reports, scenarios, vignettes) are prone to conscious (e.g., social desirability, self-presentation) and unconscious (e.g., lack of self-insight) biases. In contrast, ToM-based tasks represent a more effective alternative because ToM deficits are much harder to disguise. Careful selection of empathic individuals for client-facing IT roles can help enhance business-IT communication, collaboration, and alignment.

## Mental Health and Well-Being

IT professionals, on average, are 2.3 times more likely to encounter mental health challenges than the general population. This finding goes beyond what one could glean from existing research on IT professionals’ stress and burnout, which may not convey the same sense of urgency until examined from a mental health perspective. Employee wellbeing should be a top priority of IT managers. Since the relationship between autistic traits and well-being is partially mediated by emotional labor, managers can and should help by managing social demands in the workplace. There seems to be an increasing awareness that flexible work arrangements, such as remote work, can be an effective strategy (Greenberg, 2023).

## Limitations and Future Directions

While this work has important implications for IS research and practice, it is not without limitations. First, the small sample in Study 1 limits the generalizability of the findings. Future research should draw on larger samples to replicate its results. Such insight would not only advance IS research but could also prompt a reconceptualization of corporate neurodiversity initiatives and an adoption of individualized work design to align with employee characteristics (i.e., job crafting).

Second, the Eyes Test in Study 2 relies on static photographs. Future research should use more dynamic, context-rich stimuli (e.g., video clips, interactive tasks) to reduce the likelihood of compensatory strategies among participants. The relevance of ToM capacity could be further highlighted by demonstrating that Eyes Test performance can predict camouflaging behavior and mental health outcomes. While a fourth study included in an earlier version of this paper provided evidence for such associations, space constraints prevented its inclusion in this final version. We thus leave it to future research to assess the implications of ToM deficits.

Third, IT professionals and students were examined at the group level in all three studies. Future research should investigate within-group heterogeneity and explore how variations in autistic traits and ToM capacity may align with one’s choice of IT major (e.g., IS vs. computer science) and job roles (e.g., analyst vs. programmer). Such work could offer a nuanced understanding of person-job fit within the IT profession.

## Conclusion

All individuals are not the same; beyond race, gender, and other easily observable biological differences, there are also systematic variations in attention, learning, sociability, and other neurocognitive functions, which can be just as influential in shaping one’s daily work and personal life. Through exploring both the advantages and disadvantages associated with autistic traits, this research further demonstrates the relevance of neurodiversity and the disposition-centric paradigm to IS research. It brings us yet another step closer to a fundamental recognition of autistic traits as a distinguishing factor of IT professionals and offers a new perspective on the age-old question: “Are IS people different?” (Ferratt & Short, 1986). We hope this work stimulates more research employing the neurodiversity lens to study IS phenomena.
