# The Impact of Process- vs. Outcome-Oriented Reviews on the Sales of Healthcare Services

Abstract. The consumption of services inherently requires a process to achieve the desired outcome. For nonexperiential services (e.g., healthcare and education) primarily valued for their end outcomes, their consumption typically involves lengthy processes that are not designed for enjoyment (e.g., wound care and lecture attendance). Consequently, there exists a prominent tension between the processes (means) and outcomes (ends) of these services, which is largely ignored in the prior literature on user-generated content (UGC) that predominantly focuses on products (e.g., books and electronics) or experiential services (e.g., dining and lodging). To bridge this gap, we investigate the distinct roles of process- and outcome-oriented reviews in influencing consumers’ decisions to pursue healthcare services, leveraging a comprehensive data set collected from an online platform for cosmetic procedures. By separating process- and outcome-oriented reviews through visual content analysis, we show that the effect of outcome-oriented reviews in boosting the sales of healthcare services is almost twice stronger than that of process-oriented reviews on average. However, the relative effectiveness of these two types of reviews varies substantially across different types of healthcare services. Specifically, we find that process-oriented reviews are more influential in affecting consumers’ purchases of services with higher complexity, in contrast to outcome-oriented reviews that are more impactful for services with lower complexity. Moreover, the impact of process-oriented reviews is stronger for less popular services, whereas the impact of outcome-oriented reviews is stronger for more popular services. These nuanced findings show that consumers resort to different types of UGC while considering different types of healthcare services. Our work has important implications both theoretically and practically.

## 1. Introduction

Online reviews have become an indispensable resource for consumers, shaping their choices across a broad spectrum, from everyday products like books and electronics to a wide range of services, such as dining, lodging, healthcare, and education (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006, Chen and Xie 2008, Chen et al. 2018, Lu and Rui 2018, Fang 2022, Chakraborty and Biswal 2023). While online reviews play a critical role in both product and service consumption, there exists a notable distinction between them. Unlike products that are manufactured beforehand, services are typically consumed at the same time they are produced. This implies that the consumption of services inherently requires a process, which often involves a series of activities or interactions with the service providers, to achieve the desired outcome. For example, restaurant patrons often engage with waitstaff multiple times to have their meals served, and hotel guests may similarly interact with the staff on various occasions throughout their stay. Consequently, the evaluation of services encompasses a dual perspective that considers not only the outcome but also the process (Dabholkar and Overby 2005, Collier and Bienstock 2006).

The distinction between process and outcome is particularly pronounced for nonexperiential services that are primarily valued for their end outcomes, such as healthcare, education, and legal services. The processes of nonexperiential services, including activities like wound care and medication adherence in healthcare, lecture attendance in education, and evidence gathering and court appearances in legal services, are typically not enjoyable (Cooper-Martin 1991). Consumers endure these processes solely as a means to achieve the desired outcomes. In contrast, for experiential services that are designed for enjoyment, such as dining and lodging services, elements related to the process (e.g., staff friendliness and ambiance) are integral to the consumption experience and hence are usually considered part of the end outcome (Dixon et al. 2017). Due to the prominent tension between process and outcome for nonexperiential services, consumer reviews for these services broadly contain two types of content: (1) process-oriented reviews, which highlight the procedural aspects of the services, and (2) outcome-oriented reviews, which center on the achieved end results.

Distinguishing between process- and outcome-oriented reviews is crucial for understanding how online reviews shape consumers’ assessments of nonexperiential services, as each type of reviews influences distinct aspects of a consumer’s decision-making process. It is wellestablished that consumers weigh both the desirability and feasibility of an action when determining whether to proceed, where desirability refers to the expected value of the action’s end state and feasibility pertains to the ease or convenience of achieving that end state (Gollwitzer and Moskowitz 1996, Liberman and Trope 1998). Processoriented reviews can help consumers assess the feasibility of a service by shedding light on essential steps, time commitments, and potential risks or challenges of the service. Conversely, outcome-oriented reviews aid in assessing the desirability of a service by focusing on the quality and impact of the end result, such as improved health, acquisition of new skills, and favorable legal outcomes.

Despite the evident theoretical distinctions between process- and outcome-oriented reviews, a significant research gap exists concerning the nuanced impacts of these two types of reviews on consumers’ decisionmaking processes for nonexperiential services. Existing research on user-generated content (UGC) predominantly concentrates on the consumption of products or experiential services where the distinction between process versus outcome is less prominent or pertinent, such as books, movies, video games, restaurants, and hotels (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006, Duan et al. 2008, Zhu and Zhang 2010, Chen et al. 2018, Fang 2022). In the context of nonexperiential services such as healthcare, although some recent research has explored the relationship of online reviews with physicians’ medical quality (Lu and Rui 2018) and patients’ choices (Xu et al. 2021), these studies do not delve into the differential impacts of process- and outcome-oriented reviews. Consequently, there is a scarcity of research exploring the different roles played by process- and outcome-oriented reviews, particularly in healthcare settings where both the process and the end outcome carry substantial consequences (Gao et al. 2015, Li et al. 2023). Considering the growing availability of online reviews for healthcare services and the paramount importance of informed decision making in healthcare services, this field represents an excellent context to investigate the distinct roles of process- and outcome-oriented reviews.

In addition to exploring the distinct effects of processand outcome-oriented reviews, it is imperative to gain insights into how these effects may be influenced by various service characteristics. Service complexity and popularity, for instance, represent crucial factors that could potentially moderate consumers’ reliance on these two types of reviews. Under the context of healthcare, individuals considering highly elaborate healthcare services, such as surgical procedures, often perceive greater uncertainties. Consequently, they are more inclined to question the feasibility of these services due to factors such as the intricacies of implementation, higher likelihood of side effects, and extended recovery periods (Wharton et al. 2016). Furthermore, the degree of service popularity, a well-documented factor regulating the impact of UGC on consumer decisions (Zhu and Zhang 2010), may also influence how consumers evaluate the feasibility and desirability of services based on online reviews. For instance, in a scenario where a healthcare service is widely recognized and sought after, consumers may exhibit fewer concerns about feasibility compared with a less popular service. This interplay between service characteristics and the valuation of feasibility and desirability is likely to affect consumers’ information needs and the persuasiveness of process- and outcome-oriented reviews in their decision-making process. Notably, the existing literature has not delved into this crucial aspect either.

To bridge the aforementioned research gaps, this paper addresses the following research questions:

1) Whether process- and outcome-oriented reviews have differential impacts on the demand for healthcare services?

2) How does the impact of process- and outcomeoriented reviews vary across healthcare services characterized by differing levels of complexity and popularity?

To answer these questions, we select elective healthcare services as our research context and collect a comprehensive data set that contains online diaries and sales of cosmetic procedures from a leading Chinese online platform for cosmetic procedures. Online diaries are a series of reviews through which consumers continuously share their up-to-date experiences regarding healthcare services. Upon creation of their dairies, consumers share images depicting their pretreatment condition. As they progress, they can enrich their diaries with posts that detail their posttreatment status, which may include photos illustrating any wounds or swelling. Following a complete recovery, consumers frequently add postrecovery images to highlight the improvements in their appearance. The unique structure of online diaries facilitates the contribution of both process-oriented reviews (posts focusing on recovery processes) and outcomeoriented reviews (posts focusing on outcomes).<sup>1</sup>

To distinguish process- and outcome-oriented reviews within the posts of each online diary, we train a deep learning model to predict whether each photo contains process or outcome information and then classify a post as process-oriented if it includes at least two photos indicating that a consumer is still in the recovery process. Our empirical analyses show that, on average, outcomeoriented reviews have a stronger effect on the sales of cosmetic procedures than process-oriented reviews. Furthermore, the effectiveness of these two types of reviews varies substantially across cosmetic procedures with different levels of complexity and popularity. Specifically, process-oriented reviews are more influential for cosmetic procedures with higher complexity, whereas outcome-oriented reviews are more effective for those with lower complexity. Additionally, process-oriented reviews display higher effectiveness for less popular procedures, in contrast to outcome-oriented reviews that exhibit greater influence for more popular procedures.

This research makes several noteworthy contributions to the literature. Firstly, previous research on UGC either focuses on the consumption of products or experiential services where the distinction between process versus outcome is less salient (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006, Duan et al. 2008, Zhu and Zhang 2010, Chen et al. 2018, Fang 2022) or overlooks the differential impacts of process- and outcome-oriented reviews in the consumption of nonexperiential services (Lu and Rui 2018, Xu et al. 2021). This paper contributes to the literature by fathoming into the differential impacts of process- versus outcome-oriented UGC in the context of healthcare service consumption, highlighting the distinct roles of these two types of UGC in influencing consumer decisions.

Secondly, our research delves into the nuanced relationship between the effectiveness of process- and outcomeoriented reviews and the specific attributes of healthcare services, namely complexity and popularity. By disentangling the intriguing interplay between complexity/ popularity and the preference for process- or outcomeoriented reviews, our study contributes to a more comprehensive understanding of how consumers strategically resort to different types of UGC while evaluating services with different characteristics.

Thirdly, our work extends the nascent literature on visual marketing (Zhang et al. 2022, Hou et al. 2023) by investigating the impact of process- versus outcomeoriented visual content on demand for healthcare services. Our findings provide valuable insights into the role of visuals in contemporary marketing strategies, particularly within the realm of service consumption, and underscore the importance of visual content in shaping consumer preferences and choices.

Finally, this study offers crucial theoretical advancements by integrating the constructs of feasibility and desirability into the evaluation of healthcare services where both the process and outcome aspects are deeply consequential. While feasibility and desirability are not novel concepts, their application in understanding the influence of UGC on the consumption of healthcare services has been lacking. Our research bridges this gap by theorizing how process- and outcome-oriented reviews respectively affect the perceived feasibility and desirability of healthcare services with different levels of complexity and popularity, and then empirically testing their ultimate impact on service demand. The resulting theoretical and empirical insights not only enrich our understanding of the distinct roles of process- and outcome-oriented reviews in influencing consumer behavior in the healthcare sector but also pave the way for further exploration of their differential impacts in other service contexts.

## 2. Literature Review

Three streams of prior studies in information systems (IS) and marketing areas are related to this study. In this section, we begin by reviewing existing research that investigates the impact of UGC on the demand for products and services. Following this, we explore related studies on how textual and visual content within UGC can influence consumer decision making. Lastly, we provide a brief overview of research investigating the roles of incentives in motivating the creation of UGC.

## 2.1. Impact of UGC on Demand

The literature has extensively investigated how UGC affects consumer demand. Initial research in this domain studied the impact of UGC on sales for a variety of products, ranging from electronics (Chen and Xie 2008) to books (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006), and clothing (Sahoo et al. 2018). These studies underscored the significance of numerical UGC metrics, such as the review volume (Liu 2006, Duan et al. 2008), the valence (Dellarocas et al. 2007, Zhu and Zhang 2010), and the rating variance (Sun 2012).

Not surprisingly, researchers have further extended their studies on the impact of UGC to service contexts, especially experiential services like movies (Liu 2006, Duan et al. 2008), restaurants (Wu et al. 2015, Fang 2022), and hotels (Chen et al. 2018). For such services, consumers’ experiences during the service processes are crucial for their evaluation of the services. For example, it has been shown that waiting time, servers’ friendliness (Bai et al. 2020), and hygiene (Mejia et al. 2019) are critical aspects influencing consumers’ overall satisfaction with dining services. In essence, despite the presence of service processes in experiential services, elements associated with them are often considered an integral part of the overall evaluation of the service outcomes (Dixon et al. 2017), functioning as ends in themselves rather than just means to an end. Therefore, the differentiation between process- and outcome-oriented UGC is not a central focus in the existing studies concerning experiential services.

As the influence of UGC continues to grow, its impact on consumers’ decision making has gradually expanded to nonexperiential services, such as healthcare services (Gao et al. 2015, Yaraghi et al. 2018) and online education (Chakraborty and Biswal 2023). Literature exploring the impact of UGC on demand for nonexperiential services has emerged recently. In line with previous research on UGC for products and experiential services, Shukla et al. (2021) reveal that the volume of UGC has a positive impact on the number of appointments a doctor receives. Devising their text-mining algorithms, Xu et al. (2021) identify a mixture of process and outcome aspects of services, including the waiting time, bedside manner, service duration, and diagnosis accuracy, that affect patients’ decisions when selecting a physician. Chakraborty and Biswal (2023) examine the roles of review consistency and overall ratings in the adoption of Massive Open Online Courses (MOOCs). While these studies provide valuable insights into the demand impact of UGC for nonexperiential services, they have overlooked the nuanced distinctions between process and outcome aspects, which may exert different influences on consumers’ decisions due to their distinct focuses. This oversight is particularly noteworthy for healthcare services that require a considerable time investment and carry inherent risks in achieving desired outcomes. This study fills this literature gap by investigating the potential differential impacts of reviews related to process versus outcome in the context of healthcare services.

## 2.2. Impact of Textual and Visual Content

In addition to numerical metrics (e.g., ratings) extensively investigated in prior studies above, the UGC literature has documented the value of textual content in consumers’ decision making. Reviews contain additional information that can help prospective consumers to learn more about the quality of products and services. For instance, Bai et al. (2020) show that food quality and service quality in reviews are more informative than other aspects (e.g., ambiance) of restaurant services. Lei et al. (2022) use different data sources, such as Apple’s App Store, and show that the textual content of individual reviews is a possible source of the swaying effect of online reviews. They highlight the importance of information accessibility from the textual content of online reviews in consumers’ purchase decisions. Moreover, information cues embedded in textual content can also shift consumers’ decisions. For example, Yin et al. (2021) show that certain emotions (such as anger) can be particularly persuasive in influencing consumers’ purchase decisions.

As images and videos become increasingly pervasive in UGC during the past years, a few recent studies have started to fathom the role of visual content. Li and Xie (2020) show that including an image in a tweet can significantly increase user engagement (e.g., the number of likes on Twitter). Hou et al. (2023) demonstrate that images with certain attributes (such as color and composition) can evoke users’ positive emotions, which further lead to the success of a fundraising campaign in the crowdfunding context. Additionally, Zhang et al. (2022) show that the high-quality lodging images posted by hosts have a positive influence on consumers’ lodging demand on Airbnb. Our study adds to the rising strand of literature on visual UGC by investigating the impact of visual content posted by consumers on sales of healthcare services.

## 2.3. Incentivized UGC and Sponsored Content

Managers are increasingly leveraging UGC as a communication mix to generate positive word-of-mouth and enhance consumer engagement, leading to economic benefits (Goh et al. 2013). However, the volume of UGC is often limited due to the voluntary nature of users’ con tent production (Lu et al. 2022). Businesses, including online platforms, are implementing various strategies to motivate users to generate content, including both monetary and nonmonetary incentives (Burtch et al. 2018). Liu and Feng (2021) build a theoretical model and their results indicate that monetary incentives may either increase or decrease users’ content contribution depending on the specific conditions.

Relatedly, some firms utilize sponsored content to increase their online influence. That is, firms hire marketers (e.g., publishers and influencers) to generate or share content on their behalf (Chatterjee and Zhou 2021, Peng and Van den Bulte 2024). Different from UGC, sponsored content is tailored and often distributed through firm-owned channels. Goh et al. (2013) empirically show that sponsored content is less influential in consumers purchase decisions than UGC.

This stream of literature is relevant, because the focal platform offers monetary incentives for consumers who contribute high-quality diaries. However, we do not focus on strategies motivating content generation. Instead, we investigate the impact of already-produced UGC on sales.

## 3. Theoretical Background and Hypotheses Development

In this section, we establish the theoretical foundation that underpins our hypotheses. First, we introduce the framework of feasibility and desirability in the consumer psychology literature. We then delve into the relevant concepts regarding mental simulation, including process simulation and outcome simulation. The elaboration on mental simulation helps us understand better how process- versus outcome-oriented reviews may affect consumers’ perceptions of feasibility and desirability differently. Building on these theoretical insights, we subsequently proceed to articulate our hypotheses concerning the influence of process- and outcome-oriented reviews on demand for healthcare services. We further hypothesize how service complexity and popularity may play a moderating role.

## 3.1. Feasibility and Desirability

The goal pursuit literature has extensively explored how individuals weigh both desirability and feasibility when making decisions about whether to undertake a particular course of action (Gollwitzer and Moskowitz 1996, Liberman and Trope 1998). Desirability pertains to the perceived value or attractiveness of the ultimate outcome associated with an action, while feasibility relates to the perceived ease or convenience of achieving that desired outcome (Liberman and Trope 1998). When assessing service consumption, desirability is gauged by the end outcomes, while feasibility is determined through a comprehensive evaluation of various process-related factors including potential risks, financial costs, and the expertise of service providers. For instance, the decision to undergo surgeries is often characterized by high desirability due to their potential to significantly enhance an individual’s physical well-being. However, these procedures are also perceived as having low feasibility because they entail complex treatments and recovery processes, accompanied by substantial risks (Harris and Buntin 2008). On the other hand, physical therapy services offered by a nearby rehabilitation center are often regarded as possessing both high desirability and high feasibility. In essence, feasibility and desirability represent the practical means and desired ends of an action, respectively.

## 3.2. Mental Simulation

Mental simulation refers to the cognitive construction of hypothetical scenarios (Taylor et al. 1998). Our mind can automatically simulate many of the perceptual experiences by self-projecting into a hypothetical situation, such as a different place and a different person’s experience (Waytz et al. 2015). For example, one can imagine using a new product triggered by verbal or visual stimuli in advertising (Zhao et al. 2009). Mental simulation has been shown to play an important role in consumers decision-making process (a recent review by Elder and Krishna 2022).

Two distinct types of mental simulation have been identified in the literature: process simulation and outcome simulation. Process simulation focuses on a series of steps needed to reach a goal, whereas outcome simulation concentrates on the desirable outcome of a goal (Escalas and Luce 2004). Due to the different focuses, outcome simulation is particularly beneficial at the stage of setting a goal, whereas process simulation is more effective when executing goals (Pham and Taylor 1999). Moreover, prior literature has shown that the relative strength of process versus outcome simulation in consumers’ purchase decisions is highly contextual (Zhao et al. 2011). In the context of healthcare services, the type of mental simulation evoked by online reviews could hinge on whether consumers are primarily concerned with issues pertaining to the process or the outcome.

## 3.3. Hypotheses

3.3.1. Impacts of Process-Oriented vs. Outcome-Oriented Reviews. Process-oriented reviews may trigger process simulation and help prospective consumers imagine what they need to go through to achieve the desired outcomes. By mentally simulating how the pro gress is made over time, prospective consumers tend to be less concerned about the feasibility of healthcare services, which then increases their purchase intention (Thompson et al. 2009; Zhao et al. 2009, 2011). Besides, process-oriented reviews can enhance the credibility of reviews by providing detailed evidence of the recovery process (e.g., redness and swelling), a worse-beforebetter scenario (Cian et al. 2020). Thus, process-oriented reviews can also boost consumers’ trust and increase the likelihood of purchasing.

Conversely, outcome-oriented reviews concentrate on the outcomes of healthcare services and can show prospective consumers the benefits of postrecovery outcomes, such as the improvement in health conditions after undergoing surgery. As such, outcome-oriented reviews can stimulate consumers to engage in outcome simulation, which can increase the perceived desirability of healthcare services (Cian et al. 2020). When consumers find the outcomes more desirable, they are more likely to make their purchase decisions (Liberman and Trope 1998).

While process- and outcome-oriented reviews are both expected to have a positive effect on sales, the magnitudes of their impacts may well be different due to the specific nature of healthcare services. First, healthcare services often involve a painful and/or time-consuming recovery process (Lu and Rui 2018). Therefore, the recovery process is critical for prospective consumers in their decision making. Second, as healthcare services are deeply consequential (e.g., surgical failures or complications can lead to disfiguration and even functional loss of a body part), it is crucial for consumers to obtain reliable information (Westerman et al. 2014). However, due to a lack of expertise, it is hard for individual consumers to assess the quality and risk of healthcare services on their own (Gao et al. 2015, Li et al. 2023). Therefore, while processing online reviews shared by others, feasibility is likely to be a key concern, as also suggested by prior studies on healthcare services (Harris and Buntin 2008,

Xu et al. 2021). Hence, process-oriented reviews are expected to have a stronger impact on the sales of healthcare services than outcome-oriented reviews, leading to the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1. All else equal, the effect of process-oriented reviews on the sales of healthcare services is stronger than that of outcome-oriented reviews.

3.3.2. Moderating Effect of Complexity. The complexity of healthcare treatments and recovery processes may vary drastically across different healthcare services. Take cosmetic procedures as an example. Botox injection for facial wrinkles is a low-complexity procedure that is easy to implement and consumers usually recover in less than a week (Mobayed et al. 2020). In contrast, rhinoplasty surgery is a high-complexity procedure involving a series of operations (e.g., anesthesia, incision, and reshaping) and significant uncertainties during the recovery process (Wharton et al. 2016). Prospective consumers of high-complexity healthcare services, therefore, are more likely to question the feasibility of the services. Hence, consumers may perceive different levels of feasibility while assessing healthcare services with different levels of complexity, which may in turn affect the effectiveness of process- and outcome-oriented reviews in persuading consumers.

3.3.2.1. Process-Oriented Reviews. As discussed earlier, process-oriented reviews can trigger consumers to perform process simulations. Under the process simulation mode, consumers tend to imagine the specific steps involved in the treatment and recovery process, which improves their perceived feasibility (Cian et al. 2020). Consequently, process-oriented reviews are expected to be more useful for high-complexity services whose feasibility can be a strong concern for prospective consumers (Hildebrand et al. 2014). In addition, process-oriented reviews for high-complexity services may provide more valuable information than those for low-complex services, because high-complexity services have a longer and more demanding recovery process (Wharton et al. 2016). For example, the process-oriented reviews for high-complexity services can inform prospective consumers of potential complications and provide tips about posttreatment self-care (Zhou et al. 2022), which are usually not experienced or required for low-complexity procedures. The higher informational value of processoriented reviews for high-complexity services also suggests that process-oriented reviews can be more useful for services with higher complexity. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 2. All else equal, the positive effect of processoriented reviews on sales of healthcare services is larger for those of high complexity than for those of low complexity.

3.3.2.2. Outcome-Oriented Reviews. Outcome-oriented reviews reveal the end states of healthcare services. The information in outcome-oriented reviews often shows the benefits or end results of healthcare services, which can trigger outcome simulation. Under the outcome simulation mode, prospective consumers tend to concen trate on the desirability of the outcomes (Thompson et al. 2009). The impact of outcome-oriented reviews may differ across high- and low-complexity services. For highcomplexity services, outcome-oriented reviews need not be useful because consumers tend to be more concerned about the feasibility of these services and pay less attention to the outcome before the feasibility concern is alleviated. Nonetheless, for low-complexity services whose perceived feasibility is usually high due to the simpler treatment procedures and shorter recovery process, consumers may focus their attention on the desirability of these services (Mobayed et al. 2020). Therefore, promoting the desirability of healthcare services through outcome-oriented reviews can be particularly effective for low-complexity services. Thus, we hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 3. All else equal, the positive effect of outcome-oriented reviews on sales of healthcare services is larger for those of low complexity than for those of high complexity.

3.3.3. Moderating Effect of Popularity. Among different healthcare treatments, some are more popular (or common) than others. For example, rhinoplasty is one of the most frequently performed procedures within the category of cosmetic surgery (Daniel 2018). Consumers often rely on popularity (e.g., the sales or review volume) to make their own decisions (Lin et al. 2019). The popularity of services can influence both perceived desirability and feasibility. The fact that a service has been consumed by many consumers is a clear indication that the service has desired outcomes among consumers (Tucker and Zhang 2011). Meanwhile, it is also a signal that the service is feasible. Hence, how popularity moderates the impact of process- and outcome-oriented reviews depends on how consumers weigh feasibility versus desirability for services with different levels of popularity.

3.3.3.1. Process-Oriented Reviews. For services with low popularity, consumers are usually not familiar with such services and hence are more skeptical about their feasibility. In such a case, process-oriented reviews showcasing the treatment and recovery process can help consumers mentally simulate the process of the services and improve their perception of the feasibility of the services. Conversely, for procedures with high popularity, feasibility is not expected to be a major concern because a large number of other consumers have already consumed the service, rendering processoriented reviews that are intended to demonstrate the feasibility to be less effective. Therefore, we posit that:

Hypothesis 4. All else equal, the positive effect of processoriented reviews on sales of healthcare services is larger for those of low popularity than for those of high popularity.

3.3.3.2. Outcome-Oriented Reviews. There can be two competing arguments on how popularity moderates the impact of outcome-oriented reviews on the sales of healthcare services. On the one hand, for services with low popularity, the outcome-oriented reviews highlighting the desirability of services may be relatively less useful because consumers may be primarily concerned with feasibility while deciding whether to consume these services. For popular services whose feasibility is less likely to be a concern, the outcome-oriented reviews promoting desirability are more likely to receive a positive response from consumers (Shukla et al. 2021). This line of reasoning suggests that the impact of outcome-oriented reviews can be stronger for more popular services.

On the other hand, since the desirability of popular services is already high, outcome-oriented reviews featuring desirability can also be less effective for such services due to the law of diminishing marginal utility. In comparison, outcome-oriented reviews may be more useful for unpopular services with relatively low desirability (Tucker and Zhang 2011). These arguments suggest that the impact of outcomeoriented reviews can also be weaker for more popular services.

Based on these two opposite sets of arguments, we propose the following two competing hypotheses regarding how the impact of outcome-oriented reviews varies with popularity.

Hypothesis 5a. All else equal, the positive effect of outcome-oriented reviews on sales of healthcare services is larger for those of high popularity than for those of low popularity.

Hypothesis 5b. All else equal, the positive effect of outcome-oriented reviews on sales of healthcare services is larger for those of low popularity than for those of high popularity.

As of April 2018, there were 25 million registered users and 7,000 hospitals/clinics (service providers) on the platform. The platform allows a consumer to make an appointment for a cosmetic procedure online after paying a deposit, which is usually around 20% of the total cost of the procedure. Note that the appointment is scheduled for the completion of the cosmetic procedure, rather than for a general consultation. Upon visiting the service provider, the consumer must provide the platform’s reservation code and settle the remaining payment before receiving the treatment. Therefore, online appointments can be viewed as sales contracts secured by deposits.

Note that functionally identical procedures offered by different service providers are considered different procedures. The platform classifies all procedures into 16 broad categories. We focus on the 12 categories related to facial treatments (details in Table A1 of Online Appendix A.1), because we separate process- and outcomeoriented reviews based on an analysis of facial images.

We collected descriptive information about each procedure (e.g., price, complexity, popularity, and service provider). We then scraped all historical appointments and online diaries for each procedure. After removing inactive procedures (i.e., those with no variation in the number of diaries or sales over time), we are left with 9,796 procedures that account for 85.3% of sales transactions on this platform. We also collected 114,478 diaries with 443,947 reviews (posts) related to facial treatments. We extracted detailed information for each review, such as the creation date, the textual content, and the attached images. On the platform, each review must be accompanied by at least one photo and has 4.78 photos on average. We downloaded all images in the reviews, resulting in a set of 2,120,153 images in total.

## 4.2. Image Classification

We differentiate process-oriented reviews from outcomeoriented reviews based on whether the attached images include cues (e.g., bandages and surgical wounds) indicating the consumer is still in the recovery process. Since we have over 2 million images, it is infeasible to manually label all of them. Therefore, we manually labeled 5,000 randomly selected images and then used this labeled sample to finetune pretrained deep learning models to classify the images (see Online Appendix B for details on the image labeling process). We finetuned several stateof-the-art pretrained general-purpose image classification models, including ConvNeXt (Liu et al. 2022), Vision Transformer (Dosovitskiy et al. 2020), and Swin Transformer (Liu et al. 2021). Given that the identification of process images requires the detection of specific features including bandages and wounds, we also finetuned EfficientDet, an object detection model pretrained on the COCO data set (Tan et al. 2020), to detect whether there is a bandage or wound in an image, based on 1,409 manually tagged images. The architecture of the objective detection model is illustrated in Figure 1. With the object detection model, we classify an image as a process image if either a bandage or a wound is detected. Table 2 sum marizes the performance of various models on a 10% holdout sample. Since the object detection model performs the best, we use it for image classification.<sup>2</sup>

As a sanity check, we randomly select 100 unlabeled images that are predicted to be process images and another 100 unlabeled images that are predicted to be outcome images. Then, we hire a team of human annotators to label whether each image is a process or outcome image. The Krippendorff’s alpha for the three annotators is 0.806, indicating a high level of agreement among the three annotators. The prediction of our object detection model is consistent with the majority of the annotators in 91% of cases (very close to the 92.3% accuracy on the test set), which confirms the reliability of our image classification method.

## 4.3. Variable Construction

Based on the raw data, we constructed a monthly panel data set for procedures over four years from February 2014 to February 2018. Below, we explain the variables used in our empirical analysis.

4.3.1. Dependent Variable. The dependent variable in this study is the monthly sales (i.e., appointments) of each procedure on the platform.

4.3.2. Focal Independent Variables. Our two focal independent variables are the number of process-oriented reviews (ProcessReviews) and the number of outcomeoriented reviews (OutcomeReviews) newly created for a procedure in a given month. We classify a post in an online diary as a process-oriented review if two or more attached photos are process images, an outcomeoriented review otherwise. We use a threshold of two instead of one to minimize the risk of misclassifying an outcome-oriented review as a process-oriented review. In Online Appendix F.1, we show that our main findings still hold when using a threshold of one.

4.3.3. Moderating Variables. The platform provides information regarding the complexity and popularity for each category of cosmetic procedures on a scale of 0 to 5. Complexity is a numerical measure indicating how complex a cosmetic procedure is, such as the duration and difficulty level of operations. Invasive procedures involving surgical operations generally have a higher complexity than noninvasive procedures. The popularity of cosmetic procedures represents how many users on the platform are paying attention to a cosmetic procedure. A high level of popularity indicates that a cosmetic procedure has a large potential customer base. We transform the two moderators into dummy variables for easier interpretation in the following analyses. On the platform, procedures with complexity ratings of 4 or 5 are mostly invasive, requiring surgical operations and having longer recovery periods. Therefore, we define a procedure as having high complexity (HighComplexity) if its complexity rating is above 3 (excluding). Given that 81.6% of procedures in our sample have a popularity rating of 5, we consider a procedure to have high popularity (HighPopu larity) only if its popularity rating is 5. We also consider alternative measures for complexity and popularity in our robustness checks (Section 6.1).

4.3.4. Control Variables. We control for a number of variables that may affect the sales of cosmetic procedures. For example, we use the cumulative number of diaries to control for the potential awareness effect of online diaries (Dellarocas et al. 2007, Duan et al. 2008). We also use the cumulative sales to account for the popu larity of each procedure, which has been shown to convey quality signals (Zhu and Zhang 2010). Moreover, we consider the impact of competition by controlling for the number of functionally identical procedures offered by other hospitals/clinics in the same city and the average sales of those procedures. Table 3 outlines the descriptions of all the variables used in this study. The descriptive statistics of and the correlation among these variables are provided in Online Appendix A.

## 5.2. Identification

A major concern with our findings is that the focal variables (i.e., ProcessReviews and OutcomeReviews) may be endogenous due to the existence of unobserved timevarying factors that affect both the sales and the review volumes, such as marketing efforts of service providers. To address the endogeneity issue, we consider two types of instrumental variables (IVs) for identification: 1) exogenous shocks in the platform-wide incentive policy on diary contribution, and 2) internal IVs constructed from the generalized method of moments (GMM). We focus on the identification of the main effects in Sections 5.2.1 and 5.2.2 and then delve into the identification of the interaction effects in Section 5.2.3.

5.2.1. Instruments Based on Policy Changes in the Incentive Program. Similar to some other online platforms for consumer-generated reviews, our focal platform provides monetary incentives for users to contribute diaries (Burtch et al. 2018). Specifically, users who write diaries meeting certain requirements are qualified to apply for monetary rewards from the platform. These requirements include the minimum number of posts in a diary, the minimum number of words and images in each post, the minimum interval between posts (i.e., update frequency), and among others. The focal platform launched the incentive program since its inception. During our observation window, the platform updated the policy eight times (details in Online Appendix C). Within each policy update, the platform typically modified the thresholds on diary content (e.g., the minimum number of posts or images), which is expected to have strong effects on whether and how often users create/update their diaries. Since these changes in the threshold requirements for words, images, and interposting durations were universally applied across the platform and not tied to the characteristics of any individual procedures, the implementation of these changes is not expected to affect the perceived costs (e.g., price and risk) and benefits (e.g., improvement in appearance) of individual procedures, after explicitly controlling for the size of the monetary reward. Therefore, we posit that these policy shocks are not likely to influence the sales of individual procedures, except through their impact on the contribution of online diaries. As such, the policy changes in the minimum requirements of the incentive program are plausible IVs.

We generate three IVs based on the incentive policy: the minimal number of reviews required in a diary (Min-ReviewNum), the minimal number of images required in a review (MinImageNum), and whether there is a requirement on update frequency (HasUpdateFreqReq). Since these three instrument variables based on policy changes are essentially a function of time, the model could be over-specified if we include month-level fixed effects as in the main model. Thus, we use dummies for the 12 months of a year, instead of a dummy for every month in our observation window, to control for temporal effects in the IV regression model. A fourth variable that is part of the incentive policy is the reward size (Reward-Size). We do not use the reward size as an IV because it can affect the effective cost of a procedure and hence influence sales. Instead, we use the reward size as a control variable. Moreover, the threshold requirements of the incentive policy may depend on the deficiencies in the review generation, which can be a potential confounding factor. We address this issue by explicitly con trolling for past review volumes—the average log review number in the past quarter (i.e., AvgQtrLogReviewNum).

5.2.2. Internal Instruments from GMM. In our main model, we assume that sales in the current month depend on the cumulative sales in the past month. An alternative way to account for the dependence is to use the lags of dependent variables (Cao et al. 2018), resulting in a dynamic panel model. To fully account for the dependence between current and past sales, we keep adding lags of dependent variables into the models until there is no first-order autocorrelation in the error term. Eventually, we end up with three lags of the dependent variable in the regression model.

We estimate the dynamic panel model using system GMM, a well-established framework that supports using the differences and levels of lagged variables as IVs (Blundell and Bond 1998). In the estimation process, we treat log(ProcessReviews) <sub>�</sub> and log(OutcomeReviews) <sub>�</sub> as endogenous variables and use the differences and levels of the second to fourth lags of log(ProcessReviews) and log(OutcomeReviews) as IVs. Besides, we use the differences and levels of the second to fourth lags of log(sales) as IVs. Table 6 shows the results, in which Column 1 is estimated using one-step GMM (i.e., 2SLS) and Column 2 is estimated using two-step GMM. In both columns, the coefficients of log(ProcessReviews) and log(OutcomeReviews) <sub>�</sub> , are positive and statistically significant and the effect size of the latter becomes more than twice larger than that of the former. These results further confirm the robustness of our findings.

5.2.3. Identification of Moderating Effects. While the prior analyses demonstrate that the focal variables’ main effects are not driven by endogeneity, they omit the focal variables’ interaction terms with the moderators, which could also be endogenous. A common strategy to account for the endogeneity of the interaction terms is to include the interactions of the main terms’ IVs with the moderators as additional IVs and run 2SLS (Wooldridge 2010, p. 122; Balli and Sørensen 2013). However, this strategy ignores the fact that our endogenous variables are both counts and is particularly inefficient considering that we have up to six endogenous variables, leading to extremely large standard errors (see Table D2 in Online Appendix D.2 for details).

To improve the efficiency of the IV estimates, we consider an alternative strategy that involves two steps (Wooldridge 2010, p. 940). In the first step, we use a twoway fixed effects Poisson model to predict the two focal variables. In the second step, we use the predicted values of two focal variables (log-transformed), as well as their interactions with the moderator(s) when applicable, as IVs and then generate 2SLS estimates. This approach can enhance efficiency as the first step generates nonlinear predictions that are highly correlated with the focal variables, making them stronger IVs than the original IVs.

## 6.2. Ceiling Effect

Due to the capacity constraint, a service provider may only serve a limited number of consumers in a given month. As such, the service provider may stop accepting new appointments once the capacity limit is reached, leading to a ceiling effect. To demonstrate that our findings are not affected by the ceiling effect, we re-estimate our models in Table 4 using the quantile regression approach, which is robust to the potential ceiling effect. We employ the quantile regression for the panel data estimator (Powell 2022). Table 9 summarizes the results from quantile regression. The results are largely consistent with the main findings in Table 4, suggesting that our findings are unlikely to be an artifact of the ceiling effect.

## 7. Conclusion

Consumers in the digital age heavily depend on online reviews to inform their purchase decisions of not only products but also services. However, the consumption of nonexperiential services such as healthcare and education presents a unique challenge due to a noteworthy tension between the processes (means) and the outcomes (ends). The existing body of UGC literature has predominantly focused on contexts where the distinction between process and outcome is not salient, resulting in a significant gap in our understanding of how reviews addressing the process versus outcome aspects influence consumer choices of nonexperiential services. Our study fills this crucial gap by investigating the differential impacts of process- and outcome-oriented reviews on the demand for healthcare services, utilizing data from a digital health platform for cosmetic procedures. Furthermore, we examine how their effects vary across healthcare services with different levels of complexity and popularity.

Our empirical analysis reveals that, on average, outcome-oriented reviews have a more pronounced impact on the demand for healthcare services compared with process-oriented reviews. Furthermore, the effectiveness of these two types of reviews is contingent upon the complexity and popularity of healthcare services. Specifically, process-oriented reviews exert a greater influence on services characterized by higher complexity, while outcome-oriented reviews are more effective for those with lower complexity. Additionally, process-oriented reviews are more influential for less popular services, while outcome-oriented reviews demonstrate greater influence for more popular services. Our investigation into moderating effects also elucidates why outcome-oriented reviews have a stronger overall impact than process-oriented reviews: most services on the focal platform are low-complexity cosmetic procedures, for which outcome-oriented reviews prove to be more valuable.

## 7.1. Managerial Implications

The empirical findings from this study have important implications for both platform operators and service providers. Firstly, this study emphasizes the imperative for platform operators to reimagine their review systems, particularly within the domain of nonexperiential services where the tension between process and outcome is prominent. Conventional reviews often fail to capture the evolving experience in the consumption of nonexperiential services entailing extended processes, such as healthcare services or educational services. For these services, the adoption of online diaries, enabling consumers to continuously share their evolving experiences, emerges as a promising remedy.

Secondly, platform operators and service providers should consider promoting or incentivizing reviews tailored to the specific nature of their nonexperiential services. For services characterized by high complexity or those not yet popular, highlighting process-oriented reviews can alleviate consumer concerns about feasibility and build trust in the services. Conversely, for simpler or well-recognized services, emphasizing outcome-oriented reviews can help potential customers envision the desirable results and make informed decisions. This tailored review management strategy can enhance the effectiveness of UGC.

Thirdly, this study underscores the growing importance of visual content as a decisive factor in shaping consumer choices of nonexperiential services such as healthcare. Platform operators and service providers should actively encourage and potentially incentivize customers to contribute visual content that complements the textual aspects of reviews. Visual elements, such as images and videos, possess the unique capability to vividly portray service processes and outcomes, thereby enhancing consumer comprehension and evaluation. The investment in visual content creation has the potential to differentiate service providers from their peers and augment their online visibility.

Lastly, this research shines a spotlight on the importance of balancing feasibility and desirability in consumer decision making within the realm of service consumption. Service providers are advised not only to accentuate the appeal of desirable outcomes but also to conscientiously integrate the pragmatic and procedural dimensions within their marketing materials and reviews. By embracing this holistic approach, service providers empower consumers to make well-informed decisions that consider both the allure of the service and its practical feasibility.

## 7.2. Generalizability

Although our empirical investigation relies on data from elective healthcare services (i.e., cosmetic procedures), it’s crucial to recognize that the notion of distinguishing between process-oriented and outcome-oriented reviews is not confined to elective healthcare services but also applies to nonelective healthcare services (e.g., orthopedic surgeries). While the demand for nonelective healthcare services is less susceptible to the influence of UGC, process- and outcome-oriented reviews may still play similar roles in assisting consumers in assessing the feasibility and desirability of such services. As a result, in the context of nonelective healthcare services, process- and outcome-oriented reviews may exhibit similar effects on alternative outcome measures of interest, such as patients mental or physical readiness and their satisfaction.

Beyond healthcare services, the insights uncovered in this study hold the potential for broader relevance across a spectrum of nonexperiential service sectors, such as fitness and wellness services and educational services. These sectors share a notable common characteristic with healthcare services: achieving desired outcomes necessitates navigating a process that is not designed for enjoyment, and online reviews exert substantial influence over consumer decision making in these domains (Barton 2015, Chakraborty and Biswal 2023, Zhang and Sun 2024). For instance, in weight-loss programs, process-oriented reviews might delve into the specifics of a consumer’s workout routine and dietary plans. Conversely, outcome-oriented reviews would emphasize the final weight loss results. Moreover, educational services provide another excellent illustration of the potential generalizability. When students consider enrolling in a course (e.g., an online Business Analytics course), the process focuses on their experience during the journey of the course, whereas the outcome would involve the knowledge and skills they ultimately acquire. For these services, both the process and the outcome are vital but serve distinct roles in shaping individuals’ decision making. Nonetheless, it is essential to acknowledge that the relative importance of process- versus outcome-oriented reviews might hinge on how consumers prioritize process and outcome within the respective service sectors.

## 7.3. Limitations and Future Research

Our study has its limitations, which warrant potential directions for future research. First, while we develop our hypotheses based on a cohesive set of theoretical arguments regarding how process- and outcome-oriented reviews could increase the perceived feasibility and desirability of healthcare services through process and outcome simulations, respectively, we cannot directly test the validity of these arguments due to a lack of measures on consumers’ perceptions. It could be fruitful to further pinpoint the mechanisms behind the effects of processand outcome-oriented reviews using controlled online experiments.

Second, similar to online reviews found on other platforms (e.g., Amazon and Yelp), the online diaries available on the focal platform may be self-selected due to intrinsic and extrinsic motives (e.g., self-presentation and financial incentives). The present study concentrates on content consumption, specifically examining the impact of already-available content on sales. It is possible that our findings may not generalize to contexts where the contribution of reviews is driven by substantially different motives, though we are not overly concerned with this limitation because our hypotheses were formulated without presuming a specific mechanism for review generation. It could be a promising avenue for future research to explore the effects of various incentives on the production of process-oriented versus outcome-oriented reviews when necessary data are available.

Third, while this study categorizes reviews into process- and outcome-oriented based on information conveyed in the images, its primary focus lies in assessing the impact of the quantity of process- and outcomeoriented reviews. It does not explore the potential influences of specific image features, such as the extent of swelling in process images or the level of perceived physical attractiveness in outcome images. The rationale behind this choice is that these image features are undefined when there are no new diaries for a procedure in a particular month, which constitutes a significant proportion, approximately 61%, of observations at the procedure-month level. Future research could delve into the impact of image features in cases where missing values are less prevalent.
