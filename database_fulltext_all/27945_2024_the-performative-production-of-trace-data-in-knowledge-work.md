# The Performative Production of Trace Data in Knowledge Work

Abstract. The digitalization of organizational settings can provide seemingly unprecedented visibility into knowledge work practices through trace data. Although trace data could be a boon for monitoring knowledge work that has traditionally eluded detailed oversight, the broader literature on data suggests that increasing behavioral visibility through trace data can also motivate reactions that reduce the transparency of work practices and question the validity of the data. To reconcile these contrasting views, we draw from performativity and conduct an in-depth case study of trace data production in a learning management and analytics system at an international business school. We find that once knowledge workers become aware of trace data in the system, they start to shape not only their own actions against the anticipated uses of the data but also the actions of their colleagues, subordinates, and even their students. We conceptualize the changes as first- and second-order prefiguration and discuss how prefigurative changes go beyond gaming, impression management, and transfiguration work discussed in the literature. Finally, we theorize configuration work as metawork that emerges from the need to pay constant attention to how knowledge work practices produce trace data and to keep aligning the practices with data-based monitoring. The results contribute to the literature by shedding light on when, how, and to what effect trace data from knowledge work are performed.

## Introduction

The digitalization of organizational settings has significantly changed how knowledge work practices are carried out today (Bailey et al. 2010, Baptista et al. 2020, Leonardi 2021). Digital technologies enable new and often better ways to complete creative, complex, and nonroutine tasks that are characteristic of knowledge work, but they also offer a new kind of visibility into work practices through what have become known as trace data. Trace data are defined in the literature as traces of online behavior that are recorded as by-products of information system use (Howison et al. 2011, Berente et al. 2019, Østerlund et al. 2020). Such data can emerge from routine actions, such as loading a web page, sending a message, or submitting a web form and are assumed to offer naturally occurring representations of knowledge work practices that have traditionally eluded detailed oversight (Grover et al. 2020, Leonardi and Treem 2020, Pentland et al. 2020). Consequently, trace data have attracted considerable managerial and academic interest, and the data are increasingly harnessed to analyze and govern work environments in which knowledge workers operate (Newell and Marabelli 2015, Faraj et al. 2018, Bailey et al. 2022).

Analytics and decision making based on trace data typically assume that the data can be collected unobtrusively from the work environment (Howison et al. 2011, Grover et al. 2020, Leonardi 2021, Pentland et al. 2021) and that “their collection does not inconvenience the employees” (Hu¨ llmann 2019, p. 2). This would seemingly make trace data a boon for monitoring knowledge workers and a potent managerial resource that enables, for instance, the application of people analytics (Gal et al. 2018, Hu¨ llmann et al. 2021), algorithmic management (Pachidi et al. 2021, Waardenburg et al. 2022), and workforce monitoring systems (Delfino and van der Kolk 2021) to knowledge work. At the same time and in contrast to the idea of naturally occurring data about work practices, the broader literature on data suggests that increasing behavioral visibility granted by trace data can motivate reactions that reduce the transparency of work practices and call the validity of trace data into question. These reactions have been described as gaming (Stein et al. 2015, Marjanovic and Cecez-Kecmanovic 2017,

Faraj et al. 2018), impression management (Brivot and Gendron 2011, Leonardi 2014), and transfiguration work (Cunha and Carugati 2018), which describe how the subjects of data collection may engage in actively producing the data.

It would seem, on the one hand, that trace data can provide better insights into work practices and on the other hand, that they may end up obscuring work practices as knowledge workers start changing their behavior to shape the data. To reconcile these contrasting views on trace data, we draw on performativity as a lens to analyze how trace data shape and are shaped by knowledge work practices (Scott and Orlikowski 2008, Østerlund et al. 2020). The performativity lens helps to foreground ways in which the data are actively made to represent certain organizational realities (e.g., Østerlie and Monteiro 2020) and how the anticipated uses of trace data start shaping practices from which the data are generated (e.g., Scott and Orlikowski 2008). Instead of assuming that trace data are simply facts about behavioral events, we focus on the performative relationship between the data and work practices; knowledge workers produce the data through their practices, but data production may also end up reconfiguring those practices. Our research question is as follows. How do knowledge workers produce trace data? Previous studies have examined, for instance, how trace data have changed manual or more routine work (Rosenblat and Stark 2016, Mo¨hlmann et al. 2020) and called for research on the performativity of algorithmic systems built on the data (Faraj et al. 2018). However, we do not know how the nature of knowledge work is implicated in the production of trace data. Answering the research question allows us to reconcile contrasting views on trace data and to offer guidance on how trace data from knowledge work should be used in research and in practice.

To answer the research question, we study the production of trace data at an international business school that uses a sophisticated learning management and analytics system to monitor teaching-related practices. We find that once knowledge workers become aware of trace data collection in the system, they start to change their work practices in anticipation of benefits that the data may yield in the future. We call this prefiguration and identify two such patterns; workers engage in first-order prefiguration directed at changing their own dataproducing actions and in second-order prefiguration aimed at changing the actions of their subordinates, colleagues, and even students to produce more and better data from the perspective of the knowledge worker. We discuss how prefiguration differs from gaming and impression management as well as from post hoc performativity known as transfiguration work, and we identify boundary conditions on the performative production of trace data. Finally, we theorize configuration work as metawork that continuously transforms work practices to keep them aligned with data-based monitoring. Configuration work emerges in reaction to the need to pay constant attention to how trace data are produced from work practices and goes beyond any chilling effect that may push workers toward compliance because of panoptic visibility (Zuboff 1988). We contribute to the literature by shedding light on when, how, and to what effect trace data from knowledge work are performed.

## Monitoring Knowledge Work

Knowledge work is critical to the functioning of contemporary society, yet it has proven difficult to define exactly (Pyo¨ria ¨ 2005). The work reflects the nature of knowledge itself as an ambiguous, contested, and mediated phenomenon, and it typically entails nonroutine efforts that involve creative problem-solving by workers whose tasks are complex and varied (Blackler 1995, Alvesson 2001). Knowledge work usually involves manipulating information to produce new knowledge, which should be evaluated based on its quality rather than quantity (Drucker 1959, 1966). As such, knowledge work is often embedded in loosely structured organizational processes in which knowledge workers have considerable auton omy as well as responsibility to decide how to perform their duties (Costas and Ka¨rreman 2016). These characteristics of knowledge work have made it traditionally difficult to monitor and control knowledge workers because most direct or behavioral measures have lacked specificity, reliability, or efficacy with respect to knowledge work and its aims (Alvesson 2001, Rennstam 2012). Knowledge workers are typically evaluated using different approaches in tandem: for instance, by using normative regulation, such as performance targets, mentoring, and professional culture, in combination with punctuated bureaucratic controls, such as self-reporting and occasional performance reviews (Ka¨rreman and Alvesson 2004, Costas and Ka¨rreman 2016).

Trace data would seemingly expand opportunities to monitor digital knowledge work so that the data could help manage knowledge workers in a new way (Blackler 1995). However, using the data can also create new dilemmas, as the generally weak link between specific behaviors and performance makes it inherently difficult to quantify the performance of knowledge workers (Drucker 1959, 1966; Cunha 2013). Such issues are inherently present in the empirical context of this study. Teaching university students (and engaging in research) involves complex, nonroutine tasks that are directly related to knowledge creation, manipulation, and diffusion. Academic knowledge workers have traditionally enjoyed high levels of autonomy over the processes and practices of teaching and assessing students (Kallio and Kallio 2014, Alvesson and Spicer 2016, Kallio et al. 2016), but at the same time, academic institutions are introducing sophisticated learning management systems that can record “detailed and extensive trace data” from teaching-related practices (Winne 2020, p. 106457). The data are then represented in dashboards and through predictive analytics, and they can be further incorporated into the management of teaching operations and decision-making practices at different levels of academic organizations (Hakimi et al. 2021). However, the degree to which trace data can represent knowledge work in general and teaching in particular remains an open issue (Winne 2010, 2020).

## Trace Data

Trace data from work environment record employees actions and interactions as well as associated events unfolding in information systems (Avital et al. 2021, Leonardi 2021). The summary of trace data definitions from the literature in Online Appendix A shows that such data are commonly understood as by-products of information system usage that can be collected unobtrusively without inconveniencing the subjects of data collection. The data are identified, for instance, as “residues left behind by multiple types of behavior that are collected by digital platforms” (Lindberg 2020, p. 90), “logs of employee behavior” (Leonardi 2021, p. 250), or “timestamped event log data” (Pentland et al. 2021, p. 968). Several papers refer to Howison et al. (2011), who define digital trace data as “records of activity (trace data) undertaken through an online information system (thus, digital)” (Howison et al. 2011, p. 769). Trace data may consist of time-stamped records of actions, such as posting comments, connecting with other users, viewing or editing documents, downloading files, logging in or out of a system, or any other action performed via a system interface. As such, the data are seen as (i) strictly behavioral, as they capture traces of users’ actions but not what those actions mean for the actors (Grover et al. 2020, Pentland et al. 2020), and as (ii) naturally occurring rather than being actively produced (Howison et al. 2011, Leonardi 2021).

Fueled by the digitalization of work environments, trace data are considered a major opportunity to understand employee behavior in research and managerial practice (Andersen et al. 2016; Berente et al. 2019; Pentland et al. 2020, 2021; Leonardi 2021; Bailey et al. 2022). The fact that the data are not designed and produced for research purposes is thought to eliminate certain researcher-induced biases (Grover et al. 2020). However, it can also cast doubt on the validity of trace data as “the data are not intended as measures of a concept of theoretical interest” (Østerlund et al. 2020, p. 3). Trace data put the onus on the researcher to establish “what can be inferred about higher-order constructs from the existence of the trace data” (Howison et al. 2011, p. 770). The issues of validity and what trace data can represent become even more complicated if subjects start to perform the data (Marjanovic and Cecez-Kecmanovic 2017, Østerlund et al. 2020). For instance, the use of trace data in educational settings tends to assume that the data can be collected unobtrusively to accurately represent teaching-related practices (Macfadyen and Dawson 2010, Greller and Drachsler 2012, Hakimi et al. 2021), despite the fact that educators have been found to engage in various gaming behaviors (Dahler-Larsen 2014, Alvesson and Spicer 2016, Stein et al. 2019). Therefore, the data could seemingly provide both greater behavioral visibility and less transparency of work practices if knowledge workers start to shape the data to their own interests (Leonardi and Treem 2020).

## Performativity, Work Practices, and the Production of Trace Data

The broader literature on data challenges the depiction of trace data as naturally occurring facts that are found rather than actively produced; “trace data are performative. Not only records of performance, they also contribute to the constitution of the reality that they trace,” note Østerlund et al. (2020, p. 3). In the following, we draw from performativity and practice perspectives to introduce our theoretical lens and unit of analysis, and we discuss how they frame trace data as our phenomenon of interest.

From the perspective of performativity, organizational reality is produced and reproduced in practice (Scott and Orlikowski 2008, Cecez-Kecmanovic et al. 2014, Orlikowski and Scott 2014). Organizational entities, including technological artifacts, are seen as performative accomplishments that both shape the organizational reality and are in turn shaped by it (Callon 2009, 2010; Garud et al. 2018). In this sense, trace data are no different from any other artifact. They become organizationally effective entities by being enacted as a part of work practices and thereby being involved in performing a certain organizational reality (Jones 2019, Alaimo et al. 2020, Aaltonen et al. 2021). The performativity lens puts focus on how technological artifacts are ‘worked out’ in practice as opposed to conceiving them as abstract structures, resources, or rules that the artifacts may (or may not) stand for (Orlikowski 2002, Cunha and Carugati 2018). Work, in turn, is conceived as “knowledgeable human action and how its recurrent engagement with a technology constitutes and reconstitutes particular emergent structures” (Orlikowski 2000, p. 421). Thus, performativity suggests that an awareness of data collection could motivate knowledge workers to reflect upon the relationship between their actions and the representations of those actions that may emerge from the data and consequently, to try produce the data differently.

Organizational reality is performed through work practices that are enacted jointly by different actors and resources. Whatever structures or structural forms there may be, these must be actualized in practice to have an effect on workers and their work (Orlikowski and Scott 2015). Work practices have a certain regularity informed by shared meanings; that is, a practice is not a one-off action but a recurrent pattern of meaningful activity that is mediated by material conditions (Schatzki et al. 2001). In the context of trace data, this means that although knowledge workers may harness trace data for their work practices, they are also capable of taking into consideration that trace data will be generated from those practices and that such data may become part of subsequent iterations of their own practices or those of their subordinates and managers. Consequently, as workers become aware of trace data, they may start adapting their practices with a view to producing different data. This, in turn, may then lead knowledge workers to change their practices yet again, resulting in a mutually shaping performative relationship between the practices and trace data (Garud et al. 2018). To summarize, we harness performativity (theoretical lens) to understand the production of trace data as a mutually shaping relationship between work practices (unit of analysis) and trace data (phenomenon of interest), and we focus specifically on how the production of trace data shapes work practices and vice versa. The current literature offers a few hints as to what such performative production might entail.

To begin with, it may often be difficult to keep harvesting trace data inconspicuously from knowledge workers, who can become aware of data collection in several ways (Zuboff 1988, 2015). Employees have been found to go out of their way to be digitally seen because of the fear of being overlooked in contemporary work environments (Leonardi and Treem 2020, Hafermalz 2021) and to overcommit to their work because of the behavioral visibility that the data provide (Jones 2019, Delfino and van der Kolk 2021). Various reactions to data collection have been explained as impression management fostering a favorable image of oneself through data (Brivot and Gendron 2011, Leonardi 2014) or gaming that entails adjusting behavior to attract rewards without actually working toward the performance targets of the system (Stein et al. 2015, Marjanovic and Cecez-Kecmanovic 2017, Faraj et al. 2018). These and other similar reactions are typically based on the human capacity to make anticipatory changes driven by potential future uses of the data (Barley 2015). The actual generation of trace data is then triggered by the actual use of digital systems in work practices. Let us call this inscription, by which we refer to an operation that selectively captures human actions as data (Flyverbom et al. 2016, Alaimo and Kallinikos 2017, Alaimo et al. 2020). Only actions that are taken using a digital system can be directly inscribed and only along the attributes defined by system developers. In other words, the design of a system interface and a data struc ture define what kind of behavioral events can be seen through trace data (Kallinikos and Tempini 2014; Leonardi 2014, 2021; Alaimo and Kallinikos 2017; Leonardi and Treem 2020). The data can never be “a direct, objective and exhaustive representation of the phenomenon, but the outcome of both a number of choices … and a number of technical, social and economic contingencies” (Jones 2019, p. 11). Once the data have been captured, they need to be represented: for instance, by constructing data visualizations that can be embedded in furthe work practices (Stein et al. 2013, Barley 2015, Dourish and Cruz 2018). These operations require data work that helps to establish meaningful connections between workers, their practices, and technology and to align work practices with data infrastructures (Aaltonen et al. 2021, Parmiggiani et al. 2022). Finally, work that cannot be automatically recorded may need to be manually transcribed into the systems that support decision mak ing (Stein et al. 2019). This may entail transfiguration work by which managers and workers post hoc intervene in reporting data in information systems (Cunha 2013, Cunha and Carugati 2018).

To summarize, the broader literature questions the status of trace data as naturally occurring representations of work and calls for greater focus on how trace data are performatively produced in practice. We learn that individual workers may anticipate and try to shape the production of trace data, but we do not know how and to what effect this happens in the context of knowledge work in which the link between specific behaviors and performance is weak. Also, current research largely falls back on the notions of gaming and impression management as proxies for performativity and investigates trace data from an individual perspective that does not capture implications for practices and knowledge work itself.

## Research Design

Our research interest motivates an in-depth case study, which is a qualitative approach for studying complex events that cannot be neatly separated from their context and over which researchers have no control (Yin 2018). This approach allows us to study performativity empirically by analyzing work practices and integrating findings with the existing literature to theorize the production of trace data (Sayer 1992, Edmondson and McManus 2007). A qualitative approach is also required to understand the meanings that knowledge workers attribute to their actions as they enact work practices with trace data (Orlikowski and Baroudi 1991). We chose a higher education institution as the research site for two reasons. First, work in educational institutions is about knowledge creation, manipulation, and diffusion, which offer a perfect example of knowledge work (Alvesson 1993). Second, the setting provided unrestricted access to knowledge workers who could talk freely about their work. One of the authors previously worked as an academic at an international business school of a public university located in the United Kingdom (hereinafter the School), and we used this relationship to obtain access to the site. Although the employment relationship facilitated access to knowledge workers, we minimized its potential impact on data collection by assigning all data collection to the other author, who had no prior relationship with the School.

## Research Site

The School has over 300 employees, mostly academics, teachers, and administrators, and nearly 10,000 students enrolled in various undergraduate, postgraduate, and MBA programs. To maintain its reputation as a provider of high-quality education and for instance, to support accreditation efforts, the School has traditionally monitored teaching operations through punctuated selfreporting (e.g., annual performance reviews, teaching feedback, employee time use surveys) and accreditation exercises that require employees to occasionally report on their work and performance. As such, the monitoring of teaching-related practices used to be relatively infrequent and involved knowledge workers as actors in the reporting process. An important part of the School’s operational infrastructure is a sophisticated learning management system developed by an internal software development team. The learning management system also functions as an intranet for employees and serves many administrative functions. The system was originally developed to serve a distance learning MBA program but has been gradually expanded to support all teaching and teaching-related activities. The system is largely on par with commercial off-the-shelf systems and is developed and maintained under the supervision of the Technology Strategy Committee. In 2012, the committee approved a project presented by the Head of Teaching and Learning Support to introduce “Enhanced Support for DL [distance learning] Tutors,” and in 2013, “Monitoring Lesson Understanding and Tracking Progress” features were introduced into the system— collectively described as “engagement analytics.” The first project was superseded in 2014 by “Student and Tutor Monitoring for Online Modules,” and the development of a learning analytics system collecting trace data started at the same time. In its minutes, the committee noted that “it was felt that this technology would be well used in distance learning and was enthusiastically welcomed by the tutors.” In 2017, when we began our data collection, the use of learning analytics and the underlying trace data was becoming common across the School, providing a window of opportunity to study the production of trace data.

## Data Collection

Our relationship with the research site helped us to collect rich evidence of how trace data are produced, despite the production of such data often being an inconspicuous or even hidden aspect of work, a point to which we briefly return in the discussion of our results (Jones 2014, Baptista et al. 2020). The author responsible for data collection built a rapport with the workers in order to make them comfortable to talk about their perceptions and reactions to the collection of trace data (Myers and Newman 2007). Most of the data collection took place between March 2017 and January 2018. We began by inspecting the learning management and analytics system in the context of the courses that were made avail able to us. During the initial period of familiarization from March to June 2017, we took observation notes and screenshots of various features that represented trace data to employees and their managers. These observations were further complemented by notes taken along side the interviews, during which we asked interviewees about their perceptions of trace data and to show how they used the data in the system. Table 1 presents the summary of empirical evidence used for the study.

We identified the interviewees using a snowball sampling approach (Robson 2011). At the end of each interview, we asked who else we should interview about the system and the underlying trace data. To ensure that we were not referred only to employees who share similar views as those who we had already interviewed, we also asked for referrals to those who had different views about the data. We interviewed 29 people in academic, teaching, administrative, and systems development positions at different levels of seniority. Each interview was based on a guide adapted to the role of the interviewee (see the example in Online Appendix B) to ensure that we probed the interviewees consistently while letting the exact phrasing of questions emerge from the dis cussion with the interviewee (Orlikowski and Baroudi 1991, Myers and Newman 2007). We asked the questions in different ways and where possible, observed how the interviewee worked with trace data. In the final interviews, sampling only generated names that we had already interviewed, suggesting that the process had reached saturation and that further interviews would not provide significant additional insights. If possible, we asked to conduct the interview in the interviewee’s office and to see how they used the learning management and analytics system in practice (Hultin 2019). This was done to make relevant work practices salient and easy to verbalize in the situation (Barad 2003, Hultin 2019). Finally, we collected extensive minutes from the meetings of the Technology Strategy Committee, which provide a detailed view into technology development and adoption at the School.

## Data Analysis

Data analysis was an iterative process in which we inductively identified and theorized aspects of trace data production (Eisenhardt 1989, Yin 2018). We began by having one of the authors code each paragraph in interview transcripts, observation notes, and collected documents according to how individuals perceive, use, or otherwise relate to trace data in their work. We uncovered early in the analysis that interviewees reacted to the collection of trace data by changing their work practices to shape the collected data. To understand these changes in detail, we then followed an approach similar to that of Cunha and Carugati (2018) and analyzed each recognized practice in terms of (1) preconditions for action, (2) an issue that triggered the practice, (3) sequences of actions carried out and the explanations of these actions, (4) artifacts in use, (5) descriptions of use, and (6) the outcomes of actions, comparing these with and without the presence of trace data. Using these comparisons, we identified teaching-related practices that were being altered in response to trace data collection. Subsequently, we synthesized the codes we had attached to the data in the first round into inductively derived higher-order themes (Levallet et al. 2021). This resulted in the typification of six kinds of changes to teaching-related practices as knowledge workers became aware of and started to performatively produce the data. We then proceeded through multiple readings of the coded material to flesh out the details of each change (Eisenhardt 1989). Finally, we inductively conceptualized first- and second-order prefiguration as patterns by which knowledge workers target the actions of different organizational members to perform the data (Levallet et al. 2021).

## Findings

We first describe how the production of trace data evolved at the School and how knowledge workers became aware of the data. We then analyze changes to teaching-related practices in response to trace data awareness.

## Trace Data Awareness

The learning management and analytics system produces trace data by making a record of each action users take in the system. According to a software developer, there are “six or something like that” basic actions that a user can perform from the perspective of the system that are recorded as trace data. The system creates a data point for every click, inscribing actions into a database with the user id, type and time of action, IP address, and what resource (e.g., web page) was actioned upon. As such, the database pigeonholes the entire spectrum of teaching-related practices into a few behavioral categories defined by system developers. Meaningful work practices are broken down and inscribed by datums that represen page views, comments, video plays, log-ins, external resource creation and access, and a few other operations. These highly granular behavioral traces are then brought together, combined, and interpreted through data visualizations and analytics as proxies for teaching and learning. For instance, “log-in” becomes a proxy for engagement with teaching and learning; “view” stands in for reading a resource; “comment” inscribes participation; and “create,” such as uploading a photo or file, suggests participation in a specific learning activity.

The learning management system was initially developed to support the delivery of distance learning courses, and the aim of the analytics was to monitor student progress through materials and external tutors working remotely. Based on trace data, various analytics and visualization features were developed for the managers overseeing teaching. These include, for instance, aggregate number of views per resource, number of views per user per resource, date and time viewing statistics, individual progress per course, number of comments by each student and tutor, and aggregate data per teaching week. The use of trace data was initially limited to administrators involved in distance learning delivery who, for example, used the data to make hiring decisions. The data proved to be useful for this purpose, and as they provided a highly detailed view into the actions of teachers and their students, the data began to figure in resourcing decisions more broadly. “So in the first four weeks of the new distance learning MBA, there’s now been over 3,000 posts by students. There’s only 146 students in the course. [laughs]. It enables me to quote those stats when I’m at planning meetings and recruit meetings and say, ‘This is why we need more tutors on these programmes, because there’s a high level of interactivity.’ For example, look at this data and use that as an impetus to get people to agree to fund more online tutor [positions]” (Administrator/ Teacher 1). These experiences paved the way for expanding the use of the data across teaching programs at the School.

However, the expanding use of trace data was not broadly discussed, and there were no official announcements about it at the School. The collection of trace data and the availability of analytics features built on them were selectively communicated to those who were identified as having a potential use for the system, largely in line with the assumption in the literature that the collection of trace data is unobtrusive and does not inconvenience employees. For example, administrators monitoring teaching performance received individual, in-person training on how to use the data. Distance learning tutors were also presented with the analytics system and the underlying trace data together with an opportunity to explore the possibilities that features based on trace data could offer to them. Yet, no general announcements were made, no open training sessions were offered on the use of the data, and no written documentation or training materials were created to widely inform workers about the system. Instead, interviewees spoke about stumbling upon the data in the system or finding out about them through word of mouth. The interviewees recalled their impressions, sometimes invoking the imagery of Big Brother, panopticon, or Orwell’s 1984: “This [is] all a bit 1984 isn’t it?” (Developer 1). Another interviewee expressed their realization about the role of trace data. “[S]o now they’re able … so is somebody else using this data analytics not to help shape my teaching or the student’s learning, but [as] a managerial tool to evaluate our performance?” (Teacher 5).

## Changing Work Practices

Once knowledge workers became aware of trace data in the system, they developed various reactions to the data. We find that the workers started changing thei work practices in six different ways: curating data-based impressions, encouraging students to engage with dataproducing activities, restructuring existing teaching materials for data production, designing new learning activities that produce data, shifting pedagogical approaches to produce more data, and supporting investment in dataproducing teaching positions. In the following, we describe these changes before identifying two common patterns. Additional excerpts supporting our findings can be found in Online Appendix C.

Curating Data-Based Impressions. Because work is inscribed in the learning management and analytics system as views, comments, log-ins, and other similar events, academic knowledge workers figure out how to manage impressions about themselves and their performance so that their actions produce appropriate trace data. Perhaps the most obvious form of impression management happens when the workers occasionally engage with an online resource with the aim of producing data to give a certain impression rather than making use of the resource. This concerns both the workers and students.

I think if they knew it was collected and they knew that we could drill down on an individual basis and actually see how lazy or applied to their studies they are, it might actually increase their motivation. But the point is, it just counts clicks. So, if you want to look busy, you just click on a page … They’ll play the system. It’s good because it depends if student’s think, “I need to look as if I’m busy and engaging,” then they’ll play the system. I mean, sorry, that’s not just students, staff will play the system. If they think they’re being, you know, if staff think they’re being monitored as to how often they’re engaging, they’ll play the system. —Academic 2

In this case, engagement with a given resource loses its primary goal of learning or interacting with students. Instead, it becomes a way to produce trace data that can give a certain impression to whoever may use the data later. Although impression management has always been part of organizational life, with trace data the workers are now motivated to anticipate impressions that any potential action in a digital system may give.

Encouraging Students to Engage with Data-Producing Activities. As noted, the system also inscribes student actions as trace data, which could be used for evaluating teachers and courses, and for making decisions regarding the expansion or termination of course offerings. In the anticipation of the possibility that trace data may figure in such decisions in the future, knowledge workers encourage students to interact with the system in ways that should generate favorable data from the perspective of the worker. For instance, some instructions that are given to students are motivated by the production of additional data, yet it is usually not communicated to students that they are asked to take certain actions for this purpose. The rationale for such actions is evident in the words of an interviewee who admitted that “if we were fighting our corner, maybe we were fighting for survival as opposed to already being told we’ve been axed, if we were fighting for survival, we might use this data to construct ammunition as to how wonderful and active our students are” (Academic 2). We observe instances where students are steered to take certain actions that produce trace data even if there is no distinct pedagogical purpose for those actions or the pedagogical use of the data involves performing the data even further. For instance, another interviewee explained that she persuaded students by showing in the classroom how views are correlated with performance to increase student engagement with resources available in the system: “Ialso show them some data about how the video views have led to better performance in previous years” (Academic 1). According to the interviewee, representing the data back to students drove increased student activity that would be inscribed as more video views and could then be used to justify the success of a flipped classroom by showing that students engage with prerecorded materials. Such changes to practices become possible and make sense in the context of trace data production. If no trace data were collected to represent the learning activity at a later stage, there would be no need to motivate students to engage in specifically dataproducing activities.

Restructuring Teaching Materials for Data Production. A further change to teaching practices is that teaching materials need to be broken down into smaller chunks and reorganized according to a common template that enables the inscription of comparable trace data across courses. The data can then be used to compare, for instance, the popularity of a course or a given teaching activity against other courses and types of activities. The need to restructure materials into smaller chunks appears obvious, as “if you’ve just got a really standard module area where you’re just doing ten lectures and a seminar every week and all you’re posting up is the lecture resources and the information about the assignment, that metric screen won’t be available. It’s where you’ve got the more tailored learning unit model. Where you’ve got much richer, more structured information that the academics maybe spend quite a lot of time looking at” (Developer/Administrator 4). Granula and more voluminous data could then be used to represent a course or teaching activity as comparatively more popular and effective than others: “There are definitely institutional changes with the way we structure online teaching. So there’s been a huge push to develop a standard template for everybody who teaches. So a template for [learning management system] for uploading your materials. I guess in having that standard template it automatically standardizes the data you collect in the background because everybody’s materials are structured the same. I don’t think it’s a great idea actually” (Teacher 3). In this way, the granular nature of trace data finds its parallel in the need to restructure teaching materials to serve the production and representation of the data in the system.

## Designing New Learning Activities That Produce Data.

Knowledge workers not only encourage students to engage in certain activities and restructure teaching materials to support the production of trace data but also they design whole new learning activities so that their and their students’ actions can be even more broadly inscribed as views and comments in the system. Data from such activities could then, for instance, be presented in meetings to support extra funding for teaching-related positions. A worker who designed interactive course elements admitted that “from a very selfish level [laughter] I can get a bigger team through using those stats to prove that there’s a lot of need for more people to interact with the students that are posting those posts” (Administrator/Teacher 1), justifying her use of forum discussions and gated content in the course design by virtue of their capacity to drive the production of trace data that can be useful for the worker. To this end, new learning activities are designed with their spe cific data potential in mind: that is, by making sure that student activity will produce trace data for later use. Accordingly, pedagogy ceases to be the only explanation for activities being designed in a specific way as the workers design new learning activities to produce more trace data.

Shifting Pedagogical Approaches to Produce More Data. Because of the nature of the learning management and analytics system, the more interactive resources are, the more trace data they can produce. This, in turn, could be used as evidence for the popularity of a course or teaching activity that may be challenged. As explained by an interviewee, “instead of doing a lecture, you do a film so that the contact time is interacting rather than passive. It really matters where the people watch the video. So all the people doing that have been using analytics desperately, you know, to see whether it’s worth the investment in doing that” (Developer 1). In order to produce more trace data and to be able to use the data to represent a course or a teaching activity appropriately, employees have to change their existing teaching practices and materials to be compatible with trace data production. However, other than designing new learning activities that produce data in isolation, interviewees also discuss changes to the overall approach to teaching that go beyond piecemeal adjustments to individual activities, such as introducing online discussions. Instead, some perceive a general shift in pedagogical philosophy toward, for example, a flipped classroom and online learning even for on-campus students. The interviewees suggest that changes are needed because interactive, online teaching approaches can produce data; otherwise, certain courses may look less engaging and be at risk for termination.

Supporting Investment in Data-Producing Teaching Positions. Although teaching-related practices are increasingly inscribed as trace data in the system, other things that academic knowledge workers do, such as research, are not captured by the system or are captured to a much lesser degree. Thus, trace data can be used to support hiring for teaching-related positions, whereas arguments for other positions cannot be supported by the data in the same way. Some workers perceive a shift toward hiring teaching-only positions across the School or that certain teams are growing because the resource allocation can be justified by voluminous data: “I mean, Teaching and Learning Support here has been … is relatively new. We’ve got a dean of pedagogy now, an Associate Dean of Teaching and Learning. So there’s a whole kind of structure in there now to support teaching because I think that the data, I imagine, is saying that that is what we need” (Administrator 8). Workers with managerial responsibilities can use the data to justify hiring for teaching positions, which eventually helps to produce even more data as more teaching and learning activities are designed to produce trace data in the system. Of course, there are other reasons for investing in teaching positions, yet several interviewees make the link between recent hiring patterns and the fact that the activities of new positions are inscribed in the system and can be justified by representing them through analytics.

The six types of changes to teaching-related practices that we have discussed capture how trace data became performed at the School. We also observe that even those who decided not to change their practices felt the need to justify their attitude toward the production and potential use of trace data: “It’s [data] not how I go about things. I go about things by interacting with the students, getting to know them in a professional way, and reflecting and forever reflecting on what I’m doing” (Teacher 7). We also observe how the data are strategically represented back to students to motivate even more actions that can be inscribed as trace data. The changes involve some impression management and even gaming of the system, but importantly, they also transform teaching and learning in a way that is not captured by such labels. ## Analysis and Discussion

The findings show that as knowledge workers become aware of trace data in their work environment, the data stop occurring naturally. Most workers do not ignore the fact that their practices are now constantly producing trace data. Instead, we find that knowledge workers start making changes to their practices in reaction to the data. These changes have an unmistakably anticipatory character; whether the workers are aware of an actual managerial use for the data or not, they rationalize changing practices against anticipated future uses of the data. Adapting the term ‘figuration’ from Suchman (2012), who describes it as “an action that holds the material and the semiotic together in ways that become naturalized over time” (Suchman 2012, p. 49), we refer to such changes as the prefiguration of work practices. The term emphasizes both the anticipatory nature of the changes and the fact that the performative production of trace data entails changes to actual work practices and not just how they are reported. We specifically identify two types of performative production of trace data that operate through different subjects as shown in the third column of Table 2. We first discuss first-order prefiguration, which entails changes that shape knowledge worker’s own actions that are inscribed as trace data, and then, secondorder prefiguration, which entails changes that are aimed at shaping the ways in which others’ actions are seen through the data. We then move on to synthesize the findings about the performative production of trace data with the literature and theorize configuration work that emerges from the need to constantly manage how trace data are produced in a digital work environment.

## The Prefiguration of Trace Data

Our findings show how trace data capture the attention of knowledge workers and make them anticipate possible future uses of the data within the organization. The workers try to figure out what kind of data could be beneficial, given the anticipated uses, and change their work practices to shape the actions that are inscribed as trace data. We find that knowledge workers make such self-oriented changes to their practices by (I) curating data-based impressions of their own behavior, (III) restructuring teaching materials for data production, (IV) designing new learning activities that produce data, and (V) shifting pedagogical approaches to be more amenable for the production of trace data. We call these anticipatory adjustments to practices that are aimed at shaping the data that are inscribed from the worker’s own actions first-order prefiguration. The first-order prefiguration of work practices differs from gaming in that the expected benefits are envisioned: that is, projected to the future. Gaming, on the other hand, typically happens against immediate or almost immediate behavioral feedback from the system, such as when Uber drivers log off to trigger surge pricing (Rosenblat and Stark 2016, Faraj et al. 2018, Mo¨hlmann et al. 2020). Teaching in a higher education institution is too complex and entails dealing with ambiguity in a way that makes it difficult to assess and reward (or punish) knowledge workers’ performance based on trace data alone. Therefore, in contrast to gaming, we find that knowledge workers engage in the prefiguration of work practices largely based on their perceptions of what kind of trace data could be interpreted favorably in the future.

We also find that many of the anticipatory reactions to trace data concern how others involved in the knowledge worker’s practices produce data through their actions. A similar, other-oriented, or even hierarchical pattern in how performance data are performed has been observed in the self-reporting of performance data. Managers have been found to intervene in how employees provide data from their work through mandatory reporting practices, which is known as transfiguration work (Cunha 2013, Cunha and Carugati 2018). However, in the case of trace data, knowledge workers must intervene directly in other organizational members’ work and not just in how they report it. We call this secondorder prefiguration. The term refers to patterns by which knowledge workers change work practices to shape the actions of others, such as coworkers, subordinates, or as in our case, even students, from whom trace data are produced. Distinct from transfiguration work that focuses on post hoc reporting of work performance, secondorder prefiguration introduces changes directly to the work of others before the actions that are inscribed as data take place. To this end, the workers at the research site make other-oriented changes to work practices by (II) encouraging students to engage with dataproducing activities, (III) restructuring teaching materials for data production, (IV) designing new learning activities that produce data, (VI) supporting investment in data-producing teaching positions, and indirectly, (V) shifting pedagogical approaches to produce more data. We even observe instances in which the workers repre sent trace data from organizational members back to them to encourage further data production.

To summarize, we have analyzed how knowledge workers performatively produce trace data by making prefigurative changes to their practices in response to trace data awareness. Because work practices are jointly enacted by the actions of different parties, we are not surprised to find that some changes are related to both firstorder and second-order prefiguration. The findings show, for instance, how (III) restructuring teaching materials for data production and (V) shifting pedagogical approaches to produce more data affect how trace data are inscribed both from the focal knowledge worker initiating the changes and from students. This highlights an advantage of our research design that focuses on practices as the unit of analysis instead of studying individual workers in isolation; the findings reveal that different actors have different agencies with respect to the production of trace data. For instance, in our case, system developers have the capacity to define the categories under which actions are inscribed into the database, and teachers can affect inscription by restructuring teaching materials. On the other hand, students have little agency over the form of inscription. The question of agency in trace data production presents interesting research opportunities to be addressed in further studies.

## Configuration Work

Figure 1 provides a visual summary of our findings regarding the different types of changes that knowledge workers make to performatively produce trace data. In the figure, we use the unit of analysis (work practice) and three sensitizing concepts (anticipatory changes, inscription, and representation) from our theoretical framework as a backbone to which we map four looping arrows that represent the observed ways in which knowledge workers make anticipatory changes to trace data production. The loops stand for first- and secondorder prefigurative changes to work practices, changes to artifacts that shape the inscription of trace data, and the use of trace data representations to motivate further data-producing actions. We also indicate the types of changes associated with each loop at our research site using the enumeration in Table 2. The results answer our research question on how knowledge workers produce trace data by detailing six different changes to work practices and by relating them to each other and to the existing literature. Yet, the six changes to work practices should not be understood as one-off reactions to the ini tial ‘shock’ of becoming aware of trace data collection. Instead, we argue that such changes can become part of performative cycles in which work practices are adjusted to shape the trace data that, in turn, may variously come to shape the practices requiring further adjustments.

In contrast to traditionally punctuated and infrequent forms of self-reporting, the performative cycles emerge in the context in which the constant production of trace data requires constant attention from knowledge workers. Because knowledge workers cannot naively trust trace data to represent their work that is complex, varying, and ambiguous in nature (Blackler 1995, Costas and Ka¨rreman 2016) and that often lacks clear performance measures (Drucker 1959, 1966; Alvesson 2001; Rennstam 2012), they start variously changing their practices to produce data that could offer favorable interpretations in the future. These changes are not one-off adjustments but indicate a new kind of relationship between knowl edge workers and their work environment; the performatively produced trace data from work practices reenter their own and related managerial practices, shaping the practices and calling for renewed attention to the data that are produced from the altered practices. Consequently, the need to pay constant attention and readiness to react by making adjustment to work practices can amount to a new type of metawork.

Performing the data entails engaging in constant figuration (shaping) of how the work and its potential digital representations fit together (Suchman 2012) instead of just implementing one or more of the types of changes upon becoming aware of trace data. Figuration efforts are carried out on top of or together with (con) primary work tasks. Thus, we refer to this type of work, which is essentially about managing the performative production of trace data, as configuration work. Configuration work is unofficial and managerially inconspicuous yet real work that knowledge workers do aside their primary duties. It is distinct from specific tactics, such as specific prefigurative changes to work practices, that the work may draw upon differently at different times to adjust the production of data. Configuration work can entail first- and second-order prefiguration of work practices, changes to artifacts used to inscribe trace data, and representation of the data back to its subjects as illustrated in Figure 1. Other tactics to shape the production of trace data are also possible. Finally, to further distinguish configuration work from specific (prefigurative) changes to work practices, we observe that even those who refused to change their practices had to make an effort to justify their position vis-a\`-vis the presence of trace data in the system.

Knowledge workers were not compelled to engage in configuration work under traditional, punctuated monitoring, whereas they are drawn into it under constant monitoring that is made possible in a digital work environment (Leonardi 2014, 2021; Leonardi and Treem 2020; Hafermalz 2021). We stress that although the potential for managerial interventions based on trace data may have initially motivated the collection of data, our findings suggest that actual interventions are not a necessary condition for the emergence of configuration work. Instead, knowledge workers start to transform their work practices in response to perceived potential future uses of the data. These may be no more than educated guesses. Configuration work is not motivated by organizational aims, yet it is necessary from the perspective of workers to keep the production of trace data aligned with the way in which the workers wish their efforts to be seen within the organization. It could be seen to complement articulation work; workers engage in the latter to make technology work for an organization (Suchman 1995), whereas configuration work is about making digital systems and data work for an individual.

## Boundary Conditions

Let us observe a few boundary conditions to the performative production of trace data that help to assess the external validity of our results. To begin with, trace data subjects must be (i) aware of the data collection and presumably, also (ii) interested in how the data might portray them to start performatively producing the data. However, actual managerial interventions based on the data may not be needed to motivate such reactions. The performative production of trace data will also likely differ with respect to the strength of the link between the work and its representations. For instance, individual Uber drivers may game the system, but the data will nevertheless reveal whether the primary task of delivering the customer to their destination was completed successfully or not. In the case of knowledge work, this link is often more ambiguous; the harder it is to pin down meaningful performance as behavioral events, the more the workers may perceive a need to engage in configuration work to keep aligning the data with their anticipated uses. These observations also go some way toward reconciling the contrasting views on trace data in the literature; the degree to which trace data can be assumed to be naturally occurring or whether they should be seen as performed data depends on the setting and the type of work.

## Contributions to Research

Our study makes contributions to the literature by shedding light on when, how, and to what effect trace data from knowledge work are performed. First, our results show that trace data may not always be as naturally occurring as it is commonly assumed to be in the literature (Howison et al. 2011, Choi 2020, Pentland et al. 2020). We find that, as knowledge workers become aware of trace data in their work environment, they start to performatively produce the data. When and to what extent this happens will vary from one setting to another and can be at least provisionally assessed using the boundary conditions discussed in the previous section. The more established the specific forms of prefiguration of trace data become over time, the less the data can be said to be naturally occurring in the setting. This suggests that a researcher using trace data must be mindful of their nature as behavioral traces that may be performed for a certain purpose by the subjects. The data may represent, for instance, clicks on a user interface. However, it may be difficult to establish what those clicks say about work if work practices are shaped to produce certain data and there is a weak link between the work and its representations, as in the case of teaching in higher education settings (Winne 2020, Hakimi et al. 2021). Researchers harnessing methodologies, such as the one developed by Pentland et al. (2021) for analyzing process dynamics or that by Zhang et al. (2022) for theorizing routines from trace data, should thus establish how the data might have been performatively produced. To this end, our theorization helps by identifying the conditions of trace data awareness and interest under which the subjects are likely to engage in the performative production of trace data. Finally, we encourage attempts to tailor the aforementioned or similar methodologies to analyze how performative changes to work practices shape trace data and the practices over time.

Second, our results show how trace data are produced in and through work practices that are jointly enacted by different parties. We do so by distinguishing between first- and second-order prefiguration and related changes to practices that are made with the intent of shaping the data. Although the distinction may not always be clear cut and the same change may indeed be associated with both types of prefiguration, it is useful to keep the two types of prefiguration conceptually separate. This allows to analyze the scope and potentially hierarchical nature of trace data production and for instance, to disentangle how different actors may have different agency in shaping the data. Indeed, in contrast to studies on how individuals may game the system (Stein et al. 2015, Marjanovic and Cecez-Kecmanovic 2017, Faraj et al. 2018) or manage impressions (Brivot and Gendron 2011, Leonardi 2014) through data, our results show that the characteristics of trace data can also be understood in terms of the performative production of data in work practices. Also, although earlier works have found that intensive monitoring of knowledge workers through data can have a chilling effect on system usage and push the workers toward compliance (Zuboff 1988, 2015) or the appearance of compliance (Berente et al. 2010), our results show how trace data collection pushes knowledge workers to improvise around the data beyond mere compliance. We show that the prefiguration of trace data is driven by envisioned benefits rather than an internalized pressure to comply with rules or to respond to feedback from the system. This necessitates a closer look at the meanings that knowledge workers attribute to their practices to understand how trace data are performed.

Third, our results have implications for monitoring knowledge work through trace data (Leonardi and Treem 2020, Leonardi 2021). We show that collecting trace data to monitor knowledge workers will motivate them to engage in configuration work aimed at transforming the work itself to fit continuous data-based monitoring. The purpose of configuration work is not to improve performance but to keep transforming work practices so that they can give the impression of a certain performance at a later stage through the data. However, because trace data cannot be manipulated post hoc in the system, as has been the case previously with other types of performance-related data (e.g., Zuboff 1988, Cunha and Carugati 2018), the workers are compelled to change their actual data-producing behavior. These changes are not necessarily detrimental to the organizational purpose of work practices, but they can embed a paradox of performance (Alvesson 1993, Leonardi and Treem 2020); those who are good at doing the job itself may be less inclined to change their practices to make them look good than those who are less confident about their performance and thus, more motivated to engage in unproductive posturing (Berente et al. 2010). As a result, deep professional expertise that is often critical to successful knowledge work can become sidelined by monitoring based on trace data. Any algorithmic work system built on the data would then run the risk of not only ‘broken loop’ learning by missing out on the meanings associated with the actions captured as trace data (Tarafdar et al. 2022) but also, risk learning naively from data that certain knowledge workers have performed to enact a certain reality.

## Managerial Implications

Configuration work draws attention away from the pri mary aims of knowledge work and potentially puts trace data at cross purposes with the promise of increased efficiency through quantification and the use of data analyt ics to assess performance (Mennicken and Espeland 2019, Leonardi and Treem 2020, Alaimo and Kallinikos 2023). As a digital work environment will almost unavoidably inscribe some trace data, we suggest that organizations may find it useful to draw up a trace data policy that specifies the purposes for which trace data may and may not be used. This could not only curb unnecessary configuration work but also, be used to embrace the performativity of trace data by drawing attention to organizationally valuable behavior. Managers could use a trace data policy to specify that the organization uses the data, for instance, to measure response times to customer queries, such as emails from students at our research site. Although this might not remove the motivation to perform the data, it could help draw attention to specific behavior that is considered valuable to the organization. Also, the results call for greater focus on the design of trace data. As long as trace data are seen as naturally occurring by-products of technical operations, the specific form in which activities are inscribed is likely to be left to system developers. If trace data are to become truly valuable managerial resources, managers need to get involved in designing how and what kind of activities are inscribed as data.

## Concluding Remarks

In this study, we have analyzed how knowledge workers start to performatively produce trace data as they become aware of trace data collection in their work envi ronment. Our results show that (i) the workers make anticipatory changes to work practices that we conceptualize as first- and second-order prefiguration in response to trace data awareness and that (ii) the workers respond to ongoing trace data collection by engaging in configuration work aimed at transforming work practices so that they are inscribed appropriately in the data. A limitation of our study is that even if our results show how trace data are deeply involved in constituting changes to work practices, our research design does not allow us to claim that the presence of the data alone would cause those changes. We also note that our results may not apply, or may only partially apply, to other settings, such as social media, in which the conditions of trace data awareness and interest may not be present to a similar degree (Bailey et al. 2010, 2022; Cotter 2019;

Avital et al. 2021). Nevertheless, the results make a strong case as to why trace data should not be understood as naturally occurring representations of knowledge work by default; the data may produce a more detailed picture of work being done, but they also make people work differently.
