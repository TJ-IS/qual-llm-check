# Peer Influence and IT Career Choice

Abstract. The productivity of the information technology (IT) industry depends on the supply of high-quality human capital, especially of managers who contribute to operational, finance, sales and marketing, and leadership roles. This study examines the influence of peers on the choice a management student makes to pursue a career in the IT industry. Such a choice may be informed and driven not only by the student’s own motivation and ability but also by information gained through peers. Specifically, we analyze data on student networks at a leading business school in India, where students are exogenously assigned to peer groups, and link these to students’ choices regarding postprogram careers in the IT industry. We find that being part of a group that includes peers who have worked in IT reduces the likelihood of receiving and accepting an offer in the IT industry. If a student has had no IT experience, however, having IT peers ameliorates this effect to a certain degree. We also find differential peer effects for male and female students. Our finding are consistent with the notion that IT peers provide (largely discouraging) information about the IT industry to non-IT peers.

## Introduction

Information technology (IT) is increasingly central to corporate strategy (Sabherwal et al. 2019), not only increasing productivity and cost savings but also enhancing value (Gurumurthy et al. 2020, Tambe et al. 2021, Rahmati et al. 2021). As IT continues to inform firms’ strategic choices as they seek to understand customer preferences, which markets to compete in, and what products and services to offer, managers and executives become an important resource for realizing the role of IT in creating value and informing strategy (Luciano et al. 2020).

Although increased global IT spending to the tune of \$4.2 trillion in 2021 and a sharper, more strategic focus on IT (Gross 2016, Rimol 2021) augurs well for the growth of the IT industry worldwide, many firms— within the IT industry and those industries relying heavily on IT—struggle with finding, recruiting, and utilizing managers who know both business and technology and can manage technology-oriented projects (Fulmer and Hanson 2010, Beck and Harter 2014). These managers play vital roles in strategy, marketing and sales, and project management, as well as in organizing work for technical employees (Langer et al. 2014). Managers not only increase productivity (Bloom et al. 2010, 2013) but also play an important role in establishing organizational culture and providing the next generation of senior executives.

For these reasons, it is important from a business perspective to analyze factors that influence the entry of managerial talent into the IT industry and into IT roles outside the industry. Yet how individuals enter the IT industry, especially in managerial roles, is poorly under stood and under-researched. Our primary research question explores whether exposure to peers with IT experience influences the likelihood that a management student opts for a career in the IT industry or in a technology role in non-IT firms.

How do individuals make career decisions? Classical models of job search and matching assume that individuals have access to all information, leading to optimal labor-market decisions (Mortensen 1986). In emerging economies such as India, labor markets are characterized by severe information constraints, and therefore individuals may be unaware of information about available positions and their own suitability for these positions. In such settings, social connections become important sources of industry and job-specific information when individuals choose careers and occupations, invest in the appropriate education and skills, and search for suitable positions (Granovetter 1973, Marmaros and Sacerdote 2002, Beaman and Magruder 2012), especially in digital industries (Rajkumar et al. 2022). We posit that career choices into the technology industry and related roles may be informed and driven not only by a student’s own motivation and ability but also by the influence of experienced IT peers who might encourage or discourage IT careers.

We examine the role that IT peers play in influencing IT career choices in the context of the Indian IT industry, which posted double-digit growth rates between 2000 and 2018 (NASSCOM 2017a, b). India is widely considered an important IT destination (Sudan et al. 2010) with the sector slated to generate \$300–350 billion in revenues by 2025 (Hindu 2021). Several factors enable peers with experience in the IT industry or in IT-intensive roles to affect individuals’ choices to generate and accept positions in the IT industry. Because these peers have worked in the IT industry, they may have deeper or more extensive insights into jobs in the sector, the nature of those jobs, and factors to consider when seeking to be hired in the IT sector. As experts with experience in the IT industry, they could share intangible industry-specific details, including prospective career paths, salary expectations, and the mechanics of the hiring process. Thus, novices learn about the industry domain and future career prospects, which might subsequently lead to better decisions based on this peer-provided knowledge (McDonald and Westphal 2003).

Research examining the effects of peer networks on productivity and career outcomes confronts several empirical challenges. Many data sets from organizational settings do not include variations in career outcomes or diversity in prior student experience. Furthermore, selfselection of individuals into peer groups might confound estimates of peer effects with homophily or common environmental shocks (Manski 1993).

We address these concerns by analyzing data from a leading business school based in India, where students are required to have prior full-time work experience and come from both IT/IT-enabled services (ITES) and non-IT industries. Indeed, the plurality of students in this sample come from IT backgrounds (602 students, comprising 37.7% of the sample) and relatively fewer students come from other professional domains. Following graduation, students have opportunities to choose careers in both IT and non-IT roles and firms. To disentangle the causal effects of peers on career choices from confounding factors such as network self-selection and simultaneity, we exploit the random assignment of roommates (and to a limited extent, study-group peers), which represent the strongest social connections in this context. Using microlevel, individual data on human capital variables and peer effects and a robust identification strategy, our study is one of the first to illustrate how IT human capital is formed and selected into managerial roles in the industry.

Our main finding is that experiencing greater exposure to peers with IT experience reduces the likelihood that a student accepts an offer in the IT industry. We find, however, that compared with students with extensive IT industry experience, if a student has had no or low IT experience, having IT peers ameliorates this neg ative effect and increases the likelihood that the student accepts a job in the IT industry. In other words, IT peers might encourage their peers with little or no IT experience to join the IT industry. Our subsample analyses suggest that women with no IT experience are significantly more receptive to peer influence in making IT career choices than men with no IT experience. To com plement our empirical analysis, we conducted in-depth interviews with some graduates from the focal institute. These discussions highlighted the role of IT peers in helping their classmates make career decisions to join the IT industry. We found that IT peers often discuss the business and strategic value of IT in firms operating in today’s markets as well as the opportunities available. They also coach their peers prior to interviews with IT firms. For instance, our findings suggest that women who have worked previously in the IT industry can be champions of the industry by encouraging their roommates with no prior IT experience to embark on IT careers. Thus, IT peers emerge as an important resource that shapes the next generation of managers in the IT industry.

This study contributes to the literature on human capital in the IT industry, which has hitherto focused on (a) productivity improvements of IT human capital through training and education (Bapna et al. 2013, Mehra et al. 2014), (b) wage gains that are commensurate with human capital acquisition (Mithas and Krish nan 2008, Kim et al. 2014), or (c) how IT firms acquire human capital (Tambe and Hitt 2014). The emphasis has been on technical roles, with relatively little investigation of managerial roles, which contribute to various functions beyond technology, such as sales and marketing, finance, operations, and leadership. These managers are critical to the success of IT firms and firms that rely on IT. This is one of the first studies to examine the role of IT peer influence on the formation of managerial human capital.

This study also contributes to the peer effects literature by systematically examining the causal role of peer influence as one of the key factors that informs IT human capital. This scholarship has extensively examined how peers affect learning and academic productivity among undergraduate (Sacerdote 2001, 2011; Zimmerman 2003) and graduate students (Jain and Kapoor 2015, Jain and Langer 2019). This study contributes to this growing body of research that examines peer influence on students’ career choices (Lerner and Malmendier 2013, Shue 2013). Our study takes a step toward understanding peer influence on the talent that is available to firms as they harness IT.

Finally, our analyses point to the differential effects of peer influence on men and women as they seek to enter the IT industry, suggesting the need for policy interventions to mitigate negative peer influence on women who seek IT careers (Ahuja 2002, Sprague 2015, Sarker et al. 2018, Langer et al. 2020).

## Conceptual Framework

We draw on the literature on labor-market frictions and the role that peers play in mitigating these frictions and influencing not only individuals’ productivity but also their career choices.

Classic models of labor markets have assumed that markets are frictionless, such that individuals have access to all information and make optimal decisions, yet a “fully informed choice could not explain important features of the typical individual’s experience in the labor market” (Mortensen 1986, p. 50). Even in a simple job-search model in which an individual treats wages as the sole criterion for job selection, search costs prohibit exhaustive search of all available alternatives. Instead, individuals often rely on networking, that is, interacting through their social ties, to reduce these search costs and acquire information about available job prospects and wages (Mortensen and Vishwanath 1994, Calvo´-Armengol 2004, Beaman and Magruder 2012).

A social tie, defined as a friend, an acquaintance, a colleague, or someone with whom an individual communicates on a regular basis (Wuchty 2009), forms the background of the networks that individuals depend on in job searches (Granovetter 1995, Rajkumar et al. 2022). Although strong ties are important when seeking jobs (Gee et al. 2017, Garg and Telang 2018), weak connections help job search efforts by disseminating information on new job opportunities in the labor market, reducing search frictions (Granovetter 1995, Rajkumar et al. 2022).

Beyond simply learning of available job opportunities, the employment process depends on knowledge about specific industries and roles, assessments of the fit between individuals and job requirements, and the suitability of a job for long-term career prospects. Prospective employees may be unable to obtain such information from formal sources such as job advertisements or company websites and thus may turn to their social connections who therefore play an important role in informing a decision to enter an industry and obtain a specific position (Nanda and Sørensen 2010, Lerner and Malmendier 2013).

Peers clearly can play a critical role in career choices, yet prior research has examined peer influence primarily in relation to job search. Peers may be influential in career choices beyond aggregating and sharing information about job opportunities in a social network (Nanda and Sørensen 2010), especially when this influence is more pertinent, such as in a university setting. For instance, Lerner and Malmendier (2013) establish that peers affect entrepreneurial decisions by graduate students. We posit that peer influence is an important factor in the choice to pursue an IT career, particularly for managerial employees in the IT industry.

IT careers are considered attractive because of high earnings for challenging and fast-paced work with significant on-the-job learning and favorable nonwage benefits such as urban locations, comfortable work conditions, health insurance, and childcare. Interactions with peers who have previously worked in the IT industry may highlight the importance of IT to firms in a technologically driven economy and intensify a desire to align with an industry that offers strong growth potential. Students with experience in the IT industry can help novices who lack IT industry experience altogether by sharing intangible industry-specific details that are difficult to access from formal sources and by helping them prepare for interviews (for instance, by conducting mock interviews). Interacting closely with such peers may accelerate the learning process for the IT sector by providing important information not only about technology but also about domain and industry expertise and other skills needed (Lee et al. 1995, Bapna et al. 2013). These interactions might help an individual form a more precise assessment of their fit, such as the fit of their own skills and career aspirations with the IT industry or with a specific firm or position, giving them confidence that the skills acquired in their graduate program would be desirable in an IT career. Therefore, not only can novices learn more about a previously unknown do main, but they also may make better-informed decisions based on expert peers’ advice (McDonald and Westphal 2003). IT peers are also more likely to be familiar with the role IT plays in core corporate strategy (Sabherwal et al. 2019, Rahmati et al. 2021); therefore, these reflections may induce non-IT peers to explore the IT industry. Thus, our primary hypothesis is that IT peers will positively influence their non-IT peers by persuading them to consider IT careers.

We further seek to understand the relative impact of peer influence on men and women. Existing scholarship has noted that when individuals are faced with career choices, they often develop heuristic, simplifying procedures that help with optimum decision-making. Some of these simplifying procedures rely on peer influence for understanding hitherto unexamined opportunities (Eisenhart 1985). In that sense women may be more ready to explore nontraditional careers (Ahuja 2002, Langer et al. 2020), such as those in the IT industry, if they are adequately influenced by IT peers. On the other hand, if careers in the IT industry are perceived to be nontraditional and riskier and in conflict with their own perceived career paths, women might be negatively swayed by their IT peers and will shy away from riskier career IT alternatives (Gneezy et al. 2003, Szele´nyi et al. 2013). We therefore do not provide a directional hypothesis regarding whether women are likely to be positively or negatively influenced by their IT peers—Instead, we examine this hypothesis as an empirical question.

## Identification of Peer Effects

Our primary objective is to determine the causal impact of peer characteristics on career choices. In most empirical settings, estimating peer effects poses an econometric challenge, reflecting either sampling issues (Chandrasekhar and Lewis 2016) or endogenous self-selection into peer groups (Manski 1993). In studies that use observational data on peer groups, researchers rarely have access to complete information on peer groups; many studies use subsamples of specific peer groups under consideration. Estimates of peer effects obtained with such samples would be biased if the sampled nodes or links are systematically different from the nonsampled nodes, even with random sampling.

A second concern in research on peer effects involves accounting for unobserved factors that influence selection into social relationships that can bias estimates of peer influence (Manski 1993, Calvo´-Armengol 2004). Individuals may select into peer groups if they hope to gain utility from the association, so those who join a particular peer group may differ from those who do not. Similarly, individuals may connect with each other because of common interests or similar attributes. Thus, any outcomes that derive from self-selected networks may result from homophily rather than network effects (McPherson et al. 2001, Aral et al. 2009). Finally, peer effects may represent a common, environmental shock to all group members instead of the effects peers have on each other (Sacerdote 2001, Kremer and Levy 2008, Bapna and Umyarov 2015), making it necessary to control for any such factors in a causal analysis (Lerner and Malmendier 2013).

Observational data sets, such as employee records from IT firms on the demand side or student records from engineering colleges on the supply side, will contain neither a sufficient mix of individuals from IT and non-IT backgrounds nor sufficient diversity in career outcomes after exposure to peers. Few undergraduate students have accumulated prior professional experience, so peer influence is unlikely to be based on firsthand knowledge of IT firms.

The previous discussion highlights the difficulty of achieving clean identification of the influence of peer characteristics. To examine peer influences on IT career choice, we need data from a setting where individuals bring a diverse range of prior educational and professional experiences as well as both IT and non-IT job options at the end of the program, allowing us to investigate IT jobs offered and IT jobs accepted, as well as the complete matrix of peer influences (influence of IT background students on non-IT students and vice versa). Moreover, to identify the causal effects of peer influence, we need exogenous allocation of individuals to the network. In the next section, we describe the study design, the research setting, and the data that allow us to successfully address the abovementioned challenges.

## Research Setting and Data Description Research Setting

The source of our data is the postgraduate program (PGP) at the Indian School of Business (ISB), Hyderabad, India. Established in 2001, ISB is a large, independent provider of postgraduate management education with a one-year, full-time, residential diploma program. The school was founded in academic collaboration with the Wharton School of the University of Pennsylvania, the Kellogg School of Management at Northwestern University, and the London Business School. ISB incorporates many academic features and policies that can be found in its partner institutions.

An application to ISB consists of graduate management admission test (GMAT) scores,<sup>1</sup> essays, letters of recommendation, undergraduate and graduate transcripts, and an interview. Although the school draws from a pool of applicants who are predominantly from India, student characteristics at ISB are comparable to those at leading international business schools. Hence, ISB is arguably similar to major international business schools with respect to observable characteristics such as GMAT and years of work experience and has consistently ranked among top global business schools in the Financial Times list.<sup>2</sup>

We study four student cohorts whose graduating years were 2008, 2009, 2010, and 2011. In our study period, which spans the 2007–2008 to 2010–2011 period, classes at ISB were held for 50 weeks with no significant breaks and were divided into eight terms of six weeks each. Figure 1 illustrates the timeline to which a student is expected to adhere to while at ISB. In the first four terms, students took courses in a common “core” of 16 nonelective classes covering a range of management topics.<sup>3</sup> In the next four terms, students chose various elective courses that enabled them to concentrate in the areas of entrepreneurship, finance, information systems, operations, marketing, or strategy.

ISB maintains detailed records on the courses in which each student enrolls and the grades achieved in these courses and assignments of students to study groups and residential facilities. We obtained a complete record of all enrolled students for the four academic years running from 2007–2008 through 2010–2011. An advantage of selecting this period was the absence of major changes in the curriculum or administrative policies during this time.

## Campus Placement Process

The on-campus placement process at ISB begins at the start of the sixth term, when various prospective employers post job openings on the school’s job portal. The typical process includes applications to various posted jobs, short-listing of candidates, subsequent interviews with the employers, job offers, and, finally, job acceptance. The job offers are made with the expectation that the students will join the firms that tender the offers after successful completion of the PGP. Students are removed from the job portal as soon as they accept a job. Our data set includes records from the on-campus placement process; specifically, we have information on firms that made job offers and the roles that were offered to students. We classify a job offer as an “IT offer” if the job was predominantly in the IT sector and/or involved an IT role. This classification was carried out independently by three experts with experience in the IT domain; their ratings were evaluated for interrater reliability using Cohen’s kappa (Cohen 1960); the measurement of agreement exceeded 0.85, suggesting strong agreement. Similarly, we include an indicator variable that equals one if a student accepted an IT offer. Table 1 provides examples of jobs categorized as IT and non-IT.

Table 2 summarizes the relevant variables in the data set for the overall sample and for men and women in the sample. Table 3 shows the correlations between these variables. Enrolled students have accumulated an average of 4.9 years of full-time work experience before commencing their studies, with a mean GMAT score of 709. Regarding demographic characteristics, 73% of students are single and on average are 28.7 years of age. Twenty-six percent of the students are women, and 96% are Indian citizens. The average salary drawn before enrolling at ISB was INR 997,000.

We want to understand the influence of IT peers, so we use a dummy variable, No\_IT, to indicate whether a student has held jobs in the IT sector (No\_IT � 0) or not (No\_IT � 1), and Low\_IT to indicate whether the student has had a below (Low\_IT � 1) or above (Low\_IT � 0) median experience in the IT industry prior to embarking on their MBA studies. Peer Group Structure and Randomization Checks Our data are particularly suitable for understanding peer influence on career choices. Because we rely on administrative sources of data, the variables described previously are objectively and comprehensively measured and do not suffer from self-report bias, measurement error, or missing data. A critical criterion for identifying peer effects is random, exogenous assignment of individuals to networks. We now describe how our data meet this criterion, in that students are simultaneously and randomly assigned to multiple, nonoverlapping peer groups.

ISB’s PGP program is completely residential, and students are expected to live in on-campus residential dormitories. Students can either live in studio apartments or share a quad with three other “roommates.” These quads have four bedrooms and a shared kitchen and living space. Typically, students who want to live with their family members (e.g., a spouse and/or children) or those with special needs choose to live in a studio apartment. ISB does not solicit roommate preferences; hence, this assignment process is unlikely to result in homophilous groups that may confound peer effects. Instead, the assignment process follows two simple rules—first, each quad can house only members of the same sex, and second, housing groups cannot overlap with study groups.<sup>6</sup> Students remain in their allocated studios or quads for the duration of a given academic year. More importantly for the purposes of this study, these assignments, as shown in Table 4, manifested in the decision to live in a studio, are not based on any academic attributes and specifically are not driven by prior IT experience. Therefore, the allocation to residential groups is exogenous to a student’s IT experience.

Although roommates may comprise the primary peer groups with which students interact, especially during the placement period (Sacerdote 2001, Jain and Kapoor 2015), we also control for the effects of study group members. Before an academic year begins, ISB allocates each enrolled student to a study group. This study group is required to collaborate on coursework, particularly any work involving group-based projects and assignments. This study group allocation is fixed for the duration of the core terms; in subsequent elective terms, students can select their own courses and their own study groups. The process for allocating students to study groups and randomization checks are described in the online appendix, Section A.1.

The previous process results in mutually exclusive study and residential groups that help create peer groups for each student group in a year. The program is a one-year course, so spillovers from previous years are negligible, thereby mitigating concerns about autocorrelation in our data set.

Lerner and Malmendier (2013) suggest that peer groups can contribute to predicting career outcomes.

These groups become integral to students’ lives at ISB: The students use their peer interactions not only to complete formal coursework but also to reinforce their understanding of various academic endeavors (Jain and Kapoor 2015) and career options (Marmaros and Sacerdote 2002, Lerner and Malmendier 2013). Furthermore, most social activities take place in residential dorms, permitting significant informal interaction and exchange (Jain and Kapoor 2015). Each student is thus exposed to distinct sets of peers who vary in their academic abilities and their prior industry experience.

The previously mentioned peer group structure provides a useful framework for ascertaining peer effects only if these peers are influential in career choices. Social connections need not necessarily, however, form between students who are part of either housing or study groups. In Section A.2 in the online appendix, we check whether the housing and study group peers discussed previously are indeed those with which a student associates closely and who are most likely to be influential in IT career choices. As shown in Table A.3, peers in a student’s residential group and, to a lesser extent, those in the student’s core study group, are those with whom the student is most likely to be friends and be influenced by. In addition to this analysis, we conducted in-depth interviews with several members of the ISB class cohorts analyzed in the data (now occupying leading executive roles in India and beyond), asking interviewees about the nature of peer effects and career choices. These discussions revealed that many students considered their residential and study group members as among their closest friends while at ISB—individuals with whom they have kept in touch and remain friends.

## Analysis, Results, and Discussion Empirical Specification

To analyze the impact of peer influence on IT human capital formation, we examine two outcomes resulting from the placement process: IT\_Offer and IT\_Offer\_Accepted. IT\_Offer captures whether a student receives a job offer in the IT sector (IT\_Offer � 1) or not (IT\_Offer � 0). Likewise, IT\_Offer\_Accepted denotes whether the student accepts a job in the IT sector (IT\_Offer\_Accepted � 1) or not (IT\_ Offer\_Accepted � 0). We use an ordinary least squares (OLS) specification for each of these models; the results derived from the probit specification are qualitatively similar.<sup>7</sup> As described previously, we use two dummy variables to indicate the absence of prior IT experience (No\_IT � 1 if no prior IT experience and 0 otherwise), low prior IT experience (Low\_IT � 1 for below-median IT experience and 0 otherwise), and control for GMAT score (as a measure of academic ability), prior work experience, gender, marital status, core term GPA (representing academic achievement), undergraduate degree, and preprogram income. We also use year dummies to control for year- and cohort-specific factors. The peer group variables we use include information on IT peers in housing and study groups. We create interaction variables for residential peer groups with the no and low IT experience dummies, that is, No\_IT × IT\_Exp\_Roommates and Low\_ IT × IT\_Exp\_Roommates, respectively. These interaction variables capture the differential effect of IT peers as roommates on students who had zero or little preprogram IT experience.<sup>8</sup> We log transformed the two earnings variables (preprogram income and offer value) to account for large dispersion of the values. We also standardized all the continuous variables prior to computing these interaction variables to minimize correlation concerns (Aiken and West 1991).

The negative main coefficient and the positive interaction coefficients of IT residential peer influence on both outcome variables and in the two sets of specifi cations underscore the nuanced nature of these peer effects. Overall, the effect of highly experienced IT peers, who bring in their rich industry experience and perspective, is negative. It seems however that this effect is less negative for those with no IT experience and particularly those with just a little IT experience. The motivation that drives those with no or little IT experience likely differs from the motivation that drives those with extensive IT experience when they view the IT industry as a career option. As one student shared with us (see the online appendix, Section A.3), “I would say that I enjoyed it (being a software engineer). Over time, I realized that I was liking more of the analytical problem-solving part of it, as opposed to the core coding and other things. And that led me to actually apply for ISB to get some sort of change or a jump to move from coding to management and other allied roles in technology or beyond technology” (emphasis added). In other words, those with little IT experience might be inspired to take up exciting, management-oriented careers in the IT industry by the industry veterans. Thus, we conjecture that IT peers in general discourage others from taking up careers in the IT industry; they may, however, encourage their non-IT peers to give it a shot. Overall, our findings contrast with those reported by Jones and Kofoed (2020), who do not find peers to be influential in occupational choices made by U.S. Army cadets. This difference might be attributable to the fact that undergraduate cadets are not viewed as experts by their peers, in contrast to the experienced students in our setting.

Our empirical findings are corroborated by interviews we conducted in 2021 with 10 former students who were members of the cohorts examined; a thematic analysis of the interviews is presented in the online appendix, Section A.3. We first asked those with prior experience in the IT industry why they chose to return to the IT industry. Most of those who came to ISB from the IT industry did so to distance themselves from a technical job profile, whereas others came to ISB to understand the business and strategic rationale explaining why their companies and/or clients were investing in IT as they were. Although some were able to take up careers in non-IT sectors, others, more often than not, came across a different kind of hurdle in attempting to leverage their extensive IT experience. As one student put it, “No one was willing to give me credit for all my eight years of IT experience. So, I very quickly realized that I either have to start at a junior position or take up a meatier, more strategic role in the IT industry that is not about programming.”

We were primarily interested in understanding the nature of peer effects and asked interviewees if and why they encouraged non-IT peers to consider IT careers. The students talked about their own roles and experience in the IT industry and the value IT has (and continues to have) in many industries, excitement about IT startups, e-commerce companies, and established IT blue-chips such as Google, Facebook, and Microsoft that recruited from ISB. They also expressed wariness toward large IT services firms regarding jobs that they categorized as “back-office” jobs. These discussions also revealed an interesting factor that may account for this seeming inconsistency in the negative influence in general but the positive influence on non-IT peers: fierce competition among students for both grades and for jobs. Although any business school fosters a generally competitive environment, ISB is a prestigious but expensive school. The main objective of many students is to obtain a good job and this sense of competition is heightened during the placement period. The students we interviewed did not mention any negative actions on their own part but shared that many in their cohort would behave strategically to keep information about the best ways to interview for and convert desirable IT industry opportunities to themselves. This strategic behavior also helps to explain how IT peers could discourage others from considering IT careers.

We sought to understand why IT peers encouraged their non-IT peers or those with little IT experience, but not peers with more IT experience, to take up IT careers. Those who had already accumulated considerable firsthand experience with the IT industry may suffer from confirmation bias of why they should not go back to the IT industry. That is, discussions between IT peers with greater experience may possibly reinforce negative biases such students had formed when they left the IT industry to pursue an MBA (Plous 1993). This is echoed by some of our discussions with students who overwhelmingly did not want to return to IT services firms or to back-office jobs that required them to work with global clients, often at the cost of their own personal well-being. That is, they did not want to work late shifts or early shifts just to interact with clients in another part of the world. It is thus possible that they sought discussions with IT peers and interpreted those discussions in a way that negatively affected the likelihood that they would return to the IT industry.

In contrast, IT peers seem to have encouraged peers with no or little IT experience peers to attend guest sessions conducted at the Business Technology Club that outlined how IT helped businesses pursue their strategic and operational goals. These patterns were echoed in the complementary discussions with those students without or with very little IT credentials, but they took up managerial IT careers after graduating from ISB. These students had not seen “back offices” that seem to be the bane of their expert IT peers (or as one student said, “boring jobs”). Instead, they were excited by the prospect of taking up broad functional jobs with exciting roles that were being offered by IT companies. Some students, who were unaware of the role that IT plays in most firms, recalled animated discussions with their colleagues who had worked with clients in various industries (albeit clients who were focused on IT-centric projects). One student revealed that, although she took up an engineering/strategic job following ISB, discussions with her IT peers on the wherewithal the IT industry provides influenced her sufficiently to join an IT/Analytics firm a year after graduating from ISB. Another student, who had worked in the fashion industry prior to enrolling at ISB, was quite excited by the wide range of platform companies such as Google and Facebook that were recruiting from ISB for marketing jobs. In this way, these students were encouraged by their peers because these jobs allowed the non-IT students to participate in what they termed the “sunrise” industry sector, with job security and exciting prospects for growth.

From what we learned, it seemed location mattered for these women because not only did IT companies offer a high-quality work environment in relatively large cities, but they also offered numerous employment opportunities for spouses if need be.

## Robustness and Falsification Checks

Our results point to overall negative peer effects on students’ IT career choices. However, it is possible that these students do not rely on their peers in learning about the IT industry, but on the coursework they did as part of their graduate program. ISB’s core curriculum includes a course on the strategic impact of IT on firms, where students analyze the strategic characteristics and disruptive power of IT, as well as how firms can leverage IT in improving their operating business models. We examined the course syllabi for ISB’s core IS course for the years 2007–2011 to ascertain the broad learning objectives specified for this course. Students with no prior IT experience possibly base their learning about the IT industry not from their IT peers but from the learnings of this course because all students take it as part of the core coursework. This course may generate interest in the IT industry and steer students toward IT careers. If so, student grades in this course should be a positive and significant factor influencing IT career outcomes (Lerner and Malmendier 2013). Table A.4 in the online appendix presents the results of this analysis and shows that the core IS course grade, although positive, is not significant in either the baseline or interaction regressions for generating an IT offer, but negative and not significant for accepting the IT job offer; it is only sig nificant at p < 0.05 for the two-stage Heckman model.

Our analysis uses random allocation of students to various peer groups. Despite this, our results could be merely a result of either perfunctory correlations in the data or unobservable factors in the data that vary between these peer groups. To test the robustness of our model, we conduct a falsification check in which we randomly shuffle the peer groups (Card and Giuliano 2013). If the peer groups are truly influential, the coefficients obtained from the variables of the shuffled network groups should be smaller in magnitude and statistically insignificant. We report the results of the regressions using the shuffled groups in Table A.5 in the online appendix.

Although lack of own IT experience continues to be a negative yet significant factor for both outcomes, the coefficients for the variables capturing IT experience in peer groups and from any of their interactions are insignificant. This lends credence to our assertion that our data and model can identify the influence of peer groups on both IT\_Offer and IT\_Offer\_Accepted and that our findings are not a result of mechanical correlations or other factors that influence our outcome variables.

## Conclusion

IT has been shown to be a general-purpose technology that is core to firm strategy (Brynjolfsson and Hitt 1996, Sabherwal et al. 2019). We examine how managers, critical to realizing the promise of IT, may enter the IT industry. Our study contributes primarily to the literature on human capital in the IT industry (Mithas and Krishnan 2008, Bapna et al. 2013, Mehra et al. 2014, Tambe and Hitt 2014), which has examined the role of human capital investments in improving employee and firm-level performance in the IT industry, or the IT labor pool from which firms recruit. Yet, the scarcity of relevant data has precluded our understanding of the motivations an individual has to enter the industry in the first place into managerial roles, where they can combine technical fundamentals with business acumen. Ours is among the first studies to examine this dynamic and, using randomized allocation of IT peers, the first to rigorously analyze the role of peers in influencing the choice of IT careers.

We find that IT peers in general decrease the likelihood of an individual’s pursuit of a career in IT. The IT industry is notorious for unpredictable work schedules and the demanding nature of the job (Ahuja et al. 2007, Tarafdar et al. 2015, Subikshaa and Jasmin 2018). Given the high stress levels and interpersonal challenges associated with technical roles in the IT industry (Ahuja et al. 2007, Bharathi et al. 2015, Padma et al. 2015, Subikshaa and Jasmin 2018), students with IT experience who foray into a business school might not have had positive experiences with the IT industry, which is why they may dissuade their peers. Conversely, the post-MBA career options available to those with prior IT experience may be weightier and thus more desirable. Hence, these individuals may simply be more strategic in their choices in restricting competition for the lucrative jobs in the IT industry. Yet, the global tech industry was estimated to be \$4.5 trillion in 2021 (Alsop 2021), which is perhaps why they positively influence non-IT peers toward IT careers (Agarwal et al. 2007). We find this effect is stronger for women than men; female residential peers may have more influence on their non-IT peers in making career transitions. The attractiveness of managerial ranks in the IT industry for women may also be influenced by their desire to solve the “dual body” problem. These findings in turn call for policy interventions at IT firms to encourage more women to join managerial ranks in the IT industry by providing them more flexibility in location and hours as well as facilitating movement to different locations within the firm if need be.

Although our study exploits a natural experiment to tease out the influence of IT peers on an individual’s decision to enter the IT industry, these IT peers may not be the only source of influence and individuals may receive advice and information on the IT industry from other resources such as other classmates and from independent research. As such, our findings represent a conservative lower bound on peer influence.

The empirical findings from a single organization setting may not generalize to all settings and situations. Expert peers with positive experiences with the IT industry could possibly encourage greater entry of managers into the industry. Such peer exchanges could, for example, occur at the workplace or industry associations that positively select technology managers. Employees might have positive experiences in the technology industry in the United States and other countries, so when such employees join business schools, they might encourage their peers to enter the industry.

Nonetheless, we identify an important and novel phenomenon of IT peers influencing non-IT peers in their occupational choice that has important implications for the IT industry and for management schools. For instance, managers trying to exploit peer-to-peer learning as a way to train workers ought to be aware that negative messages could be transmitted along with positive ones, leading to productivity loss. Second, although researchers have addressed the value of effective management on firm productivity in emerging economies (Bloom et al. 2013), as well as the role of peer influences on academic outcomes among business school students (Jain and Kapoor 2015, Jain and Langer 2019), we high light that peer influences are important when managers in emerging economies choose their occupations and industries. Managers seeking to attract more talent to the IT industry should keep this channel in mind while designing recruitment strategies and if possible, encourage flexible work policies that are attractive to women. We hope that future research will extend external validity of our findings to other settings and stimulate more discussions in the future.
