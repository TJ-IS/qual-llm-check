# Fashionable Consumer Technology, IT Fashion, and Consumer Behavior

Abstract. Traditionally thought of as “uncomfortable bedfellows,” consumer IT and fashion are becoming increasingly intertwined, which has given rise to a unique phenomenon termed “consumer IT fashion”—the collective enthusiasm in society for consumer technol ogy. Nevertheless, it remains uncertain how IT fashion is perceived by individual consumers and subsequently influences consumer behavior. Our research aims to bridge this gap. By differentiating between societal-level and individual-level perceptions of IT fashion, the paper introduces novel constructs associated with IT fashion and fashionable consumer technology, particularly perceived societal-level IT fashion (PSITF, reflecting a person’s perception of society’s views on a technology) and perceived fashionableness of IT (PFIT, capturing a person’s perception of the technology’s fashionableness). Moreover, we identify drivers underlying PSITF and PFIT: collective adoption and social endorsement are the drivers of PSITF, whereas the novelty of IT and IT-identity congruity are the drivers of PFIT. A research model is developed to illustrate how IT fashion influences consumer behaviors, such as adoption and purchasing decisions. We conducted two empirical studies to assess our research model. In Study 1, we focused on consumers’ adoption deci sion of fashionable technology. The findings validate our conceptualization of IT fashionrelated constructs and suggest that consumers are inclined to adopt fashionable IT when they believe it enhances their sense of belonging to a group (i.e., external symbolic value) and allows for self-expression (i.e., internal symbolic value). In Study 2, we explored consumers’ purchasing behavior, focusing on their willingness to pay (WTP) for a fashionable technology compared with the alternative IT product with identical functionalities. The results indicate that whereas consumers do acknowledge the symbolic values associated with using fashionable IT, these values do not necessarily translate into a greater WTP. Our research opens novel theoretical avenues for the exploration of IT fashion and lays the groundwork for future investigations in this domain.

## Introduction

“It’s just shinier,” a consumer remarked when explaining the motivation to wait in line in front of Apple stores for the release of new iPhones at 4 a.m. (Sullivan 2012). The word “shinier” isn’t merely a comment on the device’s lustrous exterior; it’s an embodiment of the allure of the new and advanced features and innovations, and it’s also about the excitement of being among the first to own fashionable IT. It has long been thought that the utilitarian world of technology and the alluring world of fashion do not mix. Yet in today’s consumer IT context, they blend harmoniously. Over the past two decades, consumer technologies that offer not only utilitarian but also aesthetic and symbolic value emerged and remained an important aspect of individual daily technology use. Nowadays, not only cell phones but also software, apps, and websites can be fashionable. For example, recall the early days of e-commerce, which, beyond its revolutionary retail model, symbol ized the contemporary “wired” lifestyle (Shang et al. 2005). The Nintendo Wii’s success among the broader public wasn’t merely because of its gaming experience but was amplified by its “cool” status. Smartwatches are believed to “inherently possess unique characteristics similar to what consumers expect from luxury fash ion products” (Choi and Kim 2016, p. 779). Social platforms such as TikTok have begun to gain tremendous popularity, not just because of their innovative user experience but also because of the pervasive “fear of missing out” they engender (Herrman 2019).

Similarly, Meta Quest, an all-in-one VR system that provides an entirely free and immersed virtual reality experience, six degrees of freedom, state-of-the-art optics, and built-in audio has seen significant growth during the past decade (Kim et al. 2021). These are all examples of IT fashion—the collective enthusiasm for consumer technologies.

IT innovations that are collectively perceived as “new, efficient, and at the forefront of practice” (Wang 2010, p. 64) can be termed “in fashion,” signifying both practical and fashion merits. Some studies have begun to examine the fashion aspect of individual-level IT adoption and usage. All the above examples suggest that consumer IT fashion (hereafter referred to as IT fashion)—a term we use in this research to refer to the upbeat social phenomenon surrounding a fashionable consumer technology—is becoming ubiquitous, and consumers are inevitably influenced by IT fashions. Prior research, albeit limited, has identified several related factors as antecedents of adoption, including perceived pleasure and beauty (Tzou and Lu 2009), perceived aesthetics, and perceived critical mass (a social-psychological factor) (Yang and Hsu 2011). Furthermore, intrinsic consumer traits, such as vanity and the need for uniqueness, have been highlighted as determinants of adopting fashionable IT (Choi and Kim 2016). Additionally, technologies deemed “in fashion” also influence consumer’s postadoption, impacting both satisfaction and regret levels (Sun et al. 2014). Whereas these pioneering studies illuminate the significant role of IT’s fashionable facet and its influences on consumers, IT fashion and fashionable IT, at the core of the phenomenon, remain enigmatic. Specifically, the extant information systems (IS) research is inadequate in two ways. First, many of the constructs mentioned above (e.g., perceived playfulness or perceived aesthetics) are not unique to fashionable technology and thus do not sufficiently represent IT fashion. Second, current research on fashionable IT at the individual level is largely anchored on the technology acceptance model without an in-depth exploration of IT fashion as a social phenomenon. Consequently, it remains unclear how individual consumers formulate perceptions of IT fashion and the fashionable technology and how such perceptions influence consumer behavior. Therefore, our study aims to offer answers to two fundamental questions about IT fashion at the individual consumer level:

Research Question 1. What is IT fashion, and how do people perceive an IT fashion?

Research Question 2. How does IT fashion influence consumers’ behaviors?

To answer these questions, our research draws insights from the fashion literature, adopting an individual-in-society perspective. From this perspective, we highlight that IT fashion encompasses both individuals’ perception of the fashionable technology itself and their perception of society’s response to the IT. Hence, we distinguish two perceptions an individual forms regarding the technology at the core of the IT fashion trend: perceived societal-level IT fashion (PSITF, which is how a person perceives society’s feelings about an IT) and perceived fashionableness of IT (PFIT, an individual’s own assessment of the IT’s fashionableness). Subsequently, we identify the main characteristics of PSITF (i.e., social endorsement and collective adoption) and PFIT (i.e., novelty and IT-identity congruity). We then develop a research model that depicts how these two IT fashion perceptions significantly influence different consumer behaviors (i.e., adoption or purchase behavior) through the symbolic values of using the fashionable IT.

We conducted two empirical studies to evaluate our research model. Study 1 aims to develop and vali date instruments for measuring IT fashion perceptions (i.e., PSITF and PFIT), confirm the existence of IT fashion, and explore its impact on adoption behavior. We consider both PSITF and PFIT as second-order constructs driven by two societal-level and two individual-level characteristics, respectively. In Study 2, we expand Study 1 in several ways: it (1) incorporates global items to directly measure PSITF and PFIT, (2) triangulates the research model with two technologies at different fashion diffusion stages: MS Excel (out-offashion) and Meta Quest (in fashion), and (3) includes a different consumer behavior: willingness to pay (WTP). The two studies jointly confirm and validate our frame work. We find that individuals perceive IT fashions through a two-tier system (i.e., PSITF and PFIT): they comprehend how society views the IT and assess the IT at the individual level. Both perceptions focus on the information technologies themselves and are defined as object-based perceptions. Additionally, these object based perceptions influence consumers’ behavioral per ceptions about how individuals contemplate utilizing the technology for two primary goals: assimilating into social groups (external symbolic value) and expressing oneself (internal symbolic value). These behavioral per ceptions subsequently influence consumers’ adoption of IT, but not their willingness to pay a premium.

This research makes several contributions to the IS literature. First, it develops a research model of IT fashion at the individual level to describe how IT fashion influences consumer perceptions and behaviors. Second, this research conceptualizes several novel constructs about IT fashion and fashionable technology, highlighting the two object-based individual perceptions of IT fashion: PSITF and PFIT. They are two distinct but interrelated constructs that are conceptualized to capture the complexity of individual perceptions about an ongoing IT fashion. Third, this research contributes methodologically to IS research by offering new measures for the new constructs developed in this research (e.g., PSITF and PFIT) that can be used to further develop and test this important research topic of IT fashion.

## Theory Development

## Fashion Literature and IT Fashion

Fashion, a concept that extends beyond clothing choices, is defined as “a prevailing style during a particular time” (Merriam-Webster). It is a powerful social force that shapes individual actions, as numerous scholars have pointed out (Nystrom 1928, Robinson 1958, Reynolds 1968, Wasson 1968, Blumer 1969, Sproles 1979, Davis 1992, Miller et al. 1993, Phau and Lo 2004). Fashion trends, often initiated by institutions or entrepreneurs, lead to sustained and conscious development over time (Abrahamson and Fairchild 1999, Abrahamson and Eisenman 2008). These trends represent behaviors that become normalized and accepted, reflecting enduring trends within a particular field or society (Dunnette 1966). Fashions tend to have a more profound and lasting impact on market trends and industry standards compared with fads.<sup>1</sup>

It is also worth noting that each fashion item may go through a fashion lifecycle. When a new fashion item starts to diffuse into a social system, it is essentially an innovation (Sproles 1979). Applying innovation diffusion theory (Rogers 1962) in the fashion context, Wasson (1968) and Sproles (1979) have argued that the diffusion process of a fashion traces a bell-shaped curve and consists of five phases: market development, rapid growth, maturity, saturation, and decline. Abrahamson (1996)’s management fashion theory, which is heavily cited in IT fashion literature, asserts that fashionable management techniques (including organizational technologies) “must appear both rational (efficient means to important ends) and progressive (new as well as improved relative to older management techniques)” (p. 255). Offering an alternative to Sproles’s fashion lifecycle, Abrahamson believes that the fashion lifecycle consists of three phases: latency, growth, and decline. The latency phase resembles Sproles’s market development phase, which marks the inception of a potential fashion. The growth phase is “where it gathers public attention and makes a relatively abrupt ascent to the peak” (Lee and Collar 2003, p. 4). After the growth phase, fashion begins to decline and gradually loses public attention.

The fashion lifecycle differs from a normal product lifecycle in the rapid surge of popularity at the growth stage, which quickly reaches the point of critical mass. This is the most important stage of fashion, during which the fashion product receives the most collective public attention and so becomes a social phenomenon.

In this phase, the fashion exerts an overwhelming influence on individuals and societies. Therefore, only technologies in the growth phase can be considered fashionable technologies, or “in fashion.” After they pass the growth stage, they can be considered declining or “out of fashion.”

The fashion phenomenon in IT has been studied by IS researchers, particularly at the organizational level. Lee and Collar (2003, p. 8) demonstrated that the duration of IT fashion waves—defined as “the production and consumption of temporarily intensive [information technology] discourse”—is shorter than the duration of management fashions in the business domain. Wang (2010, p. 64) defined organizational-level IT fashion as a “transitory collective belief that an information technology is new, efficient, and at the forefront of practice,” highlighting the organizational benefits of adopting the hottest IT (e.g., gaining legitimacy). Indeed, organizations might adopt certain IT fashions for symbolic values, such as aligning with industry standards or being perceived as innovative. However, it’s worth distinguishing that consumer IT fashion can be seen as a distinct phenomenon. Whereas legitimacy and reputation are symbolic values for organizationa IT, they are usually grounded in tangible organizational outcomes such as stakeholder trust or increased market value. In contrast, the symbolic value in consumer IT might often be more intangible, rooted in personal feelings, perceptions, or societal affiliation.

Although limited, a few IS studies have explored the fashion aspect of individual-level IT adoption and usage. They have identified fashion-related constructs such as beauty of perceived aesthetics (Tzou and Lu 2009) and exposure to the fashion wave (Sun et al. 2014). Table A.1 in Online Appendix A summarizes the related IS studies and implications for our study. Intersecting IT and fashion is spurred by miniaturization (i.e., fitting more transistor nodes on a smaller integrated circuit) (Wong and Iwai 2005) and, consequently, the increasing portability of digital devices (i.e., wearable technology, smartphones) (Yang et al. 2021). As consumer technologies become more accessi ble and visible (Hirschheim et al. 1995), they may also become more fashionable.

Whereas the current IS literature has noted that IT can be fashionable, it is essential to ground our understanding of how such IT fashion is formed and diffused. Drawing from sociology literature on fashion provides valuable insights. Sociologists have proposed two primary models for fashion formation: the trickledown model (Simmel 1957) and the trickle-up model (Blumer 1969). According to the trickle-down theory (Simmel 1957), upper-class members set fashion trends to distinguish themselves from the lower classes; the upper class constantly seeks to adopt new fashion styles (Veblen 1899). As a style becomes a status symbol, aspirational members of the lower classes emulate the style, and eventually, a fashion trend emerges. However, when too many people adopt a given fashion, the upper class abandons it and seeks new trends. This cycle of fashion evolves through phases of innovation and emulation. Some examples include first ladies in the United States such as Jackie Kenney and, more recently, Michelle Obama, who are believed to have “made a mark on fashion” of what people wear (Bourne 2017).

Contrary to the trickle-down theory, Blumer (1969) created a trickle-up collective selection model in which fashion is rooted in modern societal changes in realms such as lifestyle, architecture, literature, and art (Atik and Fırat 2013). Designers and fashion leaders sense these changes, select styles or items that might reflect the societal changes, and promote the trends through various channels, including fashion magazines, fashion shows, and commercials (Barnes 2013, Park et al. 2022).

Despite their differences, both models emphasize the individual-in-society nature of fashion. IT fashion, after all, is a salient and complex social phenomenon that impacts individuals within society. Individuals experience IT fashions at both the societal level (e.g., observing many people using a fashionable technology) and the individual level (e.g., evaluating the technology’s potential to enhance their self-image) simultaneously. As Anspach (1967) noted, fashion is a collective taste that many individuals choose simultaneously but independently. Depending on individual tastes and preferences, individuals may perceive an ongoing fashion differently (Anspach 1967). For example, people may notice a significant number of their peers, influencers, and celebrities interacting with Meta Quest, a virtual reality headset. In addition, professionally done advertisements widely visible on various platforms also link the use of Meta Quest to images of being trendy and futuristic. As a result, people may perceive that there is an ongoing fashion of Meta Quest in society. However, this does not necessarily mean that everyone would believe Meta Quest is fashionable. Some individuals, drawn to its innovative design and cutting-edge features, might consider it highly fashionable, whereas others might remain skeptical, finding the device either aesthetically unappealing or cumbersome in design. In short, as depicted in Figure 1, individual consumers perceive an IT fashion through two separate but simultaneous aspects: they perceive that there is an ongoing collective enthusiasm and, at the same time, form their own perceptions about the fashionable technology at the center of the IT fashion.

Next, we discuss how individuals form different perceptions at the societal and the individual levels, respectively.

## Perception of Societal-Level IT Fashion

How does an individual perceive the societal-level IT fashion? And how does an IT fashion diffuse to indivi duals in society? To address these questions, we propose a construct of perceived societal-level IT fashion, which is defined as the degree to which one perceives that there is a transitory collective belief in the society that an IT is a prevailing style at the time and has been socially adopted and endorsed. This definition is adapted from Wang (2010)’s definition of organization-level IT fashion and is consistent with Merriam-Webster’s definition of fashion. PSITF captures individuals’ perception of technology in terms of how they think the society views it. Furthermore, we argue that two major factors characterize IT fashion at the societal level: collective adoption and social endorsement. We utilize a formative specification of PSITF to capture the multifaceted nature of how individuals perceive societal-level responses to an IT.

Collective Adoption. Collective adoption of an IT fashion (hereafter, “collective adoption”)—that an IT has been adopted by a significant and noticeable proportion of society—is a vital precondition for the formation of IT fashion. The diffusion of IT fashion follows the trajectory of innovation diffusion proposed by Rogers (1962), which suggests that the diffusion of innovation needs to reach the point of critical mass so that there are enough adopters to provide sufficient utility for potential adopters. Yang and Hsu (2011) have adopted the concept of critical mass proposed by Rogers as an antecedent of the intention to adopt fashionable technologies. Sproles (1979, p. 5) also argued that fashion items should be adopted by “a discernible proportion of members of a social group.” Thus, for an IT fashion to form, the diffusion process must reach a critical point of collective adoption so that more followers will join the herd of adopters.

It’s worth noting that when too many people adopt a fashionable technology, it may not be fashionable anymore because “the very character of fashion demands that it should be exercised at one time only by a portion of the given group, the great majority being merely on the road to adopting it”(Simmel 1957, p. 547). Similarly, when too many people in the lower class adopt a certain fashionable style, the upper class may abandon this style and seek new ones to distinguish themselves from the lower class (Blumer 1969). Thus, we follow the idea of “discernible proportion” by Sproles (1979) and define collective adoption of an IT fashion as the degree to which one perceives that a discernible proportion of people in society have adopted the fashionable technology in the center of the IT fashion.

Social Endorsement. Although collective adoption is a precondition for an IT fashion, not all massively adopted technologies are fashionable. Social endorsement— ranging from celebrities to notable figures in one’s personal network—plays a critical role in determining how an individual perceives ongoing IT fashion trends within society. This isn’t merely about popularity; it’s more about a societal validation that comes from the elite or respected figures adopting and promoting a particular product. For instance, khaki pants were once worn in armies and later became popular among civilians in the 1960s. However, they had nothing to do with fashion until Levi’s introduced a product line with khaki pants and promoted them on runways and in fashion magazines in the 1980s (Farennikova and Prinz 2011). It was the fashion leaders, experts, and elites who made khakis fashionable. Likewise, during the process of IT fashion diffusion, the elites, and people with prestige in one’s social group, legitimize the fashionable technology by making it a social norm in that group. They help build the “images” associated with a fashionable technology, which are then pursued by followers.

For example, the first generation of the iPod was described as featuring “a remarkable design, slim, understated, and white–incandescent” (Vejlgaard 2007, p. 139) and was promoted by fashion icons such as Madonna and Beck. Owning and using an iPod quickly became a prevailing style among music lovers. Dean (1999) argues that endorsement usually requires three actors: seller, endorser, and target (e.g., consumers). The endorser first needs to test a product provided by the seller and then evaluate the product and communicate their opinions to the target. The endorser also tries to convince the target to buy and use the product. Hence, we define social endorsement of IT (hereafter, “social endorsement”) as the degree to which one perceives tha people with prestige or social status use, appreciate, and advocate the technology in the center of the IT fashion. In this definition, people with prestige or social status are not only celebrities or the elite. They can also be any prestigious people or opinion leaders from consumers’ own social groups (Blumer 1969, Sproles 1979), such as influential YouTubers or widely respected people in one’s social network.

It is worth noting that prior IS literature has attempted to capture the effects of social influence, such as subjective norms (Venkatesh and Davis 2000, Venkatesh et al. 2003), which is defined as “the degree to which an indi vidual perceives that important others believe he or she should use the new system” (Venkatesh et al. 2003, p. 451). Social endorsement of IT differs from subjective norms in that it focuses on what elites do instead of what they think others should do (i.e., what elites think “I” should do). Moreover, subjective norms “act as external sanctions inducing negative emotional states when individuals do not conform” (Baddeley 2010, p. 285). In othe words, subjective norms affect individuals’ behaviors by somewhat “forcing” them to change their intentions in response to social pressure (Venkatesh et al. 2003). Social endorsement does not have such a connotation.

In sum, IT fashion is a social phenomenon that requires collective adoption and social endorsement of the technology.

## Individual Assessment of Fashionable IT

We don’t see things as they are, we see them as we are.

—Anaïs Nin (1903–1977), writer

When an IT is surrounded by societal enthusiasm, individuals see the passion it evokes, the peer approval it commands, and the identity it represents. Yet this societal enthusiasm, whereas it is influential, is filtered through individual lenses of personal preferences and needs. For example, many consumers identify with Apple’s products, and they have an inherent belief that Apple products are fashionable. Some products (e.g., Apple Vision Pro) might be considered novel, which further increases their belief that the product is fashionable. To capture this nuance, we propose the second construct that consumer perceives about the technology at the core of an IT fashion, namely, perceived fashionableness of IT, and define it as the degree to which one believes that an IT is trendy, novel, and consistent with the identities they desire. Consistent with Wang (2010)’s definition, this definition highlights the unique aspect of IT fashion in that a fashionable technology should be new and at the forefront of practice. In this study, we identify two major drivers that define IT fashion at the individual level: novelty of IT and IT-identity congruity We utilize a formative specification of PFIT to capture the multifaceted nature of how individuals perceive the fashionableness of an IT. In our model, both novelty and IT-identity congruity serve as critical, distinct dimensions that shape PFIT.

Novelty of IT. Robinson (1958, p. 127) argued that fashion is “the pursuit of novelty for its own sake.” Novelty is essential for fashionable technologies (Wang 2010). People constantly need novel stimulation and actively engage in novelty-seeking activities (Robinson 1958). Very often, the novelty (e.g., touchscreen or new exterior color) of a fashionable technology is highlighted in advertisements and promotional materials, and the new technology is often compared with alternative or old technologies to create a new IT fashion. Hence, a novel technology is likely to be perceived by individuals as fashionable. The novelty in IT fashion manifests in two equivalent facets: technical innovation and aesthetic design. On one hand, IT fashion waves often emerge with major technical breakthroughs, which provide the foundation for new, unusual, and unexpected technical functions for users. For instance, the introduction of augmented reality in devices presents unique and unprecedented functionalities for users. On the other hand, from the aesthetic perspective, novel technologies also bring forward innovative design elements that are attractive and delightful for consumers. For example, the seamless integration of edge-to-edge displays in smartphones, combined with minimalist designs, offers users a visually captivating experience that complements the device’s advanced technical features. Thus, we integrate several novelty related constructs, such as perceived novelty and product newness (Barclay and Dann 2000, Wells et al. 2010, Jeong et al. 2017), and define novelty of IT as the degree to which an IT is perceived by one as new, original, and technically advanced compared with alternative technologies. We consider novelty as an individual-level perception factor because individuals may perceive the same technology at different novelty levels, based on their own tastes, preferences, and knowledge. Hence, novelty perception primarily contributes to a person’s own evaluation of whether a technology is fashionable or not.

IT-Identity Congruity. Whereas novelty is a defining characteristic, not every novel technology is automatically perceived as fashionable by individuals. Beyond novelty, fashion has also been considered a means for self-expression and a tool to strengthen personal identity (Farennikova and Prinz 2011). By behaving consistently with their personal identities, people obtain feelings of coherence and self-respect, referring to authenticitybased self-esteem (Swann 1983, Burke 2004). Just as people might choose specific clothing to articulate their beliefs, preferences, and lifestyles, they align with technologies that resonate with their self-image.

Fashionable technologies are often associated with different identity meanings. For example, iPhones, with their sleek aesthetics, intuitive interfaces, and seamless integration within the Apple ecosystem, appeal to “Apple enthusiasts.” In contrast, Samsung phones, known for their innovative features—partially because of Android’s open-source nature—attract the “tech-savvy” crowd. Individuals actively seek oppor tunities to display their identities by adopting objects (Swann 1983, Burke and Stets 2009). When people recognize the identities associated with the fashionable technologies, they decide whether these technologies are congruent with their self-identity (or self-image) (Sirgy 1982) because people tend to behave consistently with their own personal identities (Burke 2004). Only the technologies that carry symbols consistent with a person’s self-identity would be perceived as fashionable by that person. Hence, we capture this congruence between self-identity and the identity implied by the technology and propose a construct: IT-identity congruity, which is defined as the degree to which one believes that the identity carried by an IT is congruent with their selfidentity.

Previous literature has discussed related constructs. In self-verification theory, Sirgy (1985, p. 196) defined self-congruity as “a match between a product image and a person’s actual self-image.” Similarly, Carter and Grover (2015) conceptualized IT identity, which captures the interwoven relationship between IT and self-identity. Drawing on innovation diffusion theory, Karahanna et al. (2006) further proposed two dimensions of compatibility with values and prior experience. Our conceptualization of IT-identity congruity integrates these factors and situates them in the IT fashion context by emphasizing the identities carried by a technology.

## Symbolic Values of Using Fashionable IT

How does IT fashion influence consumers’ behavior? Barnard (2007, p. 4) defines fashion as “modern, western, meaningful and communicative bodily adornments or dress.” Sproles (1979) also stresses the “social legitimacy” of fashion items. Fashion items can serve as cues to help maintain people’s social and personal identities. Thus, we believe that the use of fashionable technology can provide two types of symbolic values: external (Sproles 1979) and internal (Reynolds 1968) symbolic values. The perceptions of such symbolic values are manifested by beliefs toward the behavior of using the IT (e.g., using an IT would express my tastes or help me fit into my social group), which distinguish them from the above-mentioned four object-based characteristics of IT fashion that are tied to the IT fashion itself. Compared with object-based beliefs, behaviorbased beliefs are stronger predictors in predicting the adoption and use of information technology (Wixom and Todd 2005).

On the one hand, IT fashion involves group conformity. As Sproles (1979, p. 32) has indicated, “fashion usually gains a larger amount of social acceptance and social legitimation as it is adopted in different social groups.” The publicization of a technology makes people aware of the benefits of the technology to obtain the associated symbolic values. In this case, adopting and using the technology becomes a relatively well-defined social norm. Accordingly, we identify the construct of external symbolic value and define it as the degree to which one believes that using an IT can help them obtain affiliation in their desired social group(s). In the IS literature, Moore and Benbasat (1991, p. 195) proposed a related constructimage, which is defined as “the degree to which the use of an innovation is perceived to enhance one’s reputation or status in their social system.” However, the definition of image focuses on enhancing social status, whereas external symbolic value focuses on obtaining social affiliation or group membership.

On the other hand, another behavioral belief toward using fashionable technologies is self-expression. Fashion products reflect changes in many realms of modern society (Blumer 1969), such as people’s daily needs, politics, lifestyles, and tastes in architecture, literature, and art. Thus, using fashionable items helps people display their own tastes, lifestyles, values, and personalities (Reynolds 1968). We extend this concept to the IT fashion context and define internal symbolic value as the degree to which one believes that using an IT can display their personality, values, tastes, and ways of life. ## Consumer Behaviors

We investigate the impact of IT fashion on two distinct consumer behaviors: adoption and willingness to pay (WTP). Adoption is an important outcome behavior and has been extensively studied in the IS literature (Davis 1989, Venkatesh et al. 2003). WTP is a fundamental concept in consumer behavior in the marketing literature (Homburg et al. 2005, Bertini et al. 2012). Consistent with prior research, in this study, we define willingness to pay as the degree to which an individual is willing to pay for an IT product (Cameron and James

1987, Krishna 1991). In other words, it’s the tipping point beyond which a consumer would opt not to make the purchase. It is an important factor in the context of individual technology use because consumers normally pay for the use of technology, whereas employees in the organization do not need to bear such a cost (Venkatesh et al. 2012). WTP is also referred to by economists as the reservation price (Monroe 1990). It is the monetary value that a person places on a consumption or usage experience and is, indeed, a matter of how much value can be directly assessed (Godek and Murray 2008).

## The Research Model and Hypothesis Development

We propose a research model to explore IT fashion and its impact on consumer behaviors, as shown in Figure 2. The model is consistent with several estab lished theoretical perspectives. First, according to the theory of reasoned action (Ajzen and Fishbein 1980), external factors influence one’s beliefs, which, in turn, influence behavioral intention. Therefore, we argue that characteristics of IT fashion, which pertain to an ongoing social phenomenon external to the user, influence their perceptions of the fashion and the fashionable technology as well as the symbolic values associated with them, which, in turn, influence behav joral intention. Second, according to Wixom and Todd (2005), object-based beliefs (e.g., about the technology itself) influence behavioral beliefs (e.g., about using a technology) (Venkatesh and Davis 2000), which are direct antecedents of behavioral intentions and reflec the direct outcomes of using a technology (e.g., utilitarian outcomes). In this research, internal symbolic value and external symbolic value are behavioral beliefs because they are about the benefits of using fashionable technology, whereas PSITF and PFIT are object-based beliefs because they are about IT fashion and fashionable technology themselves. Third, our research model also highlights the cross-level influence (Maruping and Magni 2015). PSITF represents the collective responses toward an IT that are perceived and experienced by individuals. We expect PSITF to have a cross-leve influence on the PFIT of individuals embedded in soci ety. Fourth, adoption intention and purchase intention are related yet distinct constructs and may be driven by different factors (Venkatesh et al. 2012). Hence, to study the profound impact of IT fashion on consumer behaviors, we include both adoption and purchase intention.

In sum, we argue that an IT fashion is a societal phenomenon surrounding a fashionable technology and significantly influences consumer behavior. We position the four characteristics of IT fashion and fashionable IT—collective adoption, social endorsement, novel design, and IT-identity congruity, respectively— as major factors that characterize the two object-based beliefs: PSITF and PFIT. Then, PSITF and PFIT influence the two behavioral beliefs (i.e., external and internal symbolic values), which, in turn, influence consumer behaviors such as adoption or purchase.

## Hypotheses

We argue that PSITF has a significant effect on PFIT. Individual perceptions are not isolated; they are shaped and influenced by how they perceive the broader society. When individuals sense a prevailing societal trend about a technology, it can significantly alter their personal views on that technology. This cross-level effect is supported by prior research that emphasizes how collective beliefs play a role in shaping personal perception. For example, an individual’s perception of the team environment (i.e., the degree to which one feels supported and valued by their team) profoundly influences their cognitions and behaviors (Maruping and Magni 2015). Similarly, PSITF, which represents an individual’s understanding of collective beliefs of technology, can be viewed as a macrolevel influencer. This perception can exert a cross-level effect, influencing how an individual perceives the specifics of the technology at the heart of the IT fashion. Specifically, when a person observes others using the technology, the observer may infer that the technology has been widely accepted and widely recognized as high in quality, novel, and carrying identities. These social inferences, in turn, mold this person’s view of that technology.

The impact of PSITF on PFIT can also be supported by the herd literature. Herd behavior occurs when people observe and follow predecessors’ behavior because they believe that other people are better informed (Banerjee 1992, Sun 2013). When people observe or perceive that a lot of people do the same thing (e.g., adopting the same technology), they often infer that the technology must be of high quality; such inferences serve as “social proof” and influence one’s assessments of the technology (Sun 2013). Take virtual assistants such as Siri and Google Assistant, for instance; if an individual discerns a societal trend favoring these technologies, it intensifies their belief in the technology’s fashionableness, especially when they see influencers or elites embracing it. By emphasizing the impact of group perceptions on individual beliefs and drawing on herd literature, we hypothesize that

Hypothesis 1. Perceived societal-level IT fashion is positively associated with perceived fashionableness of IT.

We believe that PSITF has a positive effect on the external symbolic value of using a technology. As mentioned above, PSITF represents a belief in the technology itself, whereas external symbolic value represents a belief in using the technology. A high PSITF suggests that individuals are likely to develop a positive attitude toward the IT. Such a positive attitude consequently enhances their belief in using the technology for social affiliation purposes (Wixom and Todd 2005).

Moreover, in the above section, we identify two core characteristics of PSITF—collective adoption and social endorsement. Humans have an inherent desire to fit in with their social environment (Cialdini and Goldstein 2004). When a technology is adopted by a significant number of people in the society (i.e., collective adop tion), using the technology becomes the social norm in this group (Terry and Hogg 1996). Consequently, using it can become a medium of signaling adherence to soci etal norms and values, and individuals can feel a stronger sense of affiliation and acceptance within their social groups. On the other hand, endorsement from people with prestige in a social group leads to group conformity (Sproles 1979). Consequently, people in the social groups are more likely to believe that using the technology can help them obtain social recognition and group membership (Terry et al. 1999). In sum, when individuals perceive an IT to be in fashion in society, they are inclined to believe that using it can confer external symbolic values, enriching their social affiliations. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 2. Perceived societal-level IT fashion is positively associated with the external symbolic value of using the IT.

We posit that PFIT has positive effects on both internal and external symbolic values of using an IT. Different from PSITF, PFIT concerns an individual’s own assessment of IT in terms of its novelty and congruity with its own identity. As a result, individuals evaluate IT as a tool that embodies certain values that they personally uphold (Blumer 1969, Sproles 1979, Sirgy 1982). In this way, using the technology becomes a platform for self-expression, a canvas on which they can project their personal identities and social conformity. For instance, electric cars signal an alignment with sustain ability and environmental consciousness. The car becomes a statement of commitment to eco-friendly values. Using the electric car serves as a testament to the owner’s tech-savviness and forward-thinking attitude. In other words, if individuals feel that an IT’s identity doesn’t resonate with them, it’s logical to infer they wouldn’t perceive this IT as fashionable. In summary, an individual who perceives an IT as fashionable is also likely to view it as a tool to showcase and display their own value, tastes, and ways of life.

On the other hand, when individuals perceive the fashionableness of an IT, they view it as something they can use to tie to their social identity and enhance their social image. Individuals seek products that enhance their social image and provide external validation (Mead 1934). Using or possessing a fashionable IT can signal to others that the individual is in line with the latest trends within the social groups and increase their societal acceptance and desirability. Therefore, they believe that using this technology can enhance their belongingness to a social group (i.e., external symbolic value). Drawing from these insights, we hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 3. Perceived fashionableness of IT is positively associated with the internal symbolic value of using the IT.

## Hypothesis 4. Perceived fashionableness of IT is positively associated with the external symbolic value of using the IT.

We argue that external symbolic value is positively associated with internal symbolic value. This is evident from both group dynamics and the individual’s perspective. First, when individuals perceive that using a fashionable technology can signify their affiliation with a group, using it becomes a “prototype” within the group (Turner 1985, Turner et al. 1987). Members of a specific social group tend to converge around shared prototypes (Hogg et al. 1995) and subsequently identify with that group (Hogg and Hardie 1992). This identification also leads to internalization: members often internalize such group prototypes, molding them into extensions of their self-image and internalizing such identity into their own images. For instance, owning the latest Apple product is a signifier of belonging to a modern, design-oriented community of Apple enthusiasts. Using such a signifier would intensify one’s belief that using this technology makes one Apple enthusiast, tech-savvy, and active.

Second, the depersonalization theory (Hogg et al. 1995, p. 261) suggests that people tend to behave consistently with their group members, follow their group members’ values, lifestyles, and tastes, and consider them their own personal identities. Likewise, when people believe that using a fashionable technology can help them fit into the group (i.e., external symbolic value), it means that it becomes a group norm to use the technology for self-expression; thus, they tend to behave consistently with their group members and believe that using it is also a way to highlight their own personalities, values, and lifestyles. For instance, for a community that values staying ahead in technology, employing advanced AI models like ChatGPT in one’s professional or personal workflows is a signal of alignment with this innovative community. Over time, individuals in this community may think consistently with other people and develop a personal belief that using ChatGPT resonates with their individual aspirations and would enhance their self-expression. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

## Hypothesis 5. External symbolic value is positively associated with internal symbolic value.

We propose that both external and internal symbolic values of using fashionable IT directly influence adoption intention. From the institutional perspective (Currie 2011), prior IS research has shown that the symbolic values of IT are positively related to the adoption intention of technology (Teo et al. 2003). This influence of symbolic values on adoption intention can be seen at the individual level, too. According to Kelman (1958), human behaviors are impacted through three processes: compliance, identification, and internalization. We argue that external symbolic value influences behavior primarily through the identification process, and internal symbolic values affect users’ behaviors primarily through the internalization process. On the one hand, people intend to adopt a fashionable technology because they can use it to “establish or maintain a satisfying self-defining relationship with another per son or a group” (Kelman 1958, p. 53). People seek opportunities to obtain identification with the other members of their social group (Gecas and Schwalbe 1983, Burke and Stets 2009). Therefore, when consu mers believe that using a technology can enhance the social affiliation in their social groups (i.e., perceived external symbolic value), they have a higher intention to adopt the technology.

On the other hand, internal symbolic value is positively associated with adoption intention through the internalization process (Kelman 1958). People recognize and resonate with the symbolic meanings represented by a fashionable technology (e.g., politically liberal, physically active, tech-savvy) and decide to adopt the technology to obtain feelings of coherence and self-respect (Swann 1983, Burke 2004). Thus, a person is willing to adopt a technology because of a belief that using it can display one’s personality, values, and way of living as a person (i.e., internal symbolic value). Accordingly, we hypothesize that

Hypothesis 6. (a) External symbolic value and (b) inter nal symbolic value are positively associated with adoption intention.

We believe that both external and internal symbolic values have a significant impact on willingness to pay: people are willing to pay extra for the symbolic values rendered by a fashionable technology. This relationship can be understood through consumers’ perceived value: prior empirical evidence supports that people exhibit a willingness to pay a premium when they perceive additional value in a product. For example, Godek and Murray (2008) found that the WTP for expert advice increased when participants saw the value in the advice. Researchers also discovered that consumers’ willingness to pay for an electric vehicle is higher if the vehicle has the most desirable attributes (Hidrue et al. 2011).

The current marketing literature on luxury consumption argues that consumers often have a premium for luxury items, particularly when they perceive the brand’s logo as a potent tool to signal their wealth status and societal standing (Han et al. 2010). Analogously, when a technology holds symbolic value, it essentially serves as a token of social acceptance. Thus, individuals may be likely to pay more for its acquisition. For example, as AI’s ability to create music has evolved, some musicians and enthusiasts have embraced these tools, seeing them as innovative extensions of their musical creativity. For them, because of the symbolic values (catching up with the music enthusiasts’ group and showing their “musician” image) associated with using an AI music generator, they would likely value it more and invest more. Thus, we hypothesize that

Hypothesis 7. (a) External symbolic value and (b) internal symbolic value are positively associated with WTP.

## Research Design and Results

To investigate the theoretical framework, two empirical studies were carried out in different contexts, with three purposes in mind: (1) to develop and validate the new individual-level measures of IT fashion, (2) to investigate fashion’s impact on different consumer behaviors, and (3) to validate the research model with both fashionable and nonfashionable technologies. With these purposes in mind, in Study 1, we aimed to test our framework with technology adoption as the outcome variable. Meanwhile, we treated PSITF and PFIT as formative second-order constructs. Study 2 aimed to triangulate and extend findings from Study 1. Specifically, it tested the research model with willingness to pay as the outcome consumer behavior. Following previous practice (Hoehle and Venkatesh 2015), we also developed new global items to directly measure PSITF and PFIT. In addition, Study 2 also tested and compared how the model may work differently for both in-fashion and out-of-fashion technologies. Table 2 summarizes and compares the design of the two studies.

## Study 1: Adoption of Fashionable IT

Research Design. An online survey was designed and administered to test the research model in May 2017, with Apple Watch as the focal technology. Apple Watch was released in April 2015 and had started to grow in the market when the survey was conducted. A professional survey service company, Qualtrics, was hired to collect responses for the pilot and the full test. Qualtrics has been used by other IS and management researchers to collect responses for online surveys (Long et al. 2011, Williams et al. 2016). According to the new NPD Connected Intelligence Consumers and Wearables Report (The NDP Group 2015), 95% of smartwatch users were under the age of 55, and most users earned under \$45,000 (48%) or over \$100,000 (20%). Based on smartwatch user demographics, this study targeted younger adults—those who were inclined to use the technology but did not adopt it at the time of the survey. Accordingly, Qualtrics was asked to sample individuals between the ages of 18 and 55, equally distributed between males and females from various industries. Qualtrics estimated that roughly 2,000 respondents received an initial survey solicitation. However, 966 respondents were not eligi ble for the survey. Among the rest, 265 completed the survey. The response rate was 25.6%. Nine responses were considered invalid because of reasons such as short durations or straight-lining and were thus eliminated, resulting in a total of 256 responses.

The purpose of the study was first explained to the participants. They were assured of the survey’s anonymity and were told they could stop taking it anytime. Given the study’s focus on how IT fashion influences adoption intention (i.e., potential adopters), they were then asked if they currently used or owned the target technology. Participants who answered yes were filtered out. After basic demographic questions about age, gender, education, and income, a description of the target technology’s major features was presented to them. They were then asked to answer questions based on this description and their own knowledge of the technology. Two attention check items were included to ensure that participants were attentive to the survey and answered the questions carefully.

Measures. Following MacKenzie et al. (2011), related constructs in the literature were surveyed, and measures for the constructs in the model were developed using the domain sampling method. Online Appendix B details the measurement development process and the initial item pool. As previous studies have stated, input from practice in the early stages of conceptualization is important (Nassar-McMillan and Borders 2002, Anatchkova and Bjorner 2010). Therefore, focus groups were used to triangulate the conceptualization of the construct between the literature and practice, as presented in Online Appendix B. To further refine and test the instrument’s validity, several pretests (including a verbal protocol and three rounds of Q-sorting) and a pilot test with 50 responses were conducted. Items were adjusted based on the results. Online Appendix C details the process, whereas Online Appendix D presents the results of the pilot test and the measurement items after adjustment.

## Discussion

## Major Findings

Our first research question is, “What is IT fashion and how do individuals perceive it?” Through our study, our answer is that IT fashion can be viewed as a complex social phenomenon surrounding an information technology. It involves the interplays among individuals, society, and the IT itself. IT fashion is a collective enthusiasm to own or be associated with the fashionable IT, driven by celebrities endorsing the product, viral marketing campaigns, or the collective desire to be part of the “in-fashion” crowd. In sum, individuals perceive various societal elements to form perceptions about whether this IT is in fashion or not in the society. Simultaneously, they form their own assessment of this technology’s fashionableness.

Our second research question is, “How does IT fashion influence consumer behavior?” Our short answer is that IT fashion exerts influences on consumers behavior through perceived external and internal symbolic values of using a fashionable technology, a transition from perceiving the ongoing IT fashion in society and the fashionable IT itself to using the IT for symbolic purposes.

In our research, we observed intriguing outcomes that were initially unexpected. From Study 1, we found that IT fashion influences the perceived symbolic values of using a fashionable IT $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ external and internal symbolic values), which bolsters consumers’ adoption intention. However, the subsequent Study 2 highlighted that these symbolic values did not amplify consumers’ willingness to pay. This divergence can be attributed to the inherent differences between adoption intention and WTP. Notably, adoption intention aligns more closely with willingness to accept, representing the minimum amount a consumer would part with for a product (Hanemann 1991). On the contrary, WTP signifies the upper limit of this financial commitment (Hanemann 1991). When arriving at WTP decisions, consumers may be hesitant to exceed this baseline amount unless they identify tangible benefits (Tey et al. 2018). It suggests that the allure of intangible attributes, such as fashion elements or symbolic values, may not be compelling enough to persuade consumers to increase their monetary expenditure substantially. Furthermore, as the current marketing literature indicates, a person’s economic constraints, individual differences, and cultural and societal factors (Han et al. 2010) may also drive the gaps between adoption and consumption.

Another possible explanation is that when an IT is high in symbolic value, it may inevitably be low in uniqueness, which results in low WTP. People have a need for uniqueness (Hong and Tam 2006). The literature suggests that people actively seek to differentiate themselves from others (Lynn and Snyder 2002). When purchasing a product, there will be a trade-off between joining the in-fashion crowd and the need for uniqueness, with the former forcing group conformity and the latter constituting an internal motivation for being unique (Rahman and Cherrier 2010).

In addition, we suspect that people may demonstrate contrarian behavior during an IT fashion, where people intentionally make decisions against the herd (Cipriani and Guarino 2005). Prior research suggests that people may have contrarian behavior for reputation and compensation reasons (Bikhchandani and Sharma 2000, Compen et al. 2022). Hence, an interesting topic for future research may be why people may choose to avoid joining the herd of a fashionable technology (Hanemann 1991).

We also observe a possible association between brand impression and WTP in the case of Meta Quest. Several participants in our pretests mentioned their negative impression of the brand or the company of the technology. For example, one participant mentioned, “I wouldn’t use Oculus even if they paid me. Facebook and Instagram are a malevolence on society … I want nothing to do with it.” Similarly, another participant mentioned that “Mark Zuckerberg is a complete joke of a human—selfish and a capitalist—only motivated by monetary gains.” Participants may have associated the technology with an undesirable impression so that they attach a negative effect to the social groups who adopt or endorse it. Accordingly, it is possible that if using a technology would help one fit into the “undesirable” social group (such as owning a Meta Quest can strengthen one’s bond with an undesirable social group associated with Meta), it will lead to devaluation of the IT product, which results in decreasing of WTP.

Our research model can generalize to both in-fashion and out-of-fashion technologies or, in other words, technologies in different fashion periods using Abrahamson (1996)’s terminology. Specifically, Meta Quest is likely in its fashion growth period, and Excel is likely in the fashion decline period.<sup>4</sup> Study 2 indicates that the in-fashion Meta Quest did have higher PSITF (mean � 4.034 and 3.729, for Meta Quest and Excel, respectively) and PFIT (mean � 3.572 and 3.074, for Meta Quest and Excel, respectively) than Excel, as shown in Table G.4 in Online Appendix G. The results suggest that our conceptualizations of PSITF and PFIT are valid and can be used to measure and compare how people feel about different IT fashions and fashionable technologies. The hypothesis testing in Study 2 (Figure 4) suggests that overall, the research model works similarly for both in-fashion Meta Quest and out-of-fashion Excel. Moreover, when combining the data of the two technologies, most of the hypotheses are supported. Hence, we believe that every technology can be assessed for fashionableness at different levels. Some initial evidence can be seen in the pilot test in Study 2, where all the 18 technologies we proposed received some fashionableness scores from the participants. Hence, the fashion-related factors we developed, for example, characteristics of IT fashion and user perceptions, can be used to study virtually any technology.

## Contributions and Research Implications

This study makes several contributions to academic research. Theoretically, our study develops a novel research model of IT fashion at the individual level. Conceptually, this study conceptualizes several new constructs, among which we highlight the two-tier individual perceptions of IT fashion: PSITF and PFIT. These constructs capture the complexity of user perceptions related to IT fashion and are confirmed in this research to be important antecedents of consumer behavior. Methodologically, this research developed new instruments for measuring the new constructs, including PSITF, PSITF, external symbolic value, and internal symbolic value.

This study renders implications for the existing research on user adoption of technology (Davis 1989, Venkatesh and Davis 2000, Venkatesh et al. 2003, Van der Heijden 2004) to examine IS phenomena through a fashion lens, beyond the utility perspective that has been well studied in the extant IS research. This research explicitly shows that many technologies can be considered fashionable and consumer behaviors are greatly affected by IT fashions. People do form perceptions about IT fashion and the fashionableness of tech nologies both in and out of fashion. Specifically, a fashionable technology should be adopted by a significant number of people in the society, be endorsed by opinion leaders, possess novel features, and resonate with personal identities. When a technology becomes fashionable, users’ behaviors are driven by different motivations: group conformity, individuality, desire for novelty, and herding. For instance, many social media platforms have risen and died over the years. Users migrated from one platform to another on a large scale, and many abandoned the old platforms (e.g., Myspace) because they were no longer “cool.” These users’ behaviors resemble a fashion movement and can be studied from a fashion lens.

Our research model may be generalized to organizational IT with some contextualization. For example, when deciding to adopt a fashionable organizational IT, organizational leaders may assess the IT from both a macro level (such as observing if there is collective adoption among other organizations) and a micro level (assessing its novelty and fit with the organization). These object-based beliefs then transition into beliefs about adopting the IT for specific purposes. As Wang (2010) notes, when an IT fashion becomes prevalent, organizations that follow the trend and adopt the IT are often admired more and attain a higher reputation.

## Limitations and Future Research

As with all research, our study has limitations. First, this research did not sufficiently solve the issue of why IT fashion does not influence willingness to pay. As discussed above, there are several possible explanations for why consumers’ adoption intention, but not willingness to pay, is strongly influenced by IT fashion. Apparently, the impact of IT fashion on consumer behavior, especially consumers’ willingness to pay, needs more attention. Future research may continue to investigate what factors influence willingness to pay for a fashionable technology. As mentioned above, some constructs, such as the need for uniqueness (Hong and Tam 2006) and contrarian (antiherd) behavior (Rahman and Cherrier 2010), may be good starting points.

Second, it’s important to recognize that IT fashion is inherently a social phenomenon. However, the research model we’ve developed primarily focuses on individuals’ perceptions of both this societal phenomenon and fashionable technology. We encourage future research to further investigate the societal-level aspects of IT fashion and how perceptions of fashion may spread among individuals. Several existing theoretical frameworks, such as herding (Sun 2013) or antiherd/ contrarian behavior (Cipriani and Guarino 2005, Drehmann et al. 2005), may prove valuable in this line of research. Additionally, prior literature has posited that the diffusion of fashion follows a three-phase pattern: latency, growth, and decline (Abrahamson 1996). The latency phase marks the inception of a potential fashion, the growth phase is when it gains public attention and experiences a relatively sudden surge in popularity (Lee and Collar 2003), and after the growth phase, the fashion gradually loses public attention (Abrahamson 1996). Future research can further investigate whether IT fashion adheres to this nonlinear diffusion pattern.

Third, as this research predominantly focuses on consumer products available in the market, there was limited space to explore intriguing questions such as the impact of different product editions. It raises questions about when a technological advancement is perceived as incremental and when it’s considered novel enough to initiate a new wave of fashion. Future research can delve into these inquiries, potentially by identifying distinct fashion phases and investigating how the nature of the technology influences the duration of these phases. Moreover, it’s worth noting that Study 1 did not explicitly incorporate ownership of an iPhone as a control variable. Given that the Apple Watch requires use in conjunction with an iPhone, iPhone ownership might, indeed, influence adoption intention. Additionally, during a poststudy debriefing, several participants in Study 2 mentioned that their low willingness to pay was linked to negative impressions of the IT company or brand. Future studies can explore the impacts of company or brand perceptions on consumer behavior regarding fashionable technology.

Fourth, our postanalyses have revealed that the direct effect of utilitarian value on IT fashion perceptions and consumer behavior appears to be contingent on the type of technology. We call for future research to explore this area, specifically examining the technological context within which utilitarian values can either enhance or fail to enhance consumer perceptions of IT fashion or fashionable technology.

Lastly, whereas our research investigates the realm of IT fashion, there remain several other IS phenomena related to technology that have yet to be explored. Examples include IT fads, popular technology, and luxury technology. Such “social movements” may give rise to distinct consumer behaviors such as “bandwagon”

luxury consumption (Kastanakis and Balabanis 2012) and conspicuous consumption (Chaudhuri and Majumdar 2006). Investigating, comparing, and systematically differentiating these phenomena present intriguing avenues for further exploration.

## Practical Implications

Fashion is ubiquitous in today’s world, and IT fashion may increasingly exert considerable influence on user behavior. Our research provides several implications for practitioners and consumers. First and foremost, it’s essential to recognize that IT fashion transcends mere product functionality; it’s deeply intertwined with how it’s perceived. In terms of brand positioning and marketing strategy, companies can gain a competitive edge by making their IT products socially visible, such as aligning their branding with celebrity endorsements or telling a story that combines societal excitement with the consumer’s quest for novelty. Also, our results suggest that symbolic values could significantly affect consumers adoption intention. Thus, the marketing campaigns should be dual focused: highlighting both the functiona and symbolic values of the product. This strategy can foster a deeper consumer-product connection. Practitioners should also engage users beyond the point of purchase through community interactions and loyalty programs that amplify the symbolic value of the technology.

Second, our research also has implications for IT product development. IT fashions usually have a much longer lifetime than IT fads, as suggested by Wasson (1968), which means that fashionable technologies could have long-lasting effects. Thus, IT companies should design fashionable IT by designing the identity of new products and making the products extensions of consumers’ personalities. Products that resonate with a user’s sense of self have an edge in the market, so IT companies may integrate features or personalization options that echo the user’s identity.

Third, it is imperative for organizations to remain attuned to evolving consumer perceptions and the dynamics of IT fashion. By doing so, they can proactively adapt to these changes, seizing the opportunities and addressing the challenges that fashionable technologies bring.

Lastly, individual consumers can derive significant benefits from our research. Realizing that IT fashion is influenced by societal factors can empower consumers to make more informed purchasing decisions. Beyond the marketing buzz, understanding the authentic value and functionality of an IT product is vital. Simultaneously, IT offers opportunities for self-expression. Whether it’s a reflection of one’s unique style, values, or aspirations, the choices regarding IT should genuinely reflect an individual’s identity.

## Conclusion

Traditionally, information technology and fashion were viewed as an unlikely pairing. However, in recent times, they have become increasingly intertwined, leading to the emergence of IT fashions. An IT fashion represents a prominent social phenomenon centered around fashionable technology. It is characterized by collective adoption, social endorsement, novelty, and IT-identity congruity. Individuals perceive IT fashions from two distinct perspectives: the societal level (referred to as PSITF) and the individual level (referred to as PFIT). These perceptions play an essential role in shaping how individuals view the external and internal symbolic values associated with using such technology, subsequently influencing their behavior. The findings from our two empirical studies suggest that the research model developed in this study can be applied to technologies in various fashion stages. Considering the ubiquity of IT fashion and its substantial impact on consumer behavior, as evident in this research, we encourage IS researchers to explore IT fashion and fashionable technology further.
