# Explainable Deep Learning for False Information Identification: An Argumentation Theory Approach

Kyuhan Lee,<sup>a,</sup>\* Sudha Ram<sup>b</sup>

Abstract. In today’s world, where online information is proliferating in an unprecedented way, a significant challenge is whether to believe the information we encounter. Ironically, this flood of information provides us with an opportunity to combat false claims by understanding their nature. That is, with the help of machine learning, it is now possible to effectively capture the characteristics of false information by analyzing massive amounts of false claims published online. These methods, however, have neglected the nature of human argumentation, delegating the process of making inferences of the truth to the black box of neural networks. This has created several challenges (namely latent text representations containing entangled syntactic and semantic information, an irrelevant part of text being considered when abstracting text as a latent vector, and counterintuitive model explanation). To resolve these issues, based on Toulmin’s model of argumentation, we propose a computational framework that helps machine learning for false information identification (FII) understand the connection between a claim (whose veracity needs to be verified) and evidence (which contains information to support or refute the claim). Specifically, we first build a word network of a claim and evidence reflecting their syntaxes and convert it into a signed word network using their semantics. The structural balance of this word network is then calculated as a proxy metric to determine the consistency between a claim and evidence. The consistency level is fed into machine learning as input, providing information for verifying claim veracity and explaining the model’s decision making. The two experi ments for testing model performance and explainability reveal that our framework shows stronger performance and better explainability, outperforming cutting-edge methods and presenting positive effects on human task performance, trust in algorithms, and confidence in decision making. Our results shed new light on the growing field of automated FII.

## 1. Introduction

Deception is ubiquitous in our society, and its prevalence has created social costs in multiple domains of our lives. For instance, the price of Bitcoin surged by more than 20% on April 1, 2019. One of the reasons for the rally was an April Fool’s joke, posted on Twitter, stating that the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission might approve several types of cryptocurrencies to be used as exchange-traded funds (Marsh 2019). Although the story could have been verified using multiple reliable resources that were easy to find on the web, the public instead blindly believed and disseminated false information, resulting in individual traders purchasing Bitcoins based on illusory expectations.

The impacts of deception are becoming more pronounced in the era of social media. Many social media sites, including Twitter and Facebook, provide a convenient way to publish and share information. Any individual is free to declare his or her opinions or unverified facts as ultimate truths, thus leading to a proliferation in the amount of information whose veracity is not properly verified. Worse, considerable empirical evidence exists suggesting that such ambiguous information tends to have stronger reachability to the public than authentic facts (Vosoughi et al. 2018).

To combat false information, social media sites have heavily relied on content moderation, mostly performed by human workers (CNBC 2021). However, human content moderation entails multiple problems, including huge labor costs, ineffectiveness, and ethical issues. To overcome these concerns, social media companies are aggressively investing in the development of artificial intelligence (AI)-powered false information detection systems. The primary purposes of these systems are (i) to function as an initial filter for false information and (ii) to support content moderators in their decision making. Thus, an ideal system should not only be accurate but also, be able to explain its decision to human workers. That is, these systems should maintain an adequate performance level to automatically filter out, without much error, a large proportion of fake information. In addition, for complex cases that need human intervention, AI should be able to provide appropriate explanations on how and why certain information was classified as false to assist human workers in making an ultimate decision.

In both academia and industry, deep learning (DL) has been largely highlighted as a promising avenue for automating the process of false information identification (FII). For instance, sequential DL methods, such as recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and long short-term neural networks (LSTMs), have been widely adopted to determine text veracity (Karadzhov et al. 2017). Recently, the attention mechanism and its extensions (e.g., transformer-based models) have further improved the performance of these sequential learning methods in text classification (Vaswani et al. 2017). Additionally, attention weights, the building blocks of the attention mechanism, have been frequently used for visualizing and explaining how models produce outcomes.

These methods, however, have failed to understand the nature of human argumentation, delegating the process of making inferences of the truth to the black box of neural networks. Their approaches fail to attend to important aspects of how humans make judgments on the veracity of an argument, creating several important challenges. First, latent text representations derived from neural network-based document-embedding models contain entangled syntactic and semantic information, and thus, they are not able to precisely capture the information needed to judge the truth (Huang et al. 2021). To elaborate, the main purpose of neural network-based models is to produce latent representations of texts and make predictions based on it. These representations encode the syntactic and semantic information in a blended way, as opposed to a human linguistic process that clearly distinguishes them (Dapretto and Bookheimer 1999). Moreover, neural network-based models abstract and represent text, taking account of every word in it, which inevitably results in the inclusion of irrelevant information. Although the attention mechanism has mitigated this issue by enabling the models to learn and identify important parts of the text, many recent studies have highlighted some of its pitfalls. Particularly, the contribution of attention weights, the building blocks of the attention mechanism, to classification outcomes has been questioned in multiple studies (e.g., Jain and Wallace 2019, Serrano and Smith 2019, Vashishth et al. 2019). These studies demonstrate the existence of many cases where attention scores do not properly represent important parts of the text (Jain and Wallace 2019). Furthermore, they suggest that attention scores may not have much impact on determining the outcomes of a prediction model (Serrano and Smith 2019).

Additionally, another concern arises when we entirely delegate the process of explaining the model’s decision making to the black box of neural networks. That is, previous literature has suggested that a desirable human-computer interaction can be achieved when the messages provided by a computer (e.g., model explanation) resemble those of exemplary, real-life com munication between humans (Kim and Benbasat 2006, Abbasi et al. 2018). However, the model explanation produced by attention weights shows no such resemblance because it is purely generated by computational inference that fails to consider the nature of human argumentation. Furthermore, as mentioned, attention weights often do not highlight important parts of texts, producing misleading model explanations<sup>1</sup> (Jain and Wallace 2019, Serrano and Smith 2019, Vashishth et al. 2019).

To address these issues, we, following the design science approach, develop a theory-driven DL framework that provides a complementary view (or an alternative view) to extant FII methods. Our framework is grounded in the process of human argumentation suggested by Toulmin’s model (Verheij 2009). Although Toulmin’s model is a useful tool for explaining the human process of inferring the truth, it does not provide specific design principles for transforming the process into an algorithm (Verheij 2009). To this end, we propose a computational approach for representing the components of Toulmin’s model using the struc tural balance theory (SBT) as a design metaphor and suggest an automated FII framework.

This study makes several important contributions. First, from a methodological perspective, we propose a framework that extracts an important part of information from texts, drawing upon Toulmin’s model that describes the human argumentation process. Our approach, unlike neural network-based language models, explicitly distinguishes the structure (i.e., syntax) of text from semantics, which makes the FII process clearer and more efficient. Second, our theory-based approach provides enhanced model explainability. Our framework has enabled us to generate model explanations that resemble how humans make and understand arguments, thus improving human-AI interactions. Specifically, the results of our experiment demonstrate that our structural balance theory-based explanation (SBTX) facilitates humans’ FII, achieving higher task accuracy, trust in AI, and confidence in decision making when compared with the attention weight-based explanation (ATTNX). Third, we demonstrate the strong generalizability of our framework across multiple contexts. Most current advances in the FII literature have been derived from improvements via engineering. This often results in ad hoc methods, which can be applied to only a limited setting (Zubiaga et al. 2018). In contrast, our SBTbased model for FII demonstrates its versatility in handling multiple data sets with distinct and different characteristics. Fourth, our study makes an important addition to the scant literature on automated FII in the field of information systems (IS). In IS, several studies have paid attention to the issues of fake, deceptive information. These include studies examining the impact of people’s bias toward fake news (Moravec et al. 2019), the influence of news sources on users’ believability and engagement (Kim et al. 2019), and the relationship between fake news and stock investors’ attention (Clarke et al. 2021). There is, however, a dearth of studies to understand false information from a design science perspective, and we expect our study to be a welcome addition. Fifth, this study contributes to an emerging body of machine learning (ML)-based text analytics research in IS. Recently, a few IS studies have proposed novel ML-based text analytics methods grounded in linguistic and psychological theories (Chen et al. 2023, Yang et al. 2023). These theory-based approaches bring a fresh perspective to the extant approaches of designing ML models mainly adopted in the field of computer science, resulting in not only performance improvement but also, theoretical extension. Lastly, the explainability component of our study adds to the literature on human-AI interaction studies in IS. As it becomes increasingly important to explain and justify AI system decisions, many IS researchers have focused their efforts on investigating how AI explanation affects human perception and behavior (Bauer et al. 2023). In line with these efforts, our method proposes and evaluates a new approach to explain ML model behavior in fake news detection.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we present a comprehensive review of existing research on FII. Then, a detailed explanation of argumentation theory is provided to motivate our approach. This is followed by a description of each step in our proposed computational method for FII grounded in an SBT design metaphor. The method is compared with multiple baselines in two experiments to test its prediction performance and explainability. Lastly, we discuss the overall findings and managerial implications of this study as well as future extensions.

## 2. Related Work

The approaches to FII consist of studies on multiple interconnected research problems, including fact-checking, fake news detection, stance detection, and rumor classification. They essentially have similar goals of developing an automated algorithm for determining the veracity of content. In this section, we review major approaches that have been used in prior literature and identify research gaps. Then, we introduce argumentation theory, which provides an important theoretical motivation for our SBT-based method that addresses the research gaps. Note that we only consider studies on fact-based false information as opposed to opinionbased false information, for which there is no ground truth.

## 2.1. Literature on FII

Table 1 summarizes extant studies on automated FII and related fields. We have classified these studies using multiple criteria, including domains, methods, features, and model explainability. First, from the method perspective, ML has dominated the field. Although other approaches (e.g., information retrieval, knowledge graph) have also been proposed in prior literature, we decided not to include them in this review for brevity because their performance has been less effective compared with ML-based models (Shu et al. 2017).

We classified ML-based models into three subcategories: classic ML, DL, and attention-based DL. Classic ML refers to ML methods that do not incorporate deep neural network structures, such as logistic regression, decision trees, support vector machines, and so on. Many of the early works for automated FII are included in this category. For instance, Castillo et al. (2011) pro posed a decision tree model using text and user behavior to predict the credibility of news on Twitter. DL refers to methods with deep neural network structures. Karadzhov et al. (2017) used bidirectional LSTMs to encode multiple sources of text and added a support vector machine to determine the veracity of a claim. Lastly, attention-based DL refers to DL methods that use the attention mechanism to enhance performance. More recently, many high-performing self-attention-based language models have been proposed. For instance, Khoo et al. (2020) suggested a hierarchical self-attention neural network architecture to identify rumors on Twitter. Besides its superior performance, the attention mechanism has another important advantage, namely model explanation, which will be discussed later in this section.

From a feature perspective, two types of data have been largely used. First, people’s characteristics (e.g., demographics) have been used as social cues for identifying the veracity of the information they post or diffuse. Additionally, researchers have considered people’s behavior from a network perspective, which focuses on how people interact (e.g., support, observe, refute). Second, content-based features focus on the linguistic aspects of false information. One of the most widely used content-based features is the vector representations of texts grounded in the distributional hypothesis (Harris 1954). Word-embedding vectors, such as GloVe, have often been used to project texts in a latent vector space (Pennington et al. 2014, Zubiaga et al. 2016). Recently, transformer-based language models have been proposed to improve model performance (Shu et al. 2019, Zhang et al. 2020). In addition, the use of metadata has been highlighted in some studies. For example, the number of URLs, time stamps for writing and editing content, and the inclusion of emojis have been identified as important for identifying content veracity.

Previous studies have collectively applied a comprehensive set of approaches, and notably, attention-based methods showed state-of-the-art performance in FII. However, we identify several research gaps in this stream of research. First, neural network-based sentence- or document-embedding models produce latent text representations, which contain both syntactic and semantic information tangled up together (Huang et al. 2021). This may be problematic in a setting where we want to determine the veracity of a claim purely based on the semantic similarity between claim and evidence sentences instead of their syntactic similarity. Specifically, when measuring the similarity, the vector representations of the sentences derived from neural networks might be misleading because they contain not only semantic but also, syntactic information. Thus, despite not having semantic closeness, two sentences can be considered similar when they share comparable syntactic structures (Huang et al. 2021). Additionally, these neural networkbased models scan all the words in a document to produce its latent representation, which increases the possibility of considering irrelevant information. Although the attention mechanism alleviates this issue, many recent studies have highlighted that attention weights may not precisely attend to important input features (i.e., words), and the contribution of these weights to model outcomes is obscure (Jain and Wallace 2019, Serrano and Smith 2019, Vashishth et al. 2019).

Aside from this dispute, we found that a majority of previous studies neglected model explanations. Specifically, despite the importance of human intervention when applying these technologies in practice, previous studies did not properly examine the impact of automated FII methods on human behavior. In practice, these systems are typically used to help human users (e.g., content moderators), functioning as an initial filter for identifying content that might include false information. Therefore, how humans perceive these AI-based systems is particularly important. However, previous literature has paid only scant or no attention to model explanation. Chen et al. (2020) and Khoo et al. (2020) proposed multiple model explanation approaches, but they did not examine how these approaches might affect human perception and behavior (e.g., algorithmic trust, task accuracy, and confidence in decision making). Although

Nguyen et al. (2018), Shu et al. (2019), and Yang et al. (2019) did consider human factors when evaluating their model explanation approaches, their experiments were lacking in terms of the scope, size, and rigorousness of analysis. Nguyen et al. (2018) examined the impact of model explanation on an algorithm-assisted fact-checking task with only 23 graduate students. Yang et al. (2019) measured only quantitative aspects of model explainability (i.e., task accuracy and time to task completion) and did not examine its affective impacts (e.g., trust, confidence, and so on). Shu et al. (2019) asked participants only about their perceived usefulness of model explanation without considering other important metrics, such as its influence on task accuracy and trust in the algorithm. Moreover, all of these studies failed to take into account possible confounders (e.g., demographic variables and general trust in AI) from their analyses.

In light of these issues, we propose an argumentation theory-based DL framework for automated FII using SBT as a design metaphor. The advantages of our approach include not only its superior performance but also, enhanced explainability. For evaluation, we first compared the performance of our model with multiple cutting-edge methods. Then, in an additional experiment, we evaluated our SBTX against ATTNX using a comprehensive set of performance metrics visa\`-vis human-AI interactions. In the following sections, we first explain the nature of FII from the human perspective based on argumentation theory and describe how it motivates our methodological framework.

## 2.2. Argumentation Theory and Its Downstream Implications for FII

Although in the history of philosophy, formal logic has been the main apparatus for finding the truth, since the midtwentieth century, argumentation theory (or informal logic) has come to the fore, criticizing the inapplicability of formal logic to real-life claims that often omit formal components (e.g., premises and conclusions) and that are defeasible and context dependent (Bench-Capon and Dunne 2007, Verheij 2009, Walton 2009, Groarke 2021). Among the different argumentation models, Toulmin’s model, as one of the best known, describes human reasoning of arguments, comprising three essential components (i.e., claim, evidence, and warrant) and three optional components (i.e., qualifier, backing, and rebuttal) (Verheij 2009, Lippi and Torroni 2016). That is, argumentation is composed of an argument (i.e., claim) whose veracity needs to be verified, evidence (i.e., evidence) that forms the basis of the argument, and rules of inference (i.e., warrant) that link the argument and evidence (Lippi and Torroni 2016). The other three components, which render Toulmin’s inference process flexible (i.e., context dependent), express the certainty of the argument (i.e., qualifier), provide specific evidence to inference rules (i.e., backing), and set the boundary within which the argument holds (i.e., rebut tal) (Bench-Capon and Dunne 2007, Verheij 2009).

Toulmin’s model provides a useful guideline for building an efficient automated FII model because it delineates the human process of drawing an inference of the truth in a daily context (Lippi and Torroni 2016). By computationally representing the components of Toulmin’s model, it may be possible to develop an FII model more powerful than the current state-of-the-art methods that rely on black box inference. Additionally, the adoption of Toulmin’s model can lead to the development of an FII system whose decision-making process is explainable to humans (Mozˇina et al. 2007). That is, FII models based on Toulmin’s framework may generate trusting and convincing explanations of how they make decisions (Kim and Benbasat 2006). Indeed, Gregor and Jones (2004, p. 90) stated that “explanations that conform to Toulmin’s model of argumentation, in that they provide adequate justification for the knowledge offered, should be more persuasive and lead to greater trust, agreement, satisfaction, and acceptance— of the explanation and possibly also of the system as a whole.” This is particularly significant because the impact of AI systems on humans has become an important criterion for evaluating their values (Shu et al. 2019). Both researchers and practitioners have begun to emphasize theoretical constructs related to human-AI interactions, such as trust and confidence when evaluating AI systems (Kizilcec 2016, Honeycutt et al. 2020).

Despite their importance, little attention has been paid to the role of these argumentation models in developing automated FII models. A majority of deep learning-based FII systems delegate the process of linking claim and evidence (which corresponds to the warrant and backing process of Toulmin’s model) and reflecting context information (which corresponds to the qualifier and rebuttal components of Toulmin’s model) to the black box procedure of neural networks. This is partly because argumentation models in general (including Toulmin’s model) do not provide specific design principles for transforming human argumentation into a computational process (Verheij 2009). To fill this research gap, we propose a computational design for automated FII based on the Fregean system. Although the Fregean system has been one of the important language views in IS, connecting natural language sentences to facts in the world and determining what sentences are true or false, less attention has been paid to its close connection to automated FII (Lyytinen 1985). We use Frege’s theory of reference to computationally represent the human process of warranting in Toulmin’s model, drawing upon the design metaphor of SBT. We expect that this design grounded in linguistic theories leads us not only to develop a better-performing model but also, to better explain how the model works to human users. In the next section, we elaborate on our design.

2.3. Design Principle for Automated FII Systems In the history of ML, good metaphors have led to the development of many innovative designs. An exemplar is a neural network that closely mimics how human brains function (Hassabis et al. 2017). Additionally, metaphors often entail fortuitous benefits, such as model explainability in decision trees (Quinlan 1986). In a similar vein, SBT provides a good metaphor for understanding and explaining the human process of argumentation. In the following, we show how SBT can guide a computational implementation of Toulmin’s model and with the theory of reference, support the idea from the linguistics perspective.

SBT, which is better expressed by the quote “the enemy of my enemy is my friend,” was originally developed to explain a transitive relation between humans (e.g., if aRb and bRc, then $a { \mathcal { R } } c ,$ where {a, b, c} is a set of humans and R is a type of relationship between them such as “affection”) (Heider 1946). Subsequently, Cartwright and Harary (1956) generalized the theory by applying it to a network among any number of people (or entities). In the simplest setting (i.e., a cyclic network between three entities), SBT defines the state of balance and imbalance of a social network.

Specifically, in Figure 1, let {a, b, c} be a set of individuals. A state of three individuals is balanced if the network contains an even number of negative edges and imbalanced otherwise (Cartwright and Harary 1956). Then, the theory posits that when there is a state of imbalance among three entities, pressure may be applied to change their relationships so that a new balanced state can be achieved. For instance, if $a  ^ { - } b , \enspace a  ^ { + } c ,$ and $b  ^ { + } c ,$ where ↔ and 6, respectively, imply a relationship and its sentiment, then there is structural imbalance because it violates the rule of “a friend’s enemy is my enemy” (i.e., only one negative link in the network). Thus, according to SBT, we may predict that one of the relationships would change to form a balanced structure (e.g., from $a  ^ { - } b { \mathrm { ~ t o ~ } } a  ^ { + } b )$

A primary motivation for applying SBT to automated FII originates from the following theoretical observations of argumentation models. Suppose we have a claim whose veracity needs to be verified (i.e., claim) and evidence holding ground-truth information that can either support or refute the claim (i.e., evidence). One way to make a connection between them (i.e., warrant) is to evaluate their information consistency (Keith and Beard 2008). That is, by estimating the consistency between a claim and data, we can warrant their connection, according to Toulmin’s model. As humans, we may easily identify the consistency between two information sources (i.e., claim and evidence) without any difficulty. For instance, let us assume that we want to verify a claim “John F. Kennedy was a Republican” based on an evidence statement “John F. Kennedy was a Democrat.” We can infer that the claim contains false information by finding syntactic and semantic differences between the two sentences. Specifically, we understand that the two sentences share the same pro noun, but their predicates differ in what they refer to. That is, the predicate of the claim refers to a member of the Republican party, whereas that of the evidence indicates a member of the Democratic party (Speaks 2021). Although such a process of comparing the consistency (or inconsistency) of two sentences is natural for humans, it is a much more arduous task to develop a computational method that can closely mimic the human process. For instance, as mentioned, DL, which produces an abstract representation of text to compare text similarity, suffers from an inability to distinguish syntactic and semantic information (Huang et al. 2021). Thus, unlike humans, DL may consider these two sentences to be more similar than they are supposed to be, which may lead to the incorrect inference of the claim’s veracity. In addition, it captures unnecessary information from texts because it takes account of every word in texts to produce their abstract representation.

On the other hand, SBT provides us with a good approach for sentence comparison, closely resembling the way humans capture consistency between $( \mathrm { i . e . }$ , warrant) two sentences. The neuropsychology literature contains a plethora of empirical evidence that humans process the syntax and semantics of a sentence distinctively (Dapretto and Bookheimer 1999). At the same time, both need to be considered collectively for fullsentence comprehension. Likewise, our SBT-grounded approach represents the syntax of two sentences as a word network and uses the semantics of the sentences to assign signs $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ polarity) to the network. Then, we measure the structural balance of the signed word network, which provides us with important information about the consistency between the two sentences.

Let us describe the process in detail with the illustration in Figure 2. In Figure 2, claim and evidence sentences are decomposed into subject-verb (V)-object triples. We will assume that the subjects of the two sentences $( \mathrm { i . e . }$ , claim and evidence sentences) are the same. Then, we derive a word network from these triples, having $s _ { 1 } , \ o _ { 1 } ,$ , and $o _ { 2 }$ as its nodes and $v _ { 1 } , \ v _ { 2 }$ , and a semantic relationship between $o _ { 1 }$ and $o _ { 2 }$ as its links. However, the links of the network do not yet have a polarity assigned to them. Thus, we transform this into a signed word network using ground-truth information already provided. Specifically, we have the ground truth for two of the links within the network (i.e., the links within the dashed boxes in Figure 2(1)). First, the link between $s _ { 1 }$ and $o _ { 2 }$ is derived from the evidence. Therefore, we can assume that the semantics of the link $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , v _ { 2 } )$ accurately defines the relationship between $s _ { 1 }$ and $o _ { 2 } .$ . Second, according to the distributional hypothesis in linguistics, we can express the link between $o _ { 1 }$ and $o _ { 2 }$ as their semantic closeness is empirically measured based on a large corpus, and we assume this reveals their ground-truth relationship (Harris 1954). On the other hand, we do not know the factual relationship between $s _ { 1 }$ and $o _ { 1 }$ because it is derived from a claim whose veracity has not yet been verified (Figure

2(2)). Still, we can infer the true relationship between $s _ { 1 }$ and $o _ { 1 }$ by transforming the network into a signed network and applying SBT to measure its structural balance. As in Figure 2(3), we convert the ground-truth relationship derived from the evidence sentence $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , v _ { 2 }$ connecting s<sub>1</sub> and $o _ { 2 } )$ as a positive link.<sup>2</sup> Next, we set the sign for the relationship between $o _ { 1 }$ and $o _ { 2 }$ based on their sematic closeness. If $o _ { 1 }$ and $o _ { 2 }$ are semantically close, we assign a positive relationship to it or otherwise, a negative relationship.

Then, depending on the polarity of the relationship between $s _ { 1 }$ and $o _ { 1 } \ ( \mathrm { i . e . , } v _ { 1 } ) _ { i }$ , the structural balance of the network is determined as balanced or imbalanced. That ${ \mathrm { i } } { \mathrm { s } } ,$ although we do not know the ground-truth sign of $v _ { 1 } ,$ we can set the sign by examining its semantic closeness to $v _ { 2 } . \mathrm { I f } v _ { 1 }$ and $v _ { 2 }$ are semantically close, v gets the same sign as $v _ { 2 }$ or otherwise, the opposite sign. In Figure 2(3), we assumed that $v _ { 1 }$ and $v _ { 2 }$ share simila semantics, so we set the sign of $v _ { 1 }$ as positive. Now, we have transformed the claim and evidence sentences into a signed word network and can measure its structural balance. In this case, the network is in an imbalanced state because it has an odd number of negative relationships. Based on the network’s structural state, we can determine the veracity of the relationship derived from the claim $( \mathrm { i . e . , } s _ { 1 } - v _ { 1 } - o _ { 1 }$ triple). That is, we argue that if the signed network presents a structurally balanced (or consistent) state, the claim sentence holds true information (and consequently, the relationship among the words in the claim is true), or otherwise, it is false.

Our argument aligns with the linguistic process of identifying consistency (equivalent to the warrant process of Toulmin’s model) between two sentences, described by the theory of reference (Speaks 2021). It claims that the predicate of a sentence can be considered as a function that outputs either a true or false value depending on its input, the subject of the sentence. Thus, a predicate should produce the same truth value (either true or false) when two words referring to an identical object are fed to it as an input. Likewise, when a word is fed as the subject of two predicates connoting the same meaning, it should produce a single truth value. In Figure 2(3), the two predicates derived from the claim and evidence sentences have the opposite semantics (because $o _ { 1 }$ and $o _ { 2 }$ refer to semantically distant objects, whereas $v _ { 1 }$ carries a similar meaning to $v _ { 2 } )$ . At the same time, they have the same input, $s _ { 1 }$ . Thus, if one of the two relationships carries true information, the other should include false information according to the theory of reference. Assuming the relationship derived from the evidence sentence $( \mathrm { i . e . , } s _ { 1 } - v _ { 2 } - o _ { 1 } )$ always holds true information and its veracity cannot be changed, that from the claim sentence $( \mathrm { i . e . , } s _ { 1 } - v _ { 1 } - o _ { 1 } )$ ) must be false. Based on this linguistic observation, we propose the following design principle for developing our automated method for FII.

In the next section, we describe how this principle can be implemented in practice. We expect that our approach, using the design metaphor of SBT to computationally represent the human argumentation process, will not only present performance improvement in FII but also, enable it to explain an AI decision-making process to human users more efficiently (Kim and Benbasat 2006).

## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. Problem Definition

In this section, we describe our answers to the two most relevant research questions in the field of automated FII. How can we build an FII system with (i) stronger performance and (ii) better explainability? Specifically, the problems to be solved are (i) to build a design artifact that correctly verifies whether a claim, given its corresponding evidence, is true or false and (ii) to effectively explain its decision-making process to human users. Formally, it is to identify and explain an optimal function, $f : ( c _ { i } , e _ { i } )  \{ 0 , 1 \}$ , where $c _ { i }$ and $e _ { i }$ are elements of a claim set $\mathsf { C } = \{ c _ { 1 } , \ c _ { 2 } , \ c _ { 3 } , \ . . . , \ c _ { m } \}$ and an evidence set $\textrm { E } = \{ e _ { 1 } , e _ { 2 } , e _ { 3 } , . . . , e _ { n } \}$ , respectively. Each element in the range of the function $f ,$ respectively, implies the following classification categories: 0 � true and 1 � false. In the following, we elaborate on the method developed based on our SBT-based design principle.

## 3.2. Generalizable False Information Detector

According to our proposed design principle, the veracity of a claim can be determined by building signed word networks from the claim and its evidence and examining their states of balance. We call our method, grounded in this principle, the generalizable false information detector (G-FINDER), as illustrated in Figure 3. It incorporates a process of creating input features that can be fed into any ML or DL model, and thus, it is method agnostic (i.e., generalizable to any method).

The method consists of three parts: (i) representing the claim and evidence in a latent space using a neural network-based language model (i.e., input 1); (ii) applying multiple natural language processing (NLP) techniques to build signed word networks and SBT to measure their levels of structural balance (i.e., input 2); and (iii) training an ML model with these inputs. Input 1 is the feature set broadly used in extant methods, which encodes the syntactic and semantic information of text in an entangled way as we have described in Section 1. Note that we use either word-embedding vectors derived from a matrix factorization-based language model or transformer language models for input 1. Specifically, for the baselines that require word-embedding vectors as input, we form input 1 with those derived from GloVe (Pennington et al. 2014). For the other baselines based on self attention mechanisms (e.g., BERT), we use their own embeddings as input (Devlin et al. 2019). This will be elaborated on later. Although the value of this input $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ input 1) has been validated in many prior studies, ML models may perform better if they are complemented by additional input that clearly distinguishes syntactic and semantic information. Thus, we propose input 2 on top of input 1.

The process of obtaining input 2 consists of multiple steps (i.e., decomposition, entity recognition, part-ofspeech (POS) tagging, network construction, and structural state determination). First, we decompose each sentence in a claim and its evidence into triples of named entity (NE), V, and noun (N; e-v-n triples) by applying the multiple NLP techniques shown in Figure 4. To elaborate, we first break down a sentence into multiple clauses (step 1). A sentence with multiple clauses $( { \mathrm { i . e . , ~ } } \{ c _ { 1 } , ~ c _ { 2 } , ~ . ~ . ~ . , ~ c _ { l } \} )$ is defined as one including multiple verb phrases that can be identified by the 48 regular expressions presented in Online Appendix $\mathrm { A } .$ Then, we tokenize each clause and create a set of words $( \mathrm { i . e . , ~ } \left\{ w _ { 1 } , w _ { 2 } , \ldots , w _ { n } \right\} )$ . The words are lemmatized using a package provided by ${ \mathrm { S p a c y } } ^ { 3 }$ (step 2). Lemmatization is a process of regularizing words and finding their base forms so that we can accurately compare word semantics. From the set of lemmatized words $( \mathrm { i . e . , \it ~ \left\{ l w _ { 1 } , \right. } $ $l w _ { 2 } , \ldots , l w _ { n } \} )$ , we extract three subsets, each of which consists of NEs, Vs, and ${ \mathrm { N s } } ,$ respectively, by using a combination of named entity recognition (NER) and POS tagging methods<sup>4</sup> (step 3). Note that pronouns are matched with their corresponding NEs using the method for coreference resolution proposed by Clark and Manning (2016). The cartesian product of NEs, Vs, and Ns creates a set consisting of ne-v-n triples, which is the product of the decomposition process (step 4 and output). We apply the same process iteratively to all clauses and sentences comprising a claim and its evidence, and consequently, we get two sets of ne-v-n triples: one for the claim (i.e., claim-triple set) and the other for the evidence (i.e., evidence-triple set). Note that we use state-of-the-art NLP systems to complete these tasks. For instance, the NER and POS tagging systems that we use are highly reliable, achieving accuracy of over 95% and 85% in their respective tasks (Spacy 2021). The coreference resolution method by Clark and Manning (2016) significantly out performs existing methods, achieving approximately 75% of the F1 score. Therefore, it can be argued that our pipeline for extracting triples from text is overall highly reliable.

To exemplify this process, let us consider a very simple case where both a claim and its evidence consist of a single sentence. We have a claim “The Beatles have been nominated for a music award” and its evidence “The Beatles and Louis Armstrong have won the 7th Grammy.” Applying the decomposition method yields two sets of triples: {(Beatles, nominate, music award)} for the claim and {(Beatles, win, 7th Grammy), (Louis Armstrong, win, 7th Grammy)} for the evidence.<sup>5</sup> After this decomposition process, we now apply our network-building strategy, illustrated in Figure 5, which reveals the struc tural relationship (i.e., balanced or imbalanced) between the claim and evidence text.

## 4. Experiment

We evaluated our method with two experiments. First, we examined the contribution of G-FINDER to model performance with multiple FII data sets with distinct characteristics. Then, we assessed the impact of its explanation mechanism (i.e., SBTX) on human users using various metrics used in the human-AI interactions literature. Next, we first report on model performance.

## 4.1. Experiment I: Model Performance in FII

4.1.1. Data. To evaluate the performance of our model in FII, we used two data sets: a real-world rumor detection data set collected from Twitter and a laboratory-created fact-checking data set generated by crowdsourced human workers using Wikipedia pages. For brevity, we, in the main body of this paper, only report on model performance on the Twitter data set because it more closely resembles real-world FII scenarios. The details of our evaluation of the Wikipedia data set can be found in Online Appendix C (in summary, with the Wikipedia data set, G-FINDER significantly improved the performance of extant models by 3.83%–21.71% in accuracy).

Our Twitter data set was generated and publicly distributed by PHEME, a research project for FII funded by the European Commission (Zubiaga et al. 2018). The data set contains 5,802 rumor candidates (i.e., tweets possibly containing unverified facts) on five breaking news incidents (see Online Appendix D for the details of these incidents). A group of professional journalists manually annotated the rumor candidates, resulting in 1,972 (34%) rumors and 3,830 (66%) nonrumors. The task objective was to classify whether each candidate included rumor or only factual contents (i.e., nonrumor). Although our method needs evidence to check the veracity of each claim, the PHEME data set did not include any information other than the rumor candidates themselves. Thus, we manually collected evidential data from online articles published by major news media companies (see Online Appendix $\mathrm { E } ) . ^ { 7 }$ Specifically, for each incident, we selected the earliest article published by one of the major news publishers and used it as evidence. This enabled us to test our method in the very early phase of rumor diffusion. For instance, for the Sydney siege case, we were able to identify rumors four hours after its onset.<sup>8</sup> Considering that previous studies claiming success in early rumor detection set their thresholds to be less conservative than ours (e.g., 20.47 hours on average by Chen et al. 2018), we argue that our selection criteria for evidence articles satisfy the definition of early rumor detection.

To fully automate the FII process, we needed a com putational approach to retrieve evidence as opposed to the manual evidence retrieval described. Although there is an independent stream of research that provides many useful methods for this process (Guo et al. 2020), we have built and tested our own automated evidence retrieval model in this study. Specifically, using an extant transformer-based language model, we have built and evaluated an ML model that distin guishes relevant documents (i.e., possible evidence) from irrelevant documents. The details of its architec ture and performance can be found in Online Appendix F.<sup>9</sup> According to the result, our evidence retrieval model produced a very strong performance (97.90% in the F1 score), thus indicating its potential for deployment in a fully automated fashion. However, the evaluation was conducted in a relatively simple setting, and in a real-world environment, the quality of the retrieved evidence might be irregular and often poor, which may affect the performance of FII. To this end, as a robustness check of our model, we introduced different levels of noise to evidence documents and examined how this affected model performance. The results showed that adding G-FINDER to extant methods significantly improved their robust ness against evidence with noise. The details of this robustness check can be found in Online Appendix F.

4.1.2. Baselines, Parameter Tuning, and Evaluation Metrics. The baselines range from simple ML to cuttingedge DL methods. To evaluate the performance of the proposed approach, for each baseline, we develop two models: one vanilla model and another applied with G-FINDER. Although the general application of G-FINDER is illustrated in Figure 3, minor customizations are needed because the architecture of each baseline is different. A detailed explanation of the model architectures and their customizations is given in Online Appendix G. We provide a brief summary of the baselines included in the experiment.

1. Riedel. Riedel et al. (2017) suggested, for the Fake News Challenge (FNC),<sup>10</sup> a perceptron-based model with an input vector concatenating the term frequency (TF) vector of a claim, the TF vector of evidence, and the cosine similarity between the term frequency-inverse document frequency vectors of the claim and the evidence. The algorithm has been reported to be one of the top performers in the FNC.

2. Multilayer bidirectional long short-term neural network (MBiLSTM). MLs with deep neural architectures, such as RNNs and LSTMs, have been used to capture rich information from sequential data, such as natural language. In this study, we built an MBiLSTM model for FII. In the first layer, we applied forward and backward LSTMs for claim and evidence text, respectively, thus creating four latent vector representations of text. Then, we concatenated these vectors and fed them as the input for the next layer of multilayer regular neural networks, which made a final prediction.

3. MBiLSTM with attention (attention). The attention mechanism is a neural network model that focuses on a specific part of input data to make an inference (Vaswani et al. 2017). Many researchers have validated its use in different settings. In this study, we applied the mechanism to provide an LSTM model with additional information.

4. Transformer. Although the attention mechanism was initially designed to be applied to RNNs, Vaswani et al. (2017) proposed a new approach, the transformer, which implements the attention mechanism in neural networks without sequential encoding. In this study, we tested three variations of the transformer known to produce state-of-the-art performance on multiple NLP tasks: BERT, RoBERTa, and ELECTRA (Devlin et al. 2019, Liu et al. 2019, Clark et al. 2020).

To optimize each model’s performance, we experimented with different hyperparameter value settings (e.g., the number of network layers, the size of the neurons for each layer, and optimizer types) using a nested crossvalidation method with four outer and inner folds, respectively. Its details are elucidated in Online Appendix H. The performance metrics for evaluation are defined as precision # of correctly classified rumor instances<sub># of instances predicted to be rumor</sub> , recall # of correctly classified rumor instances , F1 score, the area under the # of rumor instances ROC curve (AUC), and accuracy # of correctly classified instances

The best-performing model, ELECTRA + G-FINDER, enhanced the F1 score by 1.57%–31.74%.<sup>11</sup> In addition, when we compared each baseline with and without G-FINDER, we observed large performance improvements with G-FINDER. Specifically, in a $2 \times 2$ contingency table with four cells—a, b, c, and d—the test examined $H _ { 0 } \colon b = c$ and $H _ { 1 } \colon b \neq c$ (a: number of instances that both models predicted correctly; b: number of instances that only the vanilla + G-FINDER model predicted correctly; c: number of instances that only the vanilla predicted correctly; d: number of instances that both models predicted incorrectly). The results revealed statistically significant differences between the MBiLSTM and BERT pairs, which further support the utility of our design principle proposed in Section 2.3. Moreover, a robustness check conducted with an additional data set (i.e., the Wikipedia data set) strongly confirmed the suggested results, with all model pairs presenting statistically significant differences (refer to Table C1 of Online Appendix C).

Furthermore, along with its contribution to performance, we claim that our approach has several additional advantages. First, it requires the collection of only minimal types of information. For instance, Zhao et al. (2015) depends on people’s reaction to a tweet (e.g., retweets and replies) to determine whether the tweet is a rumor or not. This demands additional resources to collect, process, and analyze the reactions, which may result in a longer time to make a decision. Similarly, Zubiaga et al. (2016) uses not only textual features but also, users’ behavioral characteristics to identify rumor, which are hard to collect or may even be unavailable in some cases. Second, our model can detect rumors in their early stages of generation. For example, we used, for the Sydney siege case, an evidence news article published only four hours after the incident. Considering that most rumor-candidate tweets (i.e., 779 of 1,221) were posted after the first four-hour period, we argue that our method is suitable for near-real-time rumor detection. In contrast, there is a delay for other methods because they require additional information to be collected. Furthermore, they may not be able to make a decision when there are no resources available. For instance, in a practical setting, the model of Zhao et al. (2015) cannot classify tweets without any retweets and the model Zubiaga et al. (2016) is ineffective when an account is private and thus, when no additional information can be collected.

## 4.2. Experiment II: Model Explanation

Automated FII models are primarily employed in practice to aid human users (such as content moderators in social media firms or end users) (CNBC 2021), so it is important to understand how these models influence human behavior. It is essential to explain DL model results to end users and assess how the explanation influences the users (Shu et al. 2019). However, as mentioned, very few prior studies have systematically addressed this issue. To this end, we evaluate the impact of SBTX on various metrics important for human-AI interactions and compare it with the most widely recognized model explanation approach, ATTNX. Specifically, four crucial metrics are of great significance to us. First, we investigated the relationship between model explanation and task performance as previous studies showed some meaningful findings. For instance, Tsukuda and Goto (2020) experimented with whether an explainable recommender system in the music-streaming domain could influence listeners’ repeated song consumption and found a positive relationship between model explainability and the song consumption rate. Second, in the literature, task completion time was considered another important factor when examining the effectiveness of human-AI interactions. For example, Nourani et al. (2020) evaluated the impact of AI explanations on participants’ time to complete a video-querying task. In addition to these objective measures, previous studies examined human-AI interactions with subjective measures, such as trust in algorithms and confidence in decision making. For instance, Kizilcec (2016) investigated humans’ varying levels of trust in AI when their expectations for AI were violated, whereas Honeycutt et al. (2020) examined the impact of AI explanations on participants’ confidence in decision making on an image classification task.

To maintain the quality of responses, we filtered the participants to those who had qualified as masters on MTurk. After collecting the responses, we excluded 26 who took either less than 3 minutes or more than 15 minutes to complete. Of the 254 qualifying participants, 56% and 44% were male and female, respectively. They were diverse in their education level. A total of 36% had an education level below a bachelor’s degree, 54% had completed a bachelor’s degree, and 10% had a graduate degree. Meanwhile, in terms of age, a majority of the participants (92%) were of working age (between 25 and 64), whereas college-age and retirement-age participants made up a minority (i.e., 3% and 5%, respectively).

The participants were randomly assigned into one of the following groups: control, treatment I, treatment II, and treatment III. In the control group, the participants were given five FII questions (refer to Online Appendix J). For each question, claim and evidence text was pro vided with an AI recommendation on claim veracity. However, no model explanation was presented to the participants. In treatment group I, the participants were given the same questions and AI recommendations as the control group. In addition, they were provided with ATTNX as an explanation for the AI recommendations. ATTNX was presented as a word heat map that emphasized the importance of input words using varying levels of background color bright ness (refer to Figure 6(a)). In treatment group II, the participants were given the same set of questions and AI recommendations as the control group and treatment group I. Additionally, they were provided with a summary version of ATTNX (that is, ATTNX Light (also known as ATTNXL)) as an explanation. Unlike ATTNX, ATTNXL highlighted only the top 5 and top 10 important words from a claim and evidence, respectively, resulting in less complexity in interpreting the explanation (refer to Figure 6(b)). Comparing the impact of SBTX with both ATTNX and ATTNXL increases the robustness of our experiments because different visuali zation approaches affect individuals distinctively (Abbasi and Chen 2008). Lastly, in treatment group III, the participants were provided with the same questions and AI recommendations as the other groups but given SBTX. Specifically, when an AI’s verdict was true (i.e., the claim is true), we showed the highest five SBT scores along with their corresponding claim and evidence triples, which highlighted the parts that were the most consistent between the claim and evidence text. ## 5. Concluding Remarks and Future Directions

In this study, we have proposed a novel algorithm design for automated FII, which makes several valuable contributions. First, grounded in Toulmin’s model of argumentation, we drew upon an SBT metaphor to design and implement an automated FII model that produced stronger performance and better explanations to human users. Specifically, our model facilitates the warrant process of Toulmin’s model by clarifying the connection between a claim and its evidence (Verheij 2009). Although in NLP studies, many attempts have been made to apply social theories to develop methods to tackle various real-world problems (e.g., sensemaking online discourse) (Abbasi et al. 2018), our study is one of the first to apply linguistics and psychology theories to design an automated FII model. We expect our efforts to boost interdisciplinary work for solving NLPrelated real-world problems, especially from a social science perspective.

Second, our proposed framework addressed important methodological challenges of extant methods for FII. Specifically, our SBT-based approach processes the syntax and the semantics of text separately as humans do, which mitigates the weakness of neural networkbased language models (Dapretto and Bookheimer 1999). Our experiments revealed that the proposed method indeed outperformed cutting-edge baselines in multiple performance metrics. Furthermore, our method also facilitated the early detection of false information. The methods proposed by the prior studies that claimed to enable early rumor detection were in general stabilized after the first 20 hours of an incident, whereas our method could detect rumors as early as 4 hours after an incident (Chen et al. 2018).

Lastly, this study has an important practical implication. Considering the extremely large amount of information produced daily, it is almost impossible for humans to manually check every source of information. Social media companies currently employ a large number of content moderators, which incurs huge labor costs, ineffectiveness, and other ethical issues. In response, these companies are aggressively investing in AI-based content moderation technologies, including automated FII systems. Such automated FII systems should be reliable in terms of their performance (i.e., accuracy) and induce positive human-AI interactions because they are mainly used to support the decision-making process of human users. Our method satisfies both conditions in that it outperforms state-ofthe-art methods in accuracy and its model explanation approach has a positive impact on human-AI interactions.

This study, however, is not without limitations, and hence, it can be extended further in future studies. First, our FII approach demands the retrieval of proper evidence prior to making the verdict on a claim. That is, relevant evidence that either supports or refutes a claim must be fed as input to verify whether the claim is true or false. As mentioned, we partly resolved this issue by developing a DL-based evidence retrieval method and by testing the robustness of our approach with noisy evidence. Nonetheless, we agree that the whole stream of research on evidence retrieval can be considered and incorporated into our framework to enhance the outcomes. Second, our method needs addi tional data sets for validation. Although in this study, we used two different data sets to evaluate our approach, additional data sets may ensure generalizability. Third, recent developments in pretrained language models have made the production of false information frustratingly easy, and it will be interesting to explore how our approach works on this machine-generated false information. Fourth, our AI explainability experiment requires further validation. Because multiple forms and approaches of explanations (e.g., LIME, SHAP) can affect people differently, it is necessary to evaluate the impact of AI explanations drawing upon rigorous frameworks of human-computer interaction studies (Lundberg and Lee 2017). It would also be interesting to examine the change in humans’ attitudes toward AI by measuring trust and confidence for every question. This might reveal some interesting patterns of human-AI interaction regarding factors such as the correctness of AI recommendations, the veracity and topic of a claim, and so on. Lastly, our approach should also be applied to more complex cases. For instance, it would be interesting to explore whether our method can be applied to a setting where there is no ground-truth evidence. Additionally, a timeseries investigation of how our approach performs in a setting where evidence evolves over time would be another interesting avenue to extend our study.
