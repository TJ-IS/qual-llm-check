# Dynamic Bayesian Network–Based Product Recommendation Considering Consumers’ Multistage Shopping Journeys: A Marketing Funnel Perspective

Abstract. Recommender systems are widely used by online merchants to find the products that are likely to interest consumers, but existing dynamic methods still face challenges regarding diverse behaviors, variability in interest shifts, and the identification of psychological dynamics. Premised on the marketing funnel perspective to analyze consumer shopping journeys, this study proposes a novel machine learning approach for product recommendation, namely, multistage dynamic Bayesian network (MS-DBN), to model the generative processes of consumers’ interactive behaviors with products in light of stage transitions and interest shifts. This approach features a dynamic Bayesian network model to overcome the problem of diverse behaviors and extract generalizable regularity of consumers’ psychological dynamics, two latent layers to depict variability in consumers interest shifts across multiple stages, and the identification strategies that dynamically detect the invisible stages and interests of consumers. Extensive experiments on large-scale real-world data and comprehensive robustness checks manifest the superior performance of the proposed MS-DBN approach over baseline methods.

## 1. Introduction

On e-commerce platforms, recommender systems are extensively used by online merchants to surface products that appeal to consumers. Researchers have conducted studies to investigate the impact of recommendations on consumer shopping journeys and demonstrated that the likelihood of online conversions increases considerably when consumers are interested in and accept the displayed recommendations (Wiesel et al. 2011, Abhishek et al. 2012, Hoban and Bucklin 2015). Accordingly, such recommendations need to conform to consumer interest in order to attain as many accepted recommendations as possible and thus facilitate product sales implicitly.

The collaborative filtering stream of recommendation studies commonly specifies consumer interest in a product as a whole, models interest as a probabilistic distribution over the product-level set, and learns the latent interest directly from the visible behaviors (Linden et al.

2003, He et al. 2018, Koren and Bell 2021). In this regard, consumer interest can be referred to as the attitude/ tendency to respond to a product with some degree of favorableness (Ajzen 2008) and is an important determinant of consumers’ interactive behaviors with products (e.g., click, reclick, add-to-favorites, add-to-cart, and purchase), as indicated by well-established behavioral theories, such as theory of reasoned action (Shepherd et al. 1988) and theory of planned behavior (Ajzen 1991).

Traditionally, most design science literature on recommender systems focuses on generating recommendations for consumers whose interests are assumed to be static; that is, a consumer’s attitude/tendency toward a set of products remains unchanged (Adomavicius and Tuzhilin 2005). Such static efforts explore consumers historical behaviors regardless of time or sequence, neglecting dynamic patterns, and thus can hardly surface effective recommendations in time to meet shifting interests. In recent years, some methods have started to consider learning dynamic interests (Quadrana et al. 2018, Rabiu et al. 2020) to achieve higher recommendation accuracy. However, given consumers’ diverse behaviors and the variability in interest shifts during shopping journeys, as well as underlying psychological dynamics driving these interest shifts that are difficult to identify, there remain challenges calling for more innovative explorations.

The first challenge concerns diverse behaviors of consumers. In the shopping journey, a consumer’s exhibited sequence of interactive behaviors with products can be very diversified. That is, a consumer’s behavioral sequence does not follow a certain pattern and might be influenced by behavior scattering, exogenous factors, and divided attention (Batra and Keller 2016), leading to difficulties in the effective extraction of frequent and generalizable regularity at the behavior level. For instance, two observed behavioral sequences, such as “ … –click A–click B–click C–click D–reclick $\mathrm { C } - \dots ^ { \prime \prime }$ and “ … –click C–click E–click D–click B–addto-favorites $\mathrm { C } { \mathrm { - } } \dots \mathrm { } ^ { \prime \prime }$ , involve different interactions (e.g., click, reclick, add-to-favorites, … ) on various products $( \mathrm { e . g . , A , B , C , D , E , \ldots } )$ that take place in a nondeterministic and interleaving manner. In addition, regularity extraction often suffers from the sparsity issue in realworld consumer–product interactions (Sahoo et al. 2012, Quadrana et al. 2018), which is more serious in the long-tail product recommendation scenario. Nevertheless, it is worth noting that, although the two aforementioned behavioral sequences appear to be irregular and infrequent, they might share a same pattern at the psychological dynamics level, such as “compare B, C and D, then prefer C.” This latent pattern, reflecting a generalizable regularity underlying multiple diverse behavioral sequences, is crucial in facilitating recommendations because of the capture of deep psychological dynamics. Therefore, a key challenge in dynamic recommendation is digging through diverse behaviors and unearthing the latent patterns that reflect the psychological dynamics in consumer shopping journeys.

The second challenge concerns variability in interest shifts. Driven by psychological dynamics, consumers interests in products may shift over time and result in highly diversified behaviors (Jansen and Schuster 2011). For instance, consumers may start with interests in certain products, but as the shopping journeys progress, consumers continuously inspect and compare the products and shift interests. Moreover, because of different psychological dynamics, consumers’ interest shifts greatly vary in form (Court et al. 2009, Mulpuru 2011, Goldstein and Hajaj 2022). For example, consumers may gradually concentrate interests on some products, expand interests to a more diverse range of products, or shift interests to other products. Correspondingly, consumer behaviors may be manifested as repeated clicks/add-tofavorites/ purchases on a small product set, clicking to browse more diversified products, or switching to interact with other products. If the variability in interest shifts is not captured, the prediction and thus product recommendation may not be effectively inferred. Some existing methods have considered interest shift from various angles (Liu et al. 2010, Sahoo et al. 2012), but its variability issue has not been well addressed. In addition, the variability in interest shifts leads to a high variability in time duration, interaction frequency, and the number of behaviors in consumer shopping journeys, such as staying long in comparing candidate products but quickly choosing a certain product. Because most dynamic recommendation methods do not analyze consumer shopping journeys from the perspective of psychological dynamics and variability in interest shifts, but commonly segment periods according to a preconfigured time duration or a fixed number of behaviors (Dunlavy et al. 2011, Sahoo et al. 2012), which hinder the flexibility and accuracy of dynamic pattern extraction (Montgomery et al. 2004). Thus, the challenge is how to model consumer shopping journeys by appropriately incorporating the variability in interest shifts to achieve a more reasonable and flexible segmentation from the psychological dynamics viewpoint and generate more accu rate recommendations.

The third challenge concerns the implicit nature of consumers’ psychological dynamics (e.g., psychological stages) that drive the interest shifts. Because the implicit psychological dynamics are difficult to identify, most existing dynamic recommendation methods only predict which products consumers will interact with next time. However, real-world managerial decisions usually require the effective identification of consumers’ psychological dynamics to attain deeper consumer insights and facilitate more sophisticated services (Batra and Keller 2016, Sun et al. 2021, Goldstein and Hajaj 2022). For example, suppose that both consumer a and consumer b are predicted to interact with a certain product at the next moment, in which consumer a is identified to stay at the stage of diversified interests and consumer b at the stage of converged interests. Thus, offering a price discount might yield a higher promotion effect for consumer b than for consumer a because consumer b is at a psychological stage closer to purchase and has higher conversion potentials (Batra and Keller 2016, Zhang et al. 2019). Existing dynamic methods, especially black box methods with complex model structures and massive parameter tuning (Hidasi et al. 2015; Quadrana et al. 2017), could hardly realize the identification of psycho logical dynamics simultaneously with recommendation and thus have limitations in supporting managerial decision making. Therefore, the challenge is to effectively model consumer shopping journeys with a relatively concise structure, which not only ensures high accuracy of product recommendation but also makes visible consumers’ implicit psychological dynamics by sophisticated identification strategies.

To tackle these challenges encountered by extant methods, effective product recommendation needs to incorporate and identify the psychological dynamics of consumers for capturing of the interest shifts and behavioral sequences in shopping journeys. The marketing literature provides an established perspective for understanding consumer shopping journeys with psychological dynamics, which has been recognized as stages through the metaphor of a funnel (Howard and Sheth 1969, Court et al. 2009, Mulpuru 2011). Generally speaking, a shopping journey consists of transitive psychological stages (e.g., awareness, consideration, preference, and action) where consumers gradually shift interests, which further drive the interactive behaviors with products until reaching the final purchases (Jansen and Schuster 2011, Zhang et al. 2020, Goldstein and Hajaj 2022). Concretely, consumers usually start with a number of potential candidates in mind for a product category (e.g., showing a diversified interest distribution on products at the wide end of the funnel) (Goldstein and Hajaj 2022). After methodically shifting interests over time, consumers finally arrive at the end with one or a few products chosen to take action on (e.g., showing a converged interest) (Court et al. 2009). In online shopping, consumers are easily influenced by exogenous factors, including reviews, ads, and social interactions, which make shopping journeys not necessarily linear (Bellman et al. 1999, Batra and Keller 2016, Lemon and Verhoef 2016). Consumers may come in at any stage of the funnel, often jump to other stages, stay at a stage indefinitely, or move back and forth between stages, with some probabilities (Bonchek and France 2014). Although it is considered difficult to identify consumers” invisible psychological stages, the marketing funnel perspective is substantially important to merchants; it describes the corresponding tactics a salesperson can conduct to reduce consumer leakage by agilely touching consumers’ shifting interests at different stages with appropriate strategies to lead consumers to move through the funnel and finally close a potential deal (Edelman 2010).

Figure 1 provides an illustrative example from real world data to demonstrate a consumer’s shopping journey on headphones and how psychological dynamics informed by the marketing funnel perspective facilitate product recommendation. When the consumer comes with a rough shopping plan for headphones, the journey might first be at the awareness stage. Trying to widely collect information about more candidate products, the consumer generates many clicks on a diverse set of headphones, including A, B, and C. At this stage, the consumer’s interest is distributed diversely on the products and may easily shift, so an effective recommendation is to help further broaden the awareness set with headphones of other brands, prices, or colors (e.g., product E), which the consumer might potentially be interested in. After some time of search and comparison, as the shopping journey moves down the funnel to the preference stage, the consumer repeatedly clicks on or adds-to-favorites a few similar products such as C and D, indicating that the consumer’s invisible interest may converge more to headphones with brand ”jbl” and price around \$39–\$46. With a stronger purchase propensity, the consumer may possibly think only about headphones meeting the clearer preference, imply ing an interest shift different from the awareness stage. For targeting the potential demands, recommendations like product F that features similarly to C and D might be particularly effective at this stage. Therefore, by modeling the stage transitions and their effects on interest shifts that motivate behaviors, a recommendation approach could effectively predict the consumer’s sequential interactions with products.

Enlightened by the marketing funnel perspective, this study endeavors to design a multistage dynamic Bayesian network–based approach (MS-DBN) for product recommendation. To the best of our knowledge, this is a novel attempt to model consumers’ interest shifts premised on psychological stage transitions to achieve stageaware recommendation in the online shopping context. The main contributions of this work are threefold.

First, to tackle the challenge of consumers’ diverse behaviors, the proposed approach constructs a DBN model to extract generalizable regularity with regard to psychological dynamics in consumer shopping journeys. With Bayesian inference, for the previously mentioned example, a latent pattern in terms of psychological stages can be detected, such as “awareness (of B, C, and D)→ preference (on C).” Therefore, product C may be recommended to a new consumer who has clicks on B, C, and D, even if the clicks are sequentially disordered or scattered with other behavioral interactions, because the consumer’s psychological dynamics are similar to those of the two learned consumers, that is, potentially showing strong inclination on C. In this way, MS-DBN can effectively learn the frequent latent patterns from the shopping journeys, modeled in light of the funnel, with diverse behaviors, to provide accurate recommendations.

Second, to tackle the challenge of variability in interest shifts, this study considers such shifts and the behavioral sequences that change along with consumers’ psychological stages. Concretely, two latent layers, from stages to interests, are included in the model to accommodate the variability in interest shifts. Through psychological stages, the proposed approach incorporates the implicit aggregation of interests and the observable behaviors to derive the self-learned segmentation of the changeable journeys and uneven psychological dynamics from consumers’ behavioral sequences, that is, flexible segmentation. Therefore, MS-DBN subtly captures consumers dynamic stage-interest-behavior patterns, facilitating effective recommendations.

Third, to tackle the challenge of consumers’ implicit psychological dynamics, this study incorporates the identification strategies of latent layers into the proposed approach, which achieves a relatively concise integration of variables. Informed by the differences in consumers’ purchase propensity and interest convergency at distinct funnel stages, this study designs a quantitative metric (i.e., Kurtosis) and sets two constraints on the conditional probabilities (i.e., nondecreasing purchase probabilities and interest Kurtosis as the stages proceed) in the model. Thus, MS-DBN not only outputs product recommendations but also identifies latent stages and interests, which provide valuable insights into consumer shopping journeys for supporting targeted marketing.

## 2. Related Work

This study grounds mainly in two streams of related work: the methodological knowledge base about dynamic recommendation methods and the theoretical knowledge base about the marketing funnel perspective, which jointly motivate and guide the approach design.

## 2.1. Dynamic Recommendation Methods

In line with collaborative filtering (CF) recommendation (Breese et al. 1998, Linden et al. 2003), consumer interest in a product can be specified as a whole and learned from observable behaviors. Many existing recommendation methods are based on a tacit assumption that consumer interest follows a static pattern. This assumption is rather strict because in the shopping journey, a consumer’s inclination toward products may evolve over time (Sahoo et al. 2012, Rabiu et al. 2020). As online platforms can record consumers’ finer-grained behaviors in longer periods, some research work gives rise to dynamic recommendation methods considering time or sequence factors, aiming to surface product recommen dations in alignment with consumers’ shifting interests.

One type of dynamic product recommendation adopts time window or time decay. These methods assume that recent behaviors are more relevant to consumers’ current interests than past behaviors (Dunlavy et al. 2011). For simplicity, some methods consider only historical records within a predefined time window, such as the temporal graph method that constructs a bipartite graph with time nodes (Xiang et al. 2010). Some methods further weigh the windows by estimating their relevance. For example, time-weighted CF applies time decay factors in item-based CF (Ding and Li 2005, Liu et al. 2010). Other methods attempt to learn the behaviors that contribute more to consumer interest by the model itself and to adjust their weights in recommendation with attention mechanisms, such as the deep interest network (DIN) (Zhou et al. 2019a).

Another line of efforts develops time-related variables for machine learning models. Methods in this line regard time as an extra factor influencing consumer interest, so temporal factors can be defined as additional variables (Campos et al. 2014). Discriminant models with the tree structure are suitable for this, such as naive Bayes tree (Bogina et al. 2016) and extreme gradient boosting (XG-Boost) (Chen and Guestrin 2016), which may add temporal variables to the classification. Moreover, based on existing static methods, time-related biases can be defined to describe consumers’ interest shifts, such as temporal matrix factorization (TMF) (Xiang and Yang 2009, Dror et al. 2011). Alternatively, embedded with an implicit vector about time, such as the Katz-collapsed weighted tensor method (Katz-CWT) (Dunlavy et al. 2011), consumers’ interactive behaviors over a period can be learned and predicted through a third-order tensor decomposition.

In addition, there exist dynamic recommendation methods based on sequence learning, that is, modeling consumers’ behavioral sequences to extract patterns. By describing the shifting interests in products with some latent states, these methods may capture some common patterns of the consumer group and track the heterogeneous shopping journeys of individual consumers, resulting in general performance advantages over other ways of involving time dynamics (Quadrana et al. 2018). For sequence learning, DBNs, such as the hidden Markov model (HMM) (Sahoo et al. 2012) and the hidden semi-Markov model (HSMM) (Zhang et al. 2018), are commonly used types of models, in which a consumer shopping journey is described as a stochastic Markov process over discrete random states. To learn the underlying state transitions from multiple processes of observations, existing studies also add a symmetric latent layer to HMM with either parallel (i.e., linked HMM (LHMM)) (Benmansour et al. 2017) or crossover (i.e., coupled HMM (CHMM)) (Brand 1997) dependencies. Furthermore, some methods may capture consumer interest in a latent Markov decision process (MDP) by reinforcement learning (Shani et al. 2005) or model the complex temporal dependencies in consumer behaviors with deep recurrent neural networks (RNNs) to memorize the influence of each past record (Hidasi et al. 2015, Quadrana et al. 2017).

Although these recommendation methods use various treatments to capture the dynamics, there are still gaps in terms of diverse behaviors, variability in interest shifts, and identification of psychological dynamics, as summarized in Table A1 of Online Appendix A. In brief, first, many existing methods build models purely on observable behavior records (e.g., temporal graph, timeweighted CF, naive Bayes tree, and XGBoost) and are limited in dealing with diverse behaviors. Although some methods adopt latent embeddings/vectors to capture the underlying relations between consumers and products (e.g., DIN, TMF, Katz-CWT, and RNN), few frequent and generalizable patterns inherent in the diverse behaviors are learned or summarized. Second, without fully considering the variability in interest shifts, many research attempts assume an unchanged shifting pattern of consumer interest (e.g., time-weighted CF, HMM, HSMM, and MDP) and/or segment the behavioral sequences into fixed time periods (e.g., temporal graph, TMF, Katz-CWT, HMM, and HSMM), which may hurt performance. Some methods automatically learn the variability in consumer behaviors through flexible model structures (e.g., naive Bayes tree and XGBoost) or abundant parameters/weights (e.g., DIN, LHMM, CHMM, and RNN), but do not take interest shifting mechanisms into account. Third, most existing methods do not achieve the identification of consumers’ psychological dynamics, which is desirable and meaningful. Concretely, these methods derive only patterns on behaviors (e.g., temporal graph, time-weighted CF, naive Bayes tree, and XGBoost), output some latent vectors/ states without explicit identification (e.g., TMF, Katz-CWT, HMM, HSMM, LHMM, CHMM, and MDP), or deploy multiple layers that are difficult to interpret in complicated nonlinear models (e.g., DIN and RNN), hampering their managerial implications.

## 2.2. Marketing Funnel Perspective

Marketing funnel has been an important perspective in marketing analytics, delineating a set of psychological stages that a consumer goes through in the shopping journey, such as awareness, consideration, preference, and action (Howard and Sheth 1969, Court et al. 2009, Mulpuru 2011). A consumer may initially be disengaged when being unaware of a product. After being exposed to the market, the consumer may be aware of a product category, contemplate potential choices, come to a stage with certain preferences, and finally take the purchase action on one or a few products. In the real world, especially for online shopping that is nowadays open and tightly coupled with many other information systems, consumers are easily influenced by various factors (Batra and Keller 2016). Consequently, consu mers’ shopping processes through the funnel appear as dynamically networked stage transitions. For instance, recommendations from friends often lead consumers to jump stages or move back for the exploration of alternatives (Bonchek and France 2014), time-starved consumers usually produce only a few clicks before purchases without overall consideration (Bellman et al. 1999), and cross-channel shopping causes consumers’ exhibited journeys within one channel to jump arbitrarily among the stages (Lemon and Verhoef 2016).

Research on the marketing funnel in e-commerce mainly focuses on investigating the impacts of exogenous information imposed to consumers, such as decomposing the advertising impacts into driving consumers toward the preference stage and the action stage (Hu et al. 2014), teasing out the effects of displaying recommendations and search ads on conversions at different stages (Abhishek et al. 2012), and exploring the differentiated effects of word-of-mouth informa tion at each stage (Colicev et al. 2019, Huang et al. 2019). Existing studies have also focused on the behavioral distinctions of consumers across funnel stages. In particular, e-commerce platforms usually provide a variety of tools to support buying decisions, and con sumers may interact with products in various behavior types, including but not limited to click, reclick, addto-favorites, and add-to-cart. Research suggests that consumers do not produce all types of behaviors evenly through the funnel, but tend to carry out certain types with different probabilities (Zhou et al. 2019b);

interactions would generally deepen as stages proceed (Abhishek et al. 2012). For example, at the awareness stage, the majority of consumer behaviors may correspond to initial exploration, such as (explorative) clicks, whereas in-depth interactive behavior types such as add-to-favorites and add-to-cart are more probable to occur at the consideration and preference stages (Huang et al. 2019), in which the potential of purchases is higher (Zhou et al. 2019b). Here, a purchase behavior intuitively implies the consumer’s psychological action stage at the end of the shopping journey (Abhishek et al. 2012, Todri et al. 2020). In existing studies of shopping journeys, the nondecreasing nature of purchase/action propensity as consumers move down the funnel is often referred to to identify the invisible psychological stages (Zhang et al. 2019, Todri et al. 2020).

For product recommendation, because consumers not only change the awareness or consideration sets of products (Lee and Hosanagar 2021) but also take actions of conversion when accepting the displayed recommendations (Wiesel et al. 2011, Abhishek et al. 2012, Hoban and Bucklin 2015), such recommendations are supposed to match consumers’ shifting interests at different stages and trigger consumers to move down the funnel until the final purchase. Inspired by well-established behavioral theories, such as theory of reasoned action (Shepherd et al. 1988) and theory of planned behavior (Ajzen 1991), consumer interest can be referred to as the attitude/ tendency to respond to a product with some degree of favorableness. As an important determinant of behavior, interest essentially motivates consumers’ observed interactions with products in online shopping (Howard and Sheth 1969, Jansen and Schuster 2011), often in a latent and hybrid manner because of subjectivity, uncertainty, and cognitive limitations (Edwards and Fasolo 2001, Ajzen 2008). Thus, a common treatment in recommendation studies is to model consumer interest as a probabilistic distribution over the product set (He et al. 2018, Koren and Bell 2021), whose shifting patterns can be further captured by the dynamic methods (Rabiu et al. 2020). In practical shopping journeys, the interest shifts of consumers are very complex and varying (Lam and Mostafa 2001), that is, showing variability in interest shifts, and the distinct psychological stages can act as underlying drivers that bring about different patterns of interest shifts (Court et al. 2009, Mulpuru 2011, Goldstein and Hajaj 2022). In this regard, the marketing funnel can be considered a psychological process in which consumers gradually converge the latent interests in products (Court et al. 2009). When experiencing the awareness stage, consumers may have only general needs but no clear inclinations in mind, so the interests can be quite diversely distributed and easily shifted over a wide range of products (Goldstein and Haja

2022). As the shopping journey moves on to the successive stages (e.g., preference), consumers’ interests are usually much less shifted and more likely to focus on a small group of similar products, that is, converged interests (Mulpuru 2011).

Research efforts show that it is important to determine consumers’ psychological stages in order to better target consumers using personalized marketing strategies (Batra and Keller 2016, Goldstein and Hajaj 2022), such as emphasizing display ads for earlier stages and price discounts for later stages (Abhishek et al. 2012, Zhang et al. 2019). Most work conducts marketing funne research mainly from the viewpoint of exogenous impacts and behavioral distinctions. By contrast, this study proposes that it is crucial to design a stage-aware recommendation approach premised on shifting interests throughout the funnel. First, the marketing funnel perspective, as established prior knowledge, would assist the recommendation approach to effectively extract generalizable regularity in psychological dynamics from consumers’ diverse behaviors. Second, such the oretical insights can enlighten the integrated modeling of the invisible stages and interests, as well as the observ able behavior types and products, to subtly capture the variability in shopping journeys. Third, inspired by the funnel perspective, consumers’ psychological dynamics (e.g., stages) and interests can be reasonably identified. In doing so, the approach would provide not only accurate dynamic recommendations but also managerial insights into consumer shopping journeys.

## 3. MS-DBN Approach for Product Recommendation

In this study, a novel MS-DBN approach is proposed, guided by the marketing funnel perspective, for stage-aware product recommendation. The framework is shown in Figure 2. As sketched, the approach first preprocesses consumers’ behavior records on the e-commerce platform and separates shopping journeys to obtain behavioral sequences as a training set and a test set. Then, the constructed DBN model is trained using the adapted means of expectation maximization (EM) and maximum-a-posteriori (MAP) estimations, along with the designed identification strategies to constrain the learning process, through which consumers psychological stage transitions and latent interest shifts are captured. After that, the learned parameters of the DBN are leveraged using the adapted Viterbi-like inference procedure to decode consumers’ invisible stages and interests over the shopping journeys in the test set (i.e., the dotted rounded rectangle), which are finally used by the proposed probabilistic prediction procedure to customize product recommendations for consumers (i.e., the solid rounded rectangle).

In the temporal dimension, the DBN essentially describes consumers’ psychological dynamics and sequential behaviors over time. First, the model captures the temporal dependencies between consumers psychological/funnel stages, that is, $P ( s _ { j } ^ { n + 1 } | s _ { j } ^ { n } )$ . Concretely, the successive stages could be from $s _ { j } ^ { n }$ to $\forall s _ { j } ^ { n + 1 }$ $\in S ,$ considering the networked stage transitions in shopping journeys. Second, consumers’ interest shifts along with the transitive stages are further modeled, that is, shifting from $i _ { j } ^ { n }$ to $i _ { j } ^ { n + 1 }$ within set I. Third, the sequential observations of consumer behaviors, that ${ \mathrm { i } } \mathbf { s } ,$ $( p _ { j } ^ { n } , b _ { j } ^ { n } )$ , are also integrated into the model. To accurately depict the variability in consumer shopping journeys resulting from psychological dynamics, the proposed approach does not preconfigure a fixed time length or number of behaviors as periodic segments, but it retains the complete sequential relations in behavior records as the model input. Through the selflearned segmentation of consumers’ dynamic stages, interests, and behaviors, the model can accomplish the flexible capture of the changeable and uneven shopping journeys.

In the generative dimension, the model depicts in depth how consumers’ observed behaviors are generated from psychological dynamics. On the one hand, following the insights that consumers generate deeper product interactions as the funnel stages proceed, the distinct psychological stages are modeled to result in different probabilities of behavior types, that ${ \mathrm { i } } { \mathrm { s } } ,$ $P ( b _ { j } ^ { n } | s _ { j } ^ { n } )$ . Unlike purchase that corresponds directly to the action stage, the probabilities of other behavior types conditional on each stage are to be learned, that is, the stage-dependent learning of behavior types. For the example in Figure 1, consumers at the awareness stage are more probable to generate clicks, whereas at the preference stage, consumers are more likely to interact with the products by other behavior types, such as reclick and add-to-favorites. On the other hand, consumers’ psychological stages act on the shifting patterns of invisible interests, which further determine the products that consumers interact with. The marketing funnel describes consumers’ interest convergence as the psychological stages proceed, which guides MS-DBN to model the stage’s effect on interest shift $\mathrm { ( i . e . , ~ } P ( i _ { j } ^ { n + 1 } |$ $i _ { j } ^ { n } , s _ { j } ^ { n + 1 } ) \}$ ), that is, the stage-dependent learning of interest shifts. In line with the common manipulation in CF studies, the latent interest is modeled as a probabilistic distribution over the product set and the determinant of consumers’ observable interactions with products.

Accordingly, MS- DBN models the interest’s effect on the product with which the consumer interact (i.e., $P ( p _ { j } ^ { n } | i _ { j } ^ { n } ) )$ . Captured by the proposed DBN, consumers interests in products do not necessarily shift in a certain pattern but may exhibit great variability that can be analyzed from a multistage viewpoint. As indicated by the example in Figure 1, consumer interest at the awareness stage is generally distributed diversely on the products and easily shifted, which further results in the interactions with various diversified pro ducts, such as A, B, and C. By contrast, as consumers proceed to the preference stage, the interests may become more converged and less shifted, leading to the repeated interactions with a few similar products, such as C and D.

Therefore, the generation process of a consumer shopping journey described by the DBN can be formalized as follows. The consumer’s shopping journey j is initially at stage $s _ { j } ^ { 1 } ,$ which can be drawn from a multinomial distri bution of initial psychological stages, that is, $s _ { j } ^ { 1 } \sim P ( s _ { j } ^ { 1 } )$ Then, the initial stage generates the initial interest from a multinomial distribution of all interest distributions, that is, $i _ { j } ^ { 1 } \sim P ( i _ { j } ^ { 1 } | s _ { j } ^ { 1 } )$ . Next, the consumer’s initial stage and interest in journey j generate the initial interactive behavior from the respective multinomial distributions over all the behavior types and products, that is, $b _ { j } ^ { 1 } \sim P ( b _ { j } ^ { 1 } | s _ { j } ^ { 1 } )$ and $p _ { j } ^ { 1 } \sim P ( p _ { j } ^ { 1 } | i _ { j } ^ { 1 } )$ . When journey j moves from behavior n to behavior $_ { n + 1 }$ , there are three subprocesses to go through. First, the stage for behavior n generates the stage for behavior n + 1 from a multinomial distribution, that is, $s _ { j } ^ { n + 1 } \sim P ( s _ { j } ^ { n + 1 } | s _ { j } ^ { n } )$ . Second, both the stage for behavior n+ 1 and the interest for behavior n in journey j jointly generate the consumer’s interest for behavior $n + 1$ from a multinomial distribution, that ${ \mathrm { i } } s ,$ $i _ { j } ^ { n + 1 } \sim P ( i _ { j } ^ { n + 1 } | i _ { j } ^ { n } , s _ { j } ^ { n + 1 } )$ . Third, the consumer’s stage and interest for behavior $n + 1$ generate the type and the interacted product of behavior $n + 1$ in the same manner as that for the initial behavior.

## 3.2. Parameter Estimation

Together with the novelly designed modeling of consumer shopping journeys, the proposed approach adapts the parameter estimation mathematics for the DBN in Figure 3. Given all the consumers’ historical behavior records in all shopping journeys for training, the log likelihood function of the proposed model is constructed as follows, where Θ refers to all the parameters in the model, and $p _ { j }$ and $b _ { j }$ refer to all the observations of journey j.

$$
l (\Theta) = \sum_ {j} \ln P (p _ {j}, b _ {j}; \Theta) = \sum_ {j} \ln \sum_ {s _ {j}} \sum_ {i _ {j}} P (p _ {j}, b _ {j}, s _ {j}, i _ {j}; \Theta)\tag{1}
$$

Owing to the latent variables in the problem, the parameters are estimated using the expectation maximization (EM) method, which is an iterative method to find the maximum likelihood estimates of parameters in statistical models that depend on unobserved latent variables (Dempster et al. 1977). In EM, the parameters are optimized via two alternating steps:

(1) The expectation step (E-step) performs inference on the latent variables given the observations and the current estimates of the parameters, denoted as $P ( s _ { j } , i _ { j } | p _ { j } , b _ { j } ; \Theta )$ .

(2) The maximization step (M-step) maximizes the expected lower bound of the log likelihood of the parameters.

$$
\begin{array}{l} Q (\Theta , \Theta^ {o l d}) \\ = \sum_ {j} \sum_ {s _ {j}} \sum_ {i _ {j}} P (s _ {j}, i _ {j} | p _ {j}, b _ {j}; \Theta^ {o l d}) \ln P (p _ {j}, b _ {j}, s _ {j}, i _ {j} | \Theta) \leq l (\Theta) \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

A proper estimation of the parameters can be obtained by iterating between the E-step and the M-step until the log likelihood is converged. According to the structure and the independencies in the DBN, $\check { P ( p _ { j } , b _ { j } , s _ { j } , i _ { j } | \Theta ) }$ can be expressed as (3).

$$
\begin{array}{l} P (p _ {j}, b _ {j}, s _ {j}, i _ {j} | \Theta) = P (s _ {j} ^ {1}) P (i _ {j} ^ {1} | s _ {j} ^ {1}) \\ \prod_ {n = 1} ^ {N _ {j} - 1} P (s _ {j} ^ {n + 1} | s _ {j} ^ {n}) \prod_ {n = 1} ^ {N _ {j} - 1} P (i _ {j} ^ {n + 1} | i _ {j} ^ {n}, s _ {j} ^ {n + 1}) \\ \prod_ {n = 1} ^ {N _ {j}} P (b _ {j} ^ {n} | s _ {j} ^ {n}) \prod_ {n = 1} ^ {N _ {j}} P (p _ {j} ^ {n} | i _ {j} ^ {n}) \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

After inserting (3) into (2) and simplifying, the expected log likelihood of the parameters decomposes into a sum of six components with respect to the six types of parameters in the model.

$$
\begin{array}{l} Q (\Theta , \Theta^ {o l d}) \\ = \sum_ {j} \sum_ {s _ {j} ^ {1}} P (s _ {j} ^ {1} | p _ {j}, b _ {j}; \Theta^ {o l d}) \ln P (s _ {j} ^ {1}) \\ + \sum_ {j} \sum_ {s _ {j} ^ {1}} \sum_ {i _ {j} ^ {1}} P (s _ {j} ^ {1}, i _ {j} ^ {1} | p _ {j}, b _ {j}; \Theta^ {o l d}) \ln P (i _ {j} ^ {1} | s _ {j} ^ {1}) \\ + \sum_ {j} \sum_ {n = 1} ^ {N _ {j} - 1} \sum_ {s _ {j} ^ {n}} \sum_ {s _ {j} ^ {n + 1}} P (s _ {j} ^ {n}, s _ {j} ^ {n + 1} | p _ {j}, b _ {j}; \Theta^ {o l d}) \ln P (s _ {j} ^ {n + 1} | s _ {j} ^ {n}) \\ + \sum_ {j} \sum_ {n = 1} ^ {N _ {j} - 1} \sum_ {i _ {j} ^ {n}} \sum_ {s _ {j} ^ {n + 1}} \sum_ {i _ {j} ^ {n + 1}} P (i _ {j} ^ {n}, s _ {j} ^ {n + 1}, i _ {j} ^ {n + 1} | p _ {j}, b _ {j}; \Theta^ {o l d}) \\ \ln P (i _ {j} ^ {n + 1} | i _ {j} ^ {n}, s _ {j} ^ {n + 1}) \\ + \sum_ {j} \sum_ {n = 1} ^ {N _ {j}} \sum_ {s _ {j} ^ {n}} P (s _ {j} ^ {n} | p _ {j}, b _ {j}; \Theta^ {o l d}) \ln P (b _ {j} ^ {n} | s _ {j} ^ {n}) \\ + \sum_ {j} \sum_ {n = 1} ^ {N _ {j}} \sum_ {i _ {j} ^ {n}} P (i _ {j} ^ {n} | p _ {j}, b _ {j}; \Theta^ {o l d}) \ln P (p _ {j} ^ {n} | i _ {j} ^ {n}) \end{array} \tag {4}
$$

Here, two expressions $\alpha ( s _ { j } ^ { n } , i _ { j } ^ { n } )$ and $\beta ( s _ { j } ^ { n } , i _ { j } ^ { n } )$ are defined in a recursive manner.

$$

## 3.3. Inference and Product Recommendation

After all the parameters in the DBN are learned, when a new shopping journey j arrives with historical records of interactive behaviors 1 to $N _ { j } - 1$ , the task is to recommend products to the consumer for behavior $N _ { j }$ by inferring the corresponding psychological dynamics. Therefore, a Viterbi-like inference procedure is first designed to detect the consumer’s most probable stages and interests from behaviors 1 to $N _ { j } - 1$ . Then, a prediction procedure is proposed to create a personalized product recommendation list for the consumer in the moment behavior $N _ { j }$ occurs.

The aim of the Viterbi-like inference procedure is to infer the consumer’s stages and interests from behaviors 1 to $N _ { j } - 1$ in journey j with the maximum probability.

$$
\begin{array}{l} \hat {s _ {j}} ^ {1: N _ {j} - 1}, \hat {i _ {j}} ^ {1: N _ {j} - 1} \\ = \underset {s _ {j} ^ {1: N _ {j} - 1}, i _ {j} ^ {1: N _ {j} - 1}} {\arg \max} P \Big (p _ {j} ^ {1: N _ {j} - 1}, b _ {j} ^ {1: N _ {j} - 1}, s _ {j} ^ {1: N _ {j} - 1}, i _ {j} ^ {1: N _ {j} - 1} | \Theta \Big) \end{array}\tag{16}
$$

Let $\delta _ { j k u } ^ { n }$ be the maximum probability for the consumer’s behavior n to be at stage k with interest u and observation $( p _ { j } ^ { n } , b _ { j } ^ { n } )$ ) in shopping journey j.

For $n = 1 , \ \delta _ { j k u } ^ { 1 } = P ( s _ { j } ^ { 1 } = k , i _ { j } ^ { 1 } = u , p _ { j } ^ { 1 } , b _ { j } ^ { 1 } \left| \Theta \right. ) = P ( s _ { j } ^ { 1 } = k ) P$ $( i _ { j } ^ { 1 } = u | s _ { j } ^ { 1 } = k ) P ( b _ { j } ^ { 1 } | s _ { j } ^ { 1 } = k ) P ( p _ { j } ^ { 1 } | i _ { j } ^ { 1 } = u ) .$

For $\begin{array} { r } { n = 2 , \delta _ { j k u } ^ { 2 } = P ( b _ { j } ^ { 2 } | s _ { j } ^ { 2 } = k ) P ( p _ { j } ^ { 2 } | i _ { j } ^ { 2 } = u ) { \mathrm { m a x } } _ { s _ { j } ^ { 1 } , i _ { j } ^ { 1 } } \delta _ { j s _ { j } ^ { 1 } i _ { j } ^ { 1 } } ^ { 1 } P ( s _ { j } ^ { 2 } } \end{array}$ $= k | s _ { j } ^ { 1 } ) P ( i _ { j } ^ { 2 } = u | i _ { j } ^ { 1 } , s _ { j } ^ { 2 } = k )$

Proceeding iteratively and in a similar manner, for $n = N _ { j } - 1$

$$
\begin{array}{l}\delta_{jku}^{N_{j} - 1} = P\Big(b_{j}^{N_{j} - 1}|s_{j}^{N_{j} - 1} = k\Big)P\Big(p_{j}^{N_{j} - 1}|i_{j}^{N_{j} - 1} = u\Big)\\ \qquad \times \max_{\substack{s_{j}^{N_{j} - 2},i_{j}^{N_{j} - 2}}}\delta_{j s_{j}^{N_{j} - 2},i_{j}^{N_{j} - 2}}^{N_{j} - 2}P\Big(s_{j}^{N_{j} - 1} = k|s_{j}^{N_{j} - 2}\Big)\\ \qquad P\Big(i_{j}^{N_{j} - 1} = u|i_{j}^{N_{j} - 2},s_{j}^{N_{j} - 1} = k\Big). \end{array}\tag{17}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\hat {s _ {j}} ^ {N _ {j} - 1}, \hat {i _ {j}} ^ {N _ {j} - 1} = \arg \max _ {k, u} \delta_ {j k u} ^ {N _ {j} - 1}.\tag{18}
$$

Then, it can be traced back to obtain the inference of $s _ { j } ^ { n }$ and i<sup>n</sup><sub>j</sub> $( n = 1 , \ldots , N _ { j } - 2 )$

$$
\hat {s _ {j}} ^ {n}, \hat {i _ {j}} ^ {n} = \arg \max _ {s _ {j} ^ {n}, i _ {j} ^ {n}} \delta_ {j s _ {j} ^ {n} i _ {j} ^ {n}} ^ {n} P (\hat {s _ {j}} ^ {n + 1} | s _ {j} ^ {n}) P (\hat {i _ {j}} ^ {n + 1} | i _ {j} ^ {n}, \hat {s _ {j}} ^ {n + 1})\tag{19}
$$

Based on the Viterbi-like inference, the most likely psychological stage and interest chain that the consumer experiences from behaviors 1 to $N _ { j } - 1$ in journey j can be decoded. Although the stage $\hat { s _ { j } } ^ { N _ { j } - 1 }$ and interest $\bar { i } _ { j } ^ { \bar { N } _ { j } - \bar { 1 } }$ are decoded with the maximum inference probability for behavior $N _ { j } - 1$ , there may be cases in which the consumer is at other stages with other interest distributions. To provide accurate and robust recommendations, MS-DBN adopts a probabilistic prediction procedure that considers all the possibilities of $\bar { s _ { j } ^ { N _ { j } - 1 } }$ and $i _ { j } ^ { N _ { j } - 1 }$ while executing the pruning with (17). Concretely, for $s _ { j } ^ { N _ { j } - 1 } = k$ and $\stackrel { \cdot } { i _ { j } ^ { N _ { j } - 1 } } = u$ , the procedure uses (17) to calculate the maximum probability for the consumer to be at stage k with interest u for behavior $N _ { j } -$ 1 in journey j. Then, the probability that product p will be interacted with by the consumer’s behavior $N _ { j }$ in shopping journey j is computed as (20).

$$
\begin{array}{c} P (p _ {j} ^ {N _ {j}} = p) = \sum_ {s _ {j} ^ {N _ {j} - 1}} \sum_ {i _ {j} ^ {N _ {j} - 1}} \sum_ {s _ {j} ^ {N _ {j}}} \sum_ {i _ {j} ^ {N _ {j}}} P \left(s _ {j} ^ {N _ {j} - 1}, i _ {j} ^ {N _ {j} - 1}, s _ {j} ^ {N _ {j}}, i _ {j} ^ {N _ {j}}\right) \\ P \left(p _ {j} ^ {N _ {j}} = p | s _ {j} ^ {N _ {j} - 1}, i _ {j} ^ {N _ {j} - 1}, s _ {j} ^ {N _ {j}}, i _ {j} ^ {N _ {j}}\right) \\ = \sum_ {s _ {j} ^ {N _ {j} - 1}} \sum_ {i _ {j} ^ {N _ {j} - 1}} \sum_ {s _ {j} ^ {N _ {j}}} \sum_ {i _ {j} ^ {N _ {j}}} \frac {\delta_ {j s _ {j} ^ {N _ {j} - 1} i _ {j} ^ {N _ {j} - 1}} ^ {N _ {j} - 1}}{\sum_ {s _ {j} ^ {N _ {j} - 1}} \sum_ {i _ {j} ^ {N _ {j} - 1}} \delta_ {j s _ {j} ^ {N _ {j} - 1} i _ {j} ^ {N _ {j} - 1}} ^ {N _ {j} - 1}} \\ P \left(s _ {j} ^ {N _ {j}} | s _ {j} ^ {N _ {j} - 1}\right) P \left(i _ {j} ^ {N _ {j}} | i _ {j} ^ {N _ {j} - 1}, s _ {j} ^ {N _ {j}}\right) P \left(p _ {j} ^ {N _ {j}} = p | i _ {j} ^ {N _ {j}}\right) \end{array}\tag{20}
$$

Here, $\frac { \delta _ { j _ { j } ^ { N _ { j } - 1 } i _ { j } ^ { N _ { j } - 1 } } ^ { N _ { j } - 1 } } { \sum _ { s _ { j } ^ { N _ { j } - 1 } } \sum _ { i _ { j } ^ { N _ { j } - 1 } } \delta _ { j _ { j } ^ { N _ { j } - 1 } i _ { j } ^ { N _ { j } - 1 } } ^ { N _ { j } - 1 } }$ is used to approximate $P ( s _ { j } ^ { N _ { j } - 1 } , i _ { j } ^ { N _ { j } - 1 } )$ because it can be assumed that the consu mer’s journey j reaches behavior $N _ { j } - 1$ via the stage and interest chain from behaviors 1 to $N _ { j } - 2$ with the maximum probability. The procedure prunes other chains with lower probabilities of reaching stage ${ \bar { s } } _ { j } ^ { \bar { N } _ { j } - \bar { 1 } }$ and interest $i _ { j } ^ { N _ { j } - 1 }$ while considering all the psychologi cal cases for behavior $N _ { j } - 1$ , thus obtaining an advan tage in dealing with uncertainty and noise in the data. Finally, in alignment with the consumer’s current stage and interest, the products that are most likely to be interacted with in behavior $N _ { j }$ will be recommended in descending order by (20).

## 3.4. Identification Strategies

In real-world managerial decisions, the identification of psychological dynamics is particularly beneficial to generate consumer insights (Batra and Keller 2016, Goldstein and Hajaj 2022). For a modeled variable that is latent in nature (e.g., psychological dynamics), widely used strategies are to identify it with the constrained conditional probabilities dependent on the variable (Montgomery et al. 2004). Specifically, in existing stud ies, psychological properties can be identified with activity intensity (Abhishek et al. 2012), choice/conversion probability (Zhang et al. 2019), and time duration of the exposure to display ads (Todri et al. 2020). However, adapting the identification strategies of psychologica dynamics to product recommendation remains highly challenging, especially in terms of consumers’ shifting interests. In MS-DBN, identification strategies for both psychological stages and interests in consumer shopping journeys are carefully designed.

Concretely, MS-DBN features two identification strategies of latent layers. Drawing on prior findings about consumers’ behavioral distinctions and shifting interests from the marketing funnel perspective, MS-DBN deduces the training constraints on the conditional probabilities in the model. First, the probability of consumers’ potential purchases (i.e., transitions to the action stage) is constrained to be nondecreasing as the psychological stages proceed such that the latent stages can be identified by their differences in purchase propensity. Second, for consumers who move forth through the funnel, the interests in products are restricted to be more converged, and, on the contrary, the interests become more diversely distributed as consumers move back across the stages. Thus, latent interests can be identified by their degrees of convergence.

3.4.1. Strategy 1: Stage Identification by Purchase Propensity. Studies have shown that consumers at later stages in the funnel generally possess a higher propensity of purchases/conversions (Abhishek et al. 2012, Zhou et al. 2019b). Regarding consumers’ invisible psychological stages, the conditional probability of purchase behaviors is commonly used as a direct and effective identifier (Zhang et al. 2019, Todri et al. 2020). In MS-DBN, as the observed purchase behavior corresponds to the action stage, the probability of consumers’ potential purchases conditional on stage k is equivalent to that of its transiting to the action stage, that is, $A _ { k m } = P ( s _ { j } ^ { n + 1 } = m = a c t i o n | s _ { j } ^ { n } = k )$ . In terms of the EM and MAP implementation for model training, given the total |S| stages, MS-DBN first marks the stages in ascending order, such as awareness as stage 1 and action as stage |S|. Then, the initialized values of $A _ { k | S | } = P ( s _ { j } ^ { n + 1 } = | S | | s _ { j } ^ { n } = k )$ for $\forall k = 1 , \ldots , \left| S \right| - 1$ are set as $A _ { 1 | S | } \leq A _ { 2 | S | } \leq \cdots \leq A _ { ( | S | - 1 ) | S | }$ . Furthermore, in each iteration of parameter estimation, the stages k are resorted by the nondecreasing order of $A _ { k | S | }$ , and the learned parameters with regard to $s _ { j } ^ { n } .$ , that is, $\pi , F , A , H ,$ and $D ,$ would accordingly be arranged with k. In this way, although the number of latent psychological stages is to be tuned by recommendation performance, they can always be ascendingly marked according to the order in the funnel and, more importantly, identified by the nondecreasing conditional probabilities of potential purchases at current stages.

3.4.2. Strategy 2: Interest Identification by Degree of Convergence. For consumers’ latent interests in products, the identification analogously requires a metric of conditional probabilities that are dependent on interests, that is, the product interaction probabilities $E _ { u p } = P ( p _ { j } ^ { n } = p | i _ { j } ^ { n } = u )$ conditional on interest u in the modeling scenario of MS-DBN. Moreover, existing work by funnel insights indicates that consumers usually start with wide interests in mind at early stages and finally arrive at relatively concentrated interests at the end of the shopping journeys, that is, different convergencies of interests across psychological stages (Howard and Sheth 1969, Court et al. 2009, Mulpuru 2011). In this regard, MS-DBN identifies and distinguishes latent interests by the degree of convergence, which is supposed to be an integrated function of $E _ { u p }$ over the entire product set and can be measured with the wellrecognized Kurtosis<sup>1</sup> metric; that is, a lower Kurtosis indicates a more diversified interest probabilistic distribution on products, whereas a higher Kurtosis means a more converged and concentrated interest. Considering the unordered products in the data, the Kurtosis metric K(u) of interest u can be adapted as (21).

$$
K (u) = \frac {\sum_ {p = 1} ^ {| P |} E _ {u p} (R _ {u p} - \bar {R _ {u p}}) ^ {4}}{\left(\sum_ {p = 1} ^ {| P |} E _ {u p} (R _ {u p} - \bar {R _ {u p}}) ^ {2}\right) ^ {2}},\tag{21}
$$

where $R _ { u p } \in [ 1 , | P | ]$ represents the ranking of product $p$ in the calculation of interest $u ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ Kurtosis. Specifically, product $p$ with the largest $E _ { u p }$ would be sorted to the middle, that is, $R _ { u p } = | P | / 2$ or $\dot { R } _ { u p } = ( | P | + 1 ) / 2$ , whereas product $p$ with the smallest $E _ { u p }$ would be sorted to both ends, that is, $R _ { u p } = 1$ or $R _ { u p } = | P |$ and so forth. The expression $\begin{array} { r } { \bar { \dot { R _ { u p } } } = \dot { \sum } _ { p = 1 } ^ { | P | } E _ { u p } \bar { R _ { u p } } } \end{array}$ is thus the weighted average ranking of all the products. It can be proved that, assuming that consumers have a randomly distributed interest in some products, the larger the number of these products (e.g., at the awareness stage), the lower the interest Kurtosis would be $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ , detailed as Theorem 1 in Online Appendix D). As the marketing funnel perspective describes the shopping journey as a process through which consumers methodically reduce the candidate products, the theorem illustrates that Kurtosis is a reasonable metric to identify the various interests at different stages. Therefore, in a consumer shopping journey, if $s _ { j } ^ { n + 1 } > \bar { s } _ { j } ^ { n }$ , then the interest shift is constrained to $K ( i _ { j } ^ { n + 1 } )$ $\geq K ( i _ { j } ^ { n } ) ; \mathrm { i f } s _ { j } ^ { n + 1 } < s _ { j } ^ { n }$ , then there must be $K ( i _ { j } ^ { n + 1 } ) \le K ( i _ { j } ^ { n } )$ for $P ( i _ { j } ^ { n + 1 } | i _ { j } ^ { n } , s _ { j } ^ { n + 1 } ) > 0$ . In the learning and predicting procedure of the model, an indicator function $\mathbf { 1 } ( ( s _ { j } ^ { n + 1 } - s _ { j } ^ { n } )$ $( K ( i _ { i } ^ { n + 1 } ) - K ( i _ { i } ^ { n } ) ) \ge 0 )$ is used to restrict the calculation of $\alpha , \beta , \xi , \delta _ { j k u } ^ { n } ,$ , and $P ( p _ { j } ^ { N _ { j } } = p )$ such that interests become more converged when consumers progress to a later psychological stage and diversify when the stages transit backward.

In summary, the two identification strategies and the corresponding constraints benefit the detection of consumers’ latent stages and interests. For model training, the strategies can not only help effectively capture the generalizble regularity of consumers’ psychological dynamics but also pull the learning procedure toward a theoretically substantiated direction through the reasonably narrowed solution space of parameters, that is, lifting the learning efficiency. Therefore, in the inference and prediction procedure, consumers’ invisible stages and interests can be decoded and identified, inspiring personalized recommendations with managerial insights.

## 4. Experiments with Real-World Data

The experiments collected two large real-world data sets from Tmall<sup>2</sup> and REES46<sup>3</sup> and evaluated the recommendation performance of the proposed MS-DBN approach. Both data sets record a large number of consumers’ interactive behaviors with products, which consist of the consumer ID, product category ID, product ID, behavior type, behavior time, and other pieces of information.

Consumers’ behavior records in a random month were sampled from the two data sets to ensure an adequate but manageable data scale. The next step was to separate consumer behaviors into sequences corresponding to shopping journeys. Concretely, considering the convenience brought by e-commerce that enables consumers’ simultaneous engagement in multiple journeys, such as shopping clothes and laptops at the same time, the experiments separated the behavior records with both consumer ID and product category ID. Furthermore, the behaviors that were next to purchases or longer than one week away from the previous behaviors were separated into a new journey sequence with their following records. The reason was that purchase behaviors are usually regarded as the end of shopping journeys (Abhishek et al. 2012, Todri et al. 2020), and consumers are more likely to start new journeys after a long duration between behaviors (e.g., one week) on the platform (Goldstein and Hajaj 2022). Following the common treatment in the literature (Huang and Zeng 2011, Sahoo et al. 2012), the derived data sets were further preprocessed to include only those products that had been interacted with for at least 10 times and those consumers who had produced at least 10 behaviors. The two derived data sets for the experiments are shown in Table 1. It is worth noting that the large variance of journey length and the number of products in each journey implies the high variability in consumer shopping journeys.

The experiments trained and tested the methods using 10-fold cross-validation, which divided the shopping journeys into 10 folds randomly. Each fold of data were adopted as the test set at a time, and the remainder was used as the training set so that the method performance could be calculated as the average of 10 times of tests. The training process used the shopping journeys with all behavior records. In the testing process, based on all the historical behaviors (i.e., except the last one) that a consumer produced within the shopping journey, the recommendation methods aimed to predict the product that the consumer would interact with for the last behavior. Given the top-K highest scoring products by the methods, the experiments used two metrics, HR@K and NDCG@K, to measure their recommendation accuracy and ranking performance, respectively.

## 4.1. Comparison of Baseline Methods

For the performance evaluation of MS-DBN, the experiments compared it with 12 baseline recommendation methods. Among them, there are three commonly used static methods, namely, (i) POP (i.e., popular) (Prawesh and Padmanabhan 2014), which recommends the most popular/interacted products of the same category, (ii) User-CF (Breese et al. 1998), which exhibits the products that appeal to similar consumers, and (iii) BPR (i.e., Bayesian personalized ranking) (Rendle et al. 2009), which learns consumer–product interactions with matrix factorization; six representative dynamic methods, namely, (i) TMF (Dror et al. 2011), which expends matrix factorization with time-dependent biases, (ii) Katz-CWT (Dunlavy et al. 2011), which predicts the interactions from collapsed consumer behaviors, (iii) XGBoost (Chen and Guestrin 2016), which conducts a flexible tree-based model with time-related variables, (iv) HMM (Sahoo et al. 2012), which depicts interest shifts with one type of hidden layer/state, (v) HSMM (Zhang et al. 2018), which further introduces different durations of consumers’ staying at each state, and (vi) CHMM (Brand 1997), which adds a symmetric latent layer to HMM in a crossover manner; and three state-of-the-art deep learning–based methods, namely, (i) NMF (i.e., neural MF) (He et al. 2017), which learns the latent consumer–product relations with the fusion of generalized MF and multilayer perceptron, (ii) VAE (i.e., variational autoencoders) (Liang et al. 2018), which conducts the CF recommendation using variational autoencoders and Bayesian inference, and (iii) DIN (Zhou et al. 2019a), which generates various embeddings and weights them with the attention mechanism.

(The model forbids consumers from moving back through the funnel such that the psychological stages during a shopping journey can only transit in the forward direction.)

## 4.4. Discussion on Flexible Segmentation

As stated, some existing recommendation methods segment consumer behaviors into periods before learning, such as by a preconfigured time duration or a fixed number of behaviors, and assume that consumer interest in products would not shift within a period (Dunlavy et al. 2011, Sahoo et al. 2012, Zhang et al. 2018). However, different consumers may have heterogeneous shopping paces; for example, a consumer closes a journey just after a few clicks, whereas another generates dozens of interactive behaviors, including click and add-to-favorites/cart, before purchase. The same consumer may also exhibit different shopping journeys on different products; for example, a consumer spends a week-long journey to make a buying decision on a laptop but takes only several minutes to purchase a T-shirt. Even further, consumers may show various patterns of interest shifts at different stages; for example, a consumer spends a long period and displays plenty of behaviors at the awareness or consideration stages, but makes quick decisions at the action stage.

Considering that stiff data segmentation can hardly be precise enough for dynamic pattern extraction (Montgomery et al. 2004), the proposed approach conducts flexible segmentation with the implicit aggregation of psychological stages, interests, and thus observable behaviors in consumer shopping journeys. To illustrate its advantage over fixed period segmentation in dynamic recommendation, the experiments compared the per formance of MS-DBN adopting flexible segmentation, period segmentation by time duration (i.e., one day as a period), and segmentation by behavior numbers (i.e., five behaviors as a period). Moreover, the adapted HMM baseline method was implemented with the three segmentation tactics to facilitate discussions. Table 4 summarizes the experimental results on the Tmall data set described in Table 1.

The results indicate that MS-DBN with flexible segmentation outperforms all the other methods for comparison. If embedded with the additional assumption of consumers’ unshifted interests in each time-duration/behavior-number period, its recommendation accuracy would be hurt because of ignoring the variability in interest shifts during consumer shopping journeys. With regard to the adapted HMM method, the period segmentation of behavioral sequences by time also decreases effectiveness, but the behaviornumber segmentation tactic might enhance its perfor mance, albeit the improvement is not significant. The reason might be that the record aggregation by behavior numbers could, to some extent, help HMM evade behavioral diversities (e.g., partly disordered and scattered behaviors). This highlights MS-DBN’s overall performance superiority in achieving both good tolerance of consumers’ diverse behaviors and high flexibility of interest shift modeling.

## 4.5. Discussion on Regularity Extraction from Diverse Behaviors

In alignment with existing studies of recommendation, the above experiments retained the popular products with frequent occurrences (i.e., ≥10 times) in the data set to relieve both sparsity disturbance and the curse of dimensionality in matrix computation (Huang and Zeng 2011, Sahoo et al. 2012). However, the practical context usually includes a large set of infrequently interacted products, which could be a spotlight for e-commerce platforms to target consumers and create profits (Anderson 2006). Echoing the challenge about diverse behaviors, consumers’ interactions with these products are even more scattered and diversified. Thus, for consumers in the Tmall data set, the experiments further traced the complete behavior records over the whole product set from the original data, and conducted preprocessing to derive shopping journeys. Table 5 shows the descriptive statistics of the data sets with the popular product set and the whole product set (i.e., including the products with <10 interactions). As indicated, the size of the product set was remarkably expanded from 23,793 to 81,805. Meanwhile, the average of both the journey length and the number of products in each journey increased with a significantly increased variance. These lead to more severe sparsity in consumer–product interactions, which adds to the difficulty in extracting the generalizable regularity from shopping journeys.

## 5. Conclusion

Technique design based on user behavior analysis has received widespread attention in the research field of information systems (Fang and Sheng 2004, Fang et al. 2007). Considering that the dynamic product recommendation problem is important, but extant solutions are suboptimal, this study proposes a novel multistage dynamic Bayesian network (MS-DBN) approach, positioned in the improvement quadrant of the design science research taxonomy (Gregor and Hevner 2013). Aiming to provide effective stage-aware recommendations, MS-DBN analyzes shopping journeys from the marketing funnel perspective and models the generative processes of consumers’ sequential interactive behaviors with products driven by psychological dynamics, taking both psychological stages and latent interests into account, coupled with a Viterbi-like inference procedure and a probabilistic prediction procedure, as well as the identification strategies of latent layers. Extensive real-world data experiments and robustness checks show that the proposed MS-DBN approach outperforms baseline methods in large-scale applications and in comprehensive scenarios.

Generally speaking, this work significantly enhances the intelligent analytics in dynamic product recommendation by developing a novel artifact/approach, extends the understanding and solution spaces of the dynamic recommendation problem, and provides managerial implications for practice. First, by modeling the underlying patterns of psychological dynamics, MS-DBN can effectively deal with the issue of diverse behaviors. Especially in the product recommendation scenario with highly sparse data, MS-DBN demonstrates significant performance advantage with general applicability by extracting the generalizable regularity during shopping journeys, which compensates the diversity and sparsity in consumer behaviors. Second, with the proposed dynamic Bayesian network to synthetically model psychological stages and interests, the conditional and partial dependency between them, and their effects on consumer behaviors, MS-DBN can capture the variability in consumers’ interest shifts and thus achieve flexible segmentation on shopping journeys. Therefore, MS-DBN can subtly learn consumers’ stageinterest-behavior dynamics, facilitating effective recommendation. Third, aided by the identification strategies integrated into the learning process, the latent variables in the model can be reasonably detected. Therefore, consumers’ invisible psychological stages and interests in products can be identified from the observed behaviors, shedding light on the targeted marketing of platforms/merchants and thus enriching the practical value of the approach. In this way, the derived recommendations, which are both superior in performance and with the identified psychological dynamics, would benefit not only sellers’ marketing but also consumers’ decision making.

There are also limitations to this study. First, the proposed approach conducts its analysis using data on consumers’ interactive behaviors with products. Future hybrid recommendations could be envisioned by acquiring and integrating more fruitful observed attribute/factor information of consumers (e.g., profile, online footprints, and social connections) and products (e.g., features, ratings, and comments) to analyze consu mers’ personalized interests with more details. Second, as indicated in Online Appendix G, it is frequently observed that a consumer is simultaneously engaged in multiple shopping journeys, and separating mixed journeys with product categories is a helpful treatment to facilitate dynamic recommendation. Future work could explore more precise separation of journeys, which may further benefit performance with the improved input behavioral sequences. Third, challenges in collecting sufficient data covering a consumer’s complete shopping journey in real-world applications, such as in the case of cross-channel shopping, may also limit recommendation performance. Future studies may endeavor to design effective approaches that are more suitable for these specific problems or data contexts.
