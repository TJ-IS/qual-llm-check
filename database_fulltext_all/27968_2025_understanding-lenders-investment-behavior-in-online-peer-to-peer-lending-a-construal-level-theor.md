# Understanding Lenders’ Investment Behavior in Online Peer-to-Peer Lending: A Construal Level Theory Perspective

Abstract. Online peer-to-peer lending (i.e., P2P lending) has grown rapidly in recent years and is a new source of fixed income for investors. However, there is limited understanding of factors affecting individual lenders’ decision making in this context, which is characterized as highly risky. Drawing on construal level theory, we theorize how bidding amounts lenders submit are affected by interest rates and psychological distance caused by the borrower’s demographic attributes (i.e., geographic location, age, educational degree, and marital status) relative to those of the lender. Specifically, we study how psychological distance directly influences and shapes the effects of the duality of interest rates (i.e., as rates of return and as signals of potential risk) on bidding amounts. Using a rich data set from a popular Chinese online P2P lending platform, we apply multiple identification strategies and estimation methods to conduct our analyses. We find that geographic distance decreases the lenders’ bidding amounts (i.e., home bias effect), whereas social distance increases the bidding amounts (i.e., social distance effect). In addition, the positive effects of interest rates on bidding amounts are strengthened by the geographic and social distance between the lender and the borrower. Furthermore, we conduct four controlled experi ments to explore the causality and mechanisms behind these relationships. Theoretical contributions and practical implications are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

Online peer-to-peer lending (i.e., online P2P lending) has emerged as a complement to traditional means of accessing financial capital by providing small loans for borrowers and a source of potential fixed income for lenders (Ge et al. 2017, Kim and Hann 2019, Tang 2019). As an innovative practice, online P2P lending allows individuals to lend money directly to others through platforms without the intervention of financial institutions (Lin et al. 2013). Compared with traditional finance services, online P2P lending offers simplified procedures, more transparency, and better interest rates for borrowers and, in most cases, lenders (Galak et al. 2011). According to Transparency Market Research, the global online P2P lending market will be worth \$897.85 billion by 2024, with a singular compound annual growth rate of 48.2% since $2 0 1 6 . ^ { 1 }$ That online P2P lending has attracted scholarly attention is thus not surprising (Galak et al. 2011, Burtch et al. 2014, Liu et al. 2015, Iyer et al. 2016, Lin and Viswanathan 2016, Ge et al. 2017, Xu and Chau 2018).

Our review of germane literature reveals that prior research on online P2P lending was primarily conducted from the perspective of borrowers or loan listings, with a focus on overall funding outcomes. For example, Lin et al. (2013) and Wei and Lin (2016) exam ined antecedents of funding success probability. Zhang and Liu (2012) studied factors affecting the daily funding amount received. Although these investigations have provided insights into how borrowers achieve their financing goals, their analysis at an aggregate level (e.g., a metropolitan statistical area, a loan listing)

prevents adequately learning about factors that drive an individual’s lending decisions—which are at a more granular level. Given that a successful funding outcome is rooted in individuals’ willingness to lend and their bidding amounts submitted, investigating how lenders make bidding decisions is imperative. Such research can augment understanding of the phenomenon and gain insights into the management of P2P lending.

Some studies in recent years have explored antecedents of lenders’ bidding probability (Agrawal et al. 2015, Liu et al. 2015, Lin and Viswanathan 2016); however, there is scant empiricism investigating factors affecting lenders’ bidding amounts. In their evaluation of loan listings, lenders essentially solve an optimal investment allocation problem among the investing options offered on the platform. As such, they need to decide whether to lend and, more importantly, how much to lend, which reflects the marginal returns the lender anticipates from investing in a specific loan listing. Because the expected returns encompass the interest rates and risks associated with the loan (Zhang and Liu 2012), bidding amounts—compared with bidding probabilities—can better characterize lenders’ evaluations of loan listings and thus warrant empirical scrutiny.

In addition, previous studies largely ignore the influence of the online P2P lending context, which is characterized by a much higher risk compared with traditional fixed income investment. The context of online P2P lending has two distinct characteristics. First, loans are typically issued in relatively small amounts, uncollateralized, and for specific purposes (e.g., poverty alleviation, medical treatment) (Battilana and Dorado 2010, Allison et al. 2015). Without protection against borrower default, the interest rate specified by a loan listing signals the risk involved and serves as a promised return on investment (Allison et al. 2015). The duality of interest rates implies that lenders’ decision making is subject to both economic and psychological influences (Tversky and Kahneman 1974, Loewenstein et al. 2001). Second, with limited access to borrowers’ hard financial information (e.g., credit scores), lenders make decisions largely based on their assessment of soft information borrowers or the platform report, including loan description and borrowers’ demographic attributes (Iyer et al. 2016). Such information affects lenders’ representations of loan listings and impacts their lending decisions. Examining how these characteristics in this specific context affect individuals’ lending behavior is critical to understanding the management of online P2P lending; however, systemic theory-based empirical research on this issue is lacking.

The current research addresses these foregoing shortfalls by theorizing and empirically analyzing how borrowers’ demographic attributes influence lenders evaluations of the promised interest rate and affect their decisions on the bidding amounts for a loan listing.<sup>2</sup> To this end, we draw on the online P2P literature and construal level theory (CLT) (Trope and Liberman 2010) to develop our research model. According to CLT, individuals experience psychological distance based on how geographically, temporally, socially, or hypothetically close to or far away they are from a target (Trope and Liberman 2010). Such psychological distance correlates with the construal level of individuals’ representations of the target, thereby influencing their evaluation, affect, and downstream behavior (Liberman and Trope 1998, Trope and Liberman 2010).

CLT is an appropriate theory for the current research for three reasons. First, psychological distance is activated in almost every act of perception and judgment in an automatic, often unconscious, fashion (Trope and Liberman 2010). Because virtually all stimuli in peo ple’s perceptual milieu are embedded in a certain dis tance relation to the focal self (Bar-Anan et al. 2007), psychological distance serves as a fundamental processing principle that guides human behavior. Because of its all-encompassing nature, inevitably, psychological distance is activated by the soft information of loan list ings and, consequently, affects lenders’ decision making in P2P lending. Second, CLT offers guidance on how lenders construe loan listings, which influences their lending decisions involving dual information processing (i.e., analytical—cognitive—and experiential— affective—evaluations) (Tversky and Kahneman 1974 Loewenstein et al. 2001). In particular, psychologica distance stimulates abstract high construal level repre sentations of the target; in so doing, the salience of desirability features (e.g., the rate of return on investment) increases and the weight of feasibility attributes (e.g., the potential default risk of a loan) decreases (Trope and Liberman 2010). This enables examining how psychological distance triggered by borrowers soft information impacts lenders’ assessments of the duality of the promised interest rates. Third, although psychological distance and construal level are bidirectional and have common effects on cognitive processes (Liberman and Trope 1998, Jia et al. 2009), research extending CLT further reveals that these two constructs also affect individuals’ evaluation and beha viors through affective paths (Eyal et al. 2004, Williams et al. 2014). In particular, psychological distance reduces the affect intensity an individual feels (Williams and Bargh 2008, Williams et al. 2014). In the view that both economic and psychological factors impinge on lending in P2P (Lin and Viswanathan 2016), applying CLT and drawing on related empirical studies enable us to explore how psychological distance, together with economic factors (i.e., interest rates), plays a role in P2P lending decisions.

Based on CLT, we theorize how two typical kinds of psychological distance—geographic and socia distance—lenders experience when they examine borrowers’ demographic data affect lenders’ bidding amounts through both cognitive and affective paths. Geographic distance, reflecting spatial distance, is defined as the physical distance between the residence of a lender and that of a borrower (where; here versus there; local versus 3,000 miles away); social distance refers to a lender’s subjective perception or experience of distance from a borrower (e.g., who; self versus other; in-group versus out-group) (Liberman et al. 2007). There is theoretical and consistent empirical support on the influence these two types of distance have on individuals’ decision making (Smith and Trope 2006, Galak et al. 2011, Anderson et al. 2012, Burtch et al. 2014, Lin and Viswanathan 2016). A detailed discussion of related studies is provided below. Therefore, we follow CLT and argue that geographic and social distance will strengthen the positive effect of interest rates on lenders’ bidding amounts. This is because they make interest rates—as rates of return on investment (i.e., a desirability feature)—more salient and, as a risk signal (i.e., a feasibility attribute), less concerning. Thus, with the same interest rate, geographic and social distance induce lenders to have a more favorable evaluation of the loan listing. In addition, based on the empirica studies guided by CLT that explore how psychologica distance and construal level affect individuals’ evaluation and behavior through an affective path, we contend that both types of psychological distance will lower lenders’ bidding amounts; this is because they reduce the intensity of lenders’ affect toward the target (Lermer et al. 2015). For instance, geographic distance lessens lenders’ perceived familiarity toward the borrower, thereby having a negative effect on lending behavior which results in “home bias” (Lin and Viswanathan 2016).

Our research model is tested with data from 29,025 online borrower-lender lending transactions in 2015 from a well-known online P2P lending platform in China (i.e., Renrendai). Specifically, we find that lenders bid more on a loan listed by a geographically close borrower than from a geographically distant borrower, hence confirming the home bias effect. However, the bidding amount on a loan is positively associated with the social distance between a lender and the borrower, which we refer to as the social distance effect. In addition, we reveal that the positive impact of interest rates on bidding amounts is amplified by both geographic and social distance. To explain this surprising finding and unveil its underlying mechanisms, we conduct four post hoc controlled experiments. They demonstrate that geographic and social distance affect bidding amounts by influencing the different types of affects lenders have on the borrower. These findings enrich the growing stream of research on online P2P lending and extend CLT.

Illustrated in Table A1 in the online appendix are distinctions between our undertaking and previous studies. We extend literature by providing enhanced nuanced understanding of how psychological distance activated automatically by borrowers’ soft information, relative to that of the lender, influences lenders’ evaluation of the duality of interest rates and thus affects online P2P lending behavior. In addition, we shed new light on how different types of psychological distance impact lenders decision making. Our findings on social distance’s various direct effects reveal the intricacy of social distance and highlight its multiplexity. Furthermore, as one of the first attempts to distinguish the impacts of different types of psychological distance on decision making in a risky context, we contribute to CLT by bringing in the perspective that psychological distance can arouse dissimilar types of emotion which, in turn, impinge on individual behaviors in diverse ways. As such, we extend CLT by demonstrating that psychological distance can exert its effect through routes other than being interrelated and having a bidirectional relationship with construal level.

## 2. Literature Review and Theoretical Background 2.1. Online P2P Lending

Since the start of the first P2P lending platform, Zopa, in 2006, researchers have devoted efforts to understanding this innovative practice. One stream of research has examined how traditional lending criteria affect funding outcomes of loan listings. For example, previous studies have explored the effects of promised interest rates (Zhang and Liu 2012), borrower’s credit grade (Sonenshein et al. 2011, Zhang and Liu 2012, Iyer et al. 2016), loan duration (Mollick 2014, Iyer et al. 2016), verification and endorsement mechanisms (Herzenstein et al. 2011, Zhang and Liu 2012), and collateral approaches (Jiang et al. 2020). These investigations have consistently found that interest rates remain a central factor that leads to funding success in online P2P lending (Herzenstein et al. 2011, Zhang and Liu 2012, Liu et al. 2015). However, soft information derived from borrowers or the platform serves as peripheral cues for loan quality evaluation (Herzenstein et al. 2011, Iyer et al. 2016). Yet, how sof information—such as borrowers’ demographic data— interacts with interest rates by affecting individuals lending behaviors remains understudied.

In addition, extant work has suggested that interest rates signal borrowers’ default and could be a doubleedged sword influencing funding performance (Zhang and Liu 2012, Iyer et al. 2016, Wei and Lin 2016). Interest rates as rates of return constitute the end of an investment, thus making it a desirability feature of P2P lending. In contrast, interest rates as a signal of default risk are the means of an investment and therefore are a feasibility attribute of P2P lending. Although scholars have alluded to the duality of interest rates in online P2P lending (Iyer et al. 2016, Wei and Lin 2016), there is a lack of empiricism that has considered factors that may affect the salience of these two dimensions in individual lenders’ decision making.

A second stream of research goes beyond traditional evaluation criteria and has investigated how social factors affect overall funding performance in online P2P lending. Examined factors include loan narratives (Sonenshein et al. 2011, Allison et al. 2015), borrowers facial features (Pope and Sydnor 2011, Komarova Loureiro and Gonzalez 2015), geographic proximity (Burtch et al. 2014, Mollick 2014, Agrawal et al. 2015, Lin and Viswanathan 2016), social similarities between lenders and borrowers (Galak et al. 2011, Burtch et al. 2014), online social networks (Lin et al. 2013, Liu et al. 2015), borrowers’ self-disclosed social media information (Ge et al. 2017), patterns of lender-borrower communications (Xu and Chau 2018), and anonymity of preceding lenders (Jiang et al. 2022). The phenomenon of these social factors’ effects on lending behavior is coined homophily, which refers to the tendency of individuals to associate and bond with others who are similar to them (Lazarsfeld and Merton 1954).

Similar to the previously mentioned stream of social factors research, studies have largely investigated the online P2P phenomenon from the perspective of borrowers and loan listings and scrutinized how social factors impact funding outcomes, such as funding success probability (Lin et al. 2013, Wei and Lin 2016), daily funding amount received (Zhang and Liu 2012), and loan default probability (Iyer et al. 2016, Wei and Lin 2016, Ge et al. 2017). In particular, similarity of demographic attributes between borrowers and lenders can affect funding performance. For example, loan listings attract more funding from lenders who are geographically close to borrowers (i.e., home bias effect) and socially proximate to borrowers with respect to race, gender, occupation, and first name initial (Galak et al. 2011, Burtch et al. 2014, Lin and Viswanathan 2016). Yet, this work has offered few theoretical explanations about the interplay of economic and behavioral factors involved and their underlying mechanisms. Consequently, there is a paucity of nuanced understanding of how these social factors impinge on individual lenders behaviors in online P2P lending.

Additionally, the overall funding outcomes investigated in extant empiricism have been primarily aggregate results of individual lenders’ discrete decisions. Therefore, relatively scant knowledge about an individual lender’s decision making exists; accordingly, understanding how the economic and social factors examined in prior research affect individuals’ bidding behaviors is critical. Although a few relatively recent investigations have examined antecedents of individuals’ bidding behaviors, they have mainly focused on bidding probability (e.g., Agrawal et al. 2015, Liu et al. 2015, Lin and Viswanathan 2016), with the exception of Galak et al. (2011), Jiang et al. (2020), and Jiang et al. (2022). Yet, as mentioned above, bidding amounts—compared with bidding probabilities— can better reflect lenders’ evaluation of loan listings. Hence, to gain nuanced comprehension of factors driving individual lenders’ decision making in P2P lending, exploring the phenomenon by incorporating bidding amounts as a dependent variable in the research model is requisite.

## 2.2. Construal Level Theory

CLT posits that the psychological distance of a target (i.e., an event, object, or individual) influences an individual’s mental representations, evaluation, affect, and downstream behaviors (Trope and Liberman 2010). Psychological distance is a subjective egocentric experi ence in which the target is close to or far from the observing self (Liberman and Trope 1998). The ways in which the target is away from the individual—in time, space, social distance, and hypotheticality constitute four distance dimensions (i.e., temporal, spatial, social, and hypothetical) (Trope and Liberman 2010). In addition, psychological distance is automatically activated by virtually all environmental factors in human perceptual milieu, which are embedded in a certain distance relation to the individual (Bar-Anan et al. 2007).

According to CLT, psychological distance affects the construal level at which people mentally represent the target, and the relationship between psychological distance and construal level is bidirectional (Liberman and Trope 1998, Trope and Liberman 2010, Henderson et al. 2011). Specifically, when an event occurs here—in the present—it is happening to the individual and/or with certainty, so the individual will mentally represent the event at a low construal level. In contrast, when the event takes place in a distant location—in the future—to others and/or with ambiguity, the individual’s mental representations of this event will be at a high construal level (Trope and Liberman 2003, Fujita et al. 2006). Furthermore, numerous studies have found that “the association between psychological distance and construal level can be activated automatically without conscious deliberation” (Bar-Anan et al. 2006, p. 617). For example, Bar-Anan et al. (2006) examined the implicit association between psychological distance and construal level and demonstrated that all fou dimensions of psychological distance are related to level of construal in a similar way.

It is established that psychological distance and construal level can affect individuals’ decision making through influencing both cognitive and affective processes involved (Eyal et al. 2004, Fujita et al. 2006,

Williams and Bargh 2008). Through cognitive path, psychological distance and construal level affect an individual’s evaluation of a target by shifting the weight of different judgment and decision-making inputs (i.e., desirability and feasibility features) (Trope and Liberman 2010). With a high psychological distance and construal level, the individual focuses more on the event’s desirability (i.e., the value of an event’s end state; attractiveness of an investment’s outcomes here) versus feasibility (i.e., the ease of reaching the end state; the probability of achieving the investment outcomes here) (Liberman and Trope 1998). In contrast, an individual with a low psychological distance and construal level is more sensitive to feasibility (versus desirability) considerations of the event (Trope and Liberman 2003, Trope and Liberman 2010). This important weight shifting effect has been consistently documented in different contexts (Liberman et al. 2002, Fujita et al. 2006, Alexander et al. 2008).

Although psychological distance and construal level are bidirectional and have common effects on cognitive processes, their influences on affective processes involved in people’s evaluation of a target differ (Williams and Bargh 2008, Williams et al. 2014). Specifically, construal level impacts the valence of affect toward a target and psychological distance affects the intensity of affect (Eyal et al. 2004, Williams et al. 2014). More specifically, via abstract thinking, compared with a low construal level, a high construal level prompts an individual to center more on primary (versus secondary) features of an event (Trope and Liberman 2000, Fujita et al. 2006). That is, individuals with a high construal level (versus a low construal level) will place more emphasis on the goals of their behaviors, which are largely perceived as positive and desirable (Custers and Aarts 2005). As such, a high construal level leads an individual to generate more pros than cons, which results in a more positive valence toward the target (Eyal et al. 2004, Williams et al. 2014). In contrast, psychological distance reduces individuals’ responses to distant stimuli and thus reduces affective concern about the target (McGraw et al. 2012, Williams et al. 2014). For instance, geographic distance from a distant comforting shelter would decrease the level of excitement. Such lessened affective reactions in people’s judgments and decision making would reduce the intensity of affect felt by the individual (Williams and Bargh 2008, Williams et al. 2014). For example, psychological distance decreases the intensity of empathy that promotes prosocial P2P lending behavior, but psychological closeness enhances it (Galak et al. 2011).

That psychological distance plays a prominent role in affecting individuals’ decision making under risk is well documented. For example, Lermer et al. (2015) found that, compared with psychological closeness, psychological distance leads to more risk taking. Moreover,

Wakslak and Trope (2009) ascertained that individuals with an abstract construal level made lower probability assessments than their counterparts with a concrete construal level. Considering online P2P lending as decision making under risk, we follow this research stream of the influence of psychological distance on decision making and investigate the effects of psychological distance through both cognitive and affective paths.

We do not intend to examine an exhaustive list of factors conducing to psychological distance. Rather, we focus on two types of psychological distance: namely, geographic distance and social distance. As noted earlier, geographic distance reflects the spatial dimension of psychological distance and is an important factor influencing an individual’s evaluation of a target (Kim et al. 2012, Burtch et al. 2014, Huang et al. 2016). We define geographic distance as the physical distance between the residence of a lender and that of a borrower. Our attention on geographic distance is in response to consistent findings concerning a “home bias” in online P2P lending (i.e., lenders prefer fundraisers who are geographically close to them) (Burtch et al. 2014, Lin and Viswanathan 2016). We define social distance as a lender’s subjective difference with a borrower in terms of social distinctions. Among many dimensions of social distance (e.g., gender, race, religious affiliation), we center on education, age, and marital status, of which we have data on both lenders and borrowers for two reasons. First, homophily empiricism has suggested that education and age are critical social distinctions that determine social distance (Smith et al. 2014). Also, the social psychology literature demonstrates that an individual’s marital status is an important indicator of his/her identity structure (Thoits 1992). Second, our research on these dimensions of social distance complements previous studies that have defined social distance in terms of race, gender, occupation, and first name initial (Galak et al. 2011, Burtch et al. 2014).

## 3.1. Duality of Interest Rates

Financial institutions traditionally focus on economic factors, such as return on investment, to evaluate personal loans (Krahnen and Weber 2001). Similarly, in online P2P lending, interest rates, as rates of return on investment, are the primary factor making online lending desirable to lenders (Cai et al. 2016). However, interest rates in online P2P lending are different from the traditional finance market (e.g., offline banks) in which the financial return is fully guaranteed. In online P2P lending, interest rates reflect lenders’ expectation of receiving periodic interest income that motivates their bidding behavior (Ahlers et al. 2015), although helping the borrowers in need is another possible incentive. If the loan does not default, the promised interest rate by the loan listing determines gains for lenders, and a higher interest rate allows the lender to achieve greater returns (Galak et al. 2011). As such, interest rates, as a desirability feature of a loan listing, could positively affect the lending value lenders submit.

However, the finance literature has long documented that interest rates are also one of the most critical factors in determining ex post loan default (Stiglitz and Weiss 1981, Allison et al. 2015). Borrower default is a more salient concern in the context of P2P lending (versus traditional lending), owing to the lack of collateral. Also, compared with high-quality borrowers, low-quality borrowers have fewer alternative funding options and are willing to offer a higher interest rate to increase funding success (Wei and Lin 2016). As such, interest rates are a critical signal of default risk in P2P lending and could discourage lenders’ bidding. Given such duality of interest rates (i.e., rates of return on investment and a signal of default risk), whether interest rates have a positive or negative effect on bidding amounts in P2P lending is an empirical question. To facilitate our hypothesis development, we followed the work of previous scholars (Sonenshein et al. 2011, Zhang and Liu 2012, Cai et al. 2016) and assumed a positive effect of interest rates on bidding amounts in online P2P lending.

## 3.2. Direct Effects of Psychological Distance

As mentioned previously, CLT and its extended studies suggest that psychological distance of a target influences an individual’s affect intensity toward the target (Trope and Liberman 2010), and construal level influences the valence of affect toward a target (Eyal et al. 2004, Lermer et al. 2015). These, in turn, are used as information to guide people’s decisions (Schwarz and Clore 1988, Loewenstein et al. 2001, Kahneman 2003, Slovic and Peters 2006). Specifically, with a high construal level, psychological distance leads individuals to focus more on the pros than the cons (Trope and Liberman 2003, 2010), which results in a more positive affect toward the target (Smith and Trope 2006) and more optimism toward the probability of obtaining payoffs (Nygren et al. 1996). In addition, psychological distance reduces the intensity of affect felt in situations (Wil liams and Bargh 2008, Williams et al. 2014). The effects of reduced affect intensity will compete against those of affect valence in impacting individuals’ decision making. In particular, when risk is involved, affect is a more important determinant of evaluation compared with cognitive factors; individuals’ assessment and downstream behavior rely more on their affective state than on mental cognition (Loewenstein et al. 2001). This is because using an overall affective impression can be easier and more efficient than weighing the pros and cons of an action (Zajonc 1980). Thus, the reduced affective intensity consequence of psychological distance is more pronounced than that of affect valence via construal level (Williams et al. 2014). Applying this notion to the current research, we expect that geographic distance and social distance will affect lenders bidding amounts primarily through decreasing the intensity of lenders’ affect toward the borrower.

In addition, geographic distance affects bidding amounts through shifting lenders’ hypothetical distance (Yan 2014). There is asymmetry between the spatial dimension and other dimensions of psychological distance, and a distal prime along the spatial dimension leads to greater perceived distance along the other dimensions—but not the other way around (Zhang and Wang 2009). In online P2P lending, lenders’ hypotheticality refers to their perceived probability of receiving financial returns on their bids, which can be reduced by geographic distance. Further, geographic distance induces lenders to sense familiarity with the borrower (Huberman 2001). This is consistent with the home bias effect found in previous studies on online P2P lending (Burtch et al. 2014, Lin and Viswanathan 2016). The home bias effect refers to the phenomenon wherein individuals prefer to lend to borrowers who are geographically close, even when the decision is not economically sensible (Lin and Viswanathan 2016). Hence, we propose that the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 1. Geographic distance between the lender and the borrower is negatively related to the lender’s bidding amount on the borrower’s loan listing.

Similarly, social distance reflecting interpersonal similarity in terms of attitudes, personality characteristics, or background variables has important implications for information processing about events related to others (Heider 2013). Specifically, it leads people to perceive a sense of “belongingness” or closeness between a perceiver and a target (Heider 2013). Research on intergroup relations and social categorization has consistently found that people care more about others in their in-group than those in their out-group (Flippen et al. 1996). Applying this notion to the P2P lending context, we expect that social distance’s in-group favoritism effect will have a negative influence on lenders bidding amounts. Indeed, such an impact has been observed in extant work. For instance, lenders favor individual borrowers who are socially proximate to them in aspects of race, occupation, and first name initial (Galak et al. 2011, Burtch et al. 2014). Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 2. Social distance between a lender and a borrower is negatively related to the lender’s bidding amount on the borrower’s loan listing.

## 3.3. Moderating Effects of Psychological Distance

Because of the risk involved in P2P lending, lenders decision making is not simply affected directly by the return promised by the interest rate of a loan listing. Instead, as mentioned previously, it is affected by lenders’ analytical evaluation of the duality of interest rates (i.e., return and risk signaled). Given that interest rate is an economic instrument that necessitates a cognitive assessment, psychological distance will affect interest rate’s effects on lenders’ bidding amounts pri marily through the cognitive process suggested by CLT. According to CLT, individuals’ psychological distance from events determines their construal of these events, which, in turn, influences their evaluation strategies (Liberman and Trope 1998; Trope and Liberman 2000, 2003). In particular, psychological distance leads individuals to focus more on the desirability (versus the feasibility) of the event—a conclusion that has received consistent empirical support (Liviatan et al. 2008).

Applying this notion to the current research context, we expect that geographic and social distance affect lenders’ evaluations of loan listings. In online P2P lending, interest rates serve as promised returns on investment and simultaneously signal possible borrower default (Stiglitz and Weiss 1981, Iyer et al. 2016). The former determines the value of a bidding’s endstate (i.e., the “why” aspect of the action—the desirability of the bidding), but the latter reflects the probability of reaching a bidding’s end-state (i.e., the “how” aspect of the action—the feasibility of the bidding). CLT suggests that greater geographic and social distance lead the lender to center more on the bidding’s returns (i.e., desirability of the bidding) and less on its default risk (i.e., feasibility of the bidding) (Liberman and Trope 1998, Liberman et al. 2007, Trope and Liberman 2010). That is, the salience of the interest rate of a loan listing as the rate of return is higher than that of the interest rate as the risk signal. Consequently, lenders sensing higher geographic distance or social distance have a more positive evaluation of the loan listing and submit a higher bidding amount, compared with their counterparts with lower geographic distance or social distance. Therefore, we propose the following hypotheses.

Hypothesis 3. The positive effect of interest rates on bidding amounts is strengthened by the geographic distance between the lender and the borrower.

Hypothesis 4. The positive effect of interest rates on bidding amounts is strengthened by the social distance between the lender and the borrower.

## 4. Data

To validate the previous research hypotheses empirically, we obtained data from Renrendai.com, one of the largest online P2P lending platforms in China. In 2018, despite the heightened regulatory environment and the bankruptcy and closures of platforms, the P2P lending sector still accounted for more than half (58.6%) of the global total. Hence, the Chinese P2P lending market is a major part of the global P2P lending market and is worth examining. The Internet finance authority (wdzj. com) in China has ranked Renrendai.com among the top two Chinese P2P lending platforms, along with PPdai.<sup>3</sup> Several studies have used data from this platform (Chen et al. 2020, Caglayan et al. 2021). Therefore, Renrendai.com serves as a suitable research context for online P2P lending in China.

Renrendai.com was founded in 2010, had approximately 50 million registered users when we collected data, and provided a total of 117.6 billion CNY (approximately \$18.1 billion) in funded loans as of December 2020.<sup>4</sup> The platform adopts an unsecured business model; hence, there is no collateralization mechanism. The platform implements high standards for loan approval; in fact, our consultation with Renrendai revealed that the approval rate of loan applications is only 4.17%. Its statistical 2020 report showed that the overall historical overdue principal above 90 days was 0.24%; furthermore, the platform treats loans with overdue repayments of more than 90 days as defaulting.

On Renrendai, borrowers post loan requests with a title, loan requested amount, annual interest rate, and credit score, among other details (see Figure A1 in the online appendix). Borrowers can also provide information about themselves, such as name, personal ID, cell phone number, gender, age, marital status, education, location, occupation, and income. In addition, Renrendai presents information on a borrower’s loan history, including the number of successfully funded loans. The platform calculates a credit score for each borrower based on the borrower’s borrowing/lending history, demographics, and the amount of verified information (i.e., national identification card, cell phone, and personal credit report issued by the People’s Bank of China).

A listing is typically open for several days. On the entry page, lenders can view dozens of listings, each with a loan title, annual interest rate, loan requested amount, and fulfillment progress. Loans are listed based on recency; that is, the latest posted loan is displayed at the top of the list. On the platform, lenders are unable to search, filter, or sort loans; they can only click on a particular loan request. After clicking on a loan, a potential lender can observe additional information, including the loan description/borrower demographics (see Figure A1 in the online appendix) and the entire bid list of the loan (see Figure A2 in the online appendix). Specifically, each record in Figure A2 in the online appendix presents a bid with information about a lender’s user ID, bidding amount, and bidding time. To bid on a loan, a lender can submit the bidding amount without minimal or maximal limitation, and the lender does not need to submit an interest rate, as it is solely determined by a borrower with certain judgment guidelines (e.g., annual statistics of IR) from the platform. Lenders are encouraged to bid in small amounts as a way of diversifying risks. As a result, a loan typically requires dozens of bids to become fully funded. Once fulfillment progress reaches 100% of funding status, the platform will immediately close the loan for extra bids. A loan that reaches 100% of funding status is a “successful” listing; otherwise, the borrower receives zero funding.

All successfully funded listings are forwarded to Renrendai staff for further review. Renrendai does not disclose the details of the review. Anecdotal evidence suggests that it routinely rejects borrowers who fail to obtain adequate identity verification or have overdue loans. Once a loan listing passes review, funds are transferred from lenders to the borrower, minus a 0.5%–1% service fee. Otherwise, the money is transferred back to lenders. The service fee depends on the loan duration. In subsequent months, borrowers are obligated to repay the principal and interest in monthly installments. Repayments are proportionally distributed to the lenders of the loan. If a repayment is overdue, Renrendai makes several attempts to recover the loan, including e-mailing, texting, and calling the borrower, and, in extreme cases, exposing the borrower’s identity online as a way of pressuring the borrower.

We designed a Java-based crawler for data collection, starting from the platform’s inception. The crawler first recorded all the loans on the platform’s homepage, then recorded all the information of each loan page by visiting the URLs (see Figures A1 and A2 in the online appendix). The crawler was executed daily, and new loans, as well as any changes to existing loans, were recorded in our database.

Our final data set included rich information, such as loan descriptions, borrowers’ demographics, lenders demographics, and the bid list for each loan. The platform offers automatic and manual approaches for lenders. In this study, we focused on bids that were manually decided by lenders, as such bids involved lenders’ economic and psychological evaluations. We used a 12-month period in 2015. Furthermore, we only included the first bid if there were multiple bids of a lender on a single loan in our final data set. Follow-up bids on a loan were mainly driven by a lender’s first bidding experience on the specific loan. We identified 1,867 lenders and 19,430 loans from 19,131 borrowers, with a total number of 29,025 bids.<sup>5</sup>

## 5. Model and Analysis 5.1. Empirical Model

Based on our final data set, we conducted our analysis at the lender-loan level and constructed all model variables. Let subscript i denote each individual lender in our data set, and subscript j denote each loan. Our dependent variable $B A _ { i j }$ indicates the bidding amount lender i made over loan $j .$ Lenders essentially solve an optimal investment allocation problem among the wide selection of listings offered, and the bidding amount of a listing reflects the marginal returns the lender anticipates earning from this loan listing (Zhang and Liu 2012).

There were three independent variables: $I R _ { j } , G D _ { i j } ,$ and $S D _ { i j } .$ Specifically, $I R _ { j }$ represents the interest rate offered by loan j in percentage format. $G D _ { i j }$ indicates the geographic distance between lender $i ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ location and the borrower’s location of loan $j .$ In our data set, geographical locations were at the city level. Based on the city names, we checked and obtained the longitude and latitude of a city’s municipal government through Baidu Map services. Then, we calculated $G D _ { i j }$ as the length of the shortest curve between lender $i \prime \mathrm { s }$ location and the borrower’s location of loan j using the geodist function in Stata (Vincenty 1975).<sup>6</sup> We log-transformed the resulting geographic distance measure to achieve normality before conducting regressions; we did so because of the skewed distribution $( s k e w n e s s = 0 . 5 7 0 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . We added one to the variable to avoid the log-transformation of zeros (McCune et al. 2002).

$S D _ { i j }$ indicates lender i’s subjective distance from the borrower of loan j in terms of age, level of education, and marital status. Age, level of education, and marital status are typically used to delineate social distance from self in comparisons of groups (Eveland et al. 1999). Thus, age was measured on a continuous scale, so we calculated age distance as the absolute value of lender i’s age minus the borrower of loan j’s age. Level of education was numerically coded as a four-point scale (i.e., 1 � high school and below; $2 = \mathrm { { h i g h } }$ school diploma; 3 � bachelor’s degree; 4 � master’s degree and above); therefore, educational distance was calculated as the absolute value of lender $i ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ education score minus the borrower of loan $j ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s }$ education score. Marital status was a binary variable (i.e., 1 � married; 0 � unmarried), so marital status distance was measured as one if lender i had the same marital status as the borrower of loan $j ;$ otherwise, it was zero. Because these distance variables were in different scales, we standardized them into variables ranging from zero to one. After that, $S D _ { i j }$ was measured as the mean of standardized values of age distance, educational distance, and marital status distance.<sup>7</sup>

Control variables were those identified in germane prior literature (Galak et al. 2011, Liu et al. 2015, Lin and Viswanathan 2016) and from available information in our data set. Accordingly, the control variables were as follows: (1) loan requested amount $( L A _ { j } ) _ { \ l }$ , (2) loan progress $( L P _ { i j } ) ,$ , and (3) number of prior existing bids $( P B _ { i j } )$ $L A _ { j }$ indicates the total requested amount of loan $j ; L P _ { i j } ,$ the fulfillment percentage of loan j before lender i’s bid; and $P B _ { i j } ,$ the number of prior bids that loan j had before lender $\dot { \iota } ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ bid. For loan $j ,$ our crawler recorded the entire bidder list, with the bidder’s name, bidding amount, and bidding time. After sorting according to bidding time, we calculated $L P _ { i j }$ and $P B _ { i j } .$ Additionally, we included control variables of lender i, as follows: (4) job income $( L J I _ { i } ) , ^ { 8 }$ (5) job experience $\left( L J E _ { i } \right) , ^ { 9 } \left( 6 \right)$ housing property $( L H P _ { i } ) ,$ , (7) housing mortgage (LHM<sub>i</sub>), (8) car property (LCP<sub>i</sub>), (9) car mortgage (LCM<sub>i</sub>), (10) lending experience in term of previous bidding transactions (LLE ), (11) number of previous loan applications $( L A L _ { i } )$ (12) number of previous successfully funded loans $( L F L _ { i } ) .$ , and (13) number of repaid loans $( L R L _ { i } )$ . Further, we controlled for the attributes of the borrower of loan j: (14) job income (BJI<sub>j</sub>), (15) job experience $( B J E _ { j } ) _ { j }$ (16) housing property $( B \dot { H } P _ { j } )$ , (17) housing mortgage (BHM<sub>j</sub>), (18) car property (BCP<sub>j</sub>), (19) car mortgage (BCM<sub>j</sub>), (20) number of previous loan applications $( B A L _ { j } ) ,$ , (21) number of previous successfully funded loans $( B F L _ { j } ) ,$ , (22) number of repaid loans $( \dot { B } R L _ { j } ) ,$ , (23) credit grade category dummies $( B C G _ { j } )$ (i.e., seven dummies that reflected a borrower’s credit grade at levels of AA (i.e., the highest rating), $\mathrm { A } , \mathrm { B } , \mathrm { C } , \mathrm { D } , \mathrm { E } ,$ , and HR $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ the lowest rating), and (24) loan purpose dummies $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ debt consolidation, home improvement, business, personal, and others). Presented in Table 1 are the list of variables and their descriptive statistics; shown in Table A2 in the online appendix are variable correlations.

To understand the direct impact of interest rates, geographic distance, and social distance on bidding amounts, we estimated a linear model of bidding amounts on all explanatory variables. The lender-loan linear model is specified in Equation (1):

$$
B A _ {i j} = \beta_ {1} I R _ {j} + \beta_ {2} G D _ {i j} + \beta_ {3} S D _ {i j} + \gamma X _ {i j} + \epsilon_ {i j},\tag{1}
$$

where $X _ { i j }$ comprises the sets of control variables, including those at the lender level, borrower level, and loan level. The variables $\beta$ and $\gamma$ are the model coeffi cients, and $\epsilon _ { i j }$ indicates the residual random error term.

## 5.3. Identification

Although the previous baseline results provided evidence supporting three of the four hypotheses, a concern with the previous analysis is the potential endogeneity issue. First, the data sample used in the previous analysis only included lenders’ successful bids and hence could have suffered from potential sample selection bias. Second, important relevant factors (i.e., risk tolerance and private financial knowledge) could have been excluded, which may have led to an omitted variable bias. Therefore, we applied multiple identification strategies to address these concerns. For ease of reference, below we only present results of focal variables while including all control variables during estimations.

5.3.1. Heckman Two-Stage Selection Model. Our main data sample that included all successful bids from lenders could also have been subject to potential selection bias. To address this issue, we used a Heckman twostage selection model. However, because of the unavailability of lenders’ browsing behaviors of loans on the platform, we manually constructed the loan selection process for each lender. For the 1,867 lenders, we constructed a potential pool of loans for each lender. To do so, we used the bidding time of a lender’s bid to find the available loans when the lender was browsing the Renrendai platform. Specifically, these loans—which began earlier than the bidding time of the lender and were still open for bidding (i.e., the percentage of loan progress was still below 100%)—were the potential loans from which the lender could choose. This operation provided the proximate loan selection process. We combined these loan selection observations with those used in the previous analysis. Finally, we had a bidding data sample of 333,695 observations, with 45,256 loans from 44,527 borrowers. Specifically, we created a variable $I S \_ B D _ { i j }$ to indicate whether lender i bid on loan $j ,$ with one indicating that BA is above zero, and zero otherwise. For the 1,876 lenders, the minimal number and the maximal number of available loans were 1 and 21, respectively, with an average of 6.560.

First, we included the set of studied variables and control variables in the baseline model, as these variables had been justified in shaping bidding decisions in the literature. For instance, Liu et al. (2015) documented the influences of loan progress and number of prior bids; Lin et al. (2013) presented the influences of borrowers’ hard credit information—such as credit grade and financial information—on bidding probability. 5.3.2. Instrumental Variable. Although the Heckman two-stage selection model addressed the potential selection bias, our baseline results could still have suffered from potential omitted variable bias. For instance, lenders are likely to decide on bidding amounts based on their risk tolerance (i.e., risk seeking versus risk aversion), personal financial literacy, and private discrimination tendency, which we did not observe. These omitted, unobservable factors may have biased our focal estimates of IR, GD, and SD if they were not accounted for.

To address this concern, we applied the instrumental variable (IV) approach (Heckman 1997). The main advantage of using IVs to address the potential endogeneity issue is that we could address the issue without identifying the specific sources of endogeneity (i.e., omitted variable bias or selection bias). Using IVs could attend to the potential endogeneity in terms of omitted variable bias and selection bias, among others. Ideal IVs would be factors that shift IR, GD, and SD, but not BA.

Specifically, IR of a loan was self-decided by a borrower with certain judgment guidelines (i.e., annual statistics of IR) from the platform; it reflected the financial cost of the borrower if the loan was successfully funded. Hence, it was mostly exogenous. A lender can submit a bid if IR was unchangeable once set; hence, there was a temporal order between IR and bidding amounts; reversed causality from bidding amounts to IR was unlikely to be the primary concern of endogeneity in our context. Conversely, omitted variable bias— the other potential source of endogeneity—arises when some factors that are not included in the regression equation influence lenders’ bidding amounts and correlate with borrowers’ IR. For instance, a borrower may refer to the interest rates of existing loans when decid ing the IR, and a lender may also browse IR for all available open loans before bidding on a specific loan. Unfortunately, researchers cannot observe a borrower’s or a lender’s browsing behavior on the platform. A common approach to deal with endogeneity is the instrumental variable estimation approach. In this approach, we needed to find variables that satisfied the inclusion and exclusion conditions (i.e., were correlated with potentially endogenous variables but were uncorrelated with the error term (or the dependent variable)). This, however, was a challenging task—which in many situations is extremely difficult or impossible to do— and often lacks additional data.

For IR, we used a latent-variable method (LVM) pro posed by Lewbel (2012) that enables the orthogonal construction of instruments from variables specified as exogenous in the model’s data, when a traditional and theoretically motivated instrument is unavailable. It has been successfully applied in prior studies (Kashyap and Murtha 2017, Wang et al. 2022). The identification comes from having regressors uncorrelated with the product of heteroscedastic errors, which is shown to be a feature of many models in which error correlations are due to an unobserved common factor, such as unobserved ability in returns to schooling models or measurement error in mismeasured regressor models (Lewbel 2012).

In our model, we treated borrower’s age, borrower’s educational degree, borrower’s marital status, and borrower’s applied loans as exogenous and constructed instruments for the endogenous term of IR. First, these four variables reflected personal finance capabilities and borrowing experience of a lender, and they may determine the interest rate (i.e., the financial cost the lender offers that is set for the current loan). Second, these four variables were personal characteristics that were set before initiating a loan, and hence they were exogenous. The intuition behind this approach was that the generated instruments (z<sub>j</sub>) could be constructed from the product of the mean-centered form of those exogenous variables (x<sub>j</sub>) and the residuals obtained from the “first-stage” regression: $z _ { j } = ( x _ { j } - \underline { { x } } ) \cdot \varepsilon ,$ , where ε. is the vector of residuals from the “first-stage” regression of each endogenous regressor on all exogenous regressors, including a constant vector. Accordingly, we mean-centered all exogenous variables to generate instruments for IR.

Regarding geographic distance, we used the elevation distance between the lender i’s city and the borrower’s city of loan $j \left( E L D _ { i j } \right)$ as an IV. If two cities were close to each other, they likely had similar landforms and thus similar elevations. In contrast, two distant cities in different locations likely had different landforms and elevations. This observation is especially true in China, which has a large territory. Hence, we believed that this IV would correlate with GD, but may not correlate with BA. ELD may serve as a valid IV for GD. We obtained the elevation information of the cities in our data sample from the Ministry of Natural Resources of the People’s Republic of China.<sup>10</sup> Then, we calculated ELD as the absolute value of elevation difference between the lender i’s city and the borrower of loan j’s city.

Likewise, we used the population density information of each city in our sample as an IV for SD. First, areas with high population density are usually located in more developed regions in China. These areas are attractive for young people in which to live and work and thus contain a large proportion of the young population. Hence, population density is correlated with population age. Second, education resources are highly associated with population density in China (Liu and Chen 2017). People from areas with high population density (which are typically more developed regions) have more access to education resources; thus, they are more likely to obtain a higher level of education. Third, marriage opportunities are highly correlated with population density. People living in areas with high popu lation density have more opportunities to meet their marital partner. Therefore, population density distance serves as a valid IV for SD (which can be captured by distance in education, age, and marital status). Hence, we obtained population and acreage size data of cities in 2014 from the official statistics bureaus of each city. Then, we calculated the population density of each city by dividing the population size by the acreage size. Next, we computed the population density difference $( P D D _ { i j } )$ as the absolute value of the lender i’s city population density minus the population density of the bor rower of loan j’s city.

Given that we had instrument variables, we performed two-stage least squares (2SLS) regressions with the above IVs. The results are summarized in Table 4, columns (1), (2), and (3). As indicated, the estimates of IR, GD, and SD were consistent with the baseline results. Last, we included the two interaction terms in column (4) and found consistent results of the two positive interaction effects.

## 5.4. Post Hoc Analysis on Social Distance

5.4.1. Subdimension Analysis on Social Distance. 5.4.2. Other Types of Social Distance. Because all three subdimensions of social distance examined were related to differences in terms of social status—referred to as status social distance—we further investigated the effects of social distance caused by monetary differences between lenders and borrowers (i.e., differences in job income, housing property, and car property)—namely, monetary social distance. The results are reported columns (1) to (3) in Table 6. Job income and housing property distance had a significant negative effect on bidding amounts. Although not significant, the effect of car property distance was also negative. Additionally, by making monetary social distance a second-order variable calcu lated as the means of the standardized values of its three subdimensions, we found its negative effect on bidding amounts (as reported in column (4)),<sup>13</sup> which was different from the positive impact of status social distance in the main analysis (as shown in column (5)). To unveil the influencing mechanism of monetary social distance, we conducted a controlled experiment on job income distance and report the results in Online Appendix E. Findings showed that job income distance decreased the bidding amounts.<sup>14</sup> Also, we found that the negative effects of upward and downward job income distance on the bidding amounts were mediated by envy toward and contempt against the borrower, respectively.

## 5.6. Post Hoc Experimental Studies

Although we addressed endogeneity issues using various econometric methods—such as the Heckman two-stage selection model and instrumental variable approach—we next conducted four experiments to establish further the causality and unveil the underlying mechanism of the previously identified relationships. In all experiments, we asked participants to decide how much they would bid on the loan out of an idle amount of 1,000 CNY, as a proxy for bidding amounts.

Specifically, we studied social distance at the subdimension level, including age distance, educational distance, and marriage status distance. The experiments confirmed the direct effects of geographic distance and three types of social distances on the bidding amounts that were observed in the baseline model. Furthermore, the experimental results revealed that perceived familiarity toward the borrower mediated the negative relationship between geographic distance and the bidding amounts, while perceived control over and credibility of the borrower mediated the positive relationship between downward age/educational distance and the bidding amounts, and between upward age/ educational distance and the bidding amounts, respectively. However, the mediating effects of perceived control and perceived credibility on the relationship between marital status distance and bidding amounts were not found. We elaborate on the detailed designs and results of the experiments in Online Appendix E.

## 6. Discussion and Contributions 6.1. Discussion of Findings

This research delves into the factors affecting individual lenders’ decision making in online P2P lending, which is considered highly risky. Drawing on the P2P lending literature and CLT (Trope and Liberman 2003, 2010), we propose that a lender’s bidding amount is affected jointly by both economic (i.e., interest rates offered by the loan listing) and psychological distance caused by the borrower’s geographic location, age, education level, and marital status relative to those of the lender. While acknowledging the primary influence of interest rate for bidding decision, we intend to investigate the secondary factor of psychological distance and its moderations on the influence of interest rate.

Using a rich data set from Renrendai, various identification strategies, and estimation methods, we found that geographic distance has a negative direct effect on bidding amounts, whereas social distance has a positive effect on bidding amounts. The finding on negative effects of geographic distance confirms the home bias effect, which has been identified at an aggregate level (e.g., a metropolitan area, a loan level) by prior research (Burtch et al. 2014, Lin and Viswanathan 2016). The surprising finding of positive direct effects of social distance enticed us to explore the effects of other types of social distance. The results reveal that status and monetary social distance have different effects on bidding amounts. According to a controlled experiment, a probable explanation for the negative effects of monetary social distance is that upward and downward comparisons aroused by monetary social status increase lenders’ envy toward and contempt against the borrower, respectively, which corrupts lenders. This finding is also supported by the social psychology literature on envying up and scorning down (Fiske 2010). When lenders feel that they are socially below the borrower, they may feel ashamed of their own inadequacy. Likewise, feeling socially above can desensitize lenders to the needs of the borrower (Smith 2008). As such, high monetary social distance could contaminate lenders’ evaluations of loan listings and negatively influence their bidding amounts.

Furthermore, we found that the positive effects of interest rates on lenders’ bidding amounts are strengthened by both geographic and social distance; these findings are consistent with what CLT suggests. Our results confirm that psychological distance affects individual lenders’ evaluations of the duality of interest rates by shifting more weight to interest rates as rates of return versus risks signaled. Consequently, lenders sensing higher geographic distance or social distance have a more positive evaluation of the loan listing and submit a higher bidding amount.

## 6.2. Theoretical Contributions

The current research makes three major theoretical contributions. First, as one of the first attempts to study online P2P lending from the perspective of individual lenders, our undertaking provides a nuanced understanding of the duality of interest rates in online P2P lending. Although previous studies have found that interest rates offered by a loan listing—as promised rates of return on investment—is the primary driver for lending behaviors (Zhang and Liu 2012, Liu et al. 2015), little attention has been paid to the possible influence of interest rates as a signal of potential risks (Stiglitz and Weiss 1981). We address this issue by considering when each dimension of interest rates would become more salient in influencing lenders’ behaviors. We theorize that psychological distance caused by the borrower’s demographics (i.e., geographic location and social attributes), relative to those of the lender, leads lenders to focus more on interest rates as rates of return and care less about their signaling of potential risk. The denouement is that they submit a higher bidding amount for the same level of a promised interest rate.

Second, the current research enriches understanding of human decision making under risk in general and in online P2P lending in particular. Given that online P2P lending is characterized as highly risky, it warrants a systemic investigation of the role played by both economic and behavioral factors (Schwarz and Clore 1988, Loewenstein et al. 2001, Kahneman 2003, Slovic and Peters 2006). Prior research has been conducted primarily from the perspectives of financial factors or social factors at an aggregate level (Zhang and Liu 2012, Lin et al. 2013, Burtch et al. 2014, Wei and Lin 2016, Jiang et al. 2020). Little attention has been given to how lenders’ bidding amounts are simultaneously affected by both economic and behavioral factors embedded in the loan context. Our findings confirm that lenders determine their bidding amounts through dual information processing (i.e., analytical and experiential evaluation) (Tversky and Kahneman 1974, Loewenstein et al. 2001) in which the interplay of interest rates and psychological distance exerts influence. Such understanding of individual lenders’ decision making is particularly important to advance knowledge of individuals’ decision making in a context involving risk. As shown in the Hackman two-stage selection model, interest rate is confirmed as the primary driver and matters for the whole bidding decision making process of whether to bid and how much to bid, while psychological distance factors only function in the latter one. This finding again underlines the joint effects of interest rate and psychological distance on lenders’ bidding amounts.

Third, we unveil that different types of psychological distance have various direct effects on individuals lending behaviors. Our empirical analysis shows that borrowers’ demographic information relative to that of lenders affects lenders’ bidding amounts, as such information automatically activates psychological distance, influences lenders’ construal of and affect toward loan listings, and subsequently shapes their bidding behaviors. As the first attempt to investigate how status social distance—together with monetary social distance in our robustness checks—affects individuals’ online P2P lending behaviors, our work complements previous studies that are primarily focused on finding the existence of effects of geographic distance and social distance in online P2P lending (Lin et al. 2013, Lin and Viswanathan 2016). We find that the three subdimensions of status social distance have different direct effects on bidding amounts from that of geographic and monetary social distance (e.g., job income distance) due to different types of emotion aroused (i.e., familiarity, envy, and contempt) in our post hoc experimental study. Going beyond theorizing and empirically investigating how psychological distance affects individuals’ decision making through construal level (i.e., through a cognitive path) (Liberman and Trope 1998, Bar-Anan et al. 2006) and affect valence and intensity (i.e., through an affective path) (Williams et al. 2014) in online P2P lending, we extend this stream of research by revealing that the types of emotion aroused by different psychological distance would directly influence individuals’ behaviors in varying directions and through different mechanisms. Therefore, our work offers additional insights to CLT by bringing in the perspective of emotion dimensions (i.e., type, valence, and intensity) in explaining the outcomes of psychological distances and opening a new avenue for research to examine the intricacy of different types/dimensions of psychological distance’s direct effects on individual behaviors.

## 6.3. Practical Implications

The findings of this research have three major practical implications. First, we find that soft information embedded in the loan context has significant effects on lending behaviors. It reveals that platforms should deploy mechanisms ensuring the accuracy of such soft information, especially when soft information is self-reported by borrowers (Iyer et al. 2016). In addition, given that geographic distance reduces bidding amounts, platform managers can alleviate such home bias by selecting an appropriate granularity of data on borrowers’ geographic locations and implementing a design that can address the challenge of the home bias effect—such as by making loans from distant borrowers more conspicuous in the loan list (Lin and Viswanathan 2016).

Second, our finding on status social distance’s positive effect on bidding amounts helps alleviate platform managers’ concerns about the general “knowledge gap” problem (Bonfadelli 2002) and the specific digital divide issue in which individuals with higher social status have the advantage of using the Internet to achieve their financial goals (Burtch and Chan 2019). Also, platforms could design certain loan recommendation methods by considering the social distance between borrowers and lenders. For instance, when loan requests are coming from under-privileged groups with lower educational levels, platforms can first recommend these loans to potential lenders who have higher educational levels. Doing so can help the loan listings attract higher bidding amounts and achieve improved fundraising outcomes.

Third, our research has implications for individual lenders in online P2P lending. As our research revealed, lenders’ decision making is affected by soft data reported by borrowers or the platform. Though interest rates are the primary factor determining lenders’ bidding amounts, potential risks signaled by interest rates could be underestimated by psychologically distant lenders. Also, the home bias and social distance effect found in our research show that geographic and social distance can lead lenders to become irrational and make decisions that are not economically sensible. As such, lenders need to be aware of possible “noise” that affects their decision making. Doing so would enable lenders to achieve investment outcomes that they will not regret.

## 6.4. Limitations and Opportunities for Future Research

This research has two limitations. First, our analysis was based on a secondary data set from one single online P2P lending platform (i.e., Renrendai). Empirical analysis based on secondary data are likely to suffer from a potential endogeneity issue. Although we attempted to control for many factors at various levels—applied multiple identification strategies, performed a series of robustness checks to derive rigorous research findings, and conducted several post hoc controlled experiments—possibly we did not fully control for all potential sources of endogeneity bias. Further, our data set included only successful transactions and thus lacked information on lenders’ loan selection process. We were unable to replicate ou findings by studying bidding probability, although it has been widely studied in the literature (Agrawal et al. 2015, Liu et al. 2015, Lin and Viswanathan 2016). Also, our findings based on this single source of data should be generalized with caution. In light of the cultural differences in different regions, individuals could be sensitive to economic and social factors in distinct ways. As such, future scholars can use other data from different platforms located in other regions to check the reliability of the current study’s findings.

Second, our study focused on manual bids that were the outcome of lenders’ on-site decision making. However, robot-advisor services are widely adopted by platforms to simplify their lenders’ investment decisions (i.e., automatic biddings) (Ge et al. 2021). Typi cally, these services use algorithms to help lenders determine how to invest based on their risk preferences, budget, and investment goals, thus augmenting lender intelligence in a personalized manner. Therefore, comparing how interest rates and psychological distance affect bidding amounts submitted manually versus automatically may well be a promising direction for future research.

## 7. Conclusion

This research theorizes and empirically tests how economic and behavioral factors jointly affect lenders bidding amounts in online P2P lending. We contend that the effect of duality of interest rates—namely, as promised rates of return on investment and signal of default risks on bidding amounts—is shaped by lenders’ psychological distance automatically activated by borrowers’ demographic information, relative to that of lenders. Indeed, we find that the positive effect of interest rates on bidding amounts is strengthened by both geographic and social distance. Also, geographic distance and social distance have negative and positive effects on bidding amounts, respectively. This paper complements and enriches existing literature by providing insights into how individual lenders decide the bidding amounts, which involves both analytical and experiential evaluation—an area that has been neglected in the context of online P2P lending. Our efforts also open new avenues for future research on how various types of psychological distance and different dimensions of social distance would affect individual behaviors in general and decision making under risk in particular.
