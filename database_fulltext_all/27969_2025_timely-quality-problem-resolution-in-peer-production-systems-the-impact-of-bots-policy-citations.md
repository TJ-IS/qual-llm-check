# Timely Quality Problem Resolution in Peer-Production Systems: The Impact of Bots, Policy Citations, and Contributor Experience

Vitali Mindel,<sup>a</sup> Aleksi Aaltonen,<sup>b,</sup>\* Arun Rai,<sup>c</sup> Lars Mathiassen,<sup>c</sup> Wael Jabr<sup>d</sup>

Abstract. Although online peer-production systems have proven to be effective in producing high-quality content, their open call for participation makes them susceptible to ongoing quality problems. A key concern is that the problems should be addressed quickly to prevent low-quality content from remaining in place for extended periods. We examine the impacts of two control mechanisms, bots and policy citations, and the number of contri butors, with and without prior experience in editing an article, on the cleanup time of 4,473 quality problem events in Wikipedia. We define cleanup time as the time it takes to resolve a quality problem once it has been detected in an article. Using an accelerated failure time model, we find that the number of bots editing an article during a quality problem event has no effect on cleanup time; that citing policies to justify edits during the event is associated with a longer cleanup time; and that more contributors, with or without prior experience in editing the article, are associated with a shorter cleanup time. We also find important interactions between each of the two control mechanisms and the number of different types of contributors. There is a marginal increase in cleanup time that is larger when an increase in the number of contributors is accompanied by fewer bots editing the article during a quality problem event. This interaction effect is more pronounced when increasing the number of contributors without prior experience in editing the article. Further, there is a marginal decrease in cleanup time that is larger when an increase in the number of contributors, with or without prior experience in editing the article, is accompanied by fewer policy citations. Taken together, our results show that the use of bots and policy citations as control mechanisms must be considered in conjunction with the number of contributors with and without prior experience in editing an article. Accordingly, the number of contributors and their experience alone may not explain important outcomes in peer production; it is also important to find an appropriate mix of different control mechanisms and types of contributors to address quality problems quickly.

## 1. Introduction

Wikis, open-source software development, and community question-answering forums represent an important alternative form of production known as peer production that differs from traditional firm-based production (von Hippel and von Krogh 2003, Baldwin and von Hippel 2011, Levine and Prietula 2014, Greenstein and Zhu 2016). In contrast to the rigorous quality control and punctuated product releases that are typical in firm-based production, online peer-production systems harness volunteer contributors, algorithmic agents, and community-generated rules to constantly update and refine their content. Empirical evidence shows that peer-produced content can be of comparable quality to content that is produced by traditional editorial processes, yet studies also show that various problems emerge in open content production (Giles 2005, Chesney 2006, Brown 2011). To explain the capability of peer-production systems to generate and maintain high-quality content, prior research has examined their emerging organizational structures (Kittur et al. 2009, Baldwin and von Hippel 2011, Arazy et al. 2020), participation dynamics (Ransbotham and Kane 2011, Kane and Ransbotham 2016, Zheng et al. 2023), content production (Faraj et al. 2011, Kane et al. 2014, Levine and Prietula 2014), and governance (Shah 2006, Markus 2007, Forte et al. 2009, Aaltonen and Lanzara 2015, Mindel et al. 2018).

How peer-production systems manage quality continues to be of great interest to information systems research and practice. Misinformation proliferates rapidly in open systems, which makes quality control a constant challenge and often necessitates the use of algorithmic agents to manage large-scale content production (Guo et al. 2020, Greenstein et al. 2021). Studies show that open-source software may involve contaminated data and buggy code (Stamelos et al. 2002, Howison and Crowston 2004, Gyimothy et al. 2005) and that Wikipedia articles suffer from subpar edits and vandalism (Hu et al. 2007, Wilkinson and Huberman 2007, Greenstein et al. 2021). Failure to effectively address quality problems can have serious consequences because, for instance, open-source software is constantly used by millions of individuals and organizations (Lenarduzzi et al. 2020), and low-quality code can be hacked and misused by criminals (Ransbotham 2010, Russell et al. 2018). Similarly, peer-produced content is sometimes found to be incomplete, manipulated, or erroneous, which can lead to biased decisions among its users (Holman Rector 2008, Kupferberg and Protus 2011, Lavasa et al. 2011, Greenstein and Zhu 2012).

In stark contrast to content produced by traditional publishers, peer-produced content is typically made available with little or no quality control in advance and relies on the hope that a large community of contributors will quickly notice and fix any issues, according to Linus’s law (Raymond 1998, Greenstein and Zhu 2016). Fixing quality problems in a timely manner is critical because millions of people rely on peer-produced content for learning and decision-making in various industries such as education, research, healthcare, journalism, and even in the legal system (Shaw 2008, Fessakis and Zoumpatianou 2012, Knight and Pryke 2012, Bould et al. 2014, Suwannakhan et al. 2020, Thompson et al. 2024). Also, peer-produced content feeds further content production (Aaltonen and Seiler 2016, Zheng et al. 2023) and is used to train artificial intelligence (AI) systems (Gertner 2023). The use of Wikipedia by a wide range of professionals and as training data for AI illustrates the potential for cascading—broken-phone problems caused by low-quality peer-produced content.<sup>1</sup> Quality problems also draw attention and resources away from productive work because contributors direct their efforts toward putting out fires instead of creating new content, thereby disrupting the workflow.

The longer low-quality content remains in place, the more it can harm the reputation of a peer-production system (Javanmardi and Lopes 2010) and diminish the capability of the system to maintain its contributor base (Aaltonen and Wattal 2020). Researchers have noted how the timeliness of content production (Fallis 2008, Mamykina et al. 2011, Bosu et al. 2013) and quality control (Geiger and Halfaker 2013, Juha´sz et al. 2020) are essential to peer production; for these reasons, addressing quality problems in a timely manner can be an important success factor for a peer-production system. However, most quality control mechanisms found in firm-based production do not apply to peer production characterized by open, voluntary, and fluid organizing (Faraj et al. 2011, Tiwana 2015, Aaltonen and Wattal 2020). The current literature has examined separately the impact of specific mechanisms available in peer production (Butler et al. 2008, Wessel et al. 2018) and contributor experience (Kittur and Kraut 2008, Arazy et al. 2011) on the substantive quality of content (Greenstein and Zhu 2012), but we do not know how different factors affect how quickly quality problems are addressed as they appear in peer-produced content.

Resolving quality problems can be challenging in firm-based production with managerial authority and even more so in peer-production systems in which participants self-assign tasks and can pick and choose what to work on and when and how to work (Faraj et al. 2011). However, it has been shown that a stable peer-production system that is capable of producing high-quality content can emerge from fluidity and lack of structure at the individual level (Faraj et al. 2011, Aaltonen and Lanzara 2015, Mindel et al. 2018). Arazy et al. (2016) characterize this as “turbulent stability” to explain how “a highly stable set of emergent roles” can appear “despite the absence of traditional stabilizing mechanisms” (p. 792) in peer production. An important factor contributing to such turbulent stability is the mix of distinct contributions and issues associated with experienced and inexperienced contributors (Anthony et al. 2009; Javanmardi and Lopes 2010; Arazy et al. 2011, 2020; Halfaker et al. 2011; Kane 2011). Experienced contributors are the backbone of peer-production systems, providing continuity, contextual understanding, and stability. However, natural attrition requires that peer-production systems must constantly attract new contributors who inject new energy and ideas into content production but may also cause problems due to their unfamiliarity with the system and its practices.

In addition to human contributors, algorithmic agents such as software robots, or bots, also engage in solving problems in peer production (Beschastnikh et al. 2008, Stvilia et al. 2008, Anthony et al. 2009, Morgan and Zachry 2010, Kane 2011, Aaltonen and Lanzara 2015, Wessel et al. 2018, Hukal et al. 2019). Bots are useful for noncreative tasks, whereas human contributors are needed for creative work. Ideally, this creates a complementary arrangement where algorithmic agents perform grunt work, allowing humans to focus on the creative aspects of peer production. Bots carry out tasks that directly enforce system policies, but these policies are also upheld through discussions of production practices, relationships among participants, and other content-related matters (Beschastnikh et al. 2008, Ren et al. 2018, Piezunka and Dahlander 2019). For instance, to justify edits or challenge others contributions, Wikipedia contributors routinely cite specific policies, such as those governing copyrighted content or violations of neutrality. Finally, the interactions between the different types of contributors and the two control mechanisms—bots and policy citations—add a further layer of complexity to quality control. For instance, experienced contributors may be more sensitive to policy citations because of their familiarity with system policies and practices, whereas inexperienced contributors may rely more on bots for guidance and corrections. These interactions can significantly impact the timeliness and quality of problem resolution in peer-production systems.

Presently, we know little about the impacts of the two control mechanisms (bots and policy citations) and contributor experience (in editing an article) on the timeliness of quality problem resolution, nor about the interaction effects between the mechanisms and experienced and inexperienced contributors. This leads us to ask the following questions. (i) What are the individual impacts of the two types of control mechanisms (bots and policy citations) and the two types of contributors (those with and without experience in editing the article) on the timeliness of quality problem resolution? (ii) What are the interaction effects of the two control mechanisms and two types of contributors on the timeliness of quality problem resolution? To address these questions, we study the timeliness of quality problem resolution in Wikipedia. Based on an accelerated failure time model of 4,473 quality problem events, we find important differences in how the control mechanisms and contributors affect the timeliness of quality problem resolution individually and interactively. Overall, the results suggest that the effective resolution of quality problems hinges on the integration of bots and the careful calibration of policy citations, tailored to the different levels of experience among contributors. Quality control must leverage the strengths of different contributors and harness the benefits of technological support and adaptive policy frameworks to improve productivity and achieve high-quality outcomes in a timely manner.

## 2. Peer-Production Systems

Peer-production systems are “online environments that support the collective production of an artifact through a technologically mediated collaboration platform that presents a low barrier to entry and exit and supports the emergence of persistent but malleable social structures” (Forte and Lampe 2013, p. 57). The systems create value by enabling people to participate in the production of a common good and to build freely on each other’s work while establishing just enough structure to keep the system going (Kumar et al. 2011, Aaltonen and Kallinikos 2013, Levine and Prietula 2014, Arazy et al. 2016).

## 2.1. Quality of Peer-Produced Conten

The intangible nature of peer-produced content and the fact that peer-produced artifacts continuously change through updates and edits (Kallinikos et al. 2013) make the quality of their content and the overall consumption experience a moving target for assessment. Our review of the literature, summarized in Online Appendix 1, reveals a variety of ways to conceptualize and measure quality in peer production, particularly in Wikipedia.

Although quality in Wikipedia is usually understood as article quality (Giles 2005, Clauson et al. 2008, Kittur and Kraut 2008, Kane 2011, Greenstein and Zhu 2016, Kane and Ransbotham 2016, Arazy et al. 2020), studies also assess the quality of contributors (Chesney 2006, Hu et al. 2007, Anthony et al. 2009) and of individual revisions (Anthony et al. 2009, Javanmardi and Lopes 2010, Halfaker et al. 2011). As to the specific metrics, researchers have used Wikipedia’s internal quality grades (Wilkinson and Huberman 2007, Blumenstock 2008, Kittur and Kraut 2008, Wohner ¨ and Peters 2009, Kane 2011, Liu and Ram 2011, Arazy et al. 2020); small-scale evaluations of select articles by experts (Giles 2005, Chesney 2006, Clauson et al. 2008, Arazy and Nov 2010, Arazy et al. 2011, Kane and Ransbotham 2016) or by students (De la Calzada and Dekhtyar 2010, Arazy and Kopak 2011, Lewandowski and Spree 2011), and algorithmic approaches to assess the substantive quality of articles (Anthony et al. 2007; Halfaker et al. 2011; Greenstein and Zhu 2012, 2016; Dang and Ignat 2016; Kane and Ransbotham 2016; Lewoniewski et al. 2016). These include the use of automated tools to detect errors in code (Stamelos et al. 2002) and to interpret various quality indicators in Wikipedia articles (Dalip et al. 2011) such as cleanup tags, by using machine learning models (Anderka et al. 2012, Wang and Li 2020), and the computational Objective Revision Evaluation Service (ORES) in Wikipedia to estimate the quality of content at the level of edits or articles (Arazy et al. 2020).

The literature paints a multifaceted picture of the quality issues faced by Wikipedia, yet differences in the ways quality is conceptualized and measured make it difficult to synthesize findings across studies into a compact summative account of how to achieve high quality of content. Most studies assess the quality of peer-produced content only at a specific point in time, typically when an article is deemed to have become stable and of high quality. For instance, featured article status is often used as a proxy for high quality in Wikipedia. The challenge with this snapshotin-time approach is that, unlike traditional product releases, peer-produced content is constantly evolving and may never become stable. The quality of a content item may also deteriorate over time because of a series of low-quality contributions or a lack of updates. Hence, although research tells us which factors impact the quality of peer-produced content up to a certain point in time, it provides limited insight into the factors that affect how quickly quality control operates over time. Among the few exceptions are Greenstein and Zhu (2012, 2016) and Greenstein et al. (2021), who measure an evolving “slant” of how an article deviates from a neutral point of view over time; and Geiger and Halfaker (2013) who use time-to-revert as a measure of quality problem resolution performance. Nevertheless, the evolving nature of content in general and the timeliness of quality problem resolution in particular have received little attention in the literature.

## 2.2. Control Mechanisms

Peer-production systems are open systems whose productivity is premised on the availability of selfselecting participants who voluntarily engage to create and maintain digital content (Benkler 2006, Fara et al. 2011). The openness of the systems entails that they have limited capacity to deploy quality control mechanisms that are usually found in firm-based production (Boudreau 2010, Tiwana 2015, Aaltonen and Wattal 2020). Instead, peer-production systems typically depend on the workforce itself to manage quality. The systems draw contributors’ attention to potential problems in content production and configure themselves so that their contributors can easily step in and take care of quality problems as they arise (Aaltonen and Lanzara 2015, Mindel et al. 2018). This reflects what is known as Linus’s law: “Given enough eyeballs, all bugs are shallow” (Raymond 1998), for which Greenstein and Zhu (2016) found partial empirical support in Wikipedia.

In their early stages, peer-production systems may be managed through the direct communication of praise and criticism among peers but, as the systems grow, they tend to develop various governance mechanisms to manage the efforts of a large number of volunteers (Butler et al. 2008, Forte et al. 2009). The mechanisms are typically based on evolving policies developed by long-term community members, and it is thus important to consider how the policies are brought to bear upon the resolution of quality problems in articles. The focus of studies examining peer production policies has mostly been on their role in creating order and reinforcing community norms (Beschastnikh et al. 2008, Morgan and Zachry 2010, Aaltonen and Lanzara 2015), but little is known about the mechanisms by which policies affect the timeliness of quality problem resolution. To this end, we consider two mechanisms that are commonly used to enact policies in the peer production of content: Algorithmic agents and policy citations. Algorithmic agents such as Wikipedia bots play an increasingly important role in governing many peer-production systems (Wessel et al. 2018, Hukal et al. 2019, Zheng et al. 2019). Bots are program scripts devised to uphold system policies automatically or semiautomatically. They afford routine attention to potential quality issues at scale and are instrumental in mundane tasks such as fixing grammar and spelling and reverting vandalism. Bots can also offer guidance to contributors in the form of links to resources and policies (Ren et al. 2018). It has been estimated that 22 of the top 25 most active editors by volume of edits in Wikipedia are bots (Geiger 2017).

In addition to bots, another important control mechanism is the citing of policies. Contributors may refer to policies in comments attached to their edits to justify changes and to guide future contributors. A policy citation reminds peer producers of expected standards such as keeping a neutral tone or avoiding copyright violations (Beschastnikh et al. 2008). As a quality control mechanism, policy citations align with the principles of polycentric governance that underpin peer production in general and Wikipedia in particular (Aaltonen and Lanzara 2015, Mindel et al. 2018). They promote shared accountability in enforcing system policies and facilitate learning (Ren et al. 2018, Piezunka and Dahlander 2019). To summarize, the research acknowledges that policies are an important part of the organizational fabric of peer production, but little is known about how they are brought to bear upon the timeliness of quality problem resolution through bots and policy citations.

## 2.3. Contributor Experience

In addition to the availability of bots and policy citations as control mechanisms, the mix of different types of contributors is a recurring theme that has been shown to impact content quality in peer production (Arazy et al. 2011, 2020; Halfaker et al. 2011; Morgan and Halfaker 2018). Unlike traditional production systems, in which the workforce is organized into a hierarchy of formal roles based on their skills and experience, peer-production systems are often much more fluid arrangements as contributors are allowed to join, make contributions, or stop contributing at any time (Faraj et al. 2011, Aaltonen and Lanzara 2015, Arazy et al. 2016).

Previous research differentiates between several categories of contributors, including administrators, long-time or regular contributors, newcomers, specialists, generalists, repeats, and one-timers, all of whom need to coexist in a delicate balance for a peerproduction system to function properly (Anthony et al. 2009; Javanmardi and Lopes 2010; Arazy et al. 2011, 2020; Halfaker et al. 2011; Kane 2011). The balance is delicate because different types of contributors are needed to sustain the system, but at the same time, the diversity of contributors and lack of managerial authority can give rise to tensions. Administrators are typically needed to help maintain order; long-timers supply experience and expertise; specialists provide needed focus; generalists contribute in many ways to the ongoing effort; and new contributors inject new perspectives and fresh energy into content production (Anthony et al. 2009, Arazy et al. 2011, Mindel et al. 2018). However, new contributors are also prone to making mistakes and breaking rules, which disrupts the workflow (Beschastnikh et al. 2008, Morgan and Zachry 2010). This, in turn, irritates experienced contributors who may, often unwittingly, alienate newcomers and cause them to quit (Halfaker et al. 2011, Aaltonen and Wattal 2020). Overall, despite being characterized by decentralization and soft hierarchies, successful peer-production systems develop an emergent order in which experienced contributors play a key role (Arazy et al. 2016). However, little is known about the extent to which prior experience with a particular content item impacts timely resolution of quality problems in the item.

## 3. Outcome of Interest

Our outcome of interest is the timeliness of quality problem resolution that is an important and previously overlooked outcome in peer-production systems such as Wikipedia (Greenstein and Zhu 2016, Arazy et al. 2020). In contrast to previous research that looks more narrowly at the time it takes to revert vandalism (Geiger and Halfaker 2013), we examine the time that it takes to resolve a quality problem as indicated by the posting and removal of a cleanup template. We call this cleanup time. Our approach thus extends the issues of timely quality problem resolution in peer-production beyond deliberate sabotage to cover various quality problems that may include inadvertent errors, incompleteness, lack of clarity, and other types of quality problems as defined by system policies. We base the choice of cleanup time as the outcome of interest on the fact that Wikipedia’s most widely used mechanism for signaling a quality problem in an article is the addition of a cleanup template to it (Lewandowski and Spree 2011, Anderka et al. 2012). The template appears as a message box at the top of an article page stating that the article “may require cleanup to meet Wikipedia’s quality standards.” The posting of a cleanup template automatically adds the article to a list on the Wikiproject Cleanup page for contributors interested in cleanup tasks and acts as a call to action for those who visit the page. Although this is not the only way to signal quality problems in Wikipedia, cleanup templates provide a robust way to determine when a quality problem is present in an article and when the problem is solved.<sup>2</sup> Hence, we define the posting of a cleanup template on an article as the beginning of a quality problem event and the removal of the template as an indication of the resolution of the event in the same way as the removal of the Under Repair sign from a work zone indicates the completion of the work. Similarly, we define our response variable, cleanup time, as the duration of the effort to resolve a quality problem, with a shorter cleanup time indicating a timelier quality problem resolution.

## 4. Hypotheses

Synthesizing prior empirical research that assesses substantive content quality in Wikipedia, we hypothesize how the use of bots and policy citations as control mechanisms and contributors’ prior experience in editing an article affect the timeliness of quality problem resolution.

## 4.1. Impacts of Bots and Policy Citations

Past research has found that peer-production systems enact various control mechanisms to keep contributors and their contributions aligned with the collective aims of the system (Viegas ´ et al. 2007, Wilkinson and Huberman 2007, Forte et al. 2009). In Wikipedia, these mechanisms are based on policies that are stored as pages in the system along with the encyclopedic content as a form of soft bureaucracy (Aaltonen and Lanzara 2015). They are usually not enforced ex ante but can be easily drawn on to justify actions during quality problem resolution. We examine the impacts of two different ways to enact policies on the timeliness of quality problem resolution: the use of bots and policy citations in edits.

Bots are deployed to directly execute work and hence automatically or semiautomatically enforce system policies. It is estimated that Wikipedia deploys more than 2,600 bots to help manage the massive system comprising millions of articles and other types of pages (Muller-Birn ¨ et al. 2013, Geiger 2017). Because writing a Wikipedia article is a creative task, bots do not presently engage in such tasks—though the emergence of generative AI may be changing this. However, bots can monitor the articles for policy violations and perform simple tasks such as fixing incorrect spelling and grammar, removing swear words, fixing links, and reverting vandalism (Geiger and Halfake

2013, Zheng et al. 2019). Although the relationship between the involvement of bots and the timeliness of quality problem resolution remains an open question, previous research suggests that bots can play an important role. For example, when a vandalism reverting bot went offline, the average time to revert vandalized pages nearly doubled (Geiger and Halfaker 2013). This suggests that having more mechanical eyeballs watching over quality issues is instrumental in streamlining processes (Greenstein and Zhu 2016) and that bots could expedite outcomes by carrying most of the mundane grunt work, allowing human contributors to focus on the creative aspects of fixing quality problems.

Although bots can be an efficient way to enforce policies because they can directly edit Wikipedia pages and even revert contributions made by humans, policy citations are another important mechanism for maintaining order (Butler et al. 2008). Previous research finds that citing policies in comments attached to edits stabilizes interactions in general (Beschastnikh et al. 2008, Morgan and Zachry 2010), but it is not known whether and how policy citations impact the timeliness of quality problem resolution. One way of understanding policy citations is to see them as providing guidance notes to the next contributor and thus helping contributors learn good editing standards (Ren et al. 2018). This way, policies are not as strictly enforced as those executed by bots that can directly edit articles but, instead, policy citations are intended to serve as a form of nudging that can encourage collaborative editing. Although this may help some contributors improve their editing standards, research into nudging in different contexts has found that the approach often succumbs to diminishing returns and that excessive nudging can have adverse effects (Carattini and Blasch 2020, Shah et al. 2021, Rabb et al. 2022). Thus, our assumption is that, although policy citations may be helpful for diffusing good editing standards overall, they are likely to slow down attempts to resolve a particular quality problem. Policy citations introduce an element of litigation, which causes contributors to allocate time and effort to learn and assess policies at the expense of fixing the problem. Hence, we hypothesize that the use of bots and policy citations to enforce policies results in opposite effects on the timeliness of quality problem resolution.

Hypothesis 1a. Cleanup time shortens when a higher number of bots edit an article during a quality problem event.

Hypothesis 1b. Cleanup time lengthens when a higher number of policy citations are made during a quality problem event.

## 4.2. Impacts of Experienced and Inexperienced Contributors

Contributions to the peer production of Wikipedia are made at the operational level of article editing, with different types of contributors building on each other’s work. The research on the quality of content in peer production highlights the important role played by experienced contributors (Halfaker et al. 2011, Liu and Ram 2011, Arazy et al. 2020). However, we are unaware of research examining how the participation of contributors with or without prior experience in editing a particular article affects the timeliness of quality problem resolution. Drawing on works that highlight the importance of experience in general (Anthony et al. 2007; Kittur and Kraut 2008; Stvilia et al. 2008; Javanmardi and Lopes 2010; Arazy et al. 2011, 2020; Halfaker et al. 2011; Kane 2011) and on the development of a specific article in particular (Liu and Ram 2011, Lin and Wang 2019), we theorize that greater participation of experienced contributors—that is, those with prior experience in editing the article—leads to more timely quality problem resolution. Experienced contributors, by virtue of their previous engagement with the article, are likely to have the contextual understanding to quickly interpret why a particular content item is problematic and how it can be fixed. At the same time, inexperienced contributors—those without prior experience in editing the article—can inject new perspectives and enthusiasm into the process (Anthony et al. 2007, Arazy et al. 2011). This is also what Linus’s law suggests: More hands on deck are likely to put out the fire faster; therefore, both experienced and inexperienced contributors should expedite the resolution of quality problems (Raymond 1998, Greenstein and Zhu 2016). We hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 2a. Cleanup time shortens when a higher number of experienced contributors edit an article during a quality problem event.

Hypothesis 2b. Cleanup time shortens when a higher num ber of inexperienced contributors edit an article during a qual ity problem event.

## 4.3. Interactions of Contributors with Control Mechanisms

Bots and policy citations represent different approaches to quality control in peer production. Bots introduce an element of process automation, whereas policy citations are a softer nudging mechanism used to support discourse surrounding content production and contributor behavior in peer-production systems that lack managerial authority (O’Mahony and Ferraro 2007, Ren et al. 2018). Because experienced and inexperienced contributors have different perspectives of the article with a quality problem and the community around it, the two mechanisms may work differently with respect to the two types of contributors.

Experienced users are likely to leverage their contextual knowledge of the article to resolve quality problems, which can conflict with the standardized, or even mindless, application of rules by bots (Geiger and Halfaker 2017). Although this conflict can reduce the gains in efficiency derived from adding experienced contributors to address a quality problem, there are also benefits to consider. Bots automate mundane tasks such as spell checking, grammar correction, and link verification (Zheng et al. 2019), which frees up experienced contributors to focus on more complex issues that require their expertise. Therefore, we expect that the marginal increase in cleanup time from adding experienced contributors will be larger when fewer bots edit the article. This is because despite the conflict, experienced contributors can still dedicate more of their efforts to intricate, context-specific problems when there are more bots handling mundane fixes. By contrast, adding inexperienced contributors when there are many bots editing the article should enable these contributors to spend less time on simple, rule-driven fixes and more time on tasks that cannot be automated and do not require prior article-specific knowledge. Bots can help inexperienced contributors avoid mistakes such as copyright violations and can provide feedback that facilitates learning without reverting contributions (Zheng et al. 2019). This supportive role of bots can enhance the productivity and learning experience of inexperienced contributors and thus decrease marginal cleanup time when adding such contributors when more bots edit an article. Thus, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 3a. The marginal increase in cleanup time from adding experienced contributors is larger when a low number of bots edit an article than when a high number of bots edit an article during a quality problem event.

Hypothesis 3b. The marginal decrease in cleanup time from adding inexperienced contributors is larger when a high number of bots edit an article than when a low number of bots edit an article during a quality problem event.

Policy citations reflect the enactment of a rule-based order to resolve quality problems. Thus, increasing the number of contributors, either experienced or inexperienced, when only a few policies are cited can be expected to decrease the marginal cleanup time by providing a collaborative basis for addressing a quality problem in an article. However, the citation of more policies is likely to conflict with the contextual understanding of experienced contributors who are then drawn to disputes over the rules rather than fixing the problem itself, thus increasing the marginal cleanup time. By contrast, inexperienced contributors are more likely to view policy citations as a nudge toward good practices in editing and resolving problems, instead of as a challenge to their understanding of the article. Yet excessive nudging is likely to yield diminishing returns (Carattini and Blasch 2020, Shah et al. 2021,

Rabb et al. 2022) and therefore, we expect that the marginal decrease in cleanup time from adding inexperienced contributors will be larger when accompanied by a low number of policy citations than when accompanied by a high number of citations. Thus, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 4a. The marginal decrease in cleanup time from adding experienced contributors is larger when there is a low number of policy citations than when there is a high number of policy citations during a quality problem event.

Hypothesis 4b. The marginal decrease in cleanup time from adding inexperienced contributors is larger when there is a low number of policy citations than when there is a high number of policy citations during a quality problem event.

## 5. Data and Variables

To test our hypotheses, we use data from the English Wikipedia, which is one of the most widely used peerproduction systems in the world (Forte et al. 2009, Aaltonen and Lanzara 2015).

## 5.1. Data

We downloaded a freely available Wikipedia database dump that contains all edits to the English Wikipedia from the inception of the system on January 15, 2001, to January 1, 2017. Taking a random sample of 340,000 articles, we identified 5,487 quality problem events in the sampled articles using text analysis based on regu lar expressions. The identified quality problem events appeared in 4,679 different articles (i.e., some articles contained more than one event). However, because each event appeared in exactly one article, every event have an equal probability of being included in our sample, meaning that our sampling strategy is unbiased. Also, we remove 1,014 events from the main analysis when the article at the end of the quality problem event is labeled a “stub,” which typically indicates a new or otherwise underdeveloped entry. We are thus left with a data set of 4,473 quality problem events for our analysis. For additional details on the data set construction, see Online Appendix 2.

## 5.2. Variables and Descriptive Statistics

Our variables consist of our outcome of interest, control mechanisms, contributor experience, and control variables. The outcome of interest is cleanup time (Cleanup Time), which measures the timeliness of quality problem resolution. The main explanatory variables are the number of bots editing an article during a quality problem event (Bots), the number of policy citations made during a quality problem event (PolicyCitations), the number of experienced contributors (ExperiencedOnArticle), and the number of inexperienced contributors (Inexperienced-OnArticle) who are editing an article during a quality problem event.

The set of control variables account for the heterogeneity of the observed quality problem events. Previous studies have highlighted the importance of coordination through open discussion forums and personal messaging as a form of explicit coordination in peer production (Wilkinson and Huberman 2007, Arazy and Nov 2010) and have determined that most of such explicit coordination in Wikipedia takes place on the talk pages that are attached to each article (Vie´gas et al. 2007). Talk page activity is generally important in the early stages of article development, but over time the activity on talk pages decreases and becomes less associated with the substantive quality of the article (Kittur and Kraut 2008). To make sure our results are not driven by talk page activity, we include variables that control for the number of experienced (ExperiencedOn-Talk) and inexperienced (InexperiencedOnTalk) contributors engaging in discussion on the article’s talk page. Second, we control for article characteristics in terms of article age (ArticleAge), length (ArticleLengthPre-Problem), and the number of references in the article (ArticleReferencesPreProblem) prior to the posting of the cleanup template (Stvilia et al. 2008, Greenstein and Zhu 2016, Kane and Ransbotham 2016, Arazy et al. 2020). More mature articles are likely to attract more attention when quality problems arise, and we do not want such differences to bias our results. Third, we control for the article edit history before and during the event in several ways. To begin with, we add the number of past contributors (ContributorsPreProblem) involved in the editing and discussing of the article before the quality problem event (Arazy et al. 2011, Liu and Ram 2011). The rationale is that quality problems in articles with a richer history of contributor participation are more likely to get a fast response than problems in articles with a lesser history of contributor participation. We also control for the effort put in by different types of contributors during the quality problem event as edit distance, which is measured separately for human contributors (ContributorsEditDist) and for bots (BotsEditDist) (Arazy et al. 2011). Specifically, we use the Levenshtein edit distance, which measures the total number of characters added, changed, or subtracted during a quality problem event by different types of contributors. Finally, we use the ratio of bots to human contributors in terms of the number of individual contributors (BotsToContributorsCountRatio) and edit distance (BotsToContributorsEditRatio) to control for the different configurations of article contributors. ## 6. Survival Analysis

In line with the literature (Feng and Seasholes 2005), we use survival analysis to empirically model the timeliness of quality problem resolution in Wikipedia articles. Given our interest in the time elapsed until a quality problem event is resolved, our setting is ideal for survival analysis, which models the survival time of quality problems. Survival models have a trivariate response $( t _ { 0 } , t , d )$ , where $t _ { 0 }$ is the time at which a quality problem is identified in an article, t is the time at which the quality problem is resolved $( t \geq t _ { 0 } )$ , and d is an indicator of failure, which in our context is the resolution of the quality problem, taking the value of one when the problem is resolved (Miller 2011). In our case, the response variable is the time from $t _ { 0 }$ to $t ,$ referred to as cleanup time.

Besides the assumption of the independence and identical distribution (i.i.d.) of survival data,<sup>3</sup> models commonly used to analyze survival assume that observations with the same covariate values have the same distribution of survival times (i.e., the homogeneity of individual observations in the population). In this family of models, the Cox proportional hazards (PH) model is the most widely used and accounts for factors that act linearly on the log hazard function with their effects being constant over time, which is known as the PH assumption. The two most commonly used diagnostic tests for the PH assumption analyze the interaction terms between the timevarying independent variables and the logarithm of survival time and the regression residuals. In Online Appendix $^ { 4 , }$ we apply these tests to our context and find that the PH assumption is violated, indicating that the survival of different observations is different. We then add random effects to account for the heterogeneity between quality problems (Wei 1992). More specifically, to model survival, we use an accelerated failure time (AFT) model that relates the time to resolution to a vector of covariates and a random error term through a linear equation. $\operatorname { L e t } i = 1 , \ldots , n$ be a set of observations, and $v _ { i }$ be unobservable random effects that are i.i.d. as $N ( 0 , \sigma _ { \nu } ^ { 2 } )$ ). The AFT model takes the general form $( T _ { i } | X _ { i } = e ^ { X _ { i } \beta _ { X _ { i } } } \tau _ { i } )$ , which can be transformed into $l n ( T _ { i } | X _ { i } ) = X _ { i } \beta _ { X _ { i } } + l n ( \tau _ { i } )$ , where $T _ { i }$ is quality problem $i ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ time to resolution conditional on a vector of covariates; $X _ { i } , \beta _ { X _ { i } }$ is a vector of coefficients, and $\tau _ { i }$ is the random disturbance term.

## 7. Discussion

Quality problems that are not resolved in a timely manner can adversely impact the utility of a peer-production system, stain its reputation, and propagate to other systems because peer-produced content is routinely used to train AI (Gertner 2023). Our study contributes to the literature on online peer production by advancing our understanding of the timeliness of quality problem resolution by examining the impacts of two control mechanisms, that is, bots and policy citations, and the number of contributors with and without prior experience in editing an article. The results further qualify the general approach to quality control based on drawing contributor attention to problematic content (Greenstein and Zhu 2016), and they uncover important dynamics that drive the “turbulent stability” (Arazy et al. 2016) that can be achieved in peer production with respect to timely quality problem resolution and the use of algorithmic agents.

## 7.1. Contributions to Research

First, the overall approach to quality control in peer production is typically based on the principle known as Linus’s law, which is aligned with a well-known proposition about the wisdom of crowds (Raymond 1998, Surowiecki 2004, Woolley et al. 2010, Greenstein and Zhu 2016). The principle assumes that the greater the number of people who pay attention to a quality problem in peer production, the more likely somebody will figure it out and implement a solution. This is predicated on a simple but powerful idea that individual intellect and effort, when aggregated, generate synergies in terms of marginal contributions that stabilize the system. Accordingly, peer production has proven to be effective at producing high-quality content (von Hippel and von Krogh 2003, Giles 2005). Yet, Linus’s law does not tell us what happens when there are not enough eyeballs to attend to a particular content item (Greenstein and Zhu 2016), or, as in our study, how the approach affects the timeliness of quality problem resolution. Our results address this shortcoming. Outputs from peer production are often released automatically to the users with the hope that the community will quickly fix any incomplete or even defective content; to this end, we find qualified support for Linus’s law with respect to the timeliness of quality problem resolution in peer production. More contributors paying attention to a quality problem, whether experienced or inexperienced, expedites quality problem resolution, which is aligned with predictions of Linus’s law (Raymond 1998, Greenstein and Zhu 2016). However, it is also important to find an appropriate mix of control mechanisms and different types of contributors to ensure the timely resolution of quality problems.

Second, our research contributes to the literature on the dynamics underlying peer production. Two decades of research have examined peer-production systems to better understand not only what makes them work well but also what causes them to fail (Lee and Cole 2003, Forte et al. 2009, Konieczny 2009, Faraj et al. 2011, Ransbotham and Kane 2011, Halfaker et al. 2013, Aaltonen and Lanzara 2015, Greenstein and Zhu 2016, Kane and Ransbotham 2016). The consensus among researchers is that most successful systems establish a balance between individual freedom and the use of control mechanisms that prevent anarchy. Such “turbulent stability” (Arazy et al. 2016) or polycentricity (Mindel et al. 2018) has been proven to function well when peer-production systems are examined from a bird’s eye view; however, when zooming in, we can see that the systems suffer from endemic quality problems that cannot be addressed in the same way as in firm-based production. Considering the individual effects of bots and policy citations, our results do not reveal a significant relationship between bots and timely quality problem resolution, whereas policy citations are associated with less timely quality problem resolution. This suggests that although policy citations may be useful in diffusing good editing practices in general, in the context of a specific quality problem they may also draw attention away from the problem itself to the assessment of how different policies may or may not be applicable to the situation.

However, looking at the interactions between the two control mechanisms and the number of different types of contributors working to resolve a quality prob lem, we find conditional nuances that have important implications for how different control mechanisms should be mixed with experienced and inexperienced contributors. For experienced contributors, the findings highlight a vital implication: Integrating more bots can mitigate the decreasing marginal efficiency seen when fewer bots are present by preventing congestion and enhancing workflow harmonization, which are important advantages when adding experienced contributors to resolve a quality problem (Mindel et al. 2018). This suggests that peer-production systems should bolster their technological infrastructure, ensuring a seamless integration of bots to complement the expertise of experienced contributors. By doing so, a balance between human experience that renders contextual nuance and machine efficiency can optimize the resolution of quality problems. In contrast, inexperienced contributors benefit from increased bot support, which elevates their efficiency in problem resolution. This points to the role of bots in bridging the experience gap, suggesting that bots should be viewed as essential tools for supporting and enhancing contributions from new contributors. The decrease in marginal efficiency that accompanies a reduction in bots further highlights the need for adequate automated support to ensure that inexperienced contributors can effectively contribute to problemsolving efforts.

Policy citations can be advantageous when either experienced or inexperienced contributors are added, but important contingencies need to be considered in calibrating policy citations and the composition of contributors. Considering the association of experienced contributors with timely quality problem resolution, this relationship is significantly influenced by the frequency of policy citations. Specifically, fewer policy citations tend to enhance the marginal efficiency gained from adding experienced individuals, as opposed to situations where more policies are cited. This pattern suggests that the citation of policies can aid in stream lining the cleanup process by leveraging the expertise of a certain number of experienced contributors, thus reducing inefficiencies and delays in problem resolution. Also, the inclusion of inexperienced contributors within the context of fewer policy citations presents an intriguing counterpoint with significant implications for problem resolution processes in peer-production systems. The finding that adding inexperienced contributors can lead to greater marginal efficiency in resolving problems, especially when policies are cited less frequently, suggests a potential advantage in fostering a more flexible and adaptable problem-solving environment. This environment could potentially benefit from the fresh perspectives that inexperienced contributors can bring to quality problem resolution and to the content in general, particularly when not constrained by extensive policy citations. The implication here is that peer-production systems might achieve improved problem-solving efficiency by integrating inexperienced contributors, especially in contexts where the rigidity of policy enforcement can be minimized. This approach not only capitalizes on the unique insights and creativity of those with less experience but also suggests that a more dynamic and less policy-constrained environment can facilitate quicker problem resolution by allowing for more innovative and unorthodox approaches.

Third, we operationalize the timeliness of quality problem resolution to measure an important but little studied dimension of quality control in peer production. The construct should be relatively easy to adapt, for instance, to open-source software development in which it could be measured by observing the time between opening and closing bug reports. The timeliness of quality problem resolution is more generic than the time-to-revert used by Geiger and Halfaker (2013), which focuses on undoing vandalism. Although we do not question the importance of dealing with vandalism, it represents only a fraction of all quality problems, which are often not the result of deliberate sabotage. Moreover, whereas vandalism can often be fixed with a simple reversion to the previous version by a bot, other quality problems require different types of responses and typically the involvement of human contributors. Vandalism reverts are akin to an automated defense of external attacks on the system, whereas addressing the variety of lingering quality problems is a kind of creative work that can take much more time and effort to complete. The timeliness of quality problem resolution can thus serve as an important performance metric in peer production for two reasons: the longer lowquality content remains in place, the more likely it will harm the reputation of the system and its users (Kupferberg and Protus 2011, Lavasa et al. 2011), and quality problems also draw attention and resources away from productive work, as contributors channel their energy to address problems.

Fourth, our study contributes to the literature on the impact of bots in conjunction with human contributors in peer-production systems (Hukal et al. 2019, Shaikh and Vaast 2023). Past research on Wikipedia has primarily focused on the involvement of bots in combating vandalism (Geiger and Halfaker 2013) and on the interaction of bots with each other (Geiger and Halfaker 2017, Tsvetkova et al. 2017) but pays less attention to the role that bots play in tandem with human contributors. We find that bots affect quality problem resolution differently, depending on the composition of experienced and inexperienced contributors and the number of bots. These insights suggest that a careful integration of bots within peer production systems based on contributor experience levels is necessary. Bots should be leveraged as assets to enhance human capabilities, ensuring that both experienced and inexperienced contributors are supported in a manner that promotes efficient and effective quality problem resolution. This approach not only improves productivity by addressing coordination issues and human congestion, but also fosters a learning environment conducive to continuous improvement and high-quality outcomes. The insight into the differences in the interactions of bots with human contributors of varying experience levels carries important implications beyond peer production research because bots are now widely used across a variety of systems revolving around usergenerated content (Gorwa et al. 2020) and social media (Salge et al. 2022). Moreover, as bots become more sophisticated with advances in AI (Erlenhov et al. 2019), it will be crucial to develop a better understanding of how they interact with humans.

## 7.2. Practical Implications

Our study has two key practical implications. First, a nuanced approach must be used to integrate bots within peer production systems based on contributor experience levels. Bots need to be leveraged as assets to enhance different types of human capabilities, ensuring both experienced and inexperienced contributors are supported in manners that promote efficient and effective problem resolution. For experienced contributors, bots can be used to automate mundane tasks that do not require contextual knowledge pertaining to the domain. It is also important to safeguard against policing the editing of content in ways that neglect the contextual knowledge of experienced contributors, because this can result in bot-human edit wars and lead to the disengagement of experienced contributors. In contrast, for inexperienced contributors, bots should be used to improve productivity by allowing them to spend less time on the simplest, rule-driven fixes and to foster a learning environment conducive to continuous improvement and high-quality outcomes. Second, peer production systems must implement and continually refine policies to be adaptable and supportive, ensuring they accommodate diverse perspectives and experience levels, thus fostering a culture of learning and efficiency in problem resolution. This necessitates a balanced system that effectively uses the expertise of both seasoned and new contributors. Overall, a paradigm in which both experienced and inexperienced contributors have vital roles to play in problem resolution, guided by a nuanced approach to policy citations, can leverage the strengths of each group.

## 7.3. Limitations and Future Research

Our research opens avenues for future studies on the timely resolution of quality problems in peer production. Future research could investigate other peer production systems such as open-source software development and identify additional predictors of timely problem resolution. For instance, a contributor might be inexperienced with a specific content item but experienced within the system overall. Thus, future studies could differentiate between general system experience and specific content experience. The research could also distinguish between different types of policies and bots to provide greater nuance regarding which policies and types of bots expedite resolution of quality problems. Perhaps the most intriguing future research avenue emerging from our study involves the synergy between humans and automated agents. Our research suggests that a greater number of inexperienced contributors working in tandem with numerous automated agents results in more efficient quality problem resolution compared with scenarios with fewer bots and inexperienced contributors. This raises compelling questions about how the attention and efforts of experienced and inexperienced contributors should be channeled when a varying number of bots are involved. By exploring these avenues, future research could build on our findings to develop more effective strategies that harness the synergies between human contributors and automated agents, ultimately enhancing the efficiency and effectiveness of peer-production systems.
