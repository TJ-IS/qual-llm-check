# Social Media and Political Affiliation: How Expressing Hot-Button Opinions Affects Raters’ Assessments of Job Applicants

Teagen Nabity-Grover,<sup>a</sup> Kevin D. Matthews,<sup>b,</sup>\* Philip L. Roth,<sup>c</sup> Youngjin Kwon,<sup>d</sup> Jason B. Thatcher<sup>e,f</sup>

Abstract. People post their views on hot-button issues (such as immigration) on social media platforms. In contrast to traditional environments—in which equivalent disclosures would typically be verbal, face-to-face, unrecorded, ephemeral, and addressed to known audiences—social media platforms allow for written, technology-mediated, recorded, and enduring archives of posts that can be viewed by a vast audience, much of which could be unknown to the poster. The relative permanence of social media disclosures has enabled hiring managers not only to view posters’ personal stances on hot-button issues but also to use those views to inform their decisions about the posters’ job applications. To understand how social media posts shape hiring decisions, we investigate how and where (i.e., on which social media platform) viewing posts about a hot-button political issue affects hiring managers’ evaluations of a poster’s hireability. To do so, we constructed a realistic online experiment in which we asked 377 working professionals to assess the hireability of entrylevel job applicants. Our results show that viewing applicants’ social media posts about immigration, a hot-button issue, influences decision makers’ ratings of similarity and liking and their judgments of hireability. We also find that the influence of viewing such content overshadows the influence of job-relevant information (e.g., an internship) on hiring managers’ assessments of hireability, which suggests that social media introduces new, influential forms of information into decision making. The platform on which the social media posts appeared (i.e., Facebook or Twitter) did not moderate the influence of viewing the posts on hiring decisions, suggesting hedonic platform generalizability. We explore these findings with a qualitative applicability check conducted using insights drawn from 79 hiring professionals. Taken together, our findings suggest that social media posts about hotbutton issues impact hiring managers’ assessments of the hireability of applicants regardless of the platform on which the posts appear. Based on our findings, we outline several practi cal applications for hiring managers and future directions for academic research.

## 1. Introduction

Because more than 70% of Americans actively use social media platforms to disclose a vast array of activities (Rainie 2018) and opinions (Wade et al. 2020)— from what they are having for lunch to their opinions on events in the daily news cycle—it is now easier than ever for hiring managers to access social media users’ personal information. As illuminated by the claims, affronts, response, and equilibrium (CARE) theory (Leidner and Tona 2021), the pervasive sharing of personal information on social media has created opportunities for organizations to learn more about job applicants (hereafter, applicants) than what their application materials alone make evident. Hiring managers view such social media screening, also called cybervetting, as a means of gaining insight into the real self of applicants (Van Iddekinge et al. 2016). Across industries, more than 50% of organizations report that they have viewed applicants’ social media accounts when evaluating them (for U.S. samples, see Henderson 2019, Roth et al. 2019, Hartwell and Campion 2020, Wade et al. 2020; for European samples collected before the General Data Protection Regulations took effect, see Acikgoz and Bergman 2016, Eiss 2020). More recent sources have suggested that nearly 70% of hiring managers use social media screening (Carrasco 2022b, Cotriss 2023).

Despite the ostensible benefits of viewing applicants posts, social media screening can undermine the effectiveness and fairness of hiring evaluations, especially when hiring managers are exposed to job-irrelevant information. Although 67% of hiring managers claim they use social media screening to look for reasons to hire someone (Cotriss 2023), 55% of hiring managers who use social media screening acknowledge that they have found content on social media accounts that led them to turn down applicants (Carrasco 2022b; see also Roth et al. 2019). There is little question that hiring managers can view sensitive information, particularly about entry-level employees; reports suggest that approximately 50% of employment-seeking college students share sensitive information—such as political views, religious beliefs, sexual orientation, drug use, and mental health problems—on their Facebook accounts (Zhang et al. 2020). Moreover, hiring managers are likely to view sensitive information on social media accounts because highly active and connected social media users tend to share highly sensitive information on social media (Chang and Heo 2014), such as their stances on court rulings on abortion (Carrasco 2022a) or immigration (Wilf et al. 2022). In response to these challenges, social media scrubbing services have emerged to help applicants remove job-irrelevant information that hiring managers may perceive as inappropriate or offensive (Wong and Reshef 2018, Carrasco 2022b).

Against this backdrop, we explore how social media posts about hot-button issues influence hiring managers’ evaluations of entry-level applicants. Although Zhang et al. (2020) report that a substantial number of individuals choose to post about hot-button issues— that is, controversial content that evokes emotiona responses (Merriam-Webster 2023)—on social media, relatively little is known about how viewing such content influences the evaluation of applicants. Understanding the relationship between such polarizing posts and hiring outcomes is important because these posts can elicit intense and immediate emotional responses from viewers (Nuortimo et al. 2020, Roth et al. 2024), such as disdain and loathing or respect and approval (Iyengar and Westwood 2015, McCarty et al. 2016). In online communities and political environments undergoing increased polarization (Kitchens et al. 2020), users’ reactions may extend beyond responding to a post’s content to making inferences about the poster (Wade et al. 2020); such inferences may include stereotypes about the individual’s political affiliation or assumptions about their stances on other issues and, by extension, their values, attitudes, and beliefs. Such cues about an individual’s political affiliation and associated inferences about their values, attitudes, beliefs, and behaviors may bias hiring managers for or against the applicant based on factors unrelated to job outcomes; such bias is particularly problematic in the case of hir ing decisions that should be informed only by factors relevant to predicted job performance (Vosen 2021, Mo¨nke and Scha¨pers 2022).

A growing body of information systems (IS) research emphasizes that hot-button issue social media posts can polarize online communities (Kitchens et al. 2020), that their dissemination can be accelerated by social bots (Salge et al. 2022), and that they can evoke off-line social protests (Tarafdar and Kaj al Ray 2021). Signifi cantly less IS research examines the influence of such posts on essential organizational processes, such as attracting or hiring new employees (Roth et al. 2024). Online Appendix I lists a selection of relevant studies of the relationship between social media, hot-button issues, and hiring. Although a few studies examine the implications of the content of social media posts for hiring decisions (Wade et al. 2020, Kwon et al. 2024), only one study directly measures political affiliation using scales designed to capture the intensity of identification (positive affect) and that of disidentification (negative affect) with another user’s political affiliation (Roth et al. 2020). In applied psychology, the general trend of these studies is to devote extensive speculative discus sion but little specific empirical attention to the evaluation of explanatory variables, particularly those tied to the technology itself (Gift and Gift 2015, Bermiss and McDonald 2018). Moreover, these papers tend to focus on political party–level effects (Gift and Gift 2015, Roth et al. 2020) instead of the issue-level effects one would expect to emerge for social media posts about hotbutton political issues (Wade et al. 2020). Conse quently, IS researchers have substantial opportunities to enhance the understanding of how decision makers use and are influenced by social media and of the mechanisms through which social media posts affect organizational decision-making processes, particularly the hiring process.

Furthermore, it is particularly important to consider the impact of the platform on which hiring managers view social media posts about hot-button issues. Social media platforms have a variety of spirits (e.g., features and purposes); for example, Twitter<sup>1</sup> is known for hosting rapidly evolving political conversations (Bestvater et al. 2022), whereas Facebook is known for fostering virtual communities (Noveck et al. 2021). Although the same user may post on both of these platforms, it is important to note that the way the platforms facilitate connections and curate content may influence hiring managers’ decisions. In view of these differences between Facebook and Twitter, IS research should examine whether the influence of hot-button issue posts on hiring decisions is moderated by the social media platform used to screen applicants.

To shed light on the impact of viewing hot-button issue social media posts on hiring decisions, this research note investigates the influence of how hiring managers view social media posts about hot-button issues and how platforms influence their evaluations of applicants. In an online experiment, we asked 377 working professionals to assess applicants after viewing a social media account that included political posts about immigration (a hot-button issue) on one of two social media platforms: Facebook or Twitter. Although we found evidence that viewing posts about hot-button issues influenced decision makers evaluations of applicants, we did not find evidence that the social media platform on which they viewed such content played a significant role in their evaluations. We probed this finding using a qualitative applicability check with 79 hiring managers who used social media screening.

Our findings advance the IS literature in two substantive ways. First, we demonstrate that social media posts influence organizational (i.e., hiring) decisions when they expose decision makers to another person’s issue-level political views. Second, on the basis of our findings, we suggest a novel construct: hedonic platform generalizability. That is, our online experiment shows that the influence of viewing applicants’ hotbutton issue content on hiring decisions is not moderated by the social media platform on which the content appears. Our results suggest that future IS research should examine how the type and intensity of social media content, not the hedonic social media platform on which it appears, bias important organizational decisions about individuals—such as hiring decisions—and that it should examine whether this intuition extends to other organizational processes. In the following sections, we review the relevant literature, describe our model, present the details of our online experiment and analyses, and discuss the study’s contributions and their implications.

## 2. Background and Theoretical Frameworks

Social media platforms are “internet-based channels that allow users to opportunistically interact and selectively self-present, either in real-time or asynchronously, with both broad and narrow audiences who derive value from user-generated content and the perception of interaction with others” (Carr and Hayes 2015, p. 50). Our study focuses on two kinds of social media platform: social network sites (i.e., Facebook) and microblogs (i.e., Twitter). The former are networked communication platforms on which individuals can publicly articulate connections and consume, produce, and interact with user-generated content (Chan-Olmsted et al. 2013, Ellison and Boyd 2013); the latter enable a form of real-time blogging that uses frequent, brief updates on ideas, opinions, and activities (Sakaki et al. 2010, Chan-Olmsted et al. 2013).

Although Facebook and Twitter have many features in common (as summarized in Table 1), they have theoretically important differences. First, the users of social network sites explicitly articulate and formalize social connections; microblog users do not. Platforms such as Facebook require both parties to agree to a connection, whereas platforms such as Twitter allow users to follow an account without requiring reciprocal action from that account (Chan-Olmsted et al. 2013). Second, whereas social network sites focus on facilitating social relationships and communities, microblogs focus on enabling the sharing of information and participation in online conversations (WebTegrity 2014, Davies 2020). Finally, whereas social network sites emphasize the archival nature of content (interactions around a Facebook post can occur hours or even years after its posting), microblogs emphasize real-time updates (nearly 90% of all interactions around a Twitter tweet occur within an hour of its posting) (VisualScope 2015).

The success of social media platforms—especially social network sites—depends on the sharing of personal information by users, which is known as selfdisclosure (Jourard 1959, Wheeless and Grotz 1976). The CARE theory (Leidner and Tona 2021) describes such disclosures as “showing self,” an act whereby users share diverse forms of information about themselves. Showing self has significant implications for social media screening. First, it results in a subsequent process of “knowing others,” in which the audience views users’ content and learns about those users. In social media screening, organizations leverage the disclosed personal digital data of applicants in an attempt to uncover the applicants true selves. Entry-level applicants are typically young people, who tend to disclose the most information on social media (Hollenbaugh and Everett 2013, Chen 2018) and tend to have more lenient privacy settings on their social media accounts than older people (Walrave et al. 2012, Root and McKay 2014). They also use social network sites and microblogs more frequently (Chan-Olmsted et al. 2013), which is connected to sharing more sensitive information on social media (Chang and Heo 2014). Consequently, by showing self, entry-level applicants make themselves particularly susceptible to bias in social media screening.

Second, social media screening—as a form of knowing others—can result in claims to what the CARE theory would call meritocratic dignity (i.e., appropriate hiring outcomes). An applicant who is discovered to be a member of certain favored or unfavored groups might be judged more or less favorably, respectively, than others with similar qualifications. This form of bias is particularly problematic when applicants express or share their views on contentious issues. For example, in a recent study of Black Americans, Howard et al. (2020) find that those who posted about race and race-related issues on social media were rated as less likable and were less likely to be offered a job. These findings suggest that showing self by commenting on hot-button issues could negatively impact applicants’ job searches when raters view social media posts.

To extend the CARE theory’s broad perspective on the implications of sharing personal digital data for social media screening, our study adapts the political affiliation model (PAM; see Figure 1), which Roth et al. (2017) developed to explain how social media content about hot-button issues impacts hiring outcomes. Rooted in the similarity-attraction paradigm (Byrne 1971), the PAM suggests that, when two people are similar in one dimension, they often see themselves as similar on many dimensions or in an overall sense (i.e., they experience perceived similarity). This perceived similarity leads to them like the other person; for example, they are attracted to individuals whom they perceive as similar to themselves as social identity theory posits (Tajfel 1982, Tajfel and Turner 2004). Liking should lead to higher ratings on outcomes such as hiring recommendations. Our review of the information systems, organizational behavior, and related literatures revealed that the PAM was the only model explicitly designed to explain how viewing politicized information impacts decision makers’ evaluations of applicants or coworkers.

It is of particular relevance to understanding the impact of hot-button issue social media posts that the PAM interweaves two concepts that shape similarity: identification and disidentification (Kreiner and Ash forth 2004); these concepts help to explain the general showing self and knowing others processes articulated by the CARE theory (Leidner and Tona 2021). Identifica tion is the degree of psychological oneness an individual feels between oneself and a group or organization (Mael and Ashforth 1992); it captures the intensity of that indi vidual’s positive affect. In contrast, disidentification is the degree of social distance an individual feels between themselves and a group; it represents the intensity of that individual’s negative affect (Elsbach and Bhattacharya 2001). Decision makers likely form these feelings of identification and disidentification (and may often do so quickly) as they come to know applicants based on what applicants show about themselves through social media posts and strongly influence perceived similarity.

To adapt the PAM to the domain of IS, we elaborate on the unique processes through which viewing socia media posts about hot-button issues influences hiring managers’ evaluations of applicants. Although the PAM provides a general understanding of how hiring managers’ beliefs and feelings of similarity with applicants shape their evaluations, it does not articulate how the content of social media posts enables hiring managers to know applicants’ values, attitudes, beliefs, and behaviors (Johnson and Roberto 2019, Swigart et al. 2020) or how such content triggers the identification and disidentification evaluations that inform hiring decisions. For example, a hiring manager may feel a strong negative reaction to social media posts in which an applicant expresses a stance on immigration (Daniller 2019, Pew Research Center 2019), leading the manager to form inferences about the applicant’s value system and question the applicant’s fit with the organization. Moreover, in contrast to information about political-party affiliation, which can introduce more ambiguous information about positions on specific issues (Roth et al. 2019), posts about hot-button issues more clearly reflect the applicant’s view on an issue, which may span party lines (Daniller 2019). Thus, viewing content about hot-button issues offers man agers clear insight into an applicant’s stance on an issue, more directly influencing managers’ judgments of similarity and their evaluations of hireability.

## 3. Research Model

Figure 2 presents our research model. We refer to hiring managers who evaluate hireability as raters. Our model predicts that viewing applicants’ posts about hot-button issues affects raters’ evaluations of expected task performance (i.e., performance in required parts of the job) and expected organizational citizenship behaviors (i.e., discretionary behaviors that go beyond job requirements).

We begin by contextualizing relationships that scholars examine with the similarity-attraction paradigm and the PAM theory to our examination of social media content and hiring evaluations. The relationship between a rater’s actual and perceived similarity to an applicant is typically examined in terms of political party similarity (Roth et al. 2020, 2024); such research finds some evidence that actual similarity leads to greater perceived similarity (i.e., we support the same party, so we must be similar in other ways). We examine whether this relationship holds true for views on hot-button political issues, which are more specific and potentially more impactful than political party affiliation. In the organizational behavior literature, perceived similarity is found to lead to stronger perceptions of liking because individuals tend to receive social validation from people who are similar to them (Goldberg 2005, Montoya and Horton 2013). Liking another person results in more favorable judgments of that person’s general hireability and of their ability to perform work-related tasks (Montoya and Horton 2013). In our study, hireability is represented by expected task performance and expected organizational citizenship behaviors (MacKenzie et al. 1991, Borman and Motowidlo 1997). Because these relationships are established in the literature and are fundamental to models that use the PAM and the similarity-attraction paradigm, our first hypothesis is that these established relationships hold.

Hypothesis 1a. Actual hot-button issue similarity between an applicant and a rater has a positive effect on the rater’s perceived similarity with the applicant.

Hypothesis 1b. A rater’s perceived similarity with an applicant has a positive effect on the rater’s liking for the applicant.

Hypothesis 1c. A rater’s liking for an applicant has a positive effect on the rater’s judgments of hireability in terms of expected task performance.

Hypothesis 1d. A rater’s liking for an applicant has a positive effect on the rater’s judgments of hireability in terms of expected organizational citizenship behaviors.

We extend existing research by positing that evoking affect is a critical part of the impact of disclosing hot-button issues on social media. Evidence from recent IS research suggests that the intensity of affect impacts how users interpret information they find in social media posts (Wang et al. 2022). Traditional applications of the similarity-attraction paradigm capture affect (in the form of liking) as the third construct in a logical chain of reactions that shape raters’ assessments (as depicted in Figure 1). However, given the emotionally charged nature of hot-button issues, we suggest that, in our social media context, the process begins with an affective response to the political issue (i.e., it is characterized by an early affective response) and to the applicant’s stance on it. Because of the currently high levels of political polarization, this affective response may be strong (Abramowitz and Webster 2016)

Positions on hot-button issues disclosed on social media may generate strong reactions because they convey the beliefs with greater specificity than does party affiliation. The political science literature indicates that specific candidates evoke stronger reactions than parties. For example, Abramowitz and Webster (2018) note that, in the 2016 U.S. Presidential election, individuals rated their own party and candidates in the low 60s (on a warmth scale of 1–100); in contrast, they rated the opposing party and its candidates more negatively (Dimock and Wike 2020) with opposing parties averaging 23 and candidates averaging 11 (mode � 0) on the warmth scale. To frame this in terms of identification and disidentification, when an individual identifies with a political party, the individua likely views that party’s political candidate as similar to the individual (high perceived similarity) and, thus, generally likes the candidate; however, when an individual disidentifies with a political party, the individual likely views the party’s political candidate as dissimilar to the individual (low perceived similarity) and, thus, generally dislikes the candidate. The CARE theory describes this phenomenon as the associative dignity disequilibrium (Leidner and Tona 2021); that is, raters who view an applicant’s personal data on social media evaluate the applicant favorably or unfavorably when they perceive that the applicant belongs to a favored or unfavored group, respectively. Accordingly, we propose that the best way to capture a hiring manager’s early affective response to social media content is through the hiring manager’s identification or disidentification with an applicant’s view on a hotbutton issue (Ashforth and Mael 1989, Kreiner and Ashforth 2004). Although we are unaware of previous research that investigates identification and disidentification at the issue level (as opposed to the candidate or party level), we posit that individuals will have a similar reaction to hot-button issues. Thus, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 2. A rater’s identification with an applicant’s stance on a hot-button issue is positively related to the rater’s perceived similarity with the applicant.

Hypothesis 3. A rater’s disidentification with an applicant’s stance on a hot-button issue is negatively related to the rater’s perceived similarity with the applicant.

## 3.1. Social Media Platform as Moderator

Having outlined the basic process through which applicants’ stances on hot-button issues influence raters’ evaluations, we now consider how raters are influenced by the social media platform on which they find the content. Raters often search multiple social media platforms to gain insight into the real applicant—that is, into applicant behaviors that fall outside the impression management that characterizes accounts on professional networking sites, such as LinkedIn (Berkelaar 2014, 2017; Berkelaar and Buzzanell 2015)—because they believe they can gather authentic information on applicants by viewing posts on hedonic social media platforms, such as Twitter and Facebook (Van Iddekinge et al. 2016). As Roth et al. (2019) find, approximately 55% of the recruiters they surveyed reported examining Facebook accounts, and about 23% reported examining Twitter feeds.

We maintain that viewing hot-button issue social media posts activates the PAM and the similarityattraction paradigm in raters, thus informing their evaluations of applicants’ hireability. We also argue that the platform on which raters view hot-button issue posts has a moderating effect within the PAM, specifically on the relationship between perceived similarity and liking. A parallel provided by the morality literature helps to justify our exploration of this potential moderator (Wright et al. 2008, van Zomeren et al. 2012). This research considers the impact of moral beliefs on various emotional outcomes (e.g., intolerance, anger). Moral beliefs are similar to perceived similarity because both are tied to an individual’s social identity (van Zomeren et al. 2012), whereas lik ing is an emotional outcome. Additionally, the Wright et al. (2008) study on the intensity of belief as a moderator finds that it strengthens the relationship between moral beliefs and intolerance. In our model, the social media platform can function similarly to intensity because the nature of the platform can influence how strongly an applicant is perceived to support a hotbutton issue.

As discussed above, there are some key differences between Facebook and Twitter: connections, platform purpose, and lifespan of content. We posit that these platform differences lead to a differential effect on raters’ assessments. Because Facebook encourages some privacy controls and requires a reciprocal con nection (Shane-Simpson et al. 2018), a post’s audience is generally known to the poster; additionally, Facebook facilitates long-term relationships and a more archival view of content. Although Facebook content may be more challenging for raters to access, it may communicate that an applicant has strong views on a hot-button issue because the applicant is (1) sharing the applicant’s stance with the applicant’s reciprocal connections, (2) doing so on a platform that focuses on facilitating relationships, and (3) doing so on a platform on which content has a long lifespan. Conversely, content posted on Twitter is likely to be more accessible because Twitter accounts tend to be open and pub lic, and it is likely to be posted more impulsively because Twitter enables participation in conversations and emphasizes the real-time nature of the content. As such, although Twitter content is likely easier for raters to access, it may reflect an applicant’s stance on a given issue less accurately than Facebook content because the applicant is (1) sharing the applicant’s stance publicly, (2) doing so on a platform that focuses on facilitating conversations, and (3) doing so on a platform on which content is less thoughtfully composed and more ephemeral than content posted on Facebook. Simply put, we posit that hot-button issue posts viewed on Facebook evoke a stronger response to an applicant’s expressed stance than on Twitter, which leads a rater to like an applicant more when the rater perceive oneself as similar to the applicant. Thus, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 4. The relationship between a rater’s perceived similarity and liking is strengthened when the rater views content on a Facebook account as compared with a Twitter account.

## 3.2. Controls: Individuating Information

Because research shows that individuating information has a strong impact on hiring managers’ impressions and evaluations of applicants (Olian et al. 1988, McCarthy et al. 2010), we control for the effects of finding individuating information—that is, evidence of knowledge, skills, or other attributes relevant to job performance—on raters’ evaluations of applicants. Doing so leads to insights into the power of the bias introduced by viewing hot-button issue social media posts on the decision-making process. Thus, our study considers two forms of job-relevant individuating information: academic performance and community or volunteer engagement. The former includes information about an applicant’s experience with performing a task and/or the training the applicant has undertaken to perform the task (McCarthy et al. 2010), and it should influence raters’ evaluations of expected task performance. The latter, which receives little empirica attention in the political affiliation literature, provides evidence of how an applicant has served the community, which should influence raters’ evaluations of expected organizational citizenship behaviors. Ideally, because these two forms of individuating information are relevant to evaluations of an applicant’s ability to fulfill job requirements, their effects should outweigh those of an applicant’s posts about hot-button issues. If an applicant’s views on hot-button issues have a significant effect on evaluations of hireability in the presence of individuating information, the job-irrelevant information found in hot-button issue posts would have an effect even in the presence of individuating information. That is, such job-irrelevant information has an influence even when raters are exposed to more appropriate hiring cues.

## 4. Method

## 4.1. Participants

The participants were obtained through Qualtrics. We invited only individuals with hiring experience, and we restricted the participants to working professionals who spoke English as their first language. Our power analysis indicated that a sample of 351 participants would be sufficient to detect effect sizes of 0.15 (Cohen 1988) at a 0.8 threshold. Our final sample included 377 participants, all of whom passed three attention checks and the manipulation check for the applicant’s stance on the hot-button issue.<sup>2</sup> Of the 377 participants, 51.7% identified as female. Politically, 41.4% identified as Democrats, 31.0% as Republicans, and 24.4% as other. The sample included individuals who identified as Asian (3.4%), Black (10.3%), and White (78.5%), and 6.1% of those who identified as White also identified as Hispanic. Most of the participants had completed postsecondary education, obtaining an associate’s degree (15.1%), bachelor’s degree (38.7%), master’s degree (18.8%), or higher degree (4.5%). On average, the participants had 21 years of work experience, and only 19.4% of the sample had fewer than 10 years of experience.

## 4.2. Procedure

Taking the role of a rater, each participant was directed first to review a job description for an office manager (adapted from TopResume 2018) and then to view an applicant’s social media account (on either Facebook or Twitter). Raters were not given a specific procedure for viewing the social media account because research shows that social media screening tends to be unstructured (Van Iddekinge et al. 2016) and is often completed before an interview or any other form of selection is conducted (Henderson 2019).

The applicant portrayed on the Facebook and Twitter accounts was the same in all conditions: a male, college-age, upper level university student. In addition to manipulating the social media platform, we manipulated political stance and two forms of individuating information (see Table 2). Our experiment was a randomized $2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2$ between-subjects design.

For our study, the applicant’s political stance on immigration (anti-immigration or pro-immigration) was manipulated using four simultaneous cues gathered from actual posts on social media. ## 4.3. Measurement

We selected established scales for this study (for the measurement items, see Online Appendix III). We measured perceived similarity using the Tepper et al. (2011) five-item scale of subordinate similarity, adapting it to the assessment of applicants (alpha � 0.95). To measure identification and disidentification, we used the scales developed by Mael and Ashforth (1992; alpha � 0.86) and Kreiner and Ashforth (2004; alpha � 0.96), respectively, adapting them to stances on immigration. We measured liking with the scale developed by Wayne and Ferris (1990; alpha � 0.96). The scales for expected task performance and expected organizational citizenship behaviors used high-loading items from Williams and Anderson (1991; alpha � 0.94 and 0.93, respectively). To measure actual similarity of views on hot-button issues, we compared the actual stances of raters with the stance of our simulated appli cant. We created a measure of actual hot-button issue similarity by coding the raters who reported the same stance on immigration as the applicant as similar (assign ing them a value of one) and the raters who reported a different stance as different (assigning them a value of zero; Goldberg 2005). After the raters received the experimental treatment, we asked, “Across most situations, how would you generally say you feel about the issue of immigration?” The response options were antiimmigration, neutral, or pro-immigration. The mean for actual hot-button issue similarity was 0.34, meaning 34% of the pairs were similar and 66% were dissimilar.

## 6. Discussion

This study investigates how viewing applicants’ posts about controversial issues affects hiring managers’ decisions. We conducted an experiment with 377 working professionals to investigate the impact of hot-button issue posts on evaluations of applicants. As anticipated, the PAM relationships are supported (Hypothesis 1a–1d); we also find support for the influence of early affective responses—as represented by identification (Hypothesis 2) and disidentification (Hypothesis 3)— on perceived similarity. Although the social media platform does not moderate the relationship between perceived similarity and liking (Hypothesis 4), the results indicate that liking, which is influenced by the hotbutton issue posts, has a stronger influence on the evaluations of expected task performance and expected organizational citizenship behaviors than the two rather distinct forms of job-relevant individuating information<sup>3</sup> (Gelman and Stern 2006).

The post hoc qualitative applicability check, conducted with 79 hiring managers, provides additional insights into why the social media platform is nonsignificant. In part, we expected a difference between Facebook and Twitter because Facebook offers more privacy settings and requires reciprocal connections, both of which make it more difficult for hiring managers to access content. This expectation is not reflected in the raters’ comments; instead, the raters report that they could access a plethora of information about applicants on Facebook, including an applicant’s stances on political issues. Our raters’ view is consistent with research that indicates that many individuals inadequately manage their privacy settings on social media (Root and McKay 2014, Lauriano and Coacci 2023), an oversight that facilitates social media screening by hiring managers. The raters’ comments do provide support for our claim that Facebook content represents relatively enduring beliefs, whereas Twitter content is, as a result of the platform’s conversational nature, more impulsive. Accordingly, raters tend to look for hate speech and political stance/party affiliation on Facebook, whereas they tend to assess how applicants interacted with others on Twitter. Despite these differences between platforms, the goal of social media screening is the same for both platforms: to uncover negative information about an applicant. This finding is consistent with prior research (Hartwell and Campion 2020) and may be partially because of the increased vitriol and social shaming that emerged during the COVID-19 pandemic (Nabity-Grover et al. 2023).

Our study contributes to the IS and adjacent literature in two key ways. First, by investigating how applicants’ political stances on specific issues influence hiring decisions, our study highlights the important role of social media—and online platforms more broadly—in understanding phenomena at the intersection of political science and OB. Even though many individuals use social media to express their views on hot-button issues, this study is among the first to examine how issue-level political stances (rather than party affiliation) impact hiring decisions through the mechanisms of perceived similarity and liking as defined by the PAM. Furthermore, our findings enrich the literature on the CARE theory, which emphasizes the impact of personal digital data—such as the content found on social media—on human dignity (Leidner and Tona 2021). Our findings show how the processes of showing self and knowing others as enacted through applicants’ creation and hiring managers’ viewing of social media posts about hot-button issues can diminish meritocratic dignity (i.e., appropri ate hiring outcomes), particularly when hiring managers form judgments about applicants based on applicants’ stances on those issues.

Moreover, we show that hot-button issue information obtained from social media can significantly influence evaluations of applicants, perhaps more strongly than human resources managers may realize (Roth et al. 2022). Recall that a wide variety of personal content is available on hedonic social media, including information about religion, political affiliation, sexual orientation, drug use, etc. (Zhang et al. 2020). We find that an applicant’s stance on immigration influences hiring decisions despite the presence of two types of job-relevant individuating information. This finding underscores how social media screening can introduce new information and biases into the hiring process by exposing raters to an applicant’s stance on hot-button issues. By engaging in showing self behaviors on social media that disclose their stances on such issues, appli cants inadvertently facilitate the knowing others process undertaken by raters unknown to them, which may harm their job prospects.

Our findings are in sharp contrast to those of the current management literature, which maintains that individuating information greatly diminishes or eliminates the effects of interpersonal similarity or dissimilarity on hiring decisions (Olian et al. 1988, McCarthy et al. 2010). In a sense, our findings turn the field of hiring on its head because political affiliation information effects not only occurred in the presence of individuating information but swamped the effects of individuating information. That is, in the presence of political information, the effects of individuating information, which typically influences hiring decisions, are nonsignificant. This finding indicates the potential biasing power of issue-based political information that is frequently shared on social media when it is introduced into hiring decisions. We suggest that this biasing power arises primarily because information about political affiliation elicits strong affective responses in today’s polarized environment (Abramowitz and Webster 2018). The results underscore that the vast range of personal digital information—including hot-button issue posts—encountered during social media screening can trigger a knowing others process via feelings of similarity investigated by the PAM (Roth et al. 2017, 2020; Wade et al. 2020), which can, in turn, result in inferences about applicants’ values, attitudes, and beliefs and thereby impact hireability evaluations.

Second, despite substantive differences between the hedonic social media platforms, we find no significant platform effect in our comparison of Facebook and Twitter. We, therefore, propose the novel concept of hedonic platform generalizability. In contrast to research that argues that platform type drives how individuals engage with social media posts (Voorveld et al. 2018), the concept of hedonic platform generalizability indicates that the nature of the information itself drives online engagement and shapes off-line behavior. We posit that hedonic platform generalizability applies to a variety of information types, including hot-button issues and political affiliation. As our post hoc qualitative applicability check indicates, raters engage in social media screening to look for any available negative information, much of which is irrelevant to the job in question (e.g., inappropriate content and personal beliefs or values) and may evoke an emotional response. Because these results may be influenced by the rater’s focus on hot-button issues and hedonic social media platforms, more research is needed to verify hedonic platform generalizability and determine its boundary conditions.

## 6.1. Implications for Practice

We propose three ways to address the human-actor biases introduced into hiring by the process of knowing others through social media data. The first is for hiring managers to forego gathering information from hedonic social media because of the risk of viewing irrelevant information, such as applicants’ opinions on hot-button issues. The second is for human resource managers to find ways to integrate information gathered on social media into hiring decisions via formalized, structured assessments designed to reduce if not eliminate raters’ biases. The Society for Human Resource Management (2016) reported that 60% of businesses that use social media screening lack policies for doing so, indicating a great research need in this area. The third is to incorporate our study’s insights into training programs that educate hiring managers on the risks associated with social media screening. Our recommendations echo those of Davison et al. (2016), who emphasizes the importance of standardized, consistent assessments that focus on job-related information and implement bias safeguards.

Regarding organizations more broadly, our findings emphasize that social media posts about hot-button issues may alter the decision calculus in how raters assess others. Although our study focuses on hiring, similar processes may impact consumer responses to an organization’s social media posts. For example, the negative response to Budweiser’s collaboration with the transgender social media influencer Dylan Mulvaney (Holpuch 2023) could be explained by the PAM (e.g., consumers respond negatively to posts that suggest companies are dissimilar to them). Although we do not provide prescriptions for what companies should post or avoid posting, our study helps to explain why companies encounter negative or positive reactions to stances on various hot-button issues expressed in their social media content.

At the individual level, our findings demonstrate that potentially controversial social media posts may cost applicants career opportunities. This is especially problematic for younger social media users, who often generate content with little regard for its potential audience and despite knowing that recruiters may view their online accounts (Walrave et al. 2012, Roo and McKay 2014). Cyberactivists—individuals who use social media to organize, mobilize, and disseminate information about a social cause (Harlow and Harp 2012)—are also vulnerable. Our study can serve to alert individuals to how the use of social media screening in hiring misappropriates social media data by penalizing applicants for posts about hot-button issues; alternatively, it can serve as a recommendation that they use private or anonymized social media accounts, which could limit the impact of their social media content on a wide range of organizational decisions that go well beyond hiring. In any case, users should be aware that their social media content, irre spective of the platform on which it appears, can negatively impact their careers.

## 6.2. Limitations

Our study has several limitations. Because we conducted an online experiment, it is possible that raters purposefully altered their answers in a way that did not adequately reflect their intended or actual behaviors. As a result, our data could have minimized or muffled the effects of applicants’ views on hot-button issues. Moreover, the study was conducted only in the United States. It is possible that the results would differ if the study were repeated in other countries with distinct political climates and cultures. We examined only Facebook and Twitter, but other hedonic platforms with different features (e.g., Instagram, WhatsApp) and utilitarian platforms (e.g., LinkedIn) might yield different results (though in the later case see Wade et al. 2020). Lastly, our experimental design operationalized the social media platform as a binary variable, limiting our ability to isolate and investigate specific affordances of each platform. Consequently, we were able to provide only a platform-level (instead of a feature-level) discussion of the results.

## 6.3. Future Research

One important avenue for future research is to examine the extent to which social media posts about hot-button issues, coupled with other disclosed information (e.g., application materials), shape decision making. The current study investigates only a single political stance, but people often reveal stances on multiple issues or more beliefs and behaviors on their social media accounts (Wike et al. 2022). For example, a person may disclose the person’s faith and political party and level of related activity $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ the intensity of their affiliation). In addition to the effects of applicants’ views on these sensitive topics, researchers could explore the effects of more benign content, such as sports team fandom (Kwon et al. 2021) or active support of a celebrity (White 2024). Although such content can evoke a strong emotional response, it may be seen as trivial. In general, it is necessary to realize a better understanding of how raters respond to forms of information that are perceived as controversial, divisive, or toxic on social media.

Researchers should also investigate the possibility that the broader political mood and widely reported shocking events may impact the relationships presented in this study. For example, if we collected content posted during the partial federal government shutdown that took place between December 2018 and mid-January 2019, the effects of immigration stances may have been even stronger. Similarly, if we collected data after the presidential debates held between June and September 2024, these effects might have been even more significant. These events may have introduced effects that could have amplified or diminished the ability of applicants’ stances on hot-button issues to evoke identification and disidentification in decision makers.

Future research should also explore the applicant’s perspective. For example, researchers could examine how applicants react to social media screening and its associative dignity equilibrium or disequilibrium. El Ouirdi et al. (2015) find that professionals’ concerns about their online image interacted with age, education level, and work experience to influence inappropriate disclosures on social media among Italian adults. More research is needed to understand applicants’ personal and professional online identity management, the relationship between the processes of showing self and knowing others, and the influence of these factors on hireability evaluations.

Lastly, we call for more research on hedonic platform generalizability. To the best of our knowledge, two studies have examined political affiliation information in relation to social media platforms (Wade et al. 2020, Pu et al. 2023). Although our study found no differences between hedonic platforms, Wade et al. (2020) observed some differences between a hedonic platform (Facebook) and a utilitarian platform (LinkedIn). More research is needed to determine how the attributes of disclosed information (e.g., intensity, format, content) exert a similar influence (1) in decision-making contexts and (2) across a variety of social media platforms. This would help meta-analyses better establish and determine the boundary conditions of hedonic platform generalizability and inform actionable recommendations for organizations and applicants regarding the inclusion and use of social media data in organizational decision making.

## 7. Conclusion

This study contextualizes the PAM to investigate the effects of social media posts about a hot-button issue— immigration—on hiring decisions. Even in the presence of two different types of job-relevant individuating information, only the job-irrelevant information (i.e., the applicant’s stance on the hot-button issue) has a significant influence on judgments of hireability. In other words, the influence of political affiliation information appears to swamp that of individuating information. Our findings demonstrate how social media data can reshape our current understanding of jobrelevant and job-irrelevant variables and introduce an additional source of human bias into organizational decision making. Additionally, our qualitative applicability check reveals that raters seek similar information on Facebook and Twitter, and our analyses indicate that social media information results in similar evaluations of an applicant regardless of platform, providing some support for the concept of hedonic platform generalizability. Overall, we demonstrate that social media screening impacts evaluations of hireability by introducing issue-level political affiliation into the decisionmaking process.
