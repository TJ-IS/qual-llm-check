# Linking Clicks to Bricks: Understanding the Effects of Email Advertising on Multichannel Sales

Abstract. Businesses have widely used email ads to directly send promotional information to consumers. Whereas email ads serve as a convenient tool to allow firms to target consumers online, there is little evidence of their multichannel impact on consumer spending in both online and brick-and-mortar stores. In this paper, we utilize a unique highdimensional data set from one of the world’s largest office supplies retailers to link each consumer’s online behaviors to item-level purchase records in physical stores. We employ a doubly robust estimator that incorporates nonparametric machine learning methods fo causal estimation on observational data. Our results show that email ads significantly increase the retailer’s sales across different channels. We also investigate the effects of email ads on diverse consumer behaviors along the purchase funnel and find that increased sales result from increased purchase probability and a wider variety of products purchased by consumers. Further, we examine several moderating factors, such as product types and consumer segments, that influence the multichannel effects of email advertising. Overall, our study provides empirical evidence for the economic impact of email ads on consumer behavior across different channels and the underlying mechanisms thereof. Our findings offer direct implications for multichannel retailers seeking to improve their digital marketing strategies, as well as for policymakers interested in evaluating the economic impact of prevalent email advertising.

## 1. Introduction

With the growth of technology and e-commerce, multichannel business and marketing strategies have become increasingly important.<sup>1</sup> Not only have traditional retailers launched online shopping websites but e-commerce giants like Amazon have also forayed into brick-andmortar stores. Statistics reveal that 73% of consumers use multiple channels during their purchase journey (Sopadjieva et al. 2017), with the COVID-19 pandemic boosting a 50% rise in multichannel shopping in the United States (Nielsen IQ 2020). Consequently, multichannel retail has received academic interest across disciplines such as information systems (IS), marketing, and operations management (e.g., Wang and Goldfarb 2017, Bell et al. 2018, Kumar et al. 2019, Soysal et al. 2019, Han et al. 2020, Samuel et al. 2020).

Whereas online advertising is widely used by retailers, its economic impact on multichannel sales remains understudied. Our research investigates the effects of email advertising, a prevalent marketing technique, on multichannel sales. The global email advertising market, valued at \$7.5 billion in 2020 and expected to reach \$17.9 billion by 2027 (Statista 2021), along with high usage rates among marketers and consumers, emphasizes its importance. Moreover, many retailers offer consumers a discount on their next purchase if they sign up for emails (e.g., Macy’s offers 25% off, Bed Bath & Beyond offers \$20 off, Neiman Marcus offers 10% off), indicating the importance of email subscribers to the firms. Given email advertising’s popularity and most purchases still occurring in physical stores (U.S. Census Bureau 2019), it is crucial to understand its impact on multichannel sales.

Although prior research has explored the multichannel effects of display advertising on consumers (e.g., Lewis and Reiley 2014, Lobschat et al. 2017), their findings cannot be universally applied to email advertising because of the inherent differences between the two mediums.

Email advertising and display advertising, both being online advertising strategies, vary significantly in ad format, content, and purpose. First, they differ primarily in terms of ad format. Email advertising is usually designed using HTML, integrated within email content, and sent as individual promotional messages to consumer inboxes. In contrast, display advertising typically leverages visual creatives based on eye-catching graphics and images that are placed on websites as banners. Second, email advertising often includes a mix of promotional offers and company updates, whereas display advertising typically focuses on promoting a specific brand or product to capture consumer attention online. Third, email advertising is usually used to maintain consumer relationships and stimulate loyalty and purchases, whereas display advertising is mainly used to elevate brand visibility across various platforms and direct traffic to specific landing pages to catalyze sales. As a result, their effects on consumers also differ, as suggested in the ad attribution literature (e.g., Li and Kannan 2014, Abhishek et al. 2015, Ghose and Todri-Adamopoulos 2016). Our paper specifically examines the multichannel effects of email advertising because of its unique characteristics.

Failing to capture the multichannel impact of email advertising may have dire consequences for researchers and practitioners. First, ignoring the cross-channel impact of email advertising can lead to an underestimation of their overall effectiveness, which could result in incorrect conclusions about the return on investment of marketing campaigns. Second, a lack of understanding of the multichannel impact of email advertising can lead to suboptimal allocation of marketing budgets. Firms might divert resources to other marketing channels that may not generate the same level of returns. Third, if marketers are unaware of the differential effects of email advertising across different channels, product types, and consumer segments, they may not tailor their campaigns effectively to maximize engagement and conversion. This can result in wasted resources and missed opportunities to connect with consumers. Fourth, without a clear understanding of the impact of email advertising on in-store sales, firms may not capitalize on opportunities to drive foot traffic to their physical stores and leverage the unique advantages of in-person shopping experiences. Moreover, failing to recognize the multichannel impact of email advertising may hinder retailers from integrating their marketing efforts across channels. This could lead to inconsistent messaging, disjointed consumer experiences, and missed opportunities to leverage synergies between online and offline channels.

Although it is crucial for researchers and practitioners to measure the multichannel impact of email advertising, several challenges may hinder our understanding of this. On one hand, obtaining high-quality data that capture both online and offline consumer behavior can be diffi cult. Retailers might not have the necessary infrastruc ture to track and consolidate consumer data across channels at the individual level, limiting the ability to analyze the multichannel impact of email advertising. On the other hand, accurately attributing sales to specific marketing activities can be challenging, as firms often target consumers based on their observed characteristics or behaviors. Moreover, traditional methods may not be well suited for analyzing high-dimensional granular data, which can limit the ability to uncover the multi channel effects of email campaigns. Our research aims to address these challenges and provide empirical evidence on the multichannel impact of email ads on consumers spending and the underlying mechanisms thereof which is crucial to help retailers make informed deci sions about marketing resource allocation and channe management strategy, ultimately leading to improved business performance. Specifically, we seek to answer the following research questions. What is the economic impact of email ads on the actual revenues of the retailer? If email ads influence sales, how does this effect diffe across different channels? How is this effect moderated by different factors such as product types and consumer segments? To answer these questions, we have obtained a proprietary data set from a major U.S. retailer of home and office supplies. This retailer has both a web store (online) and a chain of physical stores (offline), selling millions of products across a wide variety of categories. This data set includes detailed records of all the market ing, visitation, and e-commerce data and item-leve transaction records across channels for each consumer. Using this data set, we employ a doubly robust estimato (DRE) that incorporates nonparametric machine learning methods to estimate effects of email ads across differen channels, which protects against bias because of mode misspecification, avoids reliance on unrealistic parametric distributions, and reduces the “curse of dimensionality” commonly faced when using matching estimators in the presence of big data.

Our analysis reveals a significant positive impact of email ads on consumer spending, both online and in physical stores. Whereas the effects vary across channels, email ads effectively increase exposure to the retailer’s products and services. Moreover, receiving email ads increases consumers’ visiting frequency to the online shopping website and their browsing breadth measured by the number of unique webpages they view online, which can facilitate their discovery of different products. However, receiving email ads does not significantly affect consumers’ browsing depth measured by the duration of their visits to the digital channel. We also find that email ads drive consumers to shop in physical stores, where they can evaluate different types of products in person. This increases the sales of experience goods in physical stores, whereas, in the online channel, the impact of email ads is more pronounced on search goods. Further analysis demonstrates that the increased spending in the store results from higher purchase incidence and increased variety of products purchased. However, this effect also varies across channels. Email ads seem to draw consumer attention, reminding them of the retailer’s diverse product offerings and increasing the likelihood of consumer purchase. Overall, our paper provides direct managerial guidance for firms investing in email ads, particularly those adopting a multichannel strategy. Discussions with the firm we studied reveal that it has been underestimating the impact of email ads by ignoring its impact on offline sales.<sup>2</sup> Our findings emphasize the need to synergistically integrate different channels in the retail industry, as consumers may explore various products online but still opt to purchase in physical stores for better assessment of product quality and fit, highlighting the complementarity of digital and physical channels.

Our paper makes several contributions to the literature. First, it contributes to the online advertising literature by shedding light on the economic impact of email ads on consumers’ actual spending across different channels. Specifically, our findings indicate that the effect of email ads on consumer spending is approximately 16% in physical stores and 19% in online stores, providing practical implications for firms and policymakers to better evaluate the profitability of email ads. Second, it contributes to the multichannel literature by expanding email marketing’s single-channel scope (online) to a multichannel scope and providing empirical evidence on the multichannel effects of email ads on consumer behavior. Third, it provides insights into the mechanisms behind email ads’ effectiveness by examining moderating factors and a broad range of consumer behaviors along the purchase funnel. Last, it also helps enrich the theory on showrooming and multichannel ad attribution. Whereas prior literature has examined the showrooming phenomenon in multichannel retail where consumers evaluate products at physical stores only to purchase their chosen product online for a lower price at a competing online retailer (Mehra et al. 2018), our research documents an interesting webrooming effect of online advertising such that email ads increase consumers’ likelihood to browse a variety of products online yet opt to make their purchase in store, where they can physically interact with the product before making their final decision. Our findings contribute to a more holistic understanding of ad attribution by analyzing the impact of email advertising across channels, highlighting the necessity of considering these multichannel effects when evaluating marketing efforts.

## 2. Literature Review

## 2.1. Consumer Behavior in Multichannel Retail

Our paper contributes to the extensive body of literature that explores consumer behavior across multiple channels. As technology increasingly blurs the boundaries between online and offline realms, existing research investigates the disparities in consumer behavior across various channels. Whereas some studies identify a complementary effect across channels, others detect a substitution effect. For example, Biyalogorsky and Naik (2003) suggest that the addition of an online channel does not significantly detract from offline sales but, rather, amplifies overall purchase share. Ansari et al. (2008) also argue for this positive impact of online channel usage on merchant sales because of the small overlap between online consumers and physical store visitors. More recent stud ies find that the adoption of digital channels may prompt consumers to make more purchases (Xu et al. 2017, Sun et al. 2019) and that the opening of physical stores can boost both online and overall demand (Bell et al. 2018, Kumar et al. 2019).

Conversely, other research has demonstrated a substitution effect between different channels. For example, Forman et al. (2009) find that consumers shift away from online purchasing when a physical store opens nearby because of decreased transaction costs and enhanced access to physical facilities. Avery et al. (2012) suggest that channel substitutions occur in certain scenarios where the capability of the added channel is superior or closely duplicates that of the existing one. Wang and Goldfarb (2017) demonstrate that online and offline channels may function as substitutes in distribution but as complements in marketing communications. The interplay between online and offline channels in multichannel retail could thus manifest as either complementary or substitutive, making it challenging to predict consumers’ multichannel behavior in response to online advertising. Our paper enriches this literature by presenting empirical evidence on the multichannel impact of email ads.

## 2.2. Effect of Online Advertising on Offline Sales

Information systems and marketing researchers have long been interested in measuring the effectiveness of different online advertising formats (Li et al. 2019). Several studies have investigated consumers’ responses to email ads in the online channel. For example, Wattal et al. (2012) find that consumers may react negatively when the firm uses their personally identifiable information in email ads because of privacy concerns, whereas Sahni et al. (2018) find that personalizing the emails by adding consumer-specific information benefits the advertisers. Kumar et al. (2014) investigate the impact of marketing activities on consumers’ propensity to opt in or out of email marketing lists. Sahni et al. (2017) find that emails can positively impact consumer purchases online. Zhang et al. (2017) find that email-active consumers are not necessarily associated with a higher purchase frequency. Another study that is closely related to our research is Danaher et al. (2020), who find that emails from a focal brand have a positive influence on its multichannel sales and a negative influence on other brands within the clothing category. Our research differs from this study in that we investigate how the multichannel effects of email advertising vary across different product categories and consumer segments. Moreover, we examine its impact on diverse consumer behaviors along the purchase funnel to uncover the underlying mechanisms thereof. To the best of our knowledge, only a few papers have examined the cross-channel effect of online advertising in general, primarily because of the scarcity of high-quality, individual-level data spanning multiple channels. For example, prior research has employed aggregate-level data to establish correlations between aggregate advertising expenditures and total revenue across different channels (e.g., Naik and Peters 2009, Wiesel et al. 2011, Dinner et al. 2014). More recently, several studies have collected individual-level data to examine the crosschannel effects of display advertising (Lewis and Reiley 2014, Johnson et al. 2017, Lobschat et al. 2017), search advertising (Chan et al. 2011), and direct mailing (Lesscher et al. 2021). Our research complements this literature by investigating the cross-channel effect of email advertising, which serves as a primary marketing too for our focal retailer and many other firms.<sup>3</sup>

## 2.3. Digital Attribution

Our paper also contributes to the burgeoning literature on digital attribution. Digital attribution is concerned with allocating credit for a consumer’s purchase across different marketing channels to which the consumer was exposed during the shopping process. Recent literature has emphasized the importance of marketing attribution challenges because of variances in the effectiveness of online advertising as the marketing channel or format changes (Li and Kannan 2014, Ghose and Todri-Adamopoulos 2016, Cui et al. 2021). Given the plethora of digital advertising channels, it is imperative for managers to understand the influence of each marketing channel in order to optimize their return on investment among other advertising objectives. Our paper extends this literature by investigating the economic impact of email advertising on consumer spending across multiple channels and examining the underlying mechanisms by investigating diverse consumer activities and different moderating factors.

## 3. Theoretical Background

Prior literature suggests that consumers progress through various stages of deliberation before reaching a final purchase decision, which is often referred to as the pur chase funnel, a concept based on information processing theory (Strong 1925, Howard and Sheth 1969, Barry 1987, Bettman et al. 1998). This framework often comprises stages of awareness, consideration, and purchase (Jansen and Schuster 2011, Bruce et al. 2012, Abhishek et al. 2015). According to this framework, consumers are initially dis engaged from the seller when they are unaware of the product or are not deliberating a purchase. When consumers are exposed to an ad, it may prompt them to enter the awareness stage. For instance, upon receiving an email ad, consumers may become cognizant of the retailer. They might open the email and click on embed ded links that direct them to the shopping website. As the users in our study are already aware of the firm, we consider the top of the funnel to be an activation stage where consumers’ association with the firm is rekindled. Subsequently, if consumers find the retailer’s products appealing, they proceed to the consideration stage, where they may engage in information-seeking activities such as browsing various webpages and examining detailed product information. This may result in consumers spending more time on the shopping website, indicating that the retailer has captured their attention and that they are developing a favorable disposition toward the product or retailer. Finally, after due consideration, consumers may decide to make a purchase. Drawing upon this theoretical framework, we delineate three distinct stages in the consumer shopping process and contribute to the literature by empirically investigating how email advertising affects consumers’ various behaviors within a multichannel context. The operationalization of these stages is detailed further in Section 4.

## 4. Data

Our data set is from a leading U.S. retailer in the home and office supplies sector, ranking among the top three worldwide by revenue.<sup>4</sup> The firm operates both an online store and a chain of physical stores, managed sep arately by different teams. The retailer offers more than three million products and services across various cate gories, both online and in physical stores.<sup>5</sup> The raw data, encompassing eight months from December 2012 to August 2013, include marketing, visitation, and e-commerce data (online) as well as transaction data in physical stores (offline) for 23 million consumers. This large-scale data set was collated by combining separate data sources using a proprietary entity disambiguation technique developed by our partner, a leading data analytics company in the U.S. This company employs mapping points such as email addresses, phone numbers, loyalty cards, postal addresses, cookies, data broker information, and other online and offline activities to consolidate sources to a common grouping key through a process called digital fingerprinting.<sup>6</sup> This offers a holistic view of consumers’ interactions with the retailer across different channels, allowing for a more accurate observation of online and offline consumer behavior at the individual level. As our study focuses on email advertising, we consider only consumers enrolled in the retailer’s email-based loyalty program, reducing the data set to 11.7 million consumers.

Figure 1 depicts the number of consumers exposed to email and display ads weekly. Email marketing is the primary promotional channel for this retailer.<sup>8</sup> These email ads were sent in bulk with no personalization of content; instead, the email content is designed as a catalog showcasing various products and services offered by the retailer. Figure 2 displays the total number of daily email ads sent to consumers, which is representative of our broader data set, showing a consistent weekly schedule.<sup>9</sup> Direct marketers often refer to this regular schedule of planned campaigns as advertising cadence (Zantedeschi et al. 2016). This email cadence limits the possibility of simultaneity between advertising exposures and periods with unexplained shocks in demand, which could result in biased estimated effects of email advertising.

We randomly sampled approximately 100,000 consumers, creating a weekly panel over 10 weeks (from March 11, 2013 to May 19, 2013).<sup>10</sup> The summary statistics for the weekly panel are provided in Table 1.<sup>11</sup> The aim is to investigate the economic impact of email ads on consumers’ spending across different channels. To uncover the underlying mechanisms, we also examine how email ads affect consumers’ various behaviors through the purchase funnel. We define three stages during the funnel: activation, consideration, and purchase (Jansen and Schuster 2011, Bruce et al. 2012, Abhishek et al. 2015). We then construct a set of variables reflecting consumers’ behaviors at these stages. In the activation stage, we examine email opening frequency, measured by the total number of times the consumer opened email ads weekly, and online visit frequency, measured by the total number of visit sessions the consumer had on the shopping website weekly (Gopalakrishnan and Park 2021, Sun et al. 2022).<sup>12</sup> In the consideration stage, we investigate consumers’ browsing depth, measured by the total amount of time (in minutes) a consumer spent on the shopping website weekly, and browsing breadth, measured by the number of unique webpages viewed by the consumer weekly (Huang et al. 2009, Sun et al. 2022). When consumers spend more time on the shop ping website and browse more webpages, this indicates that consumers are likely to consider the products offered by the retailer. In the purchase stage, the key outcome variables are consumers’ actual spending across channels. We also investigate consumers’ purchase incidence, a binary variable indicating whether the consumer has made at least one purchase from that channel, and purchase variety, measured by the number of distinct product categories purchased by the consumer (Xu et al. 2017, Kumar et al. 2019). In the next section, we discuss our empirical strategy for investigating the impact of email ads on various consumer behaviors during the purchase funnel.

## 5. Empirical Strategy

## 5.1. Model Setup

We use a setup following the potential outcome framework (Rubin 1974) with N consumers, indexed by $i = 1 , \ldots , N$ . Let $Y _ { i } \in \mathbb { R }$ denote the outcome variable, a consumer’s offline spending in the target week (May 13–19, 2013), which is a common week without any special holiday events.<sup>13</sup> Let $A _ { i } \in \{ 0 , 1 \}$ } denote a binary treatment (i.e., whether the consumer received email ads or not), whose effect is in question. The treatment group contains consumers who received email ads during the week, whereas the control group comprises those who did not. Note that the number of email ads a consumer receives each week varies over time because of changes in the retailer’s targeting rules or intentional randomness. We randomly draw a sample of 50,000 consumers for our estimation, with equal representation from the treatment and control groups.<sup>14</sup> We postulate the existence of a pair of potential outcomes for each consumer, $( Y _ { i } ^ { 0 } , Y _ { i } ^ { 1 } )$ , representing the outcomes that would have been observed for a particular consumer i under control $( A _ { i } = 0 )$ and treatment $( A _ { i } = 1 )$ , respectively. The realized outcome for consumer i is the potential outcome corresponding to the treatment received: $Y _ { i } ^ { o b s } = \left\{ \begin{array} { l l } { Y _ { i } ^ { 0 } i f A _ { i } = 0 , } \\ { Y _ { i } ^ { 1 } i f A _ { i } = 1 . } \end{array} \right.$

We also consider extensive consumer data $L _ { i } ,$ a D-dimensional vector of covariates derived from consumers’ historical purchasing, coupon usage, online browsing, and advertising exposure and responses during the nine weeks preceding the target week.<sup>15</sup> In summary, our data consist of $\bar { Z _ { i } } = ( Y _ { i } ^ { o b s } , \bar { A } _ { i } , L _ { i } )$ for $i = 1 , \ldots , N$ . We assume the observed data consist of an independent and identically distributed sample of $Z = ( Y , A , L )$ according to some unknown probability distribution $P _ { 0 }$ so that there is no interference between consumers.<sup>16</sup> This is often called the consistency condition. We suppose each consumer has a nonzero probability of receiving treatment level $A = a$ such that $p ( A = a \bar { | _ { L = l } } ) > 0$ whenever $p ( L = l ) > 0$ , which is commonly known as the positivity condition. The average treatment effect (ATE) is given by $\psi = \mathbb { E } [ Y ^ { 1 } - Y ^ { 0 } ] = \breve { \mathbb { E } } \{ \mu ( 1 ) - \mu ( 0 ) \}$ , where $\mu ( a ) = \mathbb { E } ( Y \mid$ $A = a )$ : Two further conditions should be met for us to estimate the ATE correctly. The first condition is the stable unit treatment value assumption, which states that each consumer should receive exactly one treatment and this should not impact the outcome of any other consumer. This condition holds in our context because of the retailer’s separate, confidential email communications.<sup>17</sup> The second condition is the random treatment of consumers. Whereas this condition is not met because of nonrandom email ad delivery, we address this endogeneity issue in the next section.

## 5.2. Endogeneity of Treatment

Endogeneity may arise from user activity, firm targeting, and competition bias (Gordon et al. 2019). User activity bias emerges when frequent internet users receive more ads and are more likely to convert online. Firm targeting bias stems from advertisers targeting high-conversion consumers with more ads, possibly inflating the ATE. Competition bias is linked to ad auctions, where selection based on competitors’ strategies can occur. In our context of email ads, user activity, and competition bias are irrelevant, as email ads are directly sent to consumers. However, email ads could potentially be targeted according to individual characteristics and past purchases. Consequently, we need to adjust for potential confoundedness to estimate the ATE.

The causal impact of advertising has garnered signifi cant attention in the IS and marketing literature. Researchers typically employ one of two causal inference approaches: matching estimators based on observational data $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , Chan et al. 2019, Kumar et al. 2019, Zhou et al. 2020) and randomized experiments $( \mathrm { e . g . } ,$ , Sun et al. 2019, Jung et al. 2020, Yi et al. 2023). Although both methods have been extensively explored, recently, there has been considerable debate on their appropriateness, particularly in the area of advertising (Eckles and Bakshy 2017, Gordon et al. 2019), with matching estimators subject to bias because of the curse of dimensionality (Li et al. 2016). In Online Appendix A1, we demonstrate the existence of the curse of dimensionality in several prevalent approaches, including propensity score matching (PSM), using synthetic and semisynthetic experiments.

One way to address the issue of endogeneity involves using a large number of features as controls, as suggested by Eckles and Bakshy (2017). Their results indicate that high-dimensional models accounting for detailed historical individual behaviors can nearly elimi nate bias, demonstrating that high-dimensional data sets can enhance causal inference using observational data. Nonetheless, Gordon et al. (2019) argue that unobservable factors make ad exposure endogenous, and observational methods might not fully address data limitations or biases. However, both Eckles and Bakshy (2017) and Gordon et al. (2019) concur that the bias in the observational estimates is essentially a function of the effect of unobserved heterogeneity on the treatment. The less correlated the omitted variables are with the treatment and the observed outcomes, the smaller the bias. In our study, we observed all the information the retailer could access for their consumers because of the design of the email targeting software developed by our partnering firm. Therefore, we utilize this data to adjust for the endogeneity in email targeting.

## 5.3. Identification Strategy

To address the endogeneity and the curse of dimensionality, we leverage machine learning techniques for causal estimation. Specifically, we use a doubly robust estimator (Kennedy 2016). DRE relies on two nuisance functions: one for the conditional mean of outcomes (modeling the association between potential outcomes and covariates) and one for the propensity score (modeling the association between the treatment indicator and covariates). As DRE is a nonparametric data-driven method that can flexibly control for many covariates, it suffers less from the curse of dimensionality, and if it is estimated on all the variables that might influence the treatment assignment, the bias because of endogeneity will be largely eliminated. As one of the most effective causal estimation methods in observational studies (Athey and Imbens 2017, Athey et al. 2017), DRE offers several benefits in our setting. First, DRE helps protect against bias because of model misspecification. As long as the estimation for either the conditional mean of the outcomes or the propensity score is consistent, the resulting estimator for the average treatment effect is consistent, thus making it “doubly robust” (Robins and Rotnitzky 1995). This resolves concerns about model uncertainty typically associated with online advertising (Lewis and Rao 2015). Second, DRE employs nonparametric machine learning methods to estimate the nuisance functions, which allows the data-generating process to be mostly unrestricted and avoids relying on unrealistic parametric distributions commonly required in traditional approaches such as maximum-likelihood estimators and Bayesian models. This also reduces the sensitivity to the curse of dimensionality by flexibly controlling for a large number of covariates as part of the identification strategy (Eckles and Bakshy 2017, Kennedy et al. 2017). Third, DRE achieves efficiency in the sense of reaching the semiparametric efficiency bound of Hahn (1998), allowing straightforward construction of valid confidence intervals for the parameters of interest. These properties of DRE enhance the credibility of our causal analysis using the observational data set.

We also assert that the observational data used in this study is appropriate for estimating the causal effect of email ads. It is crucial that conditioned upon a set of observed pretreatment covariates $L ,$ the treatment is independent of potential outcomes. This “no unmeasured confounding” condition is formalized as $A \bot Y ^ { a } \mid L$ This condition is satisfied in our setting, as our focal retailer uses six weeks of data to target consumers, and all this information is available to us.<sup>18</sup> In addition to the raw data, we also construct a large number of pretreatment covariates derived from detailed records of consumers’ behavior over the past nine weeks, similar to the recency, frequency, and monetary (RFM) measures, to adjust for potential confoundedness as part of our estimation strategy for drawing causal inferences using observational data. The use of RFM is supported by extensive marketing literature demonstrating these measures as sufficient statistics for understanding consumer behavior in a noncontractual setting (Fader et al. 2005). We also generate detailed pretreatment covariates from consumers’ browsing history and clickstream data to augment the RFM measures. Notably, unlike Gordon et al. (2019), where many unobserved factors influence the effect of display advertising, our setting comprises loyal consumers of the focal retailer whose past behavior may be predictive of future outcomes. These characteristics of our data-generating process substantially reduce the threat of targeting bias in our estimation and enhance the validity of our causal statements. Note that our strategy is similar in spirit to PSM commonly used in the IS literature, but unlike PSM, DRE does not suffer from the curse of dimensionality in big data settings.

## 6. Doubly Robust Estimator

In this section, we use the DRE to estimate the ATE. The DRE is based on the concept of the influence function to estimate the average effect of the treatment on the outcome variable. The influence function is a foundational object of statistical theory that allows us to characterize a wide range of estimators and their efficiency. There is a deep connection between asymptotically linear estimators for a given model and the influence functions under that model (for a detailed review, see Kennedy 2016).

Our target parameter is the average treatment effect $\psi = \mathbb { E } [ Y ^ { 1 } - Y ^ { 0 } ] = \mathbb { E } \{ \mu ( L , 1 ) - \mu ( L , 0 ) \}$ , where Y denotes the outcome variable, such as consumer spending in the offline or online channel. To reduce the skewness of the variables, we perform log transformation on the values. $A \in \{ 0 , 1 \}$ denotes a binary treatment. We let $\mu ( l , a ) =$ $\mathbb { E } ( Y | L = l , A = a )$ denote the outcome regression function. Also, let $\pi ( l ) = P ( A = 1 \mid L = l )$ denote the propensity score. Both of these nuisance functions are unknown and potentially complicated. We estimate them using nonparametric machine learning methods, which allows the data-generating process to be mostly unrestricted. In this way, we can flexibly control for a large number of covariates. It has been shown that under a nonparametric model of $Z = ( Y , A , L ) \sim P _ { 0 } ,$ , the efficient influence function for ψ is known (see Online Appendix A2 for detailed explanation and proof). Given the known efficient influence function that depends on $\psi$ and $\eta = \left( \pi , \mu \right)$ , we can construct the doubly robust estimato

as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & {\hat {\psi} = \mathbb {P} _ {n} \{m _ {1} (Z; \hat {\eta}) - m _ {0} (Z; \hat {\eta}) \}, \mathrm{with} m _ {a} (Z; \eta) = m _ {a} (Z; \pi , \mu)} \\ & {\qquad = \frac {I (A = a) \{Y - \mu (L , a) \}}{a \pi (L) + (1 - a) \{1 - \pi (L) \}} + \mu (L, a).} \end{array}
$$

In observational studies, covariates L are usually high dimensional, especially in big data settings, and little is known about the propensity score and outcome regression functions. Therefore, we use ensemble machine learning to construct the nuisance function estimator $\hat { \eta } = \left( \hat { \pi } , \hat { \mu } \right)$ . It has been shown that as long as either π ˆ or $\hat { \mu }$ is consistent, the resulting estimator $\hat { \psi }$ for the average treatment effect is consistent, thus making it “doubly robust,” which protects against bias because of model misspecification (Kennedy 2016). Based on the constructed estimator $\hat { \psi } ,$ we make use of sample splitting in our estimation process, which helps avoid overfitting. The specific estimation steps are detailed in Online Appendix A3. Code examples are provided in Online Appendix $\mathbf { A 4 } ,$ as well as in the freely available R package called npcausal.<sup>19</sup> We also perform various robustness checks and sensitivity analyses as detailed in Online Appendix A5.

## 7.2. Further Analysis of Consumer Behavior in the Purchase Funnel

To uncover the underlying mechanisms behind the effects of email ads on consumers’ purchases, we delve deeper into the impact of email ads on various consumer behaviors through the lens of the purchase funnel. Following the literature, we define three distinct stages in the purchase funnel: activation (awareness), consideration, and purchase (Jansen and Schuster 2011, Bruce et al. 2012, Abhishek et al. 2015) as outlined in Section 3. We perform log transformation on the variables in each stage (as discussed in Section 4) to reduce skewness. We then apply the same DRE with different dependent vari ables to conduct further analyses of consumers’ activities during the purchase funnel. In the activation stage, we examine the effects of email ads on two types of consumer behaviors: consumers’ email opening frequency and online visit frequency. The results are summarized in Table 3, showing that receiving email ads has a signifi cant positive impact on consumers’ behaviors in the acti vation stage. When consumers open email ads more frequently, it may remind them of the retailer’s diverse product offerings. Furthermore, receiving email ads sig nificantly boosts consumers’ likelihood of visiting the online shopping website. Consumers may directly click on the links within email ads to visit the retailer’s website and place an order online. This also increases their chances of browsing the website, inspecting more products, and learning about various products and services offered by the retailer, potentially nudging consumers to transition into later stages of the purchase funnel.

In the consideration stage, we investigate the effects of email ads on consumers’ browsing depth and browsing breadth. As shown in the results summarized in Table 4, receiving email ads significantly increases consumers browsing breadth but does not necessarily impact their browsing depth. This finding suggests that email ads do not significantly affect the duration of consumers’ visits to the shopping website, but they can effectively encourage consumers to explore more extensive range of webpages, facilitating their discovery of different types of the retailer’s various products and services. Together, these results imply that some consumers may use digital channels for information gathering rather than simply as an electronic means to an end. Their shopping journey may include, but not necessarily culminate in, the online channel. Receiving email ads may create a webrooming effect by increasing consumers’ likelihood of visiting offline stores to experience some products in person and make a purchase decision in the future.

## 7.3. Moderating Effects of Product Types and Consumer Segments

To gain a deeper understanding of the effects of email advertising and further uncover the underlying mechanisms, we investigate how product types (i.e., search versus experience goods) and consumer segments (i.e., frequent versus nonfrequent consumers) moderate the impact of email ads across different channels. Nelson’s seminal work on the economics of information and advertising (Nelson 1970, Nelson 1974) classified products into search and experience goods. Search goods possess attributes that are primarily objective and discernable before purchase, whereas experience goods exhibit predominantly subjective attributes, often only discernable through product use. Exploring the moderating effects of search versus experience goods is important in the context of online advertising and multichannel retail, as online shopping websites and physical stores provide distinct shopping experiences for consumers. For instance, online shopping websites offer convenience, lower transaction costs, and the ability to compare prices and products quickly while facing challenges such as product quality and fit uncertainty. Physi cal stores, on the other hand, offer tactile interactions with the products and immediacy in obtaining them, whereas consumers may face additional costs related to time and travel. Understanding how these product types moderate the impact of email ads in multichannel retail can provide valuable insights into optimizing marketing efforts for different product types and shopping channels. Prior studies have examined how search versus experience goods affect consumer behavior on the internet (e.g., Huang et al. 2009, Animesh et al. 2010, Dimoka et al. 2012, Lee and Hosanagar 2021). However, how product types may moderate the effects of email ads on consumer purchases across different channels remains empirically unexplored in the existing literature, which is what we aim to address in our analysis.

In particular, we classify the products into search and experience goods: search goods consist of computers and accessories, business machines, office supplies, and store supplies, whereas experience goods consist of furniture, food and housewares, business services, and technology supporting services.<sup>21</sup> We observe that search goods consistently outsell experience goods in both offline and online channels. Specifically, search goods account for 65% of offline sales and 61% of online sales. We then estimate the economic impact of email ads for search and experience goods across different channels to uncover the underlying mechanisms, as presented in Table 6. Specifically, the estimated average treatment effect of email ads on consumers’ in-store spending is approximately 21% for experience goods and 13% for search goods. The estimated average treatment effect of email ads on consumers’ spending on the online shopping website is approximately 12% for experience goods and 16% for search goods. Overall, the effect on experience goods is more pronounced in the offline channel, whereas for the online channel, the effect on search goods is more pronounced. Our findings indicate that email ads may drive more consumers to visit physica stores where they can assess different product attributes in person and determine whether they meet their preferences, resulting in a relatively larger sales increase for experience goods. In contrast, the online channel exhibits a more pronounced impact on search goods, as consumers can readily compare search goods’ attributes on the online website and finalize their purchase. This effect is comparatively smaller for experience goods because some experience attributes cannot be perfectly evaluated online and experience goods are more prone to product uncertainty. We believe that our study is the first to document this moderating role of product types on the effect of email ads on consumers’ multichannel behavior and that it provides the mechanism through which the effect of email ads manifests. Our results also emphasize the differences and complementary aspects of digital and physical channels in consumers’ shopping processes.

We further investigate the moderating effect of consumer segments (i.e., frequent versus nonfrequent consumers) on the impact of email ads across different channels. Examining the differences between frequent and nonfrequent consumers is important because their prior knowledge and familiarity with the retailer may significantly vary, leading to distinct responses to email advertising and purchasing behavior across channels. For instance, nonfrequent consumers may lack familiarity and experience with the retailer and its products, whereas frequent consumers have established relationships and trust with the retailer, which can influence their receptiveness to email advertising and channel preferences. By understanding how the impact of email advertising in multichannel retail varies across consume segments, businesses can tailor their marketing strategies to effectively target and engage each group, ultimately optimizing the allocation of marketing resources and driving overall sales growth. Overall, the impact of email ads on consumer spending is more pronounced for frequent consumers. With deeper relationships and trust in the retailer, frequent consumers tend to be more receptive and responsive to email advertising. In contrast, nonfrequent consumers, who lack familiarity and experience with the retailer, may need more incentives to make a purchase because of their comparatively lower levels of trust and familiarity. Furthermore, the cross-channel effect of email ads on offline sales is primarily driven by frequent consumers, with no significant impact on nonfrequent consumers. Nonfrequent consumers might adopt a digital-first mindset when receiving email ads from the retailer, leading them to visit the shopping website first, particularly if they are unfamiliar with the location and layout of physical stores offering a vast array of products. Additionally, nonfrequent consumers may want to gather more information about the retailer and compare other retailers offerings and prices, which is easier to do online than offline. For frequent consumers with established trust and familiarity with the retailer, they are more likely to visit physical stores where they can benefit from immediate access to products and in-person shopping experiences such as personalized assistance from in-store staff. Our findings underscore the importance of understanding the distinct preferences and behaviors of different consumers when developing marketing strategies in the context of email advertising and multichannel retail. By tailoring marketing efforts to effectively target and engage each consumer segment across channels, retailers can optimize the allocation of marketing resources and better cater to their consumers’ preferences to drive overall sales growth.

## 8. Conclusions

This study improves our understanding of email ads influence on consumer purchasing behavior across different channels, providing insights into how firms can optimize their digital marketing strategies. First, our findings reveal that the effect of email ads on consumer spending is approximately 16% in physical stores and 19% in online stores. These findings provide empirical evidence to inform firms’ budget allocation decisions. Second, we broaden the email marketing literature’s single-channel scope (online) to a multichannel scope and uncover the varied effects of email ads across channels. Third, our paper delves into the mechanisms of these effects by considering various consumer behaviors during the purchase process and the moderating effects of product types. Finally, we identify variations in the effectiveness of email ads across consumer segments, informing tailored email marketing strategies. Our research contributes to the online advertising literature by offering unique insights into the multichannel effects of email advertising vis-a-vis display advertising. We also extend this literature by considering a wider range of consumer behaviors, viewing them through the lens of the purchase funnel theory, and exploring moderating factors such as product types and consumer segments.

Our paper provides important managerial implications for firms leveraging email ads, particularly those employing a multichannel strategy. Our findings underline that email ads positively influence consumer spending in both digital and physical retail spaces, suggesting firms should integrate their marketing efforts across different channels to improve overall business performance. An essential finding from our study is that email ads may serve as a potent instrument for increasing firm exposure and reminding consumers about the wide array of products and services offered. As email ads enhance consumers’ frequency of visiting the online shopping website and broaden their browsing breadth, businesses should calibrate their email marketing strate gies to foster consumers’ product discovery online and augment their digital footprint rather than increasing browsing depth. It is crucial for firms to design their email ads strategically to capture consumer attention and highlight the diversity of their product offerings. Moreover, our analysis suggests that email ads effectively drive more consumer purchases in physical stores, significantly contributing to the sales of experience goods that benefit from in-person evaluation. Our study emphasizes the integral role of physical stores in consumers shopping journeys and a complementary relationship between digital and physical channels. Our findings emphasize the importance of maintaining a synergistic integration of digital and physical channels in the multichannel retail strategy, particularly for firms selling experience goods. Finally, our study reveals that the impact of email ads varies across consumer segments, highlight ing the importance for firms to tailor their marketing strategies to target different consumer segments, which can drive overall sales growth.

We acknowledge several limitations to our study that open interesting directions for future research. Our data represent a large sample of a major retailer’s consumers who chose to subscribe to the email program, potentially limiting the generalizability of our findings. We also did not consider consumers’ exposure to offline advertising and the dynamics of promotion duration, which can be a consideration for some firms. Moreover, we did not take into account the firm’s competitors. Future research could model the dynamics between competing retailers to understand how that affects consumers’ purchase behavior. Finally, the focal retailer offers over three million products and lacks precise information on the initial release date of each product. Future research could benefit from a more in-depth exploration distinguishing between newly introduced and established products.
