# The Open Prison of the Big Data Revolution: False Consciousness, Faustian Bargains, and Digital Entrapment

Abstract. Although some scholars raise alarm about societal harm emerging from Big Data practices, critical social theory (CST) Information Systems research on the structures and dynamics driving Big Data practices is rare. In this research commentary, we interrogate how tech firms use social practices and platform design to strategically manipulate individuals into accepting datafication and data assetization that accrue positive data network effects for themselves and mostly negative data network effects (economic loss, social and privacy harm) for individuals. We draw on the ideas of Heidegger and Marcuse to critically question the Big Data paradigm in order to develop better understanding of the social implications fo individuals and society. Using the concepts of false consciousness, digital entrapment, and Faustian bargains, we critically inquire into the Big Data practices that keep us tethered to digital platforms. Specifically, we interrogate sociomaterial structures that socially condition indivi duals into a digital habitus and to identify themselves as homo digitalis, who view all their “relations” (social and economic) as digital. This social conditioning reproduces a false consciousness that constricts our worldview, undermines our rational choices, and enables the risky compromises we make with tech companies that manipulate and exploit us with their increasingly oppressive Big Data practices and related dark patterns. We critically analyze the case of Microsoft Viva to provide an illustration of how mundane digital tools can condition our real ity and entrap us into an open prison. We argue that if we do not critically interrogate our false consciousness of the digital and understand how digital giants colonize our social systems by structurally embedding Big Data practices, we will continue to be susceptible to manipulation and digital entrapment. Ongoing risky compromises with tech firms will erode the very foundations of the “good life,” freedom, liberty, and personal privacy, and they will institutionalize the open prison. The CST explanation we propose and the research agenda we outline are meant to encourage research into solutions to the digital entrapment problem

## Introduction

During the last two decades, we have seen the rise of the Big Data revolution and the enthusiastic embrace of data as a new paradigm of social organization (Sadowski 2019, Birch 2020).<sup>1</sup> In this new paradigm, human activities, such as politics, government, healthcare, education, trade, commerce, travel, and leisure, are subjected to Big Data practices, specifically datafication, dataveillance, dark patterns and machine learning analytics, in order to “extract value” and accumulate wealth from our digital footprints (Desouza 2014, Kitchin 2014, Thatcher 2014, Luguri and Strahilevitz 2021). Many digital apps and platforms now essential to our daily lives embed these Big Data practices. A core infrastructure of this new paradigm is the Internet of Things, which enables everything (cars, stoves, refrigerators, gas meters, etc.) to collect data about everybody’s life and accumulate them in Big Data repositories for others to mine (Liu and Zhou 2012, Caron et al. 2016). Although many apps and platforms offer convenience and improvements to our social and professional lives, they datafy us and track our everyday activities to assetize our data traces (Van Dijck 2014, Zuboff 2015, Ravenelle 2019, Birch et al. 2020). Business leaders, tech entrepreneurs, and innovators adopt Big Data practices as a strategy for business expansion, whereas scientists and government officials promulgate Big Data practices as solutions to problems in every sphere of society without much critical reflection (Chen et al. 2012, Lo¨fgren and Webster 2020).

In a very short period historically, Big Data practices have become normalized and now mediate almost every social interaction, whereas we seem unaware or complacent about their implications on our ways of life (Strang and Sun 2017, Southerton and Taylor 2020). The ontological shift in how we think about the remaking of our socie ties termed “digital first” (Baskerville et al. 2020) and “digital by default” (Schou and Pors 2019) is based on the ideology that digital is “best” (Rosen and Alvarez Leo´n 2022). We have rapidly come to accept the idea that we are homo digitalis and are increasingly entrapped by digital technologies (Ninet 2019, Meng et al. 2022). On the personal level, we engage with “smart” devices, gadgets, digital platforms (Facebook, WhatsApp, Instagram, Tik-Tok), and other digital infrastructures, which help to facilitate the structural imbedding of Big Data practices in our social systems (Rowe et al. 2020, Ngwenyama et al. 2023). On a professional level, platforms promoted as necessary for career progression (e.g., LinkedIn) as well as core infrastructure for knowledge work all embed Big Data practices. Take, for example, Microsoft Viva, which many organizations use. Microsoft states: “Viva is an employee experience platform that delivers insight, purpose, connection, and growth to empower employees and teams to be their best from anywhere … Viva Insights provides data-driven visibility into how work patterns affect wellbeing and productivity, and business performance” (M13).<sup>2</sup> Viva is marketed as a smart analytics tool integrated in the Microsoft 365 enterprise suite, and it invisibly surveils our activities, accumulating data traces for analysis by its machine learning algorithm and reporting to everyone who has access to its repository. Microsoft’s idea is that employee well-being and empowerment are achieved through Big Data practices of digital surveillance, datafication, self-quantification, and algorithmic management (Gal et al. 2020).

Recently, there has been a growing recognition across social science disciplines that Big Data practices are increasingly exploitative, undermining human freedom and dignity (Coeckelbergh 2018, Montag et al. 2019, Ravenelle 2019). The fierce competition to accumulate and assetize data for wealth generation has led to exploitative Big Data practices with grave consequences for individuals (Birch et al. 2021, Geiger and Gross 2021). Zuboff (2015) cautions that data capitalist practices are transforming “historical relationships between markets and democracies, creating new logics of digital hegemony in society.” Moreover, Introna (2016) highlights the challenges to democratic values we now face from algorithms acting as hybrid social agents governing our everyday social practices. Some researchers also argue that Big Data practices of datafication and self-quantification<sup>3</sup> are socially problematic (O’Neill 2017, DeMoya and Pallud 2020). Still, systematic critique of this new paradigm is very limited (Abbasi et al. 2016, Clarke 2019), and mainstream views promote a false consciousness<sup>4</sup> of Big Data practices that support uncritical colonization of our social systems (Couldry and Mejias 2019, Rowe et al. 2020). Moreover, powerful stakeholders invest huge resources in shaping and maintaining our false consciousness of Big Data practices as a mechanism for securing our digital entrapment,<sup>5</sup> manipulation, and exploitation. If we do not critically interrogate our taken for granted beliefs about Big Data practices before it is too late, we could find ourselves locked in an open prison of totalitarian control that is difficult to escape (Marcuse 1972). The goal of this research commentary is, therefore, to outline a theory of the problem<sup>6</sup> of digital entrapment. We focus on three basic questions. What is digital entrapment? What sociomaterial structures and conditions give rise to digital entrapment? Why is it difficult to recognize digital entrapment and escape it?

We propose a critical social theory (CST) explanation of how our embrace of everyday digital technologies can entrap us in an open prison of exploitive social practices that limit our rational choices (Honneth 2014, Rosen 2016). We draw on critical social theory (Heidegger 1954, Marcuse 1964, Honneth 2014) to reveal and critique value conflicts and dysfunctional social relationships with digital technologies that give rise to the social problematic of digital entrapment. Using the concepts of false consciousness and entrapment (Heidegger 1954) as well as the Faustian bargain (Marlowe 1604), we interrogate sociomaterial structures and relational dynamics of Big Data practices embedded in commonly used digital technologies. In CST analysis, it is common to use the term “we” to focus attention on both individual and shared social structures (ideology, taken for granted norms, etc.) that condition social actions (Habermas 1971, Rosen 2016). The “we” used in this paper is not designating the views of particular streams of IS research but the mundane individual and society.

The IS research streams on “emergence,” “complexity,” “unintended effects,” and “structuration theory” have squarely rejected technological determinism. Yet, in our view, they have not been critical enough in challenging the dominant technology power structures and how we interact with them. Although these research streams have fostered a tradition of illuminating complex dynamics, they did so at a time when there were no digital giants (Marjanovic et al. 2021), and the state or innovators could always intervene and rebalance the state of affairs. Now, we realize that we face a bigger societal and political problem and need to embrace a more critical discourse. This research commentary contributes to the discourse by analyzing, exposing, and critiquing ideology and other structures of domination, which inhibit rational choices and actions concerning digital technologies, even when they are against our own interests (Agger 1991, Fuchs 2016, Rosen 2016). The rest of the paper unfolds as follows. Next, we present a short summary of key themes in the emerging discourse on Big Data practices. We outline and discuss the key concepts of our framework, followed by a brief outline of the principles of critical social theory analysis. We then present our explanation of digital entrapment, followed by a critical analysis of Microsoft Viva to illustrate that our concepts have empirical validity. Finally, we discuss some implications for research and action, and we present our conclusions.

## The Emerging Discourse on Big Data Practices

In the IS literature and beyond (Calvo 2020), Big Data practices are generally characterized by (1) datafication of the individual, (2) access to and monitoring of realtime data through cloud and internet technologies, and (3) algorithmic power (Table 1). Individuals’ datafication sums up the individual in terms of behaviors that can be tracked through her digital “footprints.” Panoramic tracking of individuals requires close to real-time access to data in order to analyze and predict user behavior in the interest of platform owners. The internet, higher-bandwidth (5G) communication, and cloud architectures enable surveillance, collection, and storage of a huge volume and variety of evolving data. Big Data technologies employ machine learning and related algorithmic techniques to process these data, broadly referred to as artificial intelligence (AI). Machine learning algorithms and the growing access to data are giving rise to data network effects (DNEs) that create value for service providers and some users (Gregory et al. 2021) while increasing our exposure to social harm (DeMoya and Pallud 2020, Gal et al. 2020). However, generalizing machine learning results without considering specifics about domain practices and limitations of the training data often leads to inaccuracies and social harm (Lebovitz et al. 2021, Urquhart and Miranda 2022). In a recent review article, Koukouvinou and Holmstro¨m (2022) argue that, beyond dys topian and utopian outcomes, AI is augmenting labor, changing value creation, and raising new concerns about ethical management. In the ethical AI literature, critical theory is scarce. Although some refer to algorith mic pollution (Marjanovic et al. 2021) to emphasize that social harm is diffuse and can be associated with the idea of pollution, others propose that AI assistants be designed to emancipate users (Kane et al. 2021). Notably, in this literature, ecological harm is rarely addressed (Koukouvinou and Holmstro¨m 2022). Instead, the AI literature suggests that technology is neither good nor bad nor neutral per se (Kranzberg 1986) as it is embedded in social contexts that interact with its promoters’ or designers’ interests (Lindgren and Holmstro¨m 2020, Kane et al. 2021).

Big Data practices can be both misleading and powerful because “they create the understanding of the phenomena they are taken to describe” (Jones 2019, p. 10). What counts as Big Data is what gets recorded and what gets used (Jones 2019). Technologies and Big Data practices are first and foremost political and related to values (Kranzberg 1986, Rowe 2018), and thus, they are prone to manipulation. Typically, Big Data practices effects, such as filter bubbles, echo chambers (Kitchens et al. 2020), or knowledge hiding (Ghasemaghaei and Turel 2021), have been documented. An ongoing critical concern of these practices identified by digital humanities literature is the infringement of individual data assets ownership rights (Sadowski 2019) and abuse by surveillance capitalists (Zuboff 2015, 2019a).

A few articles have discussed emerging challenges of people analytics in terms of ethical responsibility (Gal et al. 2020) and new risks to employees and organizations (Giermindl et al. 2022). In their seminal editorial, Abbasi et al. (2016) specifically articulated the need for intensive critical research on Big Data practices and their impacts on human empowerment and freedom. Similarly, Ransbotham et al. (2016) suggested that research is necessary to develop mitigation strategies if we are to avoid disastrous consequences of the Big Data practices pursued by tech companies to disrupt and supplant long established societal norms and systems. Although the discourse is progressing, our review of the IS literature shows that there is (a) still relatively little attention paid to the dark side of Big Data practices and (b) unsystematic coverage of the entrapment phenomenon. For example, very few articles interrogate the social structures of digital capitalism and their implications for society. Leclercq-Vandelannoitte and Aroles (2020) offer a Deleuzian analysis of the progressive normalization of digita control and its implications for domination of society, whereas Zuboff (2015, 2019a) lays out broad contours of the challenges that data capitalism poses to democratic freedoms of individual and society. However, there is not any IS literature that specifically investigates the structures and dynamics of digital entrapment. Moreover, Clarke (2019) laments the inability of “mainstream IS research genres” to support investigation of values and value conflicts of data capitalism and their implications for society (Clarke 2019, p. 71). He also suggests that critical theory research can “directly address the value-conflict challenge, by recognising the effects of power and the tendency of some stakeholders interests to dominate those of other stakeholders” (Clarke 2019, p. 70). In the same vein, Hovorka and Peter (2021) argue for research that “focus on values and logic, highlighting disharmonies, encouraging critical reinterpretation, and connecting discourse in science and technology to power, social orders, and justice” (Hovorka and Peter 2021, p. 1739). Critical IS research on Big Data practices is not only necessary to protect individuals from their exploitative use but also serves our scientific responsibility of preserving the public good (Stahl 2012a, b).

## Conceptual Framework for Analysis

The focus of our paper is not an empirical account of digital entrapment but a critical explication of socio material structures and conditions that give rise to dig ital entrapment and their implications for individua and society. Our approach to critically questioning Big Data practices is rooted in a critical social theory tradi tion that is committed to Hegelian dialectics of inquiry. Our conceptual framework (see Figure 1) comprises the CST core concepts of enframing, digital entrapment, and Faustian bargains. In “The question con cerning technology,” Heidegger (1954) develops the concepts of enframing and entrapment to interrogate what he saw as our uncritical embrace of technology. Although he developed these concepts during the infancy of computers, his analysis is prescient to our digital age and can help illuminate the dynamics and potential risks of digital entrapment. A key insight of the Heidegger (1954) analysis is that our ideology of technology enframes us into a false consciousness, which reproduces an uncritical relationship to technol ogy, making us susceptible to unwise and dangerous bargains with digital technology (and those who pro vide it). The twin concepts of enframing/false consciousness refer to attitudes of cognition that disable our critical epistemic capabilities while legitimating dominant ideologies and power relations (Wood 1988, Thompson 2015, Rosen 2016). Thompson (2015) argues that “false consciousness is a product of irrationalism: a fundamental inability on the part of the subject properly to be able to conceptualize the world in any objectively valid sense” (Thompson 2015, p. 450). Furthermore, Feenberg (2005) brilliantly argues that Heidegger and Marcuse captured the illusion held by the modern world that technical action will liberate us from the human condition. Although Heidegger insists on our inauthenticity of being and frames it as an ontological problem, his student Marcuse joins Adorno in pointing out the structural power inequality by which dominant actors maintain this illusion.

False consciousness about technology paves the ground upon which regimes of exploitation and regimes of manipulation are built, which are central to digital entrapment. Regimes of exploitation refer to large-scale systematic programs that use asymmetry of power to extract and accumulate wealth by appropriating the valuable assets (labor, data, etc.) of less powerful individuals (Sindzingre 2018, McDonough et al. 2021), whereas regimes of manipulation refer to large-scale systematic programs for influencing the decisions and actions of ordinary individuals in the interest of dominant societal actors (Adorno 2006). The Faustian bargain concept (Marlowe 1604) illuminates the dynamics of manipulation when we, as individuals, make unwise and dangerous “bargains” with digital giants whose intention is to exploit our data for their gain. States and public organizations can also make Faustian bargains when they negotiate for critical digital infrastructure with digital giants who have an asymmetric information advantage (Rowe et al. 2020, Ngwenyama et al. 2023). This concept enables a critical interrogation of the unwise and dangerous bargains we make in using digital technologies and their potential consequences (Zuboff 2019a, b; Siqueira et al. 2023). Our primary interest is to develop an understanding of the digital entrapment dynamics of the Big Data practices by illuminating the roles played by enframing/false consciousness and Faustian bargains. ## Enframing: False Consciousness About Technology

Heidegger views enframing as a social psychological condition for entrapment resulting from an uncritical and ideological relationship with technology. Our ideology of technology motivates patterns of routinized uncritical thinking and doing that form a digital habitus, a collection of mental models from which we imagine and apprehend the potentialities of our societies. It nurtures a technological rationality<sup>7</sup> and sustains the false consciousness that technology (1) “is the fate of our age … an inevitable and unalterable course” of humanity and (2) is nothing more than “means for achieving the ends we desire” (Heidegger 1954, p. 25). (3) As its inventors, we are its masters, and it will do our bidding (Heidegger 1954). Technological rationality is evident in our quest to apply it to every aspect of life: “education, government, trade, office work, health care, personal psychology, sex, etc.” (Drengson 1982). The “digital first,” “digital by default” ideology (Schou and Pors 2019, Baskerville et al. 2020) is indica tive of the dominance of our technological rationality. Moreover, as Heidegger (1954) observed, we assume a technological rationality when inquiring into technology, even though “the essence of technology is nothing technological” (Heidegger 1954, p. 5) but social and transformative of our social forms of life. False consciousness prevents us from critically apprehending the potential dangers of technology; we see only its short-term benefits, whereas risks are difficult to assess, uncertain, opaque, and deferred (Feenberg 2005).<sup>8</sup> Heidegger admonishes us to confront our enframing,<sup>9</sup> which leads to alienation and the domination of our rationality by a false consciousness of technology.

Enframing does not simply endanger man in his relationship to himself and to everything that is … it banishes man into that kind of revealing which is an ordering (classifying) … regulating and securing of the standing reserve. (Heidegger 1954, p. 27)

Enframing disguises the nearness of world … just as the forgetting of something forgets itself and is drawn away in the wake of forgetful oblivion. (Heidegger 1954)

Heidegger is clear that false consciousness is a barrier to understanding the spirit of technology. He encourages critical philosophical inquiry into what technology means for the human existence as a way of securing human values. Unconcealing the essence of digital technology will require critical reflection into the “essential realm in which it moves” (Heidegger 1954, p. 32), our social forms of life. He is adamant that a social rationality is necessary for critically interrogating and understanding the limitations of technological rationality (Marcuse 1964, Beck 1992, Habermas 1992). He cautions us on the limitations of technological rationality as a strategy for uncovering the essence of technology.

## Digital Entrapment

Heidegger (1954) defines technology entrapment as our frenzied and uncritical behavior of “ordering and transforming” the world and humans into a “standing reserve” (i.e., repository of assets) for exploitation. He is clear that “there is no demonry of technology” (Heidegger 1954, p. 28); the problem is our false consciousness in as much as we mythologize technology for its civilizing powers and overlook its potential for multiple bright and dark outcomes. This magical view conditions our technological rationality—a narrow vision of technology as a means for specific desired ends while impoverishing our relationship to the world. We design and deploy technology to transform and reorder our world and to increase our wealth and power without thinking about its potential dark sides. This false consciousness leads to “the frenziedness of ordering that blocks every view into the coming-topass of revealing and so radically endangers the relation to the essence of truth” (Heidegger 1954, p. 33). For Heidegger, our uncritical attitude and behavior toward technology are dangerous and result in our entrapment into viewing everything as a “standing reserve” to be exploited. The standing reserve equates to Big Data repositories that store incomplete digital representations of the social and physical world made convenient by datafication and taken to be reality (Jones 2019), but they are part of the illusion (Thatcher 2014). From Heidegger’s perspective, such datafications are “inauthentic, truthless and without foundation,” as in this “ordering of the standing-reserve, the truth of Being remains denied as world” (Heidegger 1954).

It is remarkable how prescient Heidegger was in articulating theoretical concepts that also explain digital entrapment and the role that false consciousness plays in it. The Heideggerian idea of “ordering, transforming, securing and regulating the standing reserve” equates to practices of datafication, dataveillance, and data assetization by which Big Data are acquired, ordered, stored, and transformed into assets by data capitalists (Sadowski 2019, Birch et al. 2020, Hylving et al. 2022). The Big Data revolution expounds the necessity of universal datafication for increasing knowledge, which may, however, cause uneven distribution of wealth, power, and control of our world (Helbing 2015). Lack of critical inquiry into the potential dangers of such Big Data practices leads to hidden entrapment. Heidegger explains:

This entrapping disguises itself, in that it develops into the setting in order of everything that presences as standing-reserve, establishes itself in the standing-reserve, and rules as the standing-reserve. (Heidegger 1954)

Technology is no mere instrument for civilizing society and generating wealth and power; it is an emergent potential with many interlocking paths for transforming and reordering our world and society to our benefit or destruction. Furthermore, Heidegger (1954) cautions:

As soon as what is unconcealed no longer concerns man even as object, but … exclusively as standing-reserve, then he comes to the very brink of a precipitous fall; that is, he comes to the point where he himself will have to be taken as standing-reserve. (Heidegger 1954, p. 27)

## Faustian Bargains

The Tragedy of Dr. Faustus by Marlowe (1604) is a morality tale of two actors. (1) Faustus, who desires to share in “the good life,” knowingly enters into an exploitative relationship with Lucifer, an extremely powerful entity, losing his freedom, liberty, and life. (2) Lucifer uses asymmetric knowledge and power to manipulate Faustus with false and deceptive promises and a contract to deprive him of his freedom, liberty, and life. In our theoretical framework, we use the core concept Faustian bargain to illuminate important aspects of the relational dynamics between the digital giants and individuals in the digital universe. The allegory helps us to link false consciousness to regimes of manipulation and exploitation from Big Data practices and their implications for individuals and society. In the play, Dr. Faustus is a modern and brilliant academic who has grown dissatisfied with academic success and is intent on seeking magic for the purposes of acquiring wealth and power. Faustus daydreams of a utopian world akin to the digital global society portrayed by Meta and Amazon.

He imagined himself with spirits that he could command and he amused himself by thinking what he would tell them to do. They could bring him gold from India. They could bring him exotic food from anywhere in the world. They could debate philosophy with him. (Marlowe 1604, p. 5)

In our digitalized world, technology platforms claim such magical powers (Stahl 1995). We are told we can have the world at our fingertips to communicate with anyone and buy anything from anywhere in the world with our digital devices, but the nature of the trade-off is often unclear. Enthralled by the potential for wealth and power, Faustus instructs Mephistopheles to convey the following message to Lucifer.

Go and tell him that I am ready to give him my soul. Tell him that he must give me twenty-four years of life in exchange for it. During those twenty-four years Lucifer must give me everything I ask for. Tell him that I want you to be my servant during those twenty-four years. (Marlowe 1604, p. 9)

Dr. Faustus, fascinated by the powers he could possess to astound his friends and acquaintances, is willing to take great risks. In the middle of his daydreaming, good and bad angels appear. The bad angel encourages him in his decision and reminds him of the fame and riches he will acquire. The good angel implores him not to risk his soul and cautions of potential doom should he enter into a contract with Lucifer. Much like our tendency to set aside potential risks and accept digital service contracts (of WhatsApp, Alexa, ChatGPT, etc.), Faustus sets aside his misgivings and summons Mephistopheles.

What did Lucifer say? Faustus asked impatiently: Did he agree to everything?

He agreed, Mephistopheles said quietly. I’ll be your servant for twenty-four years and you will then give

Lucifer your soul. But there’s one condition. Lucifer wants a proper contract with you. Lucifer insists on it. (Marlowe 1604, p. 11)

Faustus agrees and writes the contract as Mephistopheles recites in detail the necessary clauses. He then signs it with his blood as required by Lucifer. Immediately, Faustus notices some writing appearing on his arm (“homo fuge”) as a feeling of dread descends upon him. On observing Dr. Faustus’ feelings of regret, Mephistopheles summons some beautiful dancers to create a distraction. After the show, Faustus inquires of Mephistopheles about some key details of the contract.

Do you really think I’ll go to hell? Faustus asked uneasily.

Of course you will! Replied Mephistopheles. I’ve got the contract.

Hell’s a fable, Faustus repeated. (Marlowe 1604, p. 14)

Signed contract in hand, Mephistopheles begins to manipulate Faustus. He organizes many forms of entertainment and gives Faustus a book of magic. However, Faustus is soon disappointed when he realizes that while Mephistopheles is amusing him, he is neither answering his deep questions nor delivering the magic to his expectations. Feeling buyer’s remorse, Faustus blames Mephistopheles for tempting him. However, Mephistopheles mocks him.

It was your own doing, he reminded Faustus. You wanted to learn about magic, and to have power and money, remember? You wanted to astonish the world with your learning and your wit. You signed the agreement with Lucifer. (Marlowe 1604, p. 16)

Faustus becomes angry and terrified. He failed to critically interrogate the conditions before becoming entangled and now wants to undo the contract. On the one hand, he finds some of the pleasures enjoyable; but on the other hand, he is unimpressed with the knowledge that he is offered. Disappointed and bitter, Faustus realizes that signing a contract was a very bad idea. Whenever Lucifer and Mephistopheles sense he was losing interest, they would offer a bit more magical power to keep him engaged. Eventually, the 24 years expire, and Faustus realizes for the first time the price he must pay, a thousand-year term in hell.

## Summary of Key Concepts

In Table 2, we summarize the key theoretical concepts and subconcepts that we use in our analysis of the problem space. From the Heideggerian perspective, technology ideology is a taken for granted worldview that gives rise to false consciousness, which conditions our dysfunctional relationship with technology. This ideology of technology is operant on both individual and collective levels (digital giants and society), reinforced by social practices. Our ideology of technology gives rise to the digital habitus, patterns of thinking (mental models) about ourselves and our world that drive the “digital-first” design rationality (Baskerville et al. 2020) and our use of digital technologies (Gam betti 2020, Romele and Rodighiero 2020). Although Marcuse (1964) did not use the term digital entrap ment, he noted that technological rationality shapes our social, cultural, and political practices and products (Marcuse 1964, pp. 157–158). Such rationality views technology as neutral and malleable to human sovereignty (Balabanian 1980, Ihde 1990) and gives it cultural primacy through the images we create of it and use to envision and order our world (Habermas 1971, Boggs 2016). The Faustian bargain concept operates on individual, industry and social system levels. It foregrounds the socioeconomic and political dimensions of Big Data practices: in particular, the power of big tech companies that own and operate (societal) digital infrastructures and their strategic manipulation of users for wealth extraction.

As individuals, we often take risks (knowingly and unknowingly) that diminish our data asset rights be cause of our beliefs about ourselves, perceptions about the benefits of digital technologies, and the role we envision they will play in our lives. On the industry level, the digital giants design and implement regimes of exploitation that include Big Data practices to entrap individuals in spite of the potential for negative conse quences (Ravenelle 2019, McDonough et al. 2021), such as negative data network effects (NDNEs) (Choi et al. 2019). They also enroll other sectors (food, energy, education, leisure, transportation, etc.) to embed Big Data technologies in their ecosystems to harvest data on their customers’ activities. On the social system level, governments also employ Big Data practices and incentivize digital giants to digitalize society without fully understanding the potential harms to their citizens (Rowe et al. 2020, Calzada 2023, Ngwenyama et al. 2023). Although democratic governments do implement social system-level Big Data technologies (e.g., coronavirus disease apps), their intention is not to entrap citizens or appropriate their data assets. On the contrary, laws prevent such exploitation, but the same cannot be said of nondemocratic countries (a point we address later). The Faustian bargain is the key mechanism of manipulation that exploits our false consciousness to agree to coercive contracts that enforce our digital entrapment. Consequently, the digital giants invest huge resources in advertising to frame narratives and condition our false consciousness about digital technologies. They extol personal benefits of homo digitalis, the illusion of a new human dependent on datafication and digital tools for identity and exis tence (Ninet 2019). Furthermore, at the social system level, they deploy public relations experts and political lobbyists to shape government regulation in their interests.

## The Critical Social Theory Approach

The aim of our analysis is theorizing the interlocking of sociomaterial structures and social dynamics of digital entrapment in the everyday use of digital platforms. Although our goal is to develop a theory of the problem based on CST concepts, we adhere to the fundamental tenet that theoretical concepts must have credibility in social reality (Ngwenyama and Lee 1997, Habermas 2021). In this regard our research approach encompasses a two-stage process. In stage 1, we use constitutive causal analysis (Markus and Rowe 2018) to theorize the relationships among critical theory concepts in the problem space and a soft systems causal loop diagram (CLD) (Rodriguez-Ulloa and Paucar-Caceres 2005, Yearworth and White 2013) to represent our theory of the problem (cf. Figure 2). This type of analysis is a core feature of critical social theory (Habermas 2021, Ngwenyama et al. 2023). As Steinberg and Kincheloe (2010) noted, it enables the researcher to “grapple with issues of power … and the ways that the economy, ideologies … and other social dynamics interact to construct the social systems that shape our consciousnesses” (Steinberg and Kincheloe 2010, p. 143). In stage 2, we collected and analyzed empirical materials (see the online appendix) from Microsoft’s websites to provide empirical illustrations for each theoretical concept (see the appendix). Our approach satisfies basic criteria for critical social inquiry (Myers and Klein 2011): (a) to use CST concepts to reveal structures of domination and destructive social practices and (b) to facilitate critical reflection and corrective action for social transformation and emancipation of individuals.

## Reinforcing Structures of Entrapment: Why Is it Difficult to Escape?

The stability and persistence of digital entrapment are generated and maintained via a set of interlocking structures and reinforcing Big Data practices. The deployment of enormous resources (political, financial) to promote digitalization as essential to modern society paves the way to the ideology of technology, which is the starting point of our theoretical model. The digitalization choices of the digital giants (and entrepreneurs) reinforce or transform our ideology on the role of digital technologies in society. Moreover, their advertising and design of their products condition us into a digital habitus (L1) and false consciousness that cognitively links digital technologies with performance, well-being, and the “good life” we desire (Stolterman and Fors 2004, Flick 2016, Chivukula et al. 2018). The digital habitus and false consciousness not only influence us to identify as homo digitalis (L2) but to view our “relations” (social and economic) as digital first (Prainsack 2020). The digital habitus, its scripts, and its patterns of thinking give rise to a (L3) technological rationality, which constricts our worldview; suppresses social rationality (Habermas 1971, Boggs 2016); and restricts potentialities of our social, cultural, and political practices to what is technologically feasible (Marcuse 1964, Drengson 1982). Thus, technological rationality reinforces the ideology of technology (L4). Governments too are subject to false consciousness; their solutions to societal problems are now “digital by default” (Schou and Pors 2019, Rowe et al. 2020). Viewed from the perspective of constitutive causality (Markus and Rowe 2018), it is our beliefs (ideology) about technology that cause the digital habitus to persist and support our willingness to accept the idea that we are homo digitalis and succumb to the regimes of exploitation and manipulation imposed on us by the digital giants (Rosen 2016). The digital habitus facilitates the development of regimes of exploitation (L5). The ideology of technology also legitimizes relentless innovation (L6) that, in turn, reinforces the ideology (L7). The digital giants relentlessly innovate their Big Data practices, and they deploy and refine regimes of exploitation (L8) and manipulation (L9) to datafy users and assetize and extract their data (L10), undermining users’ rights (L11) and reproducing their digital entrapment. In their insightful article on emancipatory design, Kane et al. (2021) note that “oppression is a state in which unequal power dynamics lead to domination or marginalization” (Kane et al. 2021, p. 373). The deeper our false consciousness of the digital, the more we legitimate our condition as homo digitalis, and the more difficult it is to contest the development of digitally enabled regimes of exploitation (L14). The regimes are diverse and oppressive, accruing positive data network effects for digital giants but mostly negative data network effects for users (L12) (Choi et al. 2019), who are subject to datafication (L10), which reinforces our condition as homo digitalis (L13) (Ninet 2019). From the critical theory perspective, negative data network effects (economic loss, social and privacy harm) that accrue to individuals are side effects of datafication and data assetization (L12) (Sadowski 2019, Birch et al. 2020). Moreover, datafication and data assetization reinforce technological rationality (L17). The digital footprints we leave behind when using platforms are taken to be valid representations of our world (Jones 2019); they have become the media and logic upon which we build, manage, control, and optimize our social and economic systems (Sadowski 2019). As Van Dijck (2014) notes, “datafication is an accepted new paradigm for understanding sociality and social behavior” (Van Dijck 2014, p. 198).

Gregory et al. (2021) tell us that a digital “platform exhibits data network effects if the more that the platform learns from the data it collects on users, the more valuable the platform becomes to each user” (Gregory et al. 2021, p. 534). Although they do acknowledge that the use of digital platforms “may cause privacy concerns” (Gregory et al. 2021, p. 536), they neither discuss the Big Data practices nor the conditions that result in such negative data network effects. Platform learning mechanisms can result in diverse negative data network effects for organizations and individuals. First, firms and managers increasingly take the data about users as unquestioned truth (as valid representation) to which they respond. Second, platforms develop extensive knowledge about their clients’ business practices, including their usage of third-party systems. This subsequent information asymmetry between platforms and clients can be used by the platform to lock in the clients (Siqueira et al. 2023) or use the information advantage against them<sup>10</sup> (L15). On the individual level, data network effects can be negative (NDNE) when platform learning is used to design and innovate Big Data practices for more surveillance, exploitation, and manipulation of users (Bodle 2016, Choi et al. 2019, Kunst et al. 2022). NDNE can also accrue when platform learning is used for (a) keeping us tethered to the digital universe and under surveillance (Mulligan et al. 2020), (b) constructing user preferences for micro targeting (Susser et al. 2019, Bakir 2020), and (c) dark patterns: addictive and persuasive digital design to manipulate our use behaviors and exploit them (Flick 2016, Chivukula et al. 2018, Mujica et al. 2022). Notably, machine learning for customization of services may also infringe on the individuals’ ability for autonomous rational choices (L16) unless it is explainable, transparent, and accountable (Susser et al. 2019, Kane et al. 2021).

The digital giants maintain their asymmetric power by relentlessly enhancing their platforms’ learning capabilities to predict and manipulate user behavior (Mayer-Scho¨nberger and Cukier 2013, Egliston and Carter 2020). Knowledge gained from platform learning is also used to design digital service contracts tha coerce users into relinquishing their data assets and pri vacy rights (Sadowski 2019) while indemnifying the digital giants against resulting user harms (Crowther 2012, Kra¨mer and Schnurr 2022). Digital platform own ers embed dataveillance and datafication capabilities (Busch et al. 2018, Gal et al. 2020, Hu¨ llmann et al. 2021), without our informed consent (Richards and Hartzog 2018), and use their asymmetric power and false and deceptive communications (Habermas 1992) to manip ulate and entrap users (L18). From a CST perspective, dark patterns (Luguri and Strahilevitz 2021), such as false and deceptive advertising promises and incomprehensible user contracts, are strategic actions (Bodle 2016) intended to influence and manipulate individuals into adopting new digital tools or functionalities (L19). Moreover, when individuals sign coercive contracts, self-justification mechanisms (e.g., minimizing and/or underestimating cost, which is the corollary to the Faustian bargain) contribute to reinforcing the manipu lation regimes (L21), provide post hoc rationalization for their acceptance (Bobocel and Meyer 1994, Zuboff 2019b), and may prevent them from exercising their rights (L20). Such self-justification mechanisms, arising in the context of the Faustian bargain, are powerful and serve to reinforce the ideology of technology. Furthermore, by lobbying governments and deploying circumvention strategies to undermine user protection legislation (e.g., European GDPR), the digital giants signal intent to continue embedding their destructive Big Data practices in our social systems without regard to the cost of risk exposure (Geradin et al. 2021, Munk 2021).

## Microsoft Viva Case Illustration

Analysis of empirical materials from Microsoft’s websites (see the online appendix) reveals the narrative framing of Viva as “an employee experience platform” and Microsoft’s vision of employee empowerment. Using critical hermeneutic analysis (Cukier et al. 2009), we interrogated these materials to surface illustrations of the theoretical concepts (see the appendix). Unlike theory-building case studies where theoretical explanations are abducted from the cases, in this paper we use the case analysis to illustrate that our theoretical concepts are relevant to and observable in the problem space. As critical social theorists, we conduct this hermeneutic analysis as “insiders” informed by our multifaceted experiences as senior academics who daily use the Microsoft Office platform like millions of workers and private individuals throughout the planet. Two of the coauthors are heads of departments/units at institutions that have adopted Microsoft Viva. This familiarity with the case enables us to connect the making of meaning to human experience and to use fragments of text for critical theoretic explanation as recommended by hermeneutics (Ngwenyama and Lee 1997, Steinberg and Kincheloe 2010, Myers and Klein 2011). In the following, we illustrate how the concepts of our theory of the problem of digital entrapment can be empirically observed in the Viva platform and its everyday use context.

## Enframing: False Consciousness

Viva is a workplace analytics platform that integrates data from Microsoft 365, Microsoft Teams, and Windows application services. Microsoft appeals to our ideology of technology, the digital habitus, and technological rationality in promoting Viva as necessary workplace technology that emphasizes data-driven metrics for people performance and well-being. To the individual, Viva is promoted as a digital assistant that offers “a modern employee experience that helps you manage communications, knowledge, learning, insights and goals as part of their natural workflow” (M03). To companies, it is promoted as infrastructure for managing individual and organizational performance. Viva embodies Microsoft’s ideology of enlightened digital human resource management (EDHR) based on datafication (Van Dijck 2014), quantified self (O’Neill 2017), and behavioral surveillance of individuals and workgroups (Schafheitle et al. 2020, Nyman et al. 2021). In support of its claim to EDHR, Microsoft argues that Viva supports best practices derived from research and analytics that help employees: “Identify opportunities to build better work habits. Prioritize wellbeing

… take regular breaks, and practice mindfulness” (M03). Microsoft does not reveal specifically on what research it builds its model of EDHR beyond a list of articles compiled in its repository (Microsoft 2022). However, in promoting Viva, it projects an image of caring for employee performance and well-being. Microsoft also promotes a datafied worldview of HR management built on users’ digital traces and adopts the “digital by default” paradigm (Schou and Pors 2019) using Big Data practices for the ontological design (Baskerville et al. 2020) of this new phenomenon of EDHR. Microsoft seems to hold the philosophy that the digital traces of users’ activities can be turned into data assets for users, their employers, and obviously, itself. It claims to provide superior insights into HR practice because of large-scale algorithmic analysis of user digital traces and data network effects, thus reinforcing the worldview of user data as standing reserve. Microsoft uses Viva’s platform learning to legitimize prescriptions for EDHR “best practices” that now shape work practices and organizational norms (Giermindl et al. 2022), whereas the extent and direc tions of its analytics operations remain opaque and outside of public oversight or control (Gal et al. 2020).

## Digital Entrapment

Viva is designed to maximize data network effects (i.e., the more comprehensive its data collection, the more it can learn and report on users’ behaviors) (Choi et al. 2019). Consequently, Viva’s Big Data practices are detailed enabling granular surveillance, resulting in positive DNE for the organization (and MS) and mostly negative DNE for users. It collects and stores in a repository (Standing Reserve) data from all Win dows 10/11 services and apps. Moreover, using a Microsoft account on a web or mobile device triggers data collection by Microsoft. The firm states that “if you use a non-work or non-school account (live.com or facebook.com) to connect to the app or service … the activity history is sent to Microsoft” (M03). Viva also has extensive data quilting capabilities: “Using AI to reason over a customer’s Microsoft 365 data … to integrate knowledge from various third-party services such as ServiceNow and Salesforce, Viva Topics automatically surfaces topic within conversations and documents across Microsoft 365 and Teams” (M03). It uses machine learning to analyze the collected data to classify employees’ behaviors, compare them against its norms of best practice, and generate metrics. Managers using Viva can generate reports on employees within their authority. “The advanced analysis tools provide different ways of analyzing and reporting custom analysis to your company’s business leaders” (M03). Microsoft claims that employees can “protect their privacy,” but it is the organization’s preroga tive on how Viva is configured. Viewed from the CST perspective, these Big Data practices for surveillance and datafication (securing, regulating, ordering, and transforming) of user activities and machine learning for analyzing the data to predict behavior and nudge actions (Gal et al. 2020) constitute entrapment. Viva reinforces the idea of homo digitalis, provides the functionality for regimes of exploitation, and can cause anxiety for working in the digitally controlled organization reality it creates. In this regard, Heidegger’s admonition on technology entrapment rings true.

This entrapping … develops into the setting in order of everything that presences as standing-reserve, establishes itself in the standing-reserve, and rules as the standingreserve. (Heidegger 1954)

## The Faustian Bargain

The Viva documentation presents images that tap into (a) the digital habitus portraying anytime-anyplace work from digital devices; (b) the zeitgeist of enlightened living (people doing yoga); and (c) ideals of inclusiveness by portraying race, gender, and age diversity. However, the contradictions are evident; the promise of support for employee well-being appears to be a marketing strategy for digital HR infrastructure rooted in Taylorism (Holmes 2016) and the quantified self (O’Neill 2017). Microsoft also claims that Viva is “not designed to enable evaluation, tracking … profiling and monitoring” employees. However, Viva provides detailed reports on employees’ activities for measuring and predicting individual performance (Bales and Stone 2020) and for eliminating organization slack (Fuller 2016). For example, Viva reports 24/7 statistica summaries on individuals: (a) meeting attendance, (b) time spent on chats during meetings, and (c) time spent networking with people (Figure A.2 in the appendix). Another troubling empirical observation of employee surveillance is Viva’s Quiet Days metric: “days without interruptions of meetings, emails, chats and calls outside your working hours set in Outlook” (M01). Viva enables managers to query “channel metrics and peer comparisons” on (a) individual communications (emails, chats, posts) during and outside of work hours and (b) the number of emails and instant messages sent during a scheduled meeting. These “insights” constitute Microsoft’s promise of “a modern employee experience … [which] fosters a culture that empowers people and teams to be their best anywhere” (M01). Viva also provides top management an “employee influence” metric to help identify workers ready for managerial roles. It reports “employees who are in non-managerial roles with influence scores greater than or within 10 percent of their immediate manager’s score” (M18). The promise to support employee wellbeing and improve work habits amounts to recommendations based on simple to use metrics of the Microsoft apps derived from research supported by limited theory and empirical evidence (Gal et al. 2020, Giermindl et al. 2022). Moreover, most employees do not have access to the contract that Microsoft makes with em ployers; even if they had, they most likely would not be able to fully comprehend its implications. The “terms of use” are intentionally long, complicated, and difficult for users to comprehend and make informed decisions about, and they are subject to change without notice. This is a common Big Data practice employed by digital giants to entrap users into coercive contracts (West 2019, Schuß¨ ler et al. 2021) while shielding themselves with extensive legal clauses. Microsoft is clear to point out that

we provide the services “as is,” “with all faults” and “as available.” We don’t guarantee the accuracy or timeliness of the services. You acknowledge and agree that computer and telecommunication systems aren’t fault-free and that occasional periods of downtime occur. We can not guarantee the services will be uninterrupted, timely, secure or error-free. We and our affiliates, resellers, distributers and vendors give no contractual guarantees or conditions. —M04

Overall, the hegemonic Big Data practices of Viva are panoramic and intensive. Viva inscribes a distinctive ideology of (a) quantified self-management and (b) managerial control by datafication. This ideology appeals to managers who want simple metrics for assessing, managing, and controlling employees, but it can restrict ethical development (Gal et al. 2020, Giermindl et al. 2022). Most employees who use the Microsoft Office platform are unaware of the extensive dataveillance. However, we encountered employees in our institutions who get Viva reports and are aware of the tracking, but they seem to dismiss the entrapment effect. We conjecture that they do not fully comprehend the implications (which is part of the false consciousness). Microsoft’s Big Data practices embedded in Viva are intrusive and enable it to control and assetize our use behaviors. Although we did not observe direct data monetization in the MS Viva case, we did observe data assetization (Birch et al. 2021) and the use of such data as strategic assets for value creation and strategic alliances (Aaen et al. 2022). Our analysis of the Viva case illustrates power imbalance and the infrastructural conditions for the behavior control of users.

## Implications for Research and Action Developing a Research Program to Confront and Defend Against Digital Entrapment

This paper is first and foremost a research commentary informed by critical theory thinking. In the following, we make some general observations about human agency and then outline a research agenda with associated questions (see Table 5) for investigating three important aspects of the digital entrapment problem: (1) false consciousness of digital technology; (2) digitalization of humanity and digital colonization of socia systems; and (3) Faustian bargains, coercive contracts, and individual data assets rights. Note our observations on the problem of regulation. This CST research program could help reinvigorate the humanistic values of the sociotechnical approach to IS research that we appear to be losing (Sarker et al. 2019). Before moving on to discuss the research agenda, we want to make clear that our critical explanation of the digital entrapment problem is not just pure speculation based on dystopian readings; we illustrated it with a case analysis of Microsoft Viva. However, our research and explanation of the sociomaterial structures and conditions of digital entrapment and the difficulty of escaping it can be further refined by investigating other cases of big data practices. Three broad empirical questions can be posed. (1) Who as socioeconomic actors is contributing to digital entrapment beyond digita giants? (2) Under what precise socioeconomic conditions does digital entrapment emerge? (3) With what combinations of technologies is digital entrapment rendered possible and through which processes? The first question is important because we do not want to imply that digital giants, seen as restricted to largesized companies, are the only actors responsible for digital entrapment. There are numerous smaller firms that are no less influential as they employ the same Big Data practices in spaces not occupied by digital giants (e.g., Siqueira et al. 2023). This increases the possibility that digital entrapment becomes even further embedded in democratic societies. The second question is more related to the macro political conditions of societies. Our theorization may be limited to democratic societies. Some autocratic governments prioritize other interests over the potential harm to citizens. Digitalization in those societies is largely oriented toward power and control of citizens. Would the concepts interact differently in such settings: (perhaps a different type of Faustian bargain between different actors)? Regarding design intentions, we could also argue that digita entrapment was at least in part discovered accidentally by those who now fully develop and exploit it. Digital giants may have discovered their own wizardry through somewhat random experiments. However, there is evidence that their platforms cause detrimental effects on societies and individuals’ health and well-being (e.g., addictive behavior, body identity dysfunction). However, they may be unaware of the deeper structural psychosocial dynamics and future societal problems they are engineering with new platforms (Metaverse, ChatGPT, Bard, etc.). The third question falls within the traditional domain of the IS discipline.

## Some General Observations on Human Agency and Unit of Observations

In theory, users have the agency to embrace or reject datafication of their professional and social lives and identities. However, in practice, datafication is pervasive and phenomenologically transparent (backgrounded and seamless), and there is a power imbalance between digi tal infrastructure owners and users. Digital giants pro vide societal infrastructures and impose norms of social and economic relationships that support their interests while suppressing our individual free will (Zuboff 2019b, Kreps and Rowe 2021). However, we can reject the insti tutionalization of their social order by vigorously defend ing our rights and asserting our will. In practice, we cannot exhort our agency to reject datafication; we can only disapprove unless we mobilize collective action to change the game and regulate it. Technology is typically presented as fait accompli; as inevitable; and as a guaran tor of progress, efficiency, and innovation. As indivi duals, we use digital technologies (smartphones, fitness trackers, social media, search engines, walking guides, etc.) for personal convenience, but the more we integrate these technologies into our everyday lives, the more we are entrapped by them. We conjecture that this vicious cycle plays out because as individuals, exclusively or cumulatively, we (a) are conditioned into a false consciousness of digital technology, (b) do not recognize our digital entrapment, (c) are dependent on some platforms for routine and professional activities, (d) believe that we benefit from digital platforms and that the harms they may cause can be mitigated by improved algorithmic control complemented by human modera tion, (e) do not see such harm as the result of deliberate design and governance of these platforms, and (f) accept our helplessness. In other words, societal harm from technology is perceived as an exceptional event rathe than a design feature (or “collateral damage” known to and accepted by the digital giants). This vicious cycle plays out again and again with most digital innovations. As Zuboff (2019b) noted:

This logic turns ordinary life into the daily renewal of a twenty-first-century Faustian pact. “Faustian” because it is nearly impossible to tear ourselves away, despite the fact that what we must give in return will destroy life as we have known it … Our dependency is at the heart of the commercial surveillance project … It disposes us to rationalize the situation in resigned cynicism, create excuses that operate like defense mechanisms, or find other ways to stick our heads in the sand, choosing ignorance out of frustration and helplessness. (Zuboff 2019b, p. 11)

There are some challenges to developing a systematic research program on digital entrapment. First, as Majchrzak et al. (2016) argue, there is rarely “a single clear consensus problem statement on a substantive [IS] problem and agreement on causes, and contributing factors, let alone on if or how the problem should be solved” (Majchrzak et al. 2016, p. 271). Second, there is a question about the unit of analysis (individual, industry, social system, or all). A core tenet of CST is that neither individual nor industry is separate from the social; social systems are reproduced by the collective actions and social practices enacted by individuals (and collectives). However, the social arrangements of society are shaped by dominant social groups (including companies) that manipulate social and political processes to reduce the rational capacities of individuals to serve their own interest (Honneth 1993, Adorno 2006). Moreover, Honneth (1993), views social orders as structures of intersubjective social domination by powerful actors who monopolize the right to articulate “conditions of life.” In this regard, critical theory analysis of society involves the interrogation of the values and political agendas of dominant social groups (digital giants and their agents) and their social practices, as well as individual actions and collective consciousness (Marcuse 1964, Lefebvre 1991, Honneth 1993).

## The Problem of False Consciousness of Digital Technology

Fundamental questions for IS research are what false consciousness is and how its interlocking structures impact our cognition and decisions about digital technology. Although it is difficult for any individual, even a very qualified IT professional, to understand many algorithms (Introna 2016) and the data they connect to, we still trust and use them. Moreover, the situation gets worst in the age of AI learning algorithms when digital platforms and other web technologies are continuously evolving. This creates digital risks that providers avoid disclosing while presenting digital services as advantageous to users. Users may become aware of these risks but think that they only manifest in some future, whereas the benefits are immediate. Although we may find ourselves unable to fully understand what a particular technology does, do we finally decide to use it on (a) the degree of trustworthiness of the technology or (b) the trustworthiness of the company that promotes it rather than on the extent to which we can understand it? Although such questions can be informed by any form of traditional empirical research, it would also be interesting to research how and to what extent technology ideology influences this trustworthiness and when this can be misplaced. More research is needed on the user side to investigate why we “tick the box.” The privacy paradox stream of research has not succeeded yet in offering convincing explanations (Kokolakis 2017). This may be because in most situations, to users of digital platforms powered by Big Data analytics, the benefits appear real and tangible, whereas the risks are vague and uncertain. Moreover, they are conditioned by false consciousness to weigh the benefits positively and to dismiss potential risks.

Other open research questions concern the processes by which false consciousness is created, maintained, or combatted and the roles of different societal actors in these processes. Key strategies for manipulating and entrapping users are creating a perception of need and/or providing services for free, creating network effects (social media, messengers), algorithmic audiencing (Riemer and Peter 2021) (i.e., shaping the information users receive), and delivering just enough benefit while obscuring the risks. We can also envisage rival theorizing about what type of manipulation is really dominating. (1) Is it about manipulating consciousness through technology ideology, or (2) is it more about manipulating action by writing unread able contracts and offering convenient services or (3) using manipulative design strategies to make digital platforms addictive to users? In the CST tradition— and this is one of our contributions—we argue that the extent of manipulation from (2) and (3) is at least partly dependent on the magnitude of (1). Naturally, we invite more empirical research on action manipula tion and related nudging (Borgefalk and Leon 2019), but our main contribution is to stress that digital entrapment is fundamentally rooted in false consciousness about technology risks, which is in fact fundamentally related to social rationality. Research is also required to explore how the perception of (a) dependence and (b) helplessness impact our assessments of risks and benefits. Why do we accept false and deceptive promises, even when we know they may be misleading? Critical theorists would argue that social scientists have a re: sponsibility to use critical inquiry and public reason to expose hidden structures of domination in defense of democratic freedoms and human values (Habermas 1971, Marcuse 1972, Agger 1991). As the fourth estate, science has a role to play in transforming societal consciousness “to liberate human beings from circumstances that enslave them” (Horkheimer 1982, p. 244).

## The Problem of Digitization of Humanity and Digital Colonization of Social Systems

The extent to which we view datafication and the trend toward homo digitalis as good needs to be challenged and discussed. What areas of their private, economic, and social lives do individuals want to exclude from digitization and digitalization by others? Zuboff (2019b) clearly described and explained the exploitation regime of surveillance capitalism, notably insisting that all behavioral modifications be tracked, and highlighted the Faustian bargains that result in diminishing individual rights and freedom. This begs the question. What are the responsibilities of tech companies in protecting humanity and democratic social systems in the context of societal digitalization? In this paper, we extend Zuboff’s analysis by developing a dynamic explanation of how false consciousness and technology ideology (both of which constitute enframing in Heideggerian language) enable entrapment. The illusion of technological progress and continual expansion of wealth seems to be an infinite loop in the ideology of social progress: homo fuge (there is nowhere to run). As early as 1980, the IS scholar Richard Mason warned of the erosion of individual freedom and autonomy from “total surveillance” and the rewriting of our societal social contract based on Faustian bargains with information technology. He argued that “these issues are weighty and deserve extensive public debate. But they are also very personal and moral. Each of us, who have chosen careers in information systems, will have to cope with some of these issues during our lifetime. Our responses will help to shape the information society” (Mason 1980, p. 73). However, we have been slow in following up on his critical insights. As some IS researchers point out, we are now at risk for becoming artefacts of the digital (Demetis and Lee 2018), and the implications of digital technologies for human dignity are becoming more difficult to disentangle (Leidner and Tona 2021).

What is needed for the emerging digital civilization is a social rationality of the digital that privileges, expands, and preserves basic human rights (health, integrity, and personal privacy) and democratic ideals of freedom and liberty. When rationality is robust and accounts for alternative perspectives to the status quo, it can emancipate society (Brubaker 1987). Such a social rationality could help us develop a normative theory of digitalization for discerning what we can do and what we must not do with our Big Data innovations, notably based on the extent to which it affects the human condition. The preceding suggests that some research directions can be outlined with the aim of developing contrastive explanations that are theoretically useful (i.e., explanations that are more focused on certain parts of the theoretical model and go deeper) (Tsang and Ellsaesser 2011). However, a research program can also be pragmatically useful (Corley and Gioia 2011), investigating questions as follows. What role should government institutions play in delimiting zones of digitalization to protect human rights and democratic freedoms? What incentive mechanisms can governments use to facilitate the development of frameworks for responsible societal digitalization? From the viewpoint of social action, we identify the ethical issue of data property and related rights as possibilities for intervention to influence regulation that can rebalance the dynamics. It is beyond this paper to consider and detail all types of social action that could be useful to improve society. Considering the CST principle of corrective action for social transformation and emanci pation of individuals (Myers and Klein 2011), we express a few thoughts about rights and regulation.

## The Problem of Faustian Bargains, Coercive Contracts, and Individual Data Assets Rights

At the societal level, Big Data entrapment practices manifest as an interlocking system of coercive eco nomic and social relations that infringe individual rights to freedom and privacy (Chandler and Fuchs 2019, Schuß¨ ler et al. 2021). The digital giants scheme to collect our digital footprints necessary for our exploitation while making sure we relinquish our rights to data privacy by ticking “yes” to an incomprehensible Faust ian contract. This raises two important questions. What ethical responsibility and accountability should tech firms have when using manipulative digital design and coercive user contracts? What role should government institutions play in protecting the data asset rights of individuals? Moreover, tech firms enroll pub lic relations experts and intensively lobby governments against regulating their Big Data practices so that the will become structurally embedded in our social sys tems (Dignam 2020). In this case, we would argue that their manipulation of politicians and the ruthlessness and recklessness of practices have been eloquentl demonstrated with the “Uber files” revelations by Mark McGann (Lewis et al. 2022). The digital entrap ment is ambivalent: on the one hand, dehumanizing, producing constant surveillance with little transpar ency (Mo¨hlmann and Henfridsson 2019), and on the other hand, seductive and treacherous because it offers flexibility, autonomy, and performance (Mo¨hlmann et al. 2021, Wiener et al. 2021). Throughout the twenti eth century, capitalists perfected technologies (and cor responding ideology, marketing, and work regimes) for exploiting individuals. The open prison is necessary for the Big Data revolution. We cannot have Big Data without big surveillance (Esposti 2014); the former is dependent on the latter. Therefore, big surveillance infrastructures (such as Microsoft Viva, Amazon Alexa, Google, Facebook, etc.) have to be built to vacuum up every trace of data from our every activity. There is a need to push further the systematic investigation of the relationship between ethical issues pointed out by those who extended the Privacy, Accuracy, Property, and Accessibility (PAPA) framework (Mason 1986) to Behavioral Surveillance, Interpretation, and Governance (BIG) PAPA (Young et al. 2020), notably between privacy and property and between accessibil ity and property.

## Toward Regulations

Although some might argue that rational choice and market competition will eventually rebalance the scales for users, we consider it unlikely that the market alone will solve the digital entrapment problem. Research has shown that the conditions of power and information asymmetry held by the digital giants undermine both rational choice and market logic (Akerlof 1970, Boudon 1998). In their insightful article on emancipatory design, Kane et al. (2021) note, “we envision an imbalance of power between those who manage the behavior control infrastructure and those who inhabit it” (Kane et al. 2021, p. 373). What mechanisms can governments use to rebalance the asymmetric power imbalance in the relationship between individuals and tech firms? Our analysis suggests that digital entrapment is not an unintended consequence of the Big Data revolution; it is a design feature to ensure that individuals and their everyday social activity remain a standing data reserve for the digital giants to exploit (Couldry and Mejias 2019). The neoliberal dream of infinite wealth accumulation free of labor costs and investment in the wellbeing of users is the motivation for exploitative Big Data practices. Big promises have to be made to entice individuals, and intensive lobbying is necessary to influence and obstruct legislation that might control the digital giants. Transforming these current structures of domination will require mobilizing sustained political action to influence governments to regulate and outlaw exploitative, manipulative, and dangerous Big Data practices. Although user anger and mobilized resistance are emerging and may signal a legitimation crisis for exploitative Big Data practices, research has shown that the digital giants push back against regulatory changes that would limit their dominance over individuals and society (Schwarz 2019, Dignam 2020). Moreover, collective action should be informed by social scientific insights regarding the problem itself and as suggested here, as a problem related to false consciousness. Research will be required to ascertain what type of regulation is necessary and sufficient to rebalance the exploitative dynamics of Big Data practices (Kra¨mer and Schnurr 2022). As Zuboff (2022) argues, “[w]ithout public institutions, charters of rights, and legal frameworks purpose built for a democratic digital century, citizens march naked, easy prey for all who steal and hunt with human data” (Zuboff 2022, p. 3). The emergent stream of social welfare computing (Clemons et al. 2022b) is addressing the need for new structural and collective arrangements (Clemons et al. 2022a) and proposes new ways to profile the digital giants using sociotechnical analysis (Rowe and Markus 2022). The present paper shows that such IS research on regulation is timely and may contribute to limiting digita entrapment.

## Conclusion

In this research commentary, we set out to develop a “theory of the problem” of digital entrapment: to conceptualize the structures and interlocking dynamics of the problem space as a first step toward developing a “theory of the solution” (Majchrzak et al. 2016). Our theorization with the concepts of false consciousness and Faustian bargain offers substantive insights into the oppressive dynamics of digital entrapment by Big Data practices. False consciousness is necessary for the perpetuation of systems of domination, such as exploitative data capitalism (Wood 1988, Rosen 2016). Moreover, it is socially engineered to condition our value orientations and social logic to secure our digital entrapment (Rindova and Petkova 2007, Prahl and Goh 2021) and necessary for “hierarchically organized elites to exert dominance over others [us]” (Thompson 2015, p. 450). The Faustian bargain metaphor reveals a fundamental conundrum of the digital society, but it has some limitations. In order to access critical digital platforms, we are forced to make bargains with all platforms that have an asymmetric power advantage (Siqueira et al. 2023). We sign digital contracts without understanding the consequences to our freedom and liberty. Although digital innovations often start with idealism and noble intentions, the bandwagon effect and fierce competition can lead to the adoption of exploitative Big Data practices. Moreover, we infer from our analysis and case illustration that these Big Data practices are designed to secure the involuntary servitude of less powerful users. If the self-perpetuating dynamics of digital entrapment remain unchanged, the situation will only become more oppressive as the digitalization of society expands.

This research commentary makes important contributions of theory and methodology to the IS discipline. First, our theorization has captured and made visible key sociomaterial structures and the dynamics of Big Data practices that have the potential to institutionalize the open prison. Moreover, it illuminates the overlooked and undertheorized issue of negative data network effects (economic loss, social and privacy harm) that accrue to individuals from platform learning and data assetization. By proposing an explanation of the problem of digital entrapment, we lay foundations that can open up scientific discourse into digitalization practices that are negatively impacting societies and challenging human values of freedom and dignity (Leidner and Tona 2021). Presently, we are dehumaniz ing ourselves with these digitalization practices because we either do not consider digital entrapment as problematic or are unconscious of it. Becoming aware of the structures and dynamics of digital entrapment is a necessary precondition for developing a new social rationality of the digital to reject the antisocial value orientations of the digital giants and the dominant logic of their Big Data practices, which are the foundations of the open prison. By introducing, to the IS discipline, the theoretical concept of false consciousness, we offer a new avenue for research to develop critical knowledge about a broad range of IS issues, such as rationality in decision making about the digitalization of society, social computing, digital risk perceptions, and digital addiction.

Second, our methodological contribution to IS research is in theorizing using a “constitutive” causal ontology (Markus and Rowe 2018, p. 1263) with CLD based on CST concepts. Whether in IS research CLD has been combined with CST concepts, such as alienation (Rowe et al. 2020), or not (Akkermans and van Helden 2002), to our knowledge, most cases we have identified could be largely interpreted as forms of complex causal theorizing from the phenomenon that we can also interpret as a particular instance of phenomenon-driven theorizing (Gregory and Henfridsson 2021). Even when using CST concepts, such as alienation (Rowe et al. 2020), to interpret the general meaning of the reinforcing feedback loops, the loops were clearly inferred from the empirical situation, an original case where digital first or digital solutionism seemed at play and where loops were related to mundane IS processes, such as design, implementation, adoption, and diffusion. In this research, the theorizing comes first and foremost from the CST literature. The explanatory model can be further interrogated as part of the research program we outlined.
