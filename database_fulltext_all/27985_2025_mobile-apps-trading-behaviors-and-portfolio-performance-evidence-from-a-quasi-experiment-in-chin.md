Abstract. Mobile apps are among the most important and widely used financial technology (fintech) innovations in the brokerage industry. Surprisingly, despite their increasing economic importance and theoretical significance, few studies examine the effects of mobile app use on individual investors’ financial decisions and performance. This study seeks to understand how mobile apps influence investors’ trading behaviors and portfolio performance by using a proprietary longitudinal data set from December 2012 to November 2015 from a large securities company in China with a quasi-experimental setting to answer our research questions. We leverage the introduction of an app to identify the effect of mobile app adoption by using a sample of 20,665 investors. We use the generalized synthetic control method and find that mobile app adoption does not affect investors’ portfolio performance when one examines aggregate impacts using a binary indicator of mobile app use. Our analyses of the mechanisms indicate that adopting mobile apps results in a noticeable decrease in time constraints, a proxy for transaction friction, and a modest increase intrend-chasing bias, reflecting tendencies toward myopic decision making. Because the reduction in time constraints can benefit investors’ perfor mance, the increase in trend-chasing can be detrimental to investors’ performance; our findings explain why mobile app adoption has no overall effect on portfolio performance. Further analyses of adopters’ postadoption behaviors provide interesting insight and show that the mobile app usage intensity has an inverted U–shaped relationship with portfolio performance. The results are robust to using different samples or exclud ing high market volatility periods and by using a variety of methods, such as propensity score matching, dynamic matching, stacked difference in differences, or an instrumental variable approach. We discuss the implications for research and practice.

## 1. Introduction

The proliferation of smartphones is driving a revolution in the financial services industry, enabling individual investors (hereinafter, referred to simply as investors) to trade through mobile applications (apps). Such digital technologies, along with the early days of computer applications, are bringing about significant changes in the financial services sector and users’ behaviors (Hendershott et al. 2021). In particular, mobile stock trading apps, a key component of financial technologies (fintech), have attracted millions of investors. For example, a popular trading app, Robinhood, in the United States accumulated more than 23.3 million customers in October 2023 (Robinhood Markets Inc. 2023); another app,

Acorns, attracted more than 10 million users in 2023 (Nasr 2024). Despite the advantages of greater convenience and accessibility, mobile apps also bring about disadvantages. Hence, it remains debatable whether investors should use mobile apps to trade.

On the one hand, the rapid growth of mobile app adoption highlights the advantages of mobile trading with its ubiquitous 24/7 access to trading. On the other hand, some caution that increasing access to financial markets via mobile apps may not be in the best interest of individual investors (Benartzi 2015). For example, relative to PCs, mobile devices have smaller screens and lower computational power, resulting in higher search costs (Ghose et al. 2013). Moreover, the ease of access and frequent updates enabled by mobile apps may direct investors’ attention to short-term volatility in their portfolios, potentially leading to short-sighted decision making and attention-induced trading (Benartzi and Thaler 1995, Thaler et al. 1997, Barber et al. 2022). Furthermore, mobile devices fragment investors’ attention, leading people to multitask and understand less about what they read, resulting in impulsive decision-making habits (Benartz 2016). Thus, some urge investors to avoid using mobile devices to make financial decisions (Benartzi 2016).

These competing views underscore the need for a deeper understanding of mobile trading. Although recent studies regarding the impacts of technology on investors’ behaviors have considerably broadened our knowledge of various technologies (such as phones and internet-enabled PCs) used for trading, the literature devotes little attention to whether and how mobile technologies affect investors’ trading behaviors and outcomes. Previous research in this domain shows that online investors trade more actively and speculatively and earn lower profits (Barber and Odean 2001, 2002, 2013). Scholars indicate that (a) such results are mainly driven by two conflicting forces, reducing transaction friction and boosting investors’ biases (i.e., overconfidence), and (b) the drawbacks associated with these biases outweigh the benefits associated with reduced transaction friction (Barber and Odean 2002). Note that, whereas these prior studies explore the impact of the internet on retail investors’ behaviors, the trading devices under consideration were mainly internet PCs (hereafter referred to as PCs for short).

Although PC and mobile channels offer users a nearly identical suite of features, including the abilities to conduct trades, access information, utilize research tools, and receive push notifications, mobile devices distinguish themselves in terms of ubiquitous access and inherent physical constraints. The portability of mobile devices, coupled with persistent connectivity, allows users to trade anytime, anywhere. However, this perpetual access can lead to continual interruptions, potentially fostering impulsive trading decisions. Additionally, whereas convenient because of their compact nature, mobile devices suffer from limited screen size and computational capabilities, which might hinder data analysis and efficient navigation.

Against this backdrop, our goal in this study is to understand how adoption and use of mobile apps affects investors’ trading behaviors and portfolio performance. In particular, we focus on examining how investors change their trading behaviors after mobile app adoption and how its use and extent of usage influences performance. We pose the following questions: (1) How does mobile app adoption alter retail investors’ trading activities, such as trading constraints, and myopic decision making? (2) Does mobile app adoption improve investors’ performance as compared with PCs? (3) How does investors’ mobile app usage intensity (i.e., the level of transition from PC to mobile) affect their portfolio performance? To answer these questions, we obtained transaction data from a large securities company from December 2012 to November 2015, during which we observed investors beginning to adopt the mobile trading app in December 2013, soon after the company launched its app. Our setting enables us to differentiate adopters and nonadopters of the mobile app during the 36-month period and to observe their trading behaviors and portfolio performance before and after mobile app adoption.

Our analyses of data in this quasi-experimental setting to address identification challenges document several new findings. First, we find that the adoption of mobile apps significantly alters investors’ trading behaviors, resulting in a reduction in time constraints, suggestive of a decrease in transaction friction, and an increase in trend-chasing bias, indicative of a rise in myopic decision-making tendencies. The results support our argument that the ubiquitous access and physical constraint (compact yet higher portability) nature of mobile apps allow investors to trade with fewer constraints, thus leading to decreased transaction friction. Yet the same ubiquitous access to mobile devices and their inherent physical constraints, notably their small screens, hinder in-depth research and foster frequent but fragmented interactions with the market, causing investors to prioritize immediate trends and nudging them toward myopic decision making. Surprisingly, mobile app adoption does not have a statistically significant effect on portfolio performance. Second, our mediation tests to explore whether shifts in trading behaviors explain investors’ portfolio performance suggest that, whereas the reduction in time constraints can be beneficial for investors’ performance, the increase in trendchasing can be detrimental to investors’ performance. These findings explain why mobile app adoption has a null effect on portfolio performance.

Finally, we find that mobile usage intensity (i.e., transition from PC to mobile) has an inverted U–shaped relationship with portfolio performance. In other words, dual-channel investors, engaging moderately with both channels, exhibit superior performance compared with those primarily using a single channel. Collectively, these findings contribute to the emerging and nascent literature (e.g., Hendershott et al. 2021, Barber et al. 2022) on the role of information technologies and fintech in influencing trading behavior and performance.

## 2. Theoretical Framework and Hypotheses 2.1. Background

Although PC and mobile channels offer users similar functionalities, such as trading, accessing information, receiving notifications, and conducting research, two characteristics differentiate mobile devices from PCs: ubiquitous access and physical constraints. First, ubiquitous access, as identified in previous works (Andrews et al. 2016, Jung et al. 2019, Lee et al. 2020), stems from the portability of mobile devices combined with data plans, turning these devices into personal companions that allow users to access the internet anytime and anywhere. Balasubramanian et al. (2002) posit that ubiquitous mobile technologies can circumvent traditional constraints, thereby increasing trading accessibility. Conversely, 24/7 connectivity may lead to constant interruptions that fragment users’ attention, leading to a more scattered and impulsive decision-making mindset. This barrage of information can make individuals more prone to hasty decisions (Oulasvirta et al. 2012, Wilmer et al. 2017), potentially compromising the quality of their investments and driving impulsive behaviors.

Second, the physical constraints of mobile devices are most evident in their smaller screen sizes and restricted computational capacities. Notably, the compact design of mobile devices, underscored by Nayebi et al. (2012), affords them unparalleled portability. This feature allows users to trade on the go. However, this agility comes with challenges. The efficiency of online navigation as well as the capability to process intricate data can be compromised on mobile interfaces, a notion supported by Lee et al. (2020). Ghose et al. (2013) further elucidate that mobile users often gravitate toward links positioned at the top of their screens, indicating an intrinsic, heightened search cost when using mobile devices. The implications of this become even more pronounced when considering tasks such as detailed technical analyses, which become cumbersome because of the spatial constraints of mobile screens.

Although prior research explores the impact of internet trading through PCs in the early 2000s (Barber and Odean 2002, Hitt and Frei 2002), the dynamics introduced by mobile apps present fresh opportunities and challenges for investors to understand the benefits and risks of mobile apps in the spirit of Barber and Odean’s (2002) work that scrutinizes the effects of internet adoption on investors’ behaviors, highlighting benefits, such as reduced transaction friction, and its potential to foster increased overconfidence.

## 2.2. Hypotheses

2.2.1. Effect of Mobile App Adoption on Investors Trading and Performances. To begin, we discuss how mobile apps affect investors’ portfolios through some aspects of trading behaviors that can be tied to two underlying mechanisms: transaction friction and investors’ behavioral bias. Consider transaction friction first. Mobile apps exert a powerful influence on investors trading behaviors by significantly reducing transaction friction. At the forefront of this transformative change is the ubiquitous access provided by mobile devices through data plans, ensuring that investors can trade without any time or location constraints, leading to a more fluid investing process. Unlike their PC counterparts, which largely depend on wired or Wi-Fi network connections at specific locations, mobile devices have the unique advantage of leveraging cellular networks that allow for a constant connection to the internet, irrespective of the user’s geographical location. Jung et al. (2019) summarize several capabilities inherent in mobile devices, such as continuity and simultaneity (being always on), immediacy (quickness of an action), and the physical constraint of their compact nature that ensures portability (easy to carry, making them highly portable). These characteristics increase the chance of integrating mobile devices into diverse environments (Andrews et al. 2016). This implies a higher likelihood of mobile app utilization in spaces in which traditional devices are less accessible or even disallowed. A clear illustration of this ubiquitous access and portability can be found within the confines of traditional workplaces wherein individuals are usually prohibited from using work computers for personal trading. Nevertheless, with mobile apps’ advantages, investors can now overcome the restriction imposed by traditional channels and engage with the market almost anytime and anywhere.

This freedom affords time and location flexibility that was previously unimaginable. Lunch breaks, commutes, and even idle moments between meetings now offer opportunities for trade, greatly reducing the inflexibility that was once a dominant component of transaction frictions. This shift grants investors greater control and autonomy over their investment. In short, mobile has redefined the boundaries of the investment landscape by offering unprecedented levels of accessibility and flexibility. By doing so, mobile effectively reduces transaction frictions that once deterred many investors. Hence, we propose the following.

Hypothesis 1a. Mobile app adoption leads to a reduction in investors’ transaction frictions as indicated by a decrease in time constraints.

Turning to behavioral biases, the extant literature underscores the dual-edged sword of mobile devices in fostering constant interruptions in investor behavior whether through active engagement with apps or via passive interactions, such as notifications. This interruption dynamic is more pronounced with mobile devices than PCs because of their portability and being always within arm’s reach. This omnipresence, combined with the immediate information, encourages frequent yet fragmented interactions with the financial market. Oulasvirta et al. (2012) highlight how habitual use of mobile devices, compared with PCs, can lead to more superficial engagement with the market. Such an environment intensifies the fear of missing out on potential market opportunities (Clor-Proell et al. 2020). Continual market updates can lead investors to prioritize immediate trends over long-term strategies, a behavior reinforced by findings that limited attention causes investors to overlook certain data and overly react to others (Hirshleifer and Teoh 2003, Cai and Lu 2019). For example, Barber et al. (2022) observe that Robinhood users often engage in attention-induced trading, favoring stocks with significant price changes. Ward et al. (2017) suggest that the mere presence of one’s smartphone can reduce cognitive capacity, leading to a shortsighted decision-making mindset.

Furthermore, the convenience of trading on the go, which allows investors to execute trades with mere swipes or taps, can result in an illusion of control, heightening the risk of overconfidence in their decisionmaking capabilities. Arguably, Barber and Odean’s (2002) assertion that active involvement in trading significantly influences an investor’s perception of control, in particular, that investors often “confuse the control they have—over which investments they make—with the control that they lack—over the return those investments realize” (Barber and Odean 2001, p. 47), applies equally well to mobile apps. Fueled by the mobile’s immediacy, such misperceptions can promote a narrow, short-term investment perspective (Gervais and Odean 2001). Overconfidence can further induce investors to rely excessively on recent market movements. As such, the perceived power to capitalize on market opportunities, exacerbated by the omnipresence of mobile devices and the immediacy of information, may overshadow the substantial benefits of long-term investing strategies, fueling a sense of urgency that promotes myopic decision making.

Also, the inherent constraints of mobile devices present unique challenges to in-depth financial research. Whereas mobile apps offer a plethora of data and tools similar to PCs, the limited screen size can increase search costs and make it less conducive for information acquisition (Ghose et al. 2013). As Oulasvirta et al. (2012) note, habitual smartphone use can lead to superficial engagement with the market because of the difficulty of navigating detailed information on a smaller screen. In comparison, the design and larger display of PCs support thorough research, allowing for a more comfortable and comprehensive review of complex financial data. Over time, the convenience of mobile devices could condition investors to prioritize quick, readily available insights over deeper analysis, nudging them toward immediate decisions rather than focusing on long-term investment strategy.

Collectively, these factors cultivate a tendency toward short-sightedness, culminating in more myopic decision-making habits. Trend-chasing, defined as “the tendency of investors to purchase stocks after a series of subsequent increases in prices” (D’Acunto et al. 2019, p. 2010), emerges as a behavior that underscores this myopia. Trend-chasing leverages short-term market fluctuations and often overlooks the importance of long-term fundamentals (Baker and Ricciardi 2014). By focusing narrowly on recent stock performances as forecasts for near-future outcomes, trend-chasing inherently disregards a comprehensive evaluation of long-term fundamentals. We therefore propose the following.

Hypothesis 1b. Mobile app adoption leads to an increase in investors’ myopic decision making as indicated by a rise in trend-chasing behaviors.

So far, we have focused on how mobile apps reshape trading constraints and myopic decision making. We next discuss how these shifts affect investors’ portfolio performance. On the one hand, transaction frictions play a significant role in investors’ performance. High transaction costs could be viewed as transaction frictions, which represent “anything that interferes with trade” (Degennaro and Robotti 2007, p. 1), and can affect investors’ investment decisions and their portfolio choices (Dumitrescu and Gil-Bazo 2018). Some researchers consider different mechanisms through which frictions operate and find that, for example, trading costs (Anderson 2013) or monitoring costs (Perraudin and Sørensen 2000) could restrict investors’ ability to hold a large number of stocks because of the lack of trading flexibility. Because investors cannot adjust their portfo lios in a financial market with frictions at no cost, those who hold suboptimal portfolios must bear the costs of rebalancing their portfolios (Degennaro and Robotti 2007). In addition, because high transaction frictions might affect investment opportunities for investors, those investors who ignore transaction frictions end up compounding the damage incurred by transaction frictions (Degennaro and Robotti 2007). As discussed previously, mobile apps could be advantageous in reducing inflexibility, which are the key transaction frictions of the stock market that impede trading flexibility and, therefore, benefit investors’ portfolio performance.

On the other hand, mobile app adoption may heighten myopic decision making in investors, emphasizing short-term gains at the expense of long-term strategies. When investors base their decisions predominantly on recent market developments, they become susceptible to making frequent changes in their portfolios, believing in the prospects of immediate rewards (Dorn et al. 2008). However, such an approach can be detrimental to performance. Continuously reacting to short-term market fluctuations increases transaction costs because of frequent trades, often leading to poorly timed market entries and exits. Similarly, relying heavily on recent market movements to predict nearfuture outcomes can be misleading. Financial markets, influenced by a vast array of unpredictable variables, can make such short-term predictions unreliable (Kurov 2008).

Prioritizing immediate market trends can also steer investors away from a deeper evaluation of an asset’s intrinsic value, resulting in overbuying during price booms and underselling during downturns. Moreover, as Keim (2003) points out, strategies anchored in short-term market fluctuations do not typically produce adequate returns to cover the costs tied to their implementation and may render higher risks. The constant accessibility of mobile apps may further amplify the aforementioned myopic tendencies, ultimately undermining the investors’ portfolio performance.

Although we recognize potential positive effects, such as efficient execution of trades, along with possible negative effects, such as myopic decision making, we hypothesize a net positive influence on performance from mobile app adoption as a baseline for the ease of describing our results as follows.

Hypothesis 1c. Mobile app adoption leads to an increase in investors’ portfolio performance.

2.2.2. The Effect of Mobile Usage Intensity (Level of Transition from PC to Mobile) on Performance. Whereas mobile technologies have brought about significant changes to financial services (Hendershott et al. 2021), many investors still find value in earlier technologies, such as PCs. Thus, examining the combined usage of PC and mobile channels on retail investors’ performance becomes crucial. Toward this end, we study the effect of mobile usage intensity, defined as a score representing an individual’s transition from using PC channels to mobile channels for trading activities. Operationally, this score is calculated as the ratio of the number of transactions conducted via mobile app to the total number of transactions (both PC and mobile app) in a given month. Given that most investors have mainly used PCs for trading before the introduction of mobile apps, a lower (higher) score signifies heavier PC (mobile app) usage, and a midrange score represents balanced use of both channels.

As we evaluate the mobile usage intensity across various investors, three distinct profiles emerge: dualchannel investors who manifest a moderate score, signifying balanced engagement with both PC and mobile channels; PC-centric investors with a lower score, suggesting an inclination toward traditional, PC-based trading even post mobile app adoption; and mobilecentric investors with a higher score, indicating a swift transition to predominantly mobile trading. Two primary scenarios arise with channel usage: the substitution effect, which might lead to outcomes even worse than using a single channel because of conflicting limitations, and the complementary effect, wherein the combined synergic benefits of both channels can surpass their individual strengths.

We argue that the combined strengths of both channels dominate trading outcomes through a complementary effect with substitution effects being less likely. This is because harnessing the individual strengths of both PC and mobile channels can lead to a potent and more balanced trading strategy. The analytical depth offered by PCs when combined with the real-time agil ity of mobile trading creates a robust trading ecosystem that is hard to replicate by either channel alone. This interplay ensures that the analytical nature of PC trad ing is complemented by the immediacy of smartphones, creating a harmonious balance. The dual usage of both channels offers unique flexibility, allowing investors to make quick decisions on mobile and then review them on PC if necessary. Therefore, using both channels could significantly reduce barriers to timely and efficient trading. As for trading biases, arguably, using both channels might further bolster investors’ confidence, potentially leading to impulsivity, even though one might argue that a judicious application of both channels may encourage more deliberate thinking. Therefore, it’s inconclusive whether dual-channel trading more likely induces impulsivity or promotes careful decision mak ing and how that may affect portfolio performance. As a baseline, we propose an inverted-U relationship for the effect of mobile usage intensity (level of transition from PC to mobile trading) on portfolio performance such that investors achieve superior performance when using both channels rather than predominantly one channel.

Hypothesis 2. The impact of mobile usage intensity (i.e., level of transition from PC to mobile) on portfolio performance demonstrates an inverted U–shaped relationship with higher performance occurring when investors make dual use of PC and mobile trading channels.

## 3. Method

## 3.1. Research Site

We conducted a field study using data from a large securities company in China that provides financial services, such as brokerage, investment banking, and asset management. Specifically, the company engages in securities brokerage, securities margin trading, and financial consulting related to securities trading and investment activities. Importantly, the company is listed on the Shanghai Stock Exchange (SSE) as a constituent of the SSE Index, suggesting its significance in this sector. Before the company introduced its mobile app, it allowed investors to trade in securities via several channels (e.g., a physical counter, telephone, and PC). The granular data provided by the company enables us to track transactions through the mobile app when investors started using it in December 2013.

Figure 1 shows an example screenshot of the iOS mobile app (the company provides both iOS and Android versions). As Figure 1 shows, investors can use the mobile app to monitor a specific investment target (e.g., a particular stock) with different types of charts, such as candlestick charts, time-share charts, and so forth. Investors can also make transactions (buy and sell) via the mobile app and track their historical trading behavior. Besides trading and monitoring their own securities, investors can use the mobile app to check stock market indices. Additionally, the mobile app provides investors with access to financial and news reports. In terms of transaction fees, the company charges a unified transactional fee across different channels. Thus, our results are unlikely driven by differences in the transactional fees among channels.

## 3.2. Quasi-Experimental Setting and Sample

The introduction of a mobile app provides a quasiexperimental setting. Our data, spanning from December 2012 to November 2015, capture the mobile adoption. We observe the channel usage for each transaction and use the first instance of mobile trading as an indicator of mobile app adoption.

Figure 2 depicts the mobile app adoption during this period. We observe that the app adoption began in December 2013 and increased gradually. Along with those who adopted the mobile app (treatment group), we also have a control group of nonadopters who never adopted the mobile app during the entire sample period. Our primary data include investors’ channel usage, monthly financial data, and mobile app adoption data. We also collected the financial status, such as stock holdings at the end of each month, investors’ total market value of all securities, cash flow for each month, and investors’ risk preferences. Furthermore, we obtained investors’ demographics, including gender, education, profession, and age. After cleaning the data to remove investors with missing values, we have a sample of 20,665 investors.<sup>2</sup>

Table 1 provides a description of the variables. Our key dependent variable is portfolio performance: we use the Modified Dietz Measure (Dietz 1968, Kramer 2012), which indicates the performance of investment portfolios when considering external cash flows. It is computed as

$$
\text { Modified   Dietz   Measure } _ {i t} = \frac {M V _ {i t} - M V _ {i t - 1} - F _ {i t}}{M V _ {i t - 1} + \sum W _ {i t} F _ {i t}},\tag{1}
$$

where $M V _ { i t }$ is the end-of-month market value of the whole portfolio for investor i in month $t ,$ and $F _ { i t }$ is the external inflow in month t (i.e., contributions to a portfolio are positive flow $^ { \prime } { \bf S } ,$ and withdrawals from a portfolio are negative flows):

$$
W _ {i t} = \frac {C _ {t} - D _ {i}}{C _ {t}},\tag{2}
$$

where $W _ { i t }$ is the weight attributed to the external inflow, which is determined by the timing of the contributions. $C _ { t }$ is the number of calendar days in month $t ,$ and $D _ { i }$ is the number of days from the start of month t until the day on which the flow $F _ { i t }$ occurred. Finally, we derive investors’ market-adjusted portfolio performance by subtracting the stock market returns from users’ monthly portfolio returns (i.e., the modified Dietz measure).

To explore how mobile app adoption affects investors’ transaction friction and myopic decision making, we construct the following variables. First, trading inflexibility is a major constraint for transaction fric tions. Therefore, we use investors’ time constraints to represent transaction frictions. For each investor, we calculate the average dispersion of transactions for each day of the week (Monday–Friday) in the pretreatment period using Blau index (Blau 1977, Woerheide and Persson 1992) to infer transaction time constraints as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text { Transaction   Time   Constraints } = 1 - \text { Time   Dispersion } \\ = 1 - \left(1 - \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} p _ {i} ^ {2}\right), \end{array} \tag {3}
$$

where $p _ { i }$ represents the proportion of transactions for each day of the week and n is five because there are five days in each week on which to transact securities. A low transaction dispersion indicates that an individual can only trade on a certain day (less flexible). A high transaction dispersion indicates that an individual has more freedom to transact (more flexible).

## 3.4. Empirical Specifications

We use several approaches for estimation. First, we employ the generalized synthetic control (GSC) method, as proposed by Xu (2017), as our principal empirical strategy for the effect of mobile app adoption on trading behaviors and performance.<sup>5</sup> GSC improves upon the traditional synthetic control method by accommodating multiple treated units, time-varying treatment effects, and the endogenous adoption of treatment. Specifically, we employed the interactive effects counterfactual (IFEct) model, a variation of FEct that incorporates the synthetic control model (Abadie et al. 2010) with the

following specification:

$$

## 4.3. Robustness Tests for Effects of Mobile App Adoption

We also conducted additional analyses deploying the stacked DID method, Callaway and Sant’Anna (2021) (CS) method, and dynamic matching.<sup>14</sup>

First, we use stacked DID (Cengiz et al. 2019, Baker et al. 2022, Garel and Petit-Romec 2022) because the two-way fixed effect DID (staggered DID) is likely biased in the presence of staggered treatment and heterogeneous treatment effects (Baker et al. 2022). The stacked DID method addresses the heterogeneity issue by aggregating multiple treatment groups and their respective control groups. Specifically, we create a data set for all treated events (adoptions) and stack each event (cohort) in relative time. We consider a 12-month window before and after each treated month (the results are not sensitive to other window sizes such as 6 or 18 months). For each cohort, we define control investors as those who never adopted the mobile app during the observed window before and after the treated month. The treated investors are those who adopted in the treated month. Both treatment and control investors must have sufficient observations before and after the treated month. We stack each cohort and estimate the following stacked DID model:

Third, we used dynamic matching, driven by a need to circumvent the limitations associated with traditional

PSM, particularly its restriction to only use data before the introduction of mobile apps on the platform. For example, in the staggered DID model, we matched investors by using the entire year of the data before the introduction of the mobile apps, which may raise the concern that using the entire year of data might be too long and does not capture investors’ latest status. The dynamic matching approach allows for real-time updating of matching pairs, mitigating concerns around static propensity scores failing to capture changes in investor characteristics over time. We constructed a matched sample with dynamic matching by iterating the matching process to match investors in the treatment and control groups dynamically.

We start our first iteration from the first month of the mobile app introduction (i.e., December 2013). Assume that the given month is m + 1; we keep treated investors who adopted the mobile app in month m + 1 and who have observations in any of the past three months (m � 2 \~ m0) before month m + 1 and observations in any of the three months (m + 1 \~ m + 3) after month m0. Likewise, for all control investors, we keep investors who have observations in any of the past three months (m � 2 \~ m0) before month m + 1 and observations in any of the three months $( m + 1 \sim m + 3 )$ after month m0. Doing so ensures that each investor, whether treated or controlled, has at least one pretreatment and one posttreatment observation.

We then match the treated and control investors by using the past three months’ observations before month m + 1. Specifically, we use a three-month average of the market value and cash flow, demographics, risk preferences, and outcome variables of interest in the matching procedures (a three-month average of portfolio performance, trading frequencies, time constraints, and trend chasing in the pretreatment period). Investors who are matched are then excluded from the sample pool and not used for matching in the next iteration. We continue our iteration from the first adoption month to the end of our observation period. In other words, for each month after the mobile app introduction, we match the treated and control investors. The results in Table 8 are broadly similar to our main findings.<sup>15</sup>

## 4.4. The Effect of Mobile Usage Intensity (Level of Transition) on Investors’ Performance

To examine the impact of mobile usage intensity on investor performance, we restricted our sample to include only those investors engaged in trading activities through either PCs, mobile devices, or both, explicitly excluding those who utilized other channels, such as telephone or counter trading. This choice allows for a more straightforward and intuitive interpretation of the variable Mobile Usage Intensity (level of transition). Furthermore, considering that nonadopters never transition from PC to mobile and based on our prior analysis that revealed no significant disparities between PC and mobile in terms of their impact on investors’ performance, we limited our sample to include only adopters. Note that including nonadopters in the sample as a reference group and coding their Mobile Usage Intensity as zero did not significantly alter the findings as the results remained largely similar.

In Table 9, column (1), the Mobile Usage Intensity (level of transition) exhibits an inverted U–shaped relationship with portfolio performance; therefore, Hypothesis 2 is supported. Thus, Table 9 supports the notion that PC and mobile channels can complement each other. We employ the inverted U–shaped test proposed by Lind and Mehlum (2010) to formally test this relationship. The results indicate that the slope from the lower bound to the midpoint is 0.065 with a p-value < 0.01, whereas the slope from the midpoint to the upper bound is �0.065 with a p-value < 0.01. Moreover, the overall test for the presence of an inverted-U shape yields a p-value < 0.01. Thus, our findings suggest that, for adopters, the Mobile Usage Intensity (level of transition) has an inverted U–shaped effect on portfolio performance.<sup>16</sup>

## 5. Discussion

## 5.1. Main Findings

The goal of this study was to examine how mobile app adoption influences retail investors’ trading behaviors (i.e., transaction frictions and myopic decision making) and investors’ portfolio performance. Using data from a large securities company in a quasi-experimental setting, we find that mobile app adoption reduces time constraints (to proximate transaction friction) and increases trend-chasing bias (to proximate myopic decision making). Yet we do not find that mobile app adoption leads to a change in portfolio performance because, whereas the reduction in time constraints can be beneficial for investors’ performance, the increase in trendchasing can be detrimental to investors’ performance. Finally, we document an inverted-U relationship between the Mobile Usage Intensity (level of transition from PC to mobile) and portfolio performance to show that dual-channel investors, balancing PC’s analytical strength with mobile’s real-time connectivity, achieve superior trading outcomes compared with singlechannel counterparts (either PC- or mobile-centric). Overall, our findings suggest ambidextrous use of both channels enables investors to achieve higher portfolio performance.

Because mobile app adoption may not be a random decision by investors, we leverage several identification strategies and robustness checks to verify our results (Mithas et al. 2022a, b). Other than the GSC and staggered DID methods, we applied the stacked DID and CS methods to address concerns of the heterogeneity bias of the staggered DID, and the results are qualitatively similar. Additionally, we use dynamic matching to ensure a balance between the treatment and control groups right before the mobile app adoption to circumvent the inherent limitations associated with traditional PSM.

## 5.2. Contributions and Research Implications

Our study makes several important contributions and has important implications for research. First, we contribute to the growing literature that examines the impacts of technologies on individual investment behaviors (Barber and Odean 2002, Liu et al. 2020, Hendershott et al. 2021, Pan et al. 2023). We extend the focus of these studies beyond traditional internet-based PCs to mobile apps, which have become increasingly prevalent yet remain under-researched. Thus, we answer a timely call to understand how mobile apps influence retail investors’ trading behaviors and outcomes. Our work bridges the information systems and behavioral finance literature by examining the effects of mobile apps on investors’ trading behaviors and trading outcomes. We extend prior work by Barber and Odean (2002) by going beyond overconfidence to also consider transaction fric tions and trend-chasing as mediating mechanisms for a more nuanced and comprehensive picture of how technology use affects trading behavior and outcomes.

Second, our study provides deeper insights into the nuanced role of multichannel use in financial activities. Prior research primarily considers the impact of individual channels (PC or mobile) on portfolio performance, often treating these channels in isolation. This study, however, introduces the notion of Mobile Usage Intensity (level of transition from PC to mobile) and documentation of an inverted-U relationship, providing a more holistic understanding of how the integrated use of PC and mobile channels influences portfolio performance.

Third, whereas this study does not find a significant effect of mobile app adoption on investors’ portfolio performances, it does shed light on the complex relationship between mobile trading and investment outcomes. Our findings demonstrate that the adoption of mobile apps reshapes trading behaviors, reducing transaction friction and fostering myopic decision making. Despite these changes, they do not translate into improved portfolio performance. The underlying mechanisms influencing portfolio performance in the context of mobile trading warrant deeper exploration. Advancements such as commission-free trading and gamification, which have been rapidly integrated into mobile trading platforms, may yield entirely different impacts on trading behaviors and portfolio performance. These features may further reshape the trading experience, possibly altering retail investors’ risk perceptions and decision-making processes. Future research can explore how these evolving features interact with investors trading behaviors and influence portfolio performance to improve our understanding of the impacts of mobile trading in the increasingly digitized world of finance.

## 5.3. Managerial Implications

Our work also provides valuable managerial implications. First, the study shows that mobile app adoption can significantly alter trading behaviors by reducing transaction friction and increasing myopic decision making. This implies a need for greater transparency and educational components in trading app design to help users make informed and less risky decisions. Developers need to consider the behavioral changes driven by the use of these apps and consider how these alterations can potentially harm or benefit users. For instance, integrating dynamic risk assessment models that adjust to real-time conditions and user behaviors can offer tailored advice, improving decision making. Additionally, customizable alert systems that respond to individual trading patterns could encourage reflection on past behaviors, directly addressing personal trading tendencies. Incorporating these elements could foster more informed decision making and mitigate the potential risks associated with increased speculative trading and trend-chasing.

Second, extending from the previous point, this study’s findings suggest that financial institutions and regulatory bodies should consider the implications of new technology adoption and the resulting changes in trading behavior when creating policies. The observed increase in myopic decision making through trendchasing among mobile app users can hurt investors portfolio performance. In response, regulators could consider implementing measures to protect investors from potential pitfalls in mobile trading.

Third, this study demonstrates that the adoption of mobile trading apps leads to a significant reduction in transaction frictions, representing a meaningful, practical implication, particularly for those who experience high transaction frictions. For retail investors who face challenges in trading because of time constraints, geographical limitations, or complexity of traditional trading platforms, the integration of mobile trading apps into their investment strategy could prove beneficial. Mobile apps, with their intuitive user interfaces and instantaneous access to markets, can streamline the trading process and reduce barriers to market participation. As such, they can democratize access to investment opportunities, helping to level the playing field for individuals who might have been disadvantaged by conventional trading methods. Financial institutions and app developers could leverage these findings to target and tailor their offerings to such user groups, emphasizing the ease of use and frictionless trading experience provided by mobile platforms.

Fourth, this research highlights a valuable practical implication regarding the usage of multiple channels for trading: namely, the complementary benefits derived from ambidextrous use of both PC and mobile trading channels. Our results show that, all else being equal, investors who use both channels evenly (i.e., 50% of Mobile Usage Intensity) gain the highest monthly performance by about 1.63%, representing a decent improvement. Investors should, therefore, conside using these two investment channels alternatively for superior performance outcomes.<sup>17</sup> Financial institutions and brokerage firms could use these insights to guide their customer engagement strategies, promoting the usage of both channels and highlighting the distinct benefits each provides. They might also consider designing their services to encourage cross-channel usage, ensuring a seamless transition between mobile and PC environments. Moreover, investor education programs could incorporate guidance on how to strategically use both platforms for maximum benefit. This implication suggests a promising direction for the future of retail investing, combining the convenience of mobile with the power of PC trading.

Finally, an additional implication arises from the evolving technological capabilities of mobile devices. With advancements in processing power and the integration of cloud services, mobile devices are increasingly capable of supporting sophisticated analytical tools traditionally reserved for PC environments. If designed appropriately, mobile apps can incorporate complex analytical functionalities, such as advanced charting tools, real-time data analytics, and comprehensive market research capabilities. This convergence of mobile convenience with PC-like analytical power could revolutionize the way investors interact with financial markets. It suggests that financial institutions and app developers should focus on redesigning and optimizing analysis tools for mobile use, thereby empowering investors to conduct thorough research and make well-informed decisions on the go.

## 5.4. Limitations and Directions for Further Research

As do most empirical studies, this work has limitations, which also provide opportunities for further research. First, our data come from only one securities brokerage firm. Although we believe that our results are generaliz able (because many incumbents and other securities brokerage firms also provide similar functionalities through mobile apps), it would be worthwhile to assess the extent to which our findings can be replicated in other contexts or economies. In particular, it may be interesting to evaluate the impact of mobile apps in emerging economies, such as India, which are witnessing significant adoption of digital technologies by households and individuals (Khuntia et al. 2021; Liu et al. 2024a, b). Second, because of data limitations, our analysis is confined to investors’ stock assets, meaning that our assessment of portfolio performance is exclusively based on investors’ securities holdings. Future studies should explore the effects of mobile apps on other types of assets, such as bonds, funds, or even digital currencies.

Third, if data are available, there might be other ways to construct measurements of different trading behaviors (e.g., using the time of day for each transaction to infer the transaction flexibility; a questionnaire to infer users’ level of bias). Fourth, because of the lack of individual-level mobile app use tracking data, we are not able to know the extent to which investors used any particular feature of this mobile app (e.g., how they used the mobile app or whether they received push notifications or not) or their purposes for using the mobile app (or PC). Another potential extension, therefore, would be to collect mobile app use tracking data and link each feature’s usage with investors’ financial decisions. It is important to understand how to design mobile app functions that best support these strategies, nudging investors toward outcomes that serve their investment goals.

Fifth, because of data limitations, the current measurement of Mobile Usage Intensity is calculated by using the number of transactions. Still, it does not inform whether (or to what extent) investors used each channe for market research. Thus, future studies with more granular data can explore how investors use different channels to conduct market research and how these differences affect investors’ portfolio performance. Finally, the Mobile Usage Intensity (level of transition) variable might not be a perfect measure. There is an underlying assumption that investors who use a channel more would naturally rely more on that channel for related activities such as information searches and processing. We could not validate this assumption because we only observe investors’ transactions. However, whereas we acknowledge that Mobile Usage Intensity is not a perfect measure, we believe it is a valid proxy to approximate investors’ information-processing behaviors. Future work with granular usage levels of data can extend and validate our arguments to determine whether a high score implies that an investor is more likely to use mobile apps, in relation to PCs, to process information.

To conclude, this paper provides new insights regarding how mobile apps influence investors’ trading behaviors and portfolio performance. Our analyses of a rare archival data set from a large securities company in China using a sample of 20,665 investors suggest that mobile app adoption does not affect investors’ portfolio performance when one examines aggregate impacts using a binary indicator of mobile app use alone. However, further analyses of the trading behaviors for the effect of mobile app usage intensity suggest that mobile app adoption reduces time constraints and increases trendchasing bias. Further analyses considering mobile usage intensity show that such transition has an inverted U–shaped relationship with portfolio performance, providing a nuanced explanation of our key findings related to the aggregate impact on portfolio performance. Together, these findings significantly extend the discourse at the intersection of information systems and behavioral finance.
