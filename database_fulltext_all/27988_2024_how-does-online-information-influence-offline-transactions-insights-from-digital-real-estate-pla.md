# How Does Online Information Influence Offline Transactions? Insights from Digital Real Estate Platforms

Abstract. Digital platforms facilitate the flow of information and the execution of transactions. This study investigates the impact of signals from platform-provided online information regarding search and experience attributes of products on the prices of their offline transactions. We situate our theorizing and empirical work in the context of digital real estate platforms. Our results suggest that online information pertaining to properties’ experience attributes has a significant influence on the prices of offline property transactions. The amount of online information relating to experience attributes—specifically, length of textual property description and the number of photos—positively influences the sale price of a property. In contrast, the amount of online property information related to search attributes—specifically, facts and features—has no significant influence on the property’s sale price. In addition, online property information on experience attributes has a significant impact on the sale price of uncommon properties (those valued significantly above or below their neighborhood averages), whereas its impact on the price of common properties (those valued close to their neighborhood averages) is insignificant. The findings are robust to various model specifications and across property transactions in different years, seasons, and geographical regions. They are also neither subject to confounding effect of real estate agents’ service quality nor driven by unobserved property heterogeneities. The findings shed light on how signals from online property information are used by home buyers and sellers for different types of properties. The insights have implications for how real estate professionals can better utilize digital platforms to convey signals regarding properties and facilitate property transactions and for how the platforms can be designed to support the exchange of information that provides signals on the quality of offline goods that are highly risky and experiential.

## 1. Introduction

The internet has had a profound impact on information flows and the conduct of business transactions across sectors of the economy. In particular, many internetenabled platforms have transformed how activities are organized and executed in diverse business and societal contexts. For instance, online shopping platforms, such as eBay and Amazon, connect buyers and sellers directly and facilitate sales transactions and sharing of product information, thus fundamentally changing the landscape of the retail industry. Social media platforms, such as Facebook, WeChat, and Twitter, help users communicate with friends and businesses connect with customers in ways not possible with traditional communication technologies. A key reason behind the rise of such platforms is the ease of obtaining and sharing a great variety of data, including not only structured data, such as product specification and price, but also unstructured multimedia data, such as photos, videos, virtual tours, and product reviews.

Today’s consumers are adept at searching for product information from different channels. A digital platform can be considered an online information source regard ing products that complements traditional offline physical channels, such as brick-and-mortar stores. Some researchers have examined how online product information improves consumer satisfaction and increases the probability of successful online transactions (Jiang and Benbasat 2007, Mudambi and Schuff 2010, Zhang et al. 2021). Others have analyzed how online information can influence transactions taking place in offline channels for new vehicle sales (Kuruzovich et al. 2008, Overby and Jap 2009) and household purchases in department stores (Pauwels et al. 2011). The practice of searching product information online, but executing transactions offline, also referred to as “web-to-store” shopping (Pauwels et al. 2011), is similar to the business context considered in the present study; however, as elaborated on next, our focus of exploration differs from that of prior studies.

Despite the rich literature on multiple information channels and their impact on consumer information search in online or offline transactions, some important voids remain. For instance, most prior studies consider sales of standard, well-specified consumer products that consume a relatively small portion of household income. To the best of our knowledge, prior research has not investigated how information on digital platforms impacts the sale price of highly heterogenous big-budget goods, such as single-family houses, that require weeks or months of research before a purchase decision can be made. In addition, prior studies have not differentiated between the types of online information and the types of products based on how easily information about them can be obtained from other channels. We suggest that some product attributes (i.e., search attributes) are structured, easy to measure, and more readily available from traditional data sources. In contrast, other attributes (i.e., experience attributes) are unstructured, difficult to capture, and not as easily attainable from traditional data channels. Further, on the product side, to the best of our knowledge, prior studies do not differentiate between common and uncommon product types, even though their availability of quality and value information—and, hence, the impact of online information on their offline transactions—is likely to differ significantly. The present study aims to fill these gaps by answering the following research questions: (1) What is the impact of the amount of online information regarding the product on its sale price? (2) How does the influence of online information on sale price differ between common and uncommon product types? (3) What is the difference in the impacts of online information on search attributes and experience attributes on product sale prices?

We situate our work in the context of the real estate industry, the largest industry in the United States and the most important contributor to most states’ economies (Stebbins et al. 2015). Today, more home buyers are using real estate websites to search for houses, gather information about houses, and assess the value of houses. According to a National Association of Realtors (2019) report, for all generations of buyers, the most common first step of a home-buying process is to search online for listed properties, and the vast majority of them use the internet frequently to search for new homes. At the conclusion of their house hunting, 50% of buyers purchased houses they found by themselves online, compared with 28% of them purchasing houses intro duced to them by real estate agents.

Capitalizing on the digitalization trend of the real estate market, many digital real estate platforms have emerged in recent years. Examples include Zillow.com, Trulia.com, Realtor.com, and Redfin.com. Among them, Zillow.com is by far the most dominant platform. According to recent reports, 80% of U.S. homes have been viewed on Zillow.com. Zillow’s database currently maintains records for 110 million homes in the United States (Smith 2020), and Zillow.com attracted 173 million visitors per month in November 2019 through January 2020 (FitzMaurice 2020). It is reported that when search ing online for property listings, the term “Zillow” is used more often than “real estate” (Zillow Group 2020).

It is important to note that the role of information in real estate transactions is underresearched in the information systems (IS) literature. Unlike standard consumer products, such as books or electronics, houses are the most expensive purchases for most consumers in their lifetimes; thus, their decision-making process to buy houses is likely different from that to buy consumer goods, such as books and electronics. Furthermore, unlike most consumer products studied in the prior literature, real estate properties are highly heterogeneous in physical characteristics and in their appeals to potential buyers; thus, home buyers typically place significantly more weight on the experience attributes, such as curb appeal, surrounding areas, architectural style, landscaping, and neigh borhood characteristics. More importantly, despite the increasing availability of online information and associated online search activities, most buyers still physically visit their selected candidate houses before extending offers, and practically all real estate transactions are still negoti ated and executed offline—this process is very different from those considered in prior studies on online-to-offline interdependencies in purchase transactions. These important characteristics of the real estate market make digital real estate platforms an ideal investigative context for this study. Using the distinctive characteristics of the real estate context, we aim to contextualize and integrate existing theoretical perspectives in the real estate setting and test the resulting hypotheses, thereby generating theoretical and practical insights regarding how the supply of online infor mation affects offline property transactions.

Using data collected from Zillow, we analyze how online property information influences offline property transactions and derive several important findings. First, we find that providing more property information on digital real estate platforms can indeed increase the sale prices of properties. Second, we conclude that information about properties’ experience attributes has a significant influence on property prices, whereas information revealing search attributes has no significant impact on property prices. Third, our results indicate that the length of property description and the number of photos have a significant influence on the sale price of uncommon properties—that is, those valued significantly lower than or higher than their neighborhood averages— whereas their impact on the price of common properties— that is, those valued close to their neighborhood average—is insignificant. Finally, we show that the findings are robust to model specifications and across property transactions in different years, seasons, and geographical regions; furthermore, the findings are free from the confounding effect of real estate agents service quality and are not driven by unobserved property heterogeneities.

## 2. Related Literature

We identify and briefly review five streams of literature as relevant to the present study: (1) the impact of online information on consumer experience and market performance; (2) the impact of online information on offline transactions; (3) consumers’ information search; (4) the role of signaling in reducing information asymmetry between sellers and buyers; and (5) the role of real estate professionals in disseminating information and affecting property prices.

In the first stream of research, researchers have investigated the influence of online information on diverse outcomes, such as customer satisfaction, intention to purchase, price premiums, and market demand. For instance, Mudambi and Schuff (2010) show that the characteristics of online product reviews have differential impacts on a buyer’s perceptions of review helpfulness for search and experience goods. Jiang and Benbasat (2007) reveal that functional mechanisms (e.g., interactivity, vividness of description) on e-commerce sites that enable buyers to experience products online are positively related to consumers’ intentions to return to a site and make purchases. Dimoka et al. (2012) show that online information helps reduce product and seller uncertainty, subsequently leading to higher price premiums. Similarly, multiple studies (e.g., Duan et al. 2008 and Gu et al. 2012) present empirical evidence showing that online product reviews can improve consumers intention to purchase, thus contributing to higher retail sales. Zhang et al. (2021) show that professional quality photos increase the demand for Airbnb properties.

The second stream of research has analyzed how online information affects offline transactions—that is, those negotiated and executed offline. Kuruzovich et al. (2008) find that the retrieval of price or product information online has strong associations with referred purchases, as well as the intensity of offline information search. Overby and Jap (2009) examine the market of used vehicles and find that vehicles with low quality uncertainty tend to be sold in electronic channels, whereas those with high quality uncertainty are more likely to be sold in physical channels. Pauwels et al. (2011) examine how the introduction of informationa websites affects offline transactions and find that an informational website’s offline revenue impact differs across product categories and customer segments.

The third stream of related literature focuses on the economics of consumer information search and the classification of products and product information. Much of the research in this stream builds on the theory of information, which stipulates that consumers will continue to search for product information until the marginal cost equals the marginal benefit of such search (Stigler 1961, Ratchford et al. 2003). Based on this theory, pioneering research classifies products into search and experience goods and product characteristics into search and experience attributes (Nelson 1970, 1974, Jiang and Benbasat 2004). Search attributes (e.g., color of a chair, number of calories per serving) are objective, simple to describe, and easy to verify before use. Conversely, experience attributes (e.g., user-friendliness of a computer, taste of a candy bar) are subjective, difficult to describe, and typically difficult to verify before use (Wright and Lynch 1995). In the internet age, researchers argue that information on experience attributes can be obtained through “virtual experience” (Klein 1998). In this study, we draw on the theory of information and the classification of search and experience attributes to refine our hypotheses and interpret our findings.

In the fourth stream of literature, signaling theory is employed as a theoretical perspective to investigate the role of signaling in facilitating business transactions. Specifically, signaling is employed as a mechanism to reduce information asymmetry regarding products (Spence 1974), which is considered the main problem in markets for “lemons” (Akerlof 1970). The signaling effect has been found to be at play in used-car markets, such as eBay Motors. Lewis (2011) examines the role of information access and shows that voluntary disclosures of private information by sellers increase the prices of used cars on eBay. In a similar vein, Dimoka et al. (2012), analyzing transaction data from eBay Motors, find information signals (diagnostic product descrip tions and third-party product assurances) to reduce product uncertainty for consumers. This study treats online property information as signals of property characteristics and value and draws on signaling theory to interpret their roles in facilitating and influencing real estate transactions.

Finally, the fifth literature stream examines real estate professionals’ role in disseminating property information and influencing property sale prices. The housing market is well known for the presence of information asymmetry. Yinger (1981) develops a theoretical mode to analyze real estate professionals’ role in reducing information asymmetry. Levitt and Syverson (2008) document that brokers have a clear information advantage over home sellers they represent—on average, brokers can sell their own houses at a 3.7% price premium compared with similar houses owned by their clients. Other researchers have examined whether brokers’ service quality has some influence on market outcomes, such as sale price. The findings are mixed. Allen et al. (2015) examine agents’ marketing efforts, such as open houses, Multiple Listing Service (MLS) virtual tours, and MLS photographs, and find evidence that such efforts can increase the realized transaction price. On the other hand, Elder et al. (2000) and Turnbull and Dombrow (2007) find no significant relationship between brokers characteristics and sale prices of properties. We observe that these studies are largely data-driven, and no attempt has been made to theoretically reconcile the differences in findings across studies. In our empirical tests, we control for the effect of real estate professionals’ service quality, while assessing the impact of online information on offline real estate transactions.

## 3. Property Information on Zillow

Digital real estate platforms, such as Realtor.com, Redfin.com, and Zillow.com, have evolved into a type of middleman called a data broker (Walker 2015). They collect property information from different sources, including Multiple Listing Services, and offer a rich set of property information, including characteristics of properties, photos, virtual tours, neighborhood characteristics, schools, property taxes, and real estate agent contact information. For sold properties, Zillow also offers transaction information, such as sale date and sale price. What distinguishes Zillow from MLS and competing real estate platforms is that Zillow offers additional valueadded information to its visitors, including web traffic information, such as the number of online views of a property and the number of buyers who have saved the property for future revisits, and the ZestimateVR , a property value estimated by Zillow’s proprietary algorithm.

We first examine the general property information displayed at the top of a property page (see Figure 1)— specifically, three information sections: (i) Photos, (2) Property Description, and (3) Facts and Features. Such information is typically provided by sellers or their agents. Based on the definitions of search and experience attributes established in the consumer information search literature (Nelson 1974), we can classify the data revealed in the information sections into search attributes and experience attributes. As explained earlier, search attributes are generally objective, easy to describe, and can be verified prior to use, whereas experience attributes are subjective, difficult to describe, and typically can only be verified after the use of a good or service (Wright and Lynch 1995).

Figure 1 shows that the Facts and Features section includes objective, factual property information, such as the year built, lot size, types of heating and cooling, etc. Such property facts are presented in attribute/value pairs with no ambiguity. Buyers can easily filter and compare real properties based on such attributes, leading them to function as “search” attributes. However, evaluating a property usually involves much more than those search attributes because rich information of an experiential nature (e.g., curb appeal, surrounding areas, architectural style, interior luxuriousness) plays a critical role to a potential buyer. Both text and visual information are typically provided on Zillow, enabling sellers or their listing agents to transmit rich information cues to potential buyers. For instance, sellers or their agents can use the Property Description section on Zillow to provide buyers with a narrative of the experience of living in the property (e.g., cozy family room, bright kitchen, wonderful view, roomy garage). They can also use the Photos section to visually present the aesthetic features of a property. Photos can also provide visual appeal and support for claims in the textual description (e.g., bright living room, high-vaulted trey ceiling, upscale bathroom fixtures) that may otherwise be seen as idealized by the seller. Both the Property Description and Photos sections are utilized by sellers and their agents to present infor mation regarding experiential property characteristics, thereby providing a “virtual experience” to potential buyers. Hence, photos and descriptions are primary carriers of information for experience attributes, whereas the attribute/value pairs in the Facts and Features section convey only information related to search attributes. For expositional convenience, we use “Photos” and “Property Descriptions” interchangeably with “experience attributes” and “Facts and Features” interchangeably with “search attributes” in the rest of the discussion.

Another important piece of property information provided by Zillow is the property value estimate, known as the Zestimate. As explained earlier, the Zestimate is calculated by Zillow using a proprietary algorithm, a feature that differentiates Zillow from most other real estate platforms. As a machine-generated value, the Zestimate initially was not highly accurate, but has improved over time, with its average error rate dropping from 13.6% in 2006 to 6.9% in 2014 (Humph ries 2014), and then to around 2.4% for on-market homes and 7.49% for off-market homes as of December 2021 (www.zillow.com/z/zestimate/). Even though the accuracy of the Zestimate varies from region to region, it nonetheless provides a valuable reference to sellers and buyers when pricing information is scarce. Zillow publishes quarterly updated reports about the accuracy of the Zestimate at the national and county levels.

We would like to emphasize that, although the precise algorithm Zillow uses to generate the Zestimate is unknown to the public, Zillow’s own description of the Zestimate offers no indication that the key explanatory variables considered in the present study, such as length of property description, number of photos, and length of the facts and features section, are used in the calculation of the Zestimate. We treat the Zestimate as a proxy for a property’s value and include it as a control variable in our model. Therefore, in essence, we are interested in learning that, on top of what has already been captured by the Zestimate, whether the type and amount of online property information can still influence the sale price of a property.

## 4. Role of Information and Hypotheses

In this section, we discuss the role of property information in property search and property transactions and how the role of online property information differs between different types of properties and different types of information. Based on these theoretical arguments, we present our hypotheses.

## 4.1. Role of Property Information in Property Search and Transaction

We first discuss the provision of property information and the process through which potential buyers collect property information, select properties to physically visit, and, in the process, shift the buyers’ and sellers market positions, as well as the final sale price. The key steps of the property search-visit-transact process are illustrated in Figure 2. Buyers’ behaviors and information acquisition in this process can be explained by the consumer information search theory and signaling theory, as elaborated on next.

According to consumer information search theory, the search for product information itself is an investment. Hence, a rational consumer will keep searching for prod uct information until the marginal cost of the search exceeds the expected marginal benefit (Stigler 1961, Ratchford et al. 2003). For most consumers, a house is the largest investment in their lifetime; hence, the cost of not making the right decision due to insufficient property information—or, equivalently, the benefit of collecting more property information—is substantial. This explains why home buyers are willing to spend significantly more time searching for property information from online and offline sources, followed by even more involved physical visits to shortlisted properties.

Based on signaling theory, online property information serves as signals of the quality of a property; thus, having more online property information can help reduce the information asymmetry between buyers and sellers, which is beneficial to buyers. It is also important to note that when property information is provided online, search cost is reduced significantly (Klein 1998). Therefore, the cost (time spent) of acquiring property information from online sources is insignificant, whereas the benefit of gaining more detailed information about the property (less uncertainty and risk, higher chance of finding the ideal property) is significant. Consequently, having more complete online property information is almost always a plus for home buyers.

Even if the initial search for online/offline property information is relatively inexpensive, paying a physical visit to a property is a time-consuming and costly process; hence, buyers have to limit the number of properties they can visit. According to signaling theory and consumer information theory, all else being equal, properties with more signals or more complete information can deliver a higher net benefit; hence, rational home buyers will choose properties with more complete information to visit. Essentially, visiting properties with more complete information is less likely to result in wasted trips, thereby lowering expected search costs. From a related perspective, prior research on signaling theory suggests that the usefulness of a signal depends on its signal fit, defined as the extent to which the signal is correlated with a signaler’s private information or unobservable trait (Connelly et al. 2011, Ho and Rai 2017). Essentially, when the receiver of a signal has less information about the unobservable trait that the signal is used to communicate, the signal fit is higher. As the receiver is more reliant on the signal in this case, the signal is more effective. In our real estate context, signal fit is high when a potential buyer starts to acquire information about a property. Once the buyer learns more and reduces information asymmetry about the property, the signal fit decreases, thus lowering the effectiveness of online information as signals of property quality. Once the signal effectiveness drops below a certain threshold, the marginal return of additional online information becomes minimal; thus, physically visiting the property becomes the next natural step to take. This step of the house-buying process is illustrated by the transition from box (I) to box (II) in Figure 2.

The transition from box (II) to box (III) in Figure 2 is quite intuitive—all else being equal, a higher probability of visit by a buyer translates to a larger number of visits with all buyers considered. We should point out that Zillow does not record the number of offline visits to specific properties; hence, it is not possible to directly verify the association between the amount of online information and the number of offline visits. However, Zillow does record the number of saves for a property, representing the number of potential buyers who have saved their search for the property for possible revisits. Even though not everyone who saves a property on Zillow will physically visit the property, we nonetheless expect a larger number of saves to be associated with a larger number of offline visits; hence, the number of saves could be treated as a proxy for the number of offline visits. We then find empirically that the amount of online information (i.e., length of property description, number of photos, length of facts and features section) indeed has a strong positive association with the number of saves. The study by Singh et al. (2014) on consumer information search for used cars reports that more online searches on dealers’ websites are associated with more offline dealer visits, which suggests that more product information obtained online can reduce the amount of product uncertainty and, hence, the likelihood of wasted offline trips, thus leading to more offline visits.

A larger number of offline visits to a focal property can send a strong signal to both buyers and sellers about the focal property’s popularity or demand, which, in itself, is a signal of high quality or high value of the property. Such a signal can put more pressure on buyers and less pressure on sellers, likely leading to a shorter time on market, a higher likelihood of a bidding war, and a higher expected sale price at the closing. This process is illustrated by the transition from box (III) to box (IV) in Figure 2.

To summarize, based on signaling theory and consumer information search theory, potential buyers are more likely to pay offline visits to houses with more complete online information, and more offline visits can send a strong signal to both buyers and sellers that a property is in high demand, thus leading to a higher sale price. Because a longer property description, more photos, and longer facts and features section represent more property information, we have the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1. Amount of online property information (i.e., length of a property description, number of photos, length of property facts and features) is positively associated with the sale price of the property.

This hypothesis sets the baseline relationship between the amount of online property information and the property’s sale price. The theoretical arguments for this hypothesis also lay the foundation for arguments to develop the next two hypotheses.

## 4.2. Role of Online Property Information Differs between Different Types of Properties

Box (I) in Figure 2 conveys that home buyers can collect property information from both offline sources and online sources. Although the focus of this study is on the role of online property information, the emergence of digital real estate platforms such as Zillow is a relatively new phenomenon. Real estate professionals, traditional print media, family, friends, and neighbors have been the primary information sources for home buyers for decades. Despite the inroad of digital real estate platforms, such alternative information sources continue to exist.

The marginal contribution of online property information to buyers’ overall information about a property depends on how much information is already available from offline sources. As illustrated in Figure 3, the information that potential buyers can acquire from offline and online sources is represented by a light-colored circle and a darker-colored circle, respectively. In the scenario in Figure 3(a), there is extensive information about a property from offline sources; thus, acquiring property information from a digital platform such as Zillow only marginally improves consumers’ total knowledge about the property, and, hence, the contribution of online information will be small. Conversely, as illustrated in the scenario in Figure 3(b), if the amount of offline property information is limited, then acquiring property information from a digital platform can significantly improve a buyer’s total knowledge of a property; hence, the contribution of online property information will be more significant.

Motivated by the two scenarios illustrated in Figure 3, we can classify properties based on how much information is available from offline sources. Although it is impossible to precisely measure the amount of information available from offline sources, we can look within the real estate plat form (Zillow) to infer the availability of property-value information from nonplatform (non-Zillow) sources. We suggest that common properties, those with Zestimates closer to their neighborhood average Zestimates, should have more abundant offline information available to buyers because a larger number of comparable properties should exist. In contrast, uncommon properties—that is, those with Zestimates significantly higher or lower than their neigh borhood average Zestimates—should have less offline information available to buyers because it is more difficult to find similar properties from which value information can be drawn.<sup>2</sup> In Figure 3, the scenario in (a) represents the case for common properties and the scenario in (b) for uncommon properties.

Because less information is available and pricing is less transparent for uncommon properties, the problem of information asymmetry is more serious for uncommon properties than for common properties. Based on signaling theory, online property information can reduce the information asymmetry between buyers and sellers by a larger extent for an uncommon property compared with a common one. As a result, compared with that of common properties, the price of uncommon properties is more likely to be influenced by additional online property information.

We can reach a similar conclusion from the angle of signal fit. Specifically, because comparable property and pricing information from offline sources is scarcer for uncommon properties, the signal fit of online property information should be higher for uncommon properties relative to common properties. Therefore, potential buyers are expected to have a higher reliance on online property information when making decisions on an uncommon property; consequently, compared with common properties, the price of uncommon properties is more likely to be subject to the influence of online property information.

Based on the reasoning above, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2. The impact of online property information on the sale price of a property is stronger for uncommon properties than for common properties.

## 4.3. Role of Online Property Information Differs for Different Types of Information

Whereas Hypotheses 1 and 2 concern the amount of online property information and the type of property, respectively, our next hypothesis focuses on types of information about the property. As explained in the previous section, among the property information provided on Zillow, photos and property descriptions primarily convey information about the experience attributes of a property, whereas facts and features carry information about search attributes. We next draw on the prior literature on signaling theory and theory of consumer information search, particularly the distinction between experience and search attributes, to postulate how the information provided on digital real estate platforms pertaining to these attributes may have differential influences on the sale prices of properties.

Because search attributes are objective, more straightforward, and easier to verify, consumers tend to trust advertising claims about search attributes, while being skeptical of claims about experience attributes (Nelson 1970, Ford et al. 1990). Furthermore, because experience attributes are subjective, more abstract, and ambiguous, they require more time to acquire (Huang et al. 2009).

The distinctions between search and experience attributes apply to real estate as well. Search attributes included in the facts and features section, such as year built and lot size, are easy to obtain, verify, and understand. The values of search attributes could also easily be obtained from other sources, such as public records, friends, or relatives. In contrast, experience attributes, such as curb appeal, surrounding areas, and the luxuriousness of interior finishing, which could be learned from photos and property descriptions, are more ambiguous, subjective, and harder to obtain from alternative sources. More often than not, it can be argued that experience attributes account for most of the information asymmetry between buyers and sellers of properties.

Furthermore, the difference in roles played by search attributes and experience attributes can also be explained based on pooling equilibrium and separating equilibrium in signaling games (Nian and Sundararajan 2022). Specifically, on the Zillow platform, because the facts and features section is similar for all types of properties, the search attributes conveyed in this section are more likely to lead to a pooling equilibrium, whereas the expe rience attributes conveyed in the textual description and photos are more likely to produce a separating equilibrium; thus, information on experience attributes is more likely to help buyers infer the quality of listed properties. For the aforementioned reasons, information on experience attributes should be more effective in informing potential buyers and, subsequently, have a larger impact on their selection of properties and the final real estate transactions. For this reason, sellers and their agents are expected to provide more signals concerning the experience attributes of their properties, and most home buyers likely spend more time acquiring signals on experience attributes. Once acquired, such information can reduce the information asymmetry about a property to a greater extent than does information about search attributes. In fact, a report by the National Association of Realtors (2019) concludes that, among the provided information, photos and detailed information about properties are the two most helpful pieces of information to buyers.

In selecting properties to visit or buy, search attributes primarily play a filtering role—helping home buyers narrow down the set of properties that are of interest to them. In most real estate markets, after this filtering step, a large number of properties could still remain. Home buyers will likely search for additional property information to help decide on the candidate houses they would like to physically visit. In this step, information about experience attributes conveyed by textual description and photos, in particular, can help buyers run a mental simulation of living in the house before making their purchase decisions (Schlosser 2003). In other words, experience attributes play more of a deciding role in buyers’ purchase decisions.

Furthermore, prior research has shown that consumers are willing to pay a price premium for subjective attributes of experience goods (Adhikari 2015), and travelers are more willing to book Airbnb properties with highquality photos because such photos better reflect room quality and can help gain more consumer trust (Zhang et al. 2021). Therefore, we argue that online information revealing experience attributes can increase buyers’ likelihood of visiting a property and their willingness-to-pay for the property.

For the aforementioned reasons, we posit that, compared with search attributes, online information for experience attributes (i.e., those conveyed in photos and property descriptions) has a stronger influence on property prices. Formally, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 3. The amount of online information for experience attributes has a stronger influence on the sale price of a property than does the amount of online information for search attributes.

## 4.4. Control Variables

The control variables of our models include key structural characteristics and neighborhood characteristics; the Zestimate, which serves as a proxy for the value or luxuriousness of property; time on market; assessment value; real estate agent’s service rating; and the number of saves or save-view-ratio by online visitors. Here, save-viewratio, which is the ratio of the number of saves and the number of views, reflects the aggregate level of potential buyers’ interest in a property. Including these control variables allows us to investigate whether the explanatory variables have a significant impact on the price of a property above and beyond the known determinants of property values.

## 5. Data and Empirical Methodology 5.1. Data

Much of the data used in this study is collected from Zillow.com. To obtain a representative sample of the U.S. housing market, we first select the largest, median, and smallest Metropolitan Statistical Areas (MSAs) from each of the four regions of the United States (Northeast, Midwest, South, and West) and then find all zip codes in the selected MSAs, resulting in a total of 1,894 zip codes. Subsequently, from each selected zip code, we randomly choose five houses that were listed for sale on Zillow in June 2016 as our target houses.

Our data collection consists of three phases. In the first phase, we collect the general property information, such as an address, size, numbers of bedrooms and bathrooms, and school ratings, as well as additional data provided by Zillow, including the Zestimate, the number of photos, property descriptions, and property facts and features. At the time of the first phase of data collection, the vast majority of the target houses have not been sold; hence, the most important attribute, sale price, is missing. The problem could be addressed by selecting sold houses only, but the challenge is that Zillow removes a few key attributes (e.g., number of views, number of saves, and time on market) soon after a house is sold. To address this problem, in the second phase of data collection, we revisit the original target houses in September 2016, identify the houses that have been sold, and collect the sale price and updates on previously collected attributes.

Because of the restrictions put in place by Zillow (e.g., Zillow blocks excessive requests) and other anticrawling measures, we supplement automatic crawling with a substantial amount of manual effort, which constitutes our third phase of data collection. In this last phase, for sold properties only, we manually save data (such as price changes and tax history) that cannot be collected automatically. In addition, we manually clean school rating data and parse some of the textual data for subsequent analyses.

## 5.2. Empirical Methodology

Economists have long been studying how to measure the value of goods, such as automobiles and houses, that are heterogeneous in nature and have many attributes. The main approach proposed in the literature is the hedonic pricing model. The perspective of viewing assets as bundles of attributes and assessing their associated “hedonic” value has grown in popularity in recent literature (e.g., Malpezzi 2003 and Robinson and Sanderford 2017).

Several forms of the hedonic model have been suggested in the prior literature. The most popular one is the log-linear form (DiPasquale and Wheaton 1996). With the Zestimate, online property information, and structure and neighborhood characteristics included, we specify the following cross-section model:

$$

## 6.2. Differences between Common and Uncommon Properties

To test Hypotheses 2 and 3 and check the robustness of the findings derived based on model (2) in Table 2, we split our sample into two groups—that is, common properties and uncommon properties. A property belongs to the common type if its Zestimate is within 70%–130% of the neighborhood average Zestimate; otherwise, it is an uncommon property. Recall that this classification is developed based on the richness of property-value information available from alternative data sources. Specifically, because a common property’s value is close to that of an average house in its neighborhood, there should be a larger number of similar houses in the neighborhood. Hence, it is easier for its owner, potential buyers, and real estate professionals to obtain information regarding the value of the property from information sources other than Zillow. Conversely, for uncommon houses, because it is harder to find similar properties from the same neighborhood, alternative property-value information is scarcer. We next examine whether the scarcity of alternative property-value information is a contingency in the impact of online property information on the sale price of a property.

We evaluate the differences across common and uncommon properties of the theorized variables pertaining to online property information using two approaches: (i) estimations with subsamples for common and uncommon properties, and (ii) estimations with the pooled sample (where we add the dummy variable uncommon (zero for common properties, one for uncommon properties) and its interactions with the variables of interest (i.e., log(- description), #photos, and log(facts) to the model).

Based on the definition of common and uncommon properties, we split our sample into two groups: 436 common properties and 283 uncommon properties. We first run separate regressions for common and uncommon properties; the results are summarized under model (3) and model (4), respectively, in Table 3. Consistent with model (2) in Table 2, we include save-view-ratio and log(Zestimate) in the three new models. Note that for space considerations, we show in Table 3 an abridged version of the regression result. The full version of the results is summarized in Table OA1 of the Online Appendix.

Table 3 reveals that the impact of online information on offline transaction price differs significantly between common and uncommon properties. Specifically, in model (3), the coefficients for the three main types of online property information (i.e., log(description), #photos, and log(facts)) are all insignificant, suggesting that the impact of online information is limited for common properties. In sharp contrast, for uncommon properties, the results for model (4) show that both log(description) and #photos have a positive and significant impact at the 5% level. Further, by comparing the results of models (3) and (4), we find that the absolute values of the coefficients for uncommon properties are significantly larger than those for common properties.

In model (5), we use the pooled sample, merging common and uncommon properties and using a dummy variable to indicate type of property (uncommon; 0 � common property; 1 � uncommon property). We add three interaction terms into model (5) to capture the differential influence of online property information (log(- description), #photos, and log(facts)) on the sale price of uncommon and common properties. As the coefficient for log(description), #photos, or log(facts) in model (5)

captures the total effect of the corresponding property information on the sale price of common properties, the significance of the total effect is directly evaluated by the respective t-test. In contrast, for uncommon properties, the total effect of given property information is the sum of the estimated main effect and the interaction effect. We conduct a Wald test with the null hypothesis that the total effect for uncommon properties is equal to zero for the given property information (i.e., log(description), #photos, and log(facts)).<sup>3</sup>

From the coefficients for log(description), #photos, or log(facts) in model (5), we conclude that the impact of online property information is insignificant for common properties. From the Wald test results summarized in the last column of Table 3, we conclude that the length of property description and the number of photos both have a positive and significant impact on the sale price of uncommon properties. These conclusions are consistent with those obtained from split-sample analyses using models (3) and (4). In model (5), the coefficient for #photos × uncommon is positive and significant, whereas the coefficient for log(description) × uncommon is not significant; thus, Hypothesis 2, stating that the impact of online property information on sale price is stronger for uncommon properties than for common properties, is partially supported.

Overall, our finding that the impact of online property information is significant for uncommon properties and insignificant for common properties suggests that, for a less common property in a neighborhood, due to the scarcity of relevant information from nearby comparable properties, buyers and sellers are more likely to rely on online information to infer the value of the property. Conversely, the demand for online property information is much lower for common properties because more similar properties exist, and it is easier for buyers and sellers to find information elsewhere.

## 7.1. Additional Data

We collect additional data sets to conduct robustness checks and address potential empirical issues. ## 7.2. Agents’ Service Quality

A potential confounder in our model is the real estate agents’ service quality. One can argue that sellers’ agents with higher capability or better work ethic can provide more property information online, while simultaneously working harder to secure a better deal for their clients. Although agents’ service quality is not directly observable, we can use their ratings on Zillow as a proxy for agents’ service quality. According to Zillow, agent ratings run on a scale of 1–5, with 5 indicating that clients are “very likely” to recommend the agent and 1 indicating that a recommendation is “highly unlikely.” Such information indicates that agent rating is a reasonable proxy for agents’ service quality.

Using the Augmented 2016 Data Set and treating the seller agent rating as an additional control variable, we run three regression models that are similar to those shown in Table 3. The key results are summarized in Table 4, which is an abridged version of the full table (Table OA2) in the online appendix. This table shows that seller agents’ rating does not have a significant impact on the sale price of either common or uncommon properties. These results suggest that seller agent ratings do not confound the main relationships in this research. Furthermore, after adding agent ratings into the model, the key findings observed from Table 3 remain valid. Specifically, the two variables that capture properties experience attributes—that is, length of property descrip tion and number of photos—remain significant predictors for sale prices of uncommon properties and insignificant predictors for prices of common properties. The variable that reflects search attributes—that is, the length of the facts and features section—is again not a significant predictor of the sale prices for either common or uncommon properties. Furthermore, consistent with what is observed from Table 3, the coefficient for interaction term #photos × uncommon in model (8) is significant, whereas that for log(description)× uncommon is insignificant, again showing that Hypothesis 2 is partially supported.

One technical note is that using the first-stage residual as a control variable in a secondstage regression induces the classic generated regressor problem in statistical inference. Based on the new empirical results shown in Tables 4 and 5, we reach several important conclusions. First, because the main results remain valid after unobserved variables are controlled for with alternative qualityrelated variables and empirical techniques, we conclude with confidence that our main findings are not driven by unobserved property-quality heterogeneities. Second, given that our main findings remain consistent after controlling for the agents’ service quality, we rule out the possibility that it is the agents’ service quality that drives both the explanatory variables and the dependent variable in our empirical results. Third, the results obtained based on the 2020 Chicago MSA Data Set further underscore the robustness of our findings. Specifically, the new data were collected for houses sold in winter months (as opposed to summer months for the 2016 data set), and they are for houses sold in one major MSA (as opposed to a random sample of small, medium, and large MSAs across the United States in the 2016 data). In addition, the variables are slightly different in the two data sets (with save-view-ratio available only in the 2016 data and last hedonic residual as control for unobserved quality only in the 2020 data). The fact that the same patterns are observed from property transactions in different years, seasons, and geographical regions lends strong support to the robustness of our findings.

## 7.4. Further Robustness Checks

To further evaluate the consistency of our main findings, we conduct three additional series of robustness checks. In the first series of checks, we keep the main model specification, but try a new estimation method and alternative measurements of key variables. Specifically, we (1) change the standard error estimator from the clusterrobust estimator to the Hubert-White heteroscedasticity robust estimator; (2) replace the missing neighborhood average Zestimate with the city average Zestimate in the classification of common versus uncommon properties; and (3) change the range used to define common properties from 70%–130% of the neighborhood average Zestimate to 65%–135%, 75%–125%, and 80%–120%. In all these new analyses, our main findings remain valid qual itatively. Details about these robustness checks are provided in Section II of the online appendix.

In the second series of robustness checks, we derive an alternative model specification based on a linear combination of parameters obtained by rearranging the independent variables. This new model specification allows us to use the pooled sample and the standard t-tests to test our hypotheses on both common and uncommon properties. The results from these tests also confirm the robustness of our main findings, details of which are provided in Section III of the online appendix.

## 8. Discussion

We now summarize our main findings and discuss their implications for theory and practice.

## 8.1. Summary of Findings

Our empirical analysis produces several important findings. First, we find evidence that providing more property information on digital real estate platforms can increase sale prices of real properties. However, not all property information is created equal. Online information on experience attributes of a property—the length of textual description and the number of photos, in particular—has a positive influence on the offline property sale price. In contrast, the amount of information on search attributes, represented by the facts and features of a property, does not have significant influence on the sale price of the property. Second, the impact of online property information on the sale price of uncommon properties is significant, whereas the impact on that of common properties is insignificant. This is because, compared with uncommon properties, information about common properties is typically more readily available from traditional sources; hence, online property information for common properties does not help as much as it does for uncommon properties. Third, additional empirical tests show that our main findings are robust to various model specifications and empirical methodologies, and across property transactions in different years, seasons, and geographical regions. Furthermore, we present empirical evidence to show that our key results are not subject to the potential confounding effect arising from real estate agents’ service quality, nor are driven by unobserved property heterogeneities.

## 8.2. Implications for Theory

To our best knowledge, our study is the first to examine the impact of information provided by digital platforms on the real estate market, thus contributing to the broad literature regarding the impact of the internet and digital platforms on the efficiency of business transactions, the formation of new business models, and the disruptions to existing business models.

Our findings show that information provided by digital platforms not only improves the flow of information and facilitates the execution of transactions, but also increases the value of real estate properties that, unlike most standard consumer goods considered in prior stud ies, are highly heterogeneous and expensive and require much longer time for information acquisition and purchase decision making. This adds to the emergent literature on how digital platforms increase the value of products and services (e.g., Dimoka et al. 2012). Collectively, our findings suggest that an integrated theoretical perspective that combines three viewpoints pertaining to information—completeness of information from classical information theory, search versus experience attribute from consumer information search theory, and transpar ency of information for common versus uncommon products from signaling theory—provides a nuanced explanation of the impact of information provided by digital plat forms on the economic value of highly heterogeneous big-budget goods. Although past work has examined these perspectives individually to understand the impact of different types of information and the signaling effect on aspects of consumer decision making and behavior (e.g., Jiang and Benbasat 2007, Dimoka et al. 2012, Adhikari 2015, and Zhang et al. 2021), our study makes important theoretical contributions by (1) combining the complementary theoretical perspectives on information and signaling to provide a nuanced lens to discern types of information provided by digital platforms and characteristics of goods; and (2) explaining the economic value of the offline transaction created by the online information.

Our result regarding the positive impact of experiencerelated online information on property value is consistent with our expectation based on the premises of theories of information and signaling and consumer information search (Stigler 1961, Nelson 1970, Spence 1974, Ratchford et al. 2003). In particular, the positive association observed between experience attributes and the sales price of the property suggests that greater signal fit is achieved for these attributes. Accordingly, a platform’s features to represent information on experience attributes can provide a mechanism to enhance signal fit and effectively convey information that increases the value of the good. Overall, this study adds to our understanding of the role of digita platforms in creating value by conveying signals regarding experience attributes that are costly to access through other channels.

Our finding regarding the differential impact of online property information on sale price for common and uncommon properties is also consistent with the expectation based on the notion of signal fit in signaling theory. Because information for uncommon properties is more costly for buyers to obtain, its signal fit is higher; thus, signals for such properties are more valuable to buyers. With the plethora of digital platforms introduced in different markets and the increasing salience of IS research on digital platforms, this finding has a broader theoretical implication. Specifically, in theorizing and assessing the value of signals carried by digital platforms, it is necessary to consider not only signal strength, but also signal fit that may vary across product categories.

## 8.3. Implications for Practice

This study shows that digital real estate platforms can serve as a channel to transmit property signals from sellers to buyers, thus helping to not only facilitate real estate transactions, but also improve the value of transactions. Further, we show that the signal fit is higher—or the effect of signals is more pronounced—for uncommon properties and experience-related attributes, for which information is scarcer and more costly for buyers to obtain. The findings have significant practical implications to key stakeholders in the real estate market, including buyers, sellers, real estate professionals, and digital real estate platforms.

For buyers, our findings can help them better utilize property information provided by real estate platforms in the home search and buying process. For instance, our results show that real estate platforms can be a reliable source of property information, especially when information from other sources is scarce, and online property information can offer a reasonable prediction of the value of a property.

For sellers, we show that online property information does matter, and the richness of online property information can affect the offline sale price. Therefore, sellers or their agents should try to provide adequate information online, especially if their properties are significantly higher or lower than the neighborhood averages. Moreover, they should utilize digital real estate platforms to achieve a high signal fit—that is, focus on using experiential features, such as descriptions and images, to communicate signals such as curb appeal and surrounding areas of properties to potential buyers.

Our findings are also valuable to real estate professionals. Recall that prior studies have shown that real estate professionals can help reduce information asymmetry between buyers and sellers and influence transaction outcomes, such as prices. The present study, however, reveals that the service quality of agents does not have a significant impact on sale prices, suggesting that real estate platforms may be eroding some of the roles traditionally played by agents. Our findings also suggest that the platforms are creating opportunities for revised and new roles for real estate agents. It is important for real estate professionals to understand the role played by digital platforms and adjust their business practice to leverage digital platforms to deliver better services to clients.

Finally, our study provides strong empirical evidence regarding the value of property information supplied by real estate platforms such as Zillow. Specifically, our findings regarding what types of information are more impactful on offline transactions can help platforms improve their design and focus on collecting and displaying the most valuable information to potential buyers.

## 8.4. Limitations and Future Research Directions

Our work has a few limitations that represent interesting avenues for future studies. In particular, the data we use in this study are limited in sample size (e.g., five properties were selected per zip code in our 2016 national-level sample) and set of collected features (e.g., photos were not collected because they are removed soon after properties are sold); as more data become available, the impact of online property information can be more elaborately and rigorously studied. First, even though Zillow is the most dominant digital real estate platform, accounting for 57% of online real estate traffic, alternative online property information sources do exist. It would be useful to replicate our analysis using data from alternative online sources, which can help further assess the robustness of our find ings. Second, we focus primarily on how the amount of information, instead of the quality of information, influ ences property transactions. A future study could, for example, employ tools of image analytics to further explore how the quality of photos affects the level of interests from potential buyers and subsequently a property’s sale price. Third, digital platforms are incorporating emerging infor mation technologies such as augmented reality/virtua reality, metaverse, and nonfungible tokens, which are providing novel ways to represent information about offline entities and for consumers and other agents to interac with these new technologies in their information-search process. These platforms provide exciting avenues for future research to further develop our understating regard ing the impact of buyer attention to online information and the influence on offline business transactions. Overall, building on the foundation established in this study on the effects of online property information on property prices, future work can evaluate the influences of alternative sources, quality, and representation of online information on property prices, as well as the heterogeneity of these effects based on geographical and socio-economic neighborhood characteristics. Insights from such work will lead to a more complete—and much-needed—understanding of how online information on digital platforms affects real estate economics related to home purchases.
