# Growing Technological Relatedness to the ICT Industry and Its Impacts

Subrahmanyam Aditya Karanam,<sup>a,</sup>\* Deepa Mani,<sup>b</sup> Rajib L. Saha<sup>b</sup>

Abstract. We create an intersectoral citation network using 1.3 million patents granted between 1981 and 2010 across all industries in the United States. In this network space, we find a significant increase over time in the proximity of non–information and communication technologies (ICT) industries to the ICT industry. Such “ICT-closeness” of an industry relates to a significant increase in the proportion of ICT technologies in the industry’s patent portfolio and complementarity between ICT and non-ICT patents in its portfolio. We hypothesize and find that ICT-Closeness has a significant positive impact on innovation outcomes of the citing industry, including innovative efficiency, recombinant capabilities, and creation of new products, services, and business methods. ICT-closeness also engenders hypercompetition in the citing industries, as reflected in greater winner-take-all dynamics and turbulence. Our results point to a paradigm shift in knowledge production, and in turn, sources of competitive value across diverse industries.

## 1. Introduction

In his letter to shareholders in $2 0 1 3 , ^ { 1 }$ General Electric (GE) chief executive officer (CEO) Jeffrey R. Immelt remarked, “We believe that every industrial company will become a software company.” As proof points for his vision, he included snapshots of technologyenabled product innovations across different verticals that aimed to improve asset utilization and revenue for GE’s customers. A rich body of anecdotal evidence from industry suggests that GE is not alone in its experience of significant dependence on information and communication technologies (ICT) for product differentiation and performance. Indeed, ICT is embodied in smart screens of a variety of devices ranging from phones to home appliances to medical devices, an estimated 80% of car innovations from entertainment to crash-avoidance systems,<sup>2</sup> and wearable devices that monitor the health and performance of athletes and patients alike. These examples emphasize the growing embeddedness of ICT technologies in a range of downstream industrial age products and services.

We interpret this growing integration of ICT technologies in application sectors and its impacts through the lens of design science and the economic literature on general-purpose technologies (GPTs). Specifically, prior research suggests that digital technologies yield a product architecture characterized by reprogrammability and data homogenization that together engender convergent and generative innovations. In turn, this ICT-enabled product architecture facilitates shifts in modal invention trajectories of firms and industries that are best viewed in the context of observations about GPTs. Bresnahan and Trajtenberg (1995) argue that GPTs are pervasive, able to be improved on over time, and spawn complementary innovations, calling for more microstudies that estimate the magnitude and nature of complementarities with ICT technologies, firms, and sectors. This study advances the body of work that responds to this call through an estimation of complementarities between ICT industries (that create GPT technologies) and other industries (that use the technologies), and the impacts thereof in the using industries.

Our study empirically identifies for all non-ICT industries, the extent of technological relatedness to ICT industries, and the impact of such relatedness on innovation performance and competitive dynamics. Using information on 1.31 million U.S. patents granted between 1981 and 2010, we construct a network of al patent citations across all sectors of the economy. Following prior research (Kim et al. 2016b), we then classify industries having two-digit standard industrial classification (SIC) codes: 35, 36, 73, 38, and 48 as ICT and others as non-ICT.<sup>3</sup> Subsequently, we estimate for each year, the technological relatedness of each industry to the set of ICT industries in this intersectoral citation network. We define relatedness between a non-ICT industry and the ICT industries as the closeness or inverse of the average shortest path between them in the innovation network. We call this measure of relatedness ICT-closeness. The conceptualization of technological relatedness to the ICT industry as a network measure allows us to focus, not only on the widespread use of digital technologies and the complementarities that they spawn across diverse application sectors to create and extract value, but also on the generative nature of these technologies that enables innovation in the using sectors of these application sectors. ICT-closeness of an industry is positively associated with the proportion of ICT technologies in the industry’s patent portfolio and complementarity between ICT and non-ICT technologies in the portfolio.

We hypothesize and demonstrate the positive impact of ICT-closeness of an industry on its innovation outcomes and competitive dynamics. We find that the ICT-closeness of a non-ICT industry is associated with a significant increase in the proportion of ICT technologies in the industry’s patent portfolio and complementarity between ICT and non-ICT technologies in the portfolio. In turn, ICT-closeness results in greater innovation efficiency (the number of patents per research and development (R&D) capital), recombinant creation (creation of new technological combinations), recombinant reuse (refinement and reuse of known technological combinations to solve new problems and develop new applications), and creation of new business models, products and services. We also provide evidence of a shift in the competitive dynamics of citing industries, as reflected in industry concentration, cash flow spread, and cash flow volatility. In particular, we find that industries that are closer to the ICT industry in the innovation network demonstrate hypercompetition, as manifest in greater concentration, volatility, and performance spread than peripheral industries. Our analyses correct for the endogenous nature of the citation network through an instrumented network approach, where we estimate the network using exponential random graph models (ERGMs) with patent examiner characteristics as an exogenous source of variation in patent citations (or tie formation). We also demonstrate the robustness of our results to alternate methods of endogeneity correction, alternate classification of industries, and alternate variable measures in the data.

Our study documents and demonstrates the dominant role played by the ICT industry in innovation across all industries of the economy. In turn, we contribute to the information systems literature in important ways. First, we extend the focus of prior research on the impacts of digital innovation beyond software technologies and industries to include, more broadly, ICTs and ICT-producing industries. The new product architecture that leverages digital technologies, articulated in prior research (Adomavicius et al. 2008, Yoo et al. 2010), assumes “combinations of information, computing (hardware and software), communication, and connectivity technologies” (Bharadwaj et al. 2013, p. 471). Applications of the Internet of Things are a canonical example of this phenomenon. In the late 1990s, the ICT industry began high-volume deployment of wireless sensors while reducing the cost and energy per sensor and development and maintenance of such sensors. However, the objective of widespread deployment of wireless sensors was made possible with the accelerated development of complementary technologies such as complementary metal oxide semiconductor (CMOS)-based semiconductor devices, networking protocols, and energy storage generation technologies. Thus, the emergent Internet of Things is a culmination of the decades-long effort of technological innovation from the electrical and electronics industry and the software industry. That is, our study emphasizes that the content of technological change across diverse industries is not just software biased but more broadly ICT biased. Therefore, in only estimating pervasiveness of software technologies (Branstetter et al. 2019) or product proximity to the software industry (Rahmati et al. 2021), prior research might be underestimating the impact of digitization on innovation and firm performance.

Second, our construct of closeness to the ICT industry provides a novel lens for conceptualization and assessment of digital intensity of innovations. Branstetter et al. (2019) conceptualize digitization as the proportion of software patents in nonsoftware industries, whereas Rahmati et al. (2021) conceptualize it as proximity of a firm to the software industry in a product sales network. Our conceptualization complements this literature by allowing for the exploration of two key attributes of digital innovations: (a) their widespread use across diverse sectors to create and extract value, and (b) their generative and general-purpose nature that spawns innovation complementarities across diverse application sectors to enable innovation in these sectors and their using sectors. We provide face validity for the construct by relating “closeness” to the ICT industry in an intersectoral technology citation network to (a) proportion of ICT patents in the citing industry’s patent portfolio and (b) complementarity between non-ICT and ICT patents in the citing industry.

Third, we demonstrate the impact of closeness to the ICT industry on an expanded set of outcomes that have been theorized but empirically under-investigated in prior research. Specifically, prior research (Adomavicius et al. 2008, Yoo et al. 2010, Rahmati et al. 2021) argues that digital technologies are modular, self-referential, generative, and distributed, thereby, enabling more innovations, new opportunities for combinatorial innovation, and in turn, opportunities for new products, services, and business models. Yet, the limited research on the impacts of digital technologies on innovation largely focuses on innovative efficiency to the exclusion of other outcomes. This study addresses this gap in the literature to examine the impact of digital technologies, in addition to innovative efficiency, on recombinant creation and reuse, and new business methods, products, and services.

Finally, we assess the impact of closeness to the ICT industry on competitive dynamics. McAfee and Brynjolfsson (2008) document a significant discontinuity in competitive dynamics of industries that coincided with the start of the period of innovation in corporate information technology (IT). They note that, although much attention has been paid to the link between IT investments and productivity growth, hardly any has been directed to “nature of the link between IT and competitiveness.” Emergent research notes that “digital proximity entails a complex competitive environment” (Rahmati et al. 2021). This is because digitization facilitates discontinuous expansion of product boundaries, entry of new classes of competitors, and in turn, disruption, or reconfiguration of value capture. Our study examines how closeness to the ICT industry impacts key measures of competition: concentration, volatility, and performance spread.

Section 4 describes our construction of the intersectoral innovation network and key variables. Section 5 articulates our model and identification strategy, notably, the instrumented network approach and the instrumental variable used to correct for endogeneity of network formation. Section 6 discusses the main results. Section 7 presents robustness checks and Section 8 concludes.

## 2. Literature Review

## 2.1. General Purpose Nature of ICT

A rich body of work points to the criticality of ICTs in innovation and knowledge creation in the firm. Table H.1 in the Online Appendix summarizes this literature. One class of studies focuses on IT capital, the critical information processing and knowledge management capabilities that it helps the firm create (Joshi et al. 2010, Saldanha et al. 2017), reconfigure in response to turbulent environments (Pavlou and Sawy 2010), and the impact, therefore, of IT capital on innovation performance and market value (Kleis et al. 2012, Bardhan et al. 2013, Ravichandran et al. 2017). These studies largely draw on a process improvement perspective to theorize the positive impact of ICT on innovation: better management of knowledge assets, greater precision, reduction of costs of innovations through better tools for product test, design and simulation, and better interorganizational coordination and knowledge acquisition (see Kleis et al. (2012) for a review of mechanisms).

This early body of work has yielded important and significant advances in our understanding of the impacts of IT on innovation. More recently, however, scholars in the field (Yoo et al. 2010, Bharadwaj et al. 2013) have shifted attention from ICT-enabled changes in innovation processes to embedded technologies in downstream industrial-age products and services. These digital technologies, they argue, are giving rise to a new product architecture (Yoo et al. 2010, Henfridsson and Bygstad 2013, Porter and Heppelmann 2015), defined by the presentation of functional logic, the embedding of functional logic in physical components, and the specification of interfaces amongst the physical components. Yoo et al. (2012) argue that the fundamental properties of this digitally enabled architecture are reprogrammability and data homogenization, which together engender new theoretical accounts on product development and innovation. Specifically, in contrast to “physical” modular design, modules in the digital product architecture are often designed without a complete understand ing of how they will be integrated with one another (Gawer 2009, Tiwana et al. 2010, Yoo et al. 2012, Tiwana 2015). More important, components in the layered digital architecture are product agnostic, have fluid instantiations, and produce differences, not only in degree, but also in kind (Yoo et al. 2010, Um et al. 2013). Because of these factors, the digitally enabled layered product architecture offers generativity, or “an overall capacity to produce unprompted change driven by large, varied, and uncoordinated audiences” (Zittrain 2006, p. 1980).

Thus, shifts in modal invention trajectories of firms and industries facilitated by ICT-enabled product architecture are best viewed in the context of observations about general purpose technologies (GPTs). GPTs may be viewed as enabling technologies rather than core technologies that directly shift the production function of firms that use them; in turn, they shift the invention possibility frontier of the economy, setting off waves of valuable coinvention (Bresnahan and Traj tenberg 1995, Jovanovic and Rousseau 2005). That is, GPTs are able to evolve and advance over time, spawn complementary innovations, and be pervasive in their impact. Prior empirical studies have analyzed patent citations to identify specific classes of ICT technologies that may be characterized as GPTs and shifts in modal invention trajectories facilitated by these technologies. For example, Hall and Trajtenberg (2004) identify individual patents as general purpose basis their generality and association with rapidly evolving technology classes. Similarly, Koutroumpis et al. (2017) identify technology classes or fields that were adopted and cumulatively invented by other technology fields. The assumption underlying these approaches is that GPTs are best characterized as generative clusters of inventions with spillovers generated by the technological subfields indicating their broad applicability and pervasive impacts on invention. Both studies emphasize “the rise of ICTs as invention machines” (Koutroumpis et al. 2017) that play a critical role in the process of invention in diverse technology fields.

Our study also advances this literature and emphasizes that ICT innovations have pervasive impact. However, although we use patent data, we focus on innovation complementarities across industries rather than across technology fields. Specifically, we estimate the magnitude and nature of complementarities between the ICT industry (that creates GPT technologies) and other industries (that use the technologies). Our analyses therefore respond to the call for more microstudies (Bresnahan and Trajtenberg 1995, Koutroumpis et al. 2017) that estimate the magnitude and nature of interactions between ICT and using sectors, including shifts in products, processes, and competitive structures of the citing industries.

In doing so, we advance an emergent body of rich work that empirically identifies innovation complementarities with ICT technologies by demonstrating: (a) new measures of digital innovation or digital intensity of innovations, (b) the pervasiveness of such digitization, and (c) its impacts on performance. For example, Branstetter et al. (2019) document a software-biased shift in technological change across four manufacturing industries and find that firms that draw more on software-related technologies in their inventive activity are increasingly outperforming their less softwareintensive peers. This widening gap is evident in both the average patent productivity of R&D and equity market valuations of the firms’ R&D investments. Similarly, Rahmati et al. (2021) find that “digital proximity” of a firm, as measured by the shortest path between its industry and the software industry in an intersectoral product sales network, is associated with greater intangible value, measured in terms of Tobin’s Q.

In this study, we assess whether the salience of ICTs to inventive activity in an industry, as reflected in the closeness of the industry to the ICT industry in an intersectoral citation network, impacts its innovative performance and competitive intensity. That is, we conceptualize closeness to the ICT industry to represent rent-generating digital innovation capabilities in non-ICT industries. We investigate the efficacy of non-

ICT industry’s closeness to ICT industries by examining its effect on the proportion of ICT patents and complementarity between non-ICT and ICT technologies in the patent portfolio of the industry.

## 2.2. ICT-Biased Shift in R&D: An Illustration

Innovation complementarities with ICT technologies are borne out by anecdotal evidence and industry reports, which emphasize that ICTs are becoming integral to the content of a range of industrial age products from cars to books to automobiles (Yoo et al. 2012, Porter and Heppelmann 2014, Branstetter et al. 2019). This “digitization” of products has evolved over time along with the evolution of innovation trajectory in the ICT industry that counts its components as miniaturization of hardware, increased computing power, ubiquitous wireless communication, and low-cost storage media, among others (Porter and Heppelmann 2014). For example, consider the evolution of sensor technology and its transformational impact on the products and business models of automobile manufacturers over time. Traditional electromechanical sensors were standalone nodes that were specific to a context, measured a limited number of parameters, were costly to develop and implement, and as a result, were limited in their capability and scope. Manufacturers made little use of sensors or electronics. In general, products were standalone and mechanically controlled, and product development was largely limited to mechanical engineering. Not surprisingly, through the early 1990s, product differentiation in the automobile sector centered on physical attributes (e.g., size, horsepower, and esthetics of automobiles). Manufacturers had little to no access to data from their products and relied on downstream players in the value chain for feedback on product performance. Data collected during maintenance checks and repairs were used to develop insights on various components and discern performance.

In the late 1990s, the ICT industry began high-volume deployment of wireless sensors while reducing the cost and energy per sensor, as well as development and maintenance of such sensors. Examples of enabling initiatives include NASA Sensor Webs (2001), ZigBee Alliance (2002), and Center for Embedded Network Sensing (2002), and Smart Dust, among others. The subsequent accelerated development of sensor technologies, including microelectromechanical systems, CMOSbased sensors, and LED sensors along with advances in complementary technology areas such as CMOS-based semiconductor devices, networking protocols, and energy storage/generation technologies, helped achieve the ICT industry’s objective of widespread deployment of wireless sensors. The emergent Internet of Things is but a culmination of this decades-long effort of techno logical innovation.

The previously mentioned evolution of sensor technologies in the ICT industry has profoundly impacted technology development in the automobile industry. Modern sensors power automated tire pressure monitoring, accelerometers, and high-speed actuators in airbags, on-board diagnostics modules, toll passes, theft recovery devices, and drive-by-wire systems that have replaced traditional mechanical control systems, among other functionalities in the modern automobile. Light detection and ranging (LIDAR) sensors that offer detection and control even in the complete absence of light yield even better and more consistent and reliable features such as blind spot monitoring, adaptive cruise control and braking, collision avoidance, and pedestrian protection.

The impact of sensor technologies is but an illustration of how technological innovation in the ICT industry has significantly influenced the product design and innovation principles of the automotive industry. “Mechatronic features,” which refer to the integration of mechanical systems with electronic and computational systems for provision of product functionality, are increasingly replacing mechanical features as the basis of competitive differentiation in modern automobiles. Indeed, industry observers contend that more than 70% of all innovations in the contemporary automotive sector are driven by ICT (Grimm 2003, Shorey 2011). To increase the pace of innovation, automakers are going beyond proprietary technologies to open up their operating systems (e.g., Ford OpenXC) to thirdparty developers to create varied apps for navigation, communication, and entertainment, as well as connect automobiles with mobile devices. In turn, they have several conduits to collect data, including “in use,” for feedback and product modifications akin to software updates (Iyer and Venkatraman 2015). Not surprisingly, value creation and capture in the automobile industry today is increasingly realized through ICT capabilities enabled by inventions of the ICT industry (Grimm 2003, Frischkorn 2004).

## 3. Theory

## 3.1. Digital Technologies and Innovation Outcomes

We hypothesize that ICT-enabled technological innovations positively impact returns to R&D investments in the citing industries. These testable predictions are borne of prior research (Yoo et al. 2010), which emphasizes three defining characteristics of ICT-enabled technological innovations, reprogrammability, homogenization of data, and self-reference, that give rise to a “layered modular architecture,” a new scheme for the presentation of functional logic, the embedding of functional logic in physical components, and specification of interfaces among the physical components. This new scheme, we posit, impacts a range of innovation outcomes.

Digitization involves separation of data and logic from a physical artifact that embodies the data and executes the logic. This separation renders a digital device reprogrammable to execute multiple functional logics that reflect diverse product functions (such as calculating distances, word processing, and video editing). Repro grammability, therefore, enables scale economies in new product development. It also reduces the marginal costs of new product development, including prototyping, designing variability, and modifying product designs. For example, testing different levels of horsepower in traditional automotive engines required manufacturing multiple engines, each providing a different level of horsepower. However, today, engine manufacturers can easily reprogram the horsepower of a standard physical engine using software alone.

The decoupling between data and the devices that store, transmit, process, and display data yields standardized or homogenized data that can be shared, reused, and accessed by a range of digital devices. As a result, the same data can be used to deliver diverse product functionalities and services and enable interoperability across products that are traditionally considered dispa rate. For example, the data on GE’s Industrial Internet platform, Predix, are drawn from a variety of industrial equipment manufacturers across diverse sectors ranging from aviation to energy. These data are then combined to deliver a range of services and applications, including data management and analytics, visualization, asset optimization, and geospatial intelligence, that are again used across sectors. The informational component of the product also allows for real-time and emergent needs to be addressed in a predictive manner and for ongoing quality management that extends the useful life of products. Consider that Tesla performs over-the-air software updates to improve the safety performance of its products.

Finally, ICTs are self-referential in that they leverage and requires an ecosystem of other ICTs, including digital devices, networks, and services to create value (Benkler 2006, Hanseth and Lyytinen 2016). For example, consider Nest Labs, which introduced a learning thermostat<sup>4</sup> in 2011 that programmed itself for energy optimization. In contrast to an analog counterpart that is a standalone product, the digital thermostat is a networked product in that its value increases with the number of other products that it can connect to, personalization, and contextual awareness. Not surprisingly, in subsequent product versions, Nest created a platform, which linked a set of its own products in the home via a smartphone app and established an ecosystem involving major players such as Whirlpool, Mercedes-Benz, Fitbit, and others. The products of the latter set of companies had been digitized and connected with the Nest platform to create a broader digital ecosystem of hardware, software, connectivity, and services with many different business models. The example emphasizes that self-referential ICT-enabled innovations, in engendering positive network externalities, accelerate innovative activity through “a virtuous cycle of lowered entry barriers, decreased learning costs, and accelerated diffusion rates” (Yoo et al. 2010, p. 726). Together, these arguments suggest the following.

Hypothesis 1(a). Innovative efficiency of an industry increases with the closeness of its technologies to those of the ICT industry.

The previous defining characteristics of ICTs should improve not only innovative efficiency but also another key dimension of innovative performance recombinant capabilities (Henderson and Clark 1990, Yayavaram and Ahuja 2008, Carnabuci and Operti 2013) that capture the ability to recombine existing technologies to generate technological innovations. Carnabuci and Operti (2013) disentangle two kinds of recombinant capabilities: recombinant creation or the ability to envision and create new technological combinations using technologies that have never been combined before, and recombinant reuse or the ability to refine and reuse systematically known technological combinations to solve new problems and develop new applications. Recombinant capabilities are critical for the evolution of an organization’s knowledge base in response to new technological possibilities and business opportunities (Yayavaram and Ahuja 2008, Carnabuci and Opert 2013): “knowledge bases that are conducive to exploring, evolving, and absorbing new knowledge should demonstrate changes in their patterns of coupling over time” (Yayavaram and Ahuja 2008, p. 342).<sup>5</sup>

Prior research (Ulrich 1995, Yayavaram and Ahuja 2008), rooted in Simon’s design theory (Simon 1996), characterizes knowledge bases in terms of their decomposability and demonstrates that a “nearly decomposable” knowledge base, comprising loosely coupled modules interconnected through prespecified interfaces, significantly increases the malleability of the knowledge base to change and the likelihood of recombinant innovations. This is because a modular knowledge base reduces complexity and increases flexibility to encourage exploratory innovations relative to a more integrated knowledge base (creation), and the ability to integrate innovation modules into different applications clusters enhances the likelihood of discovering crosscluster interdependencies and absorbing the innovation<sup>6</sup> (reuse). However, the architecture discussed in these studies is product specific, and the relationship between products and their components is assumed to be nested and fixed.

The nature of digital technologies extends the previous conceptualization of a modular architecture to a layered one comprising (a) reprogrammable devices and operating systems (that control and maintain the device and connect it to other layers), (b) standardized data and content, (c) applications that create, manipulate, store, and consume content to embody functional logic that serves users, and (d) networks that connect the layers with each other through standardized interfaces. This digitally enabled layered product architecture engenders new theoretical accounts on product development and innovation that positively impact recombinant capabilities (Yoo et al. 2012).

First, in contrast to traditional “physical” modular design, where the product is designed first and then the modular components are designed with standardized physical interfaces, modules in the digital product architecture are most often designed without requiring complete knowledge of how each will be integrated with another (Gawer 2009, Tiwana et al. 2010, Yoo et al. 2012, Tiwana 2015). A second implication for innovation, therefore, is that the boundary and life cycle of a product are unknowable, and the product remains incomplete as users continue to add, remove, and modify functional capabilities. That is, in contrast to traditional modular design, components in the layered architecture are product agnostic, have fluid instantiations, and produce differences, not only in degree, but also in kind (Yoo et al. 2010, Um et al. 2013). For both of these reasons, the digitally enabled layered product architecture offers generativity, or “an overall capacity to produce unprompted change driven by large, varied, and uncoordinated audiences” (Zittrain 2006, p. 1980). Innovations in any layer are amenable to independent exploration, can be easily combined with each other to create new technological innovations, and have cascading effects in other layers (recombinant creation).

As an illustration of how digital technologies facilitate recombinant creation, consider again the example of GE. GE manufactures industrial machines such as power generating turbines, medical devices, diagnostic products, and commercial engines, among others. In turn, the company has patented several discrete technologies such as machine status notification systems, comparators, and self-learning evidential reasoning systems that minimize discrepancy between actual output and desired output, and methods for analyzing error logs and other big data, all of which seek to detect machine failure and provide a quick and timely maintenance support in response. For example, patent number US 6591296 B1 automatically generates and sends an email message to a machine diagnostic center when a sensor detects a machine failure or predefined abnormality. Over the last decade, GE has also combined these hitherto discrete technologies to create systems and methods for prognostics and increasing the efficiency of an entire ecosystem of machines. For instance, a set of filters, receptive of information on operational parameters of a machine, were combined with connectors to other machines and operating environments. These were further integrated with comparators and methods for analyses of big data to create a degradation monitoring system (patent number US 7571057 B2) that indicates degradation of the machine and an underlying cause. Similarly, the combination of these technologies has also yielded a predictive condition monitoring system (patent number US 9256224 B2) that forecasts the future operational condition of the machine. ICT technologies such as sensors, communication systems and protocols, and computing algorithms played a critical role in these instances of recombinant creation.

In addition to rendering innovation modular through standardized interfaces and fluid instantiations, digital technologies also render innovation activities horizontal and subject to contagion through reprogrammability and self-reference (Yoo et al. 2012). The horizontal nature of innovation activities implies that efficiencies are gained by applying the same activities and knowledge across multiple products or platforms. Furthermore, the contagious spread of these innovations and their mutation and evolution as they spread marks a departure from the traditional S-curve diffusion model (Boland et al. 2007, Yoo et al. 2012). For both these reasons, digital innovations enable discovery of new instantiations and applications more easily, improving recombinant reuse.

As an illustration of recombinant reuse, consider that the degradation monitoring system, although developed in the context of wind turbines, is applicable to a range of other industrial equipment of the firm. Such reuse of ICT technologies is an outcome of their unique properties discussed earlier, notably data homogenization and reprogrammable functionality. These properties render disparate ICT technologies interoperable and scalable across business contexts. We posit the following.

Hypothesis 1(b). Recombinant capabilities of an industry increase with the closeness of its technologies to those of the ICT industry.

The layered modular architecture engendered by digital technologies not only facilitates more opportunities for combinatorial innovation but also dissolves product boundaries to enable the development of entirely new business methods, products and services. This is because of the ways in which digital technologies create generative innovations. First, prior research (Zittrain 2006; Yoo et al. 2010, 2012) emphasizes that digital technologies, on account of reprogrammability, allow for procrastinated binding of form and function or the addition of new functional capabilities well after the product has been created.

Second, this emergent property of digital innovation has enabled and incentivized a shift in the locus of technology-enabled innovation from firms to ecosystems, notably platforms (Gawer 2009, Tiwana et al. 2010, Boudreau 2011), whose success is linked to orchestrating participation from a myriad of actors, often outside firm boundaries, resulting in both technical and social heterogeneity that is distributed across diverse disciplines and communities. This dimension of generativity is manifested in “wakes of innovation” (Boland et al. 2007) in products and business models.

Finally, “big data” or digital traces that are byproducts of digital technologies spawn new (unanticipated) derivative innovations that add new layers to the original product. These attributes of digital technologies and the shifts that they engender in innovation processes lead us to posit:

Hypothesis 1(c). The creation of new business methods, products and services in an industry increases with the closeness of its technologies to those of the ICT industry.

## 3.2. Digital Technologies and Competitive Dynamics

McAfee and Brynjolfsson (2008) document the existence of hypercompetition in traditional industries that were more ICT intensive, notably a kind of Schumpeterian competition, since the mid-1990s. They posit that this emergence of hypercompetition was because the advent of enterprise technologies allowed firms to implement, share, scale, and replicate innovations in processes and operating models much faster than they could before. Bolder and more frequent competitive moves and ensuing customer adoption resulted in competition becoming more fast-paced, intense, and dynamic than before.

The shifts in innovation processes engendered by digital technologies also engender a shift in competitive dynamics. Rahmati et al. (2021, p. 1028) argue that “digital proximity entails a complex competitive environment in which a firm operates.” This is because the generativity that comes from combining heterogeneous technologies across layers is amplified not only through the distribution of knowledge of products that instantiate those technologies across heterogeneous product disciplines and communities but also across heterogeneous firms in diverse sectors. In turn, digitization facilitates expansion of product boundaries that is unknown ex ante, often discontinuous, entry of new classes of competitors, and in turn, disruption or reconfiguration of how value is captured. For instance, in the auto industry, as noted earlier, traditional car manufacturers have incorporated various digital components into their physical products, thereby drawing on innovation sources that would have been otherwise unavailable and innovating in downstream directions. A new class of technology companies, Uber, Tesla, Google, and Apple, among others, have emerged as competitors in this industry and succeeded in using digital technologies to reconfigure upstream and physical resources to drive new business models. The product is no longer a car but mobility. These companies have also leveraged resources in seemingly unrelated industries such as the food industry to create new value pathways for their digital resources.

In brief, digital technologies create a hypercompetitive environment in which competitive advantage is achieved through continual exploitation of short-term opportunities and creative destruction of competitors advantage. Performance spreads between leaders and laggards, and concentration increases as the most successful exploiters pull away. Yet, industry volatility and churn increase as new classes of competitors enter the market with new ICT-based innovations and existing rivals introduce further ICT-based innovations. These theoretical arguments suggest the following.

Hypothesis 2. The closeness of an industry to the ICT industry leads to hypercompetition as evidenced by a greater industry concentration, volatility, and performance spread.

## 4. Data

Our objective is to quantify the impact of innovation in ICT on the innovation of an average firm in each sector of the economy. Addressing this question at the firm level would require us to deal with a lot of heterogeneity across firms that makes it a difficult problem. Hence, we conduct industry-level analyses. Our sample, constructed at the industry-year level, is at the intersection of Compustat, Center for Research in Security Prices (CRSP), and patent data provided by the U.S. Patents and Trademark Office (USPTO). We retrieve accounting data for constituent firms in the industry from Compustat and market returns data from CRSP. We obtain patent application data from Patent Examination Research Data set (PatEx).<sup>6</sup> From this data set, we extract patent bibliographic information such as application number, filing date, classification of the invention, examiner id, status, patent number, art unit, and issue date on all the patent applications. We also obtain information on average number of claims per application of a patent from the Patent Claims database, and patent family size, set of patent applications filed with different patenting authorities that refer to the same invention, from Foreign Priority data. These data have been used in prior research (Lemley and Sampat 2012) and are available for the time period of 1931–2017. We obtain data on patent citations from Google’s patent data to construct the citation network. A major challenge in using these patent data in our research is the name disambiguation of inventors and patent assignees, which helps us match patent data to firm-level data obtained from CRSP or Compustat. In our study, consistent with prior research (Rahmati et al. 2021), we use the data released by Kogan et al. (2017), who obtained patent assignees of all utility patents granted between 1926 and 2010. Thus, firm identifiers for the patent data in our study are only available until 2010.

Although our patent data begins in 1926, we construct the intersectoral citation network beginning 1981 to ensure the quality of R&D expenditure data. The accounting treatment of reporting R&D expenses was standardized in 1975 (Financial Accounting Standards Board Statement no. 2). Consistent with prior research (Cohen et al. 2013, Hirshleifer et al. 2013), we estimate R&D capital (that is the denominator for our dependent measure of innovative efficiency) beginning 1979 to allow for a full five-year period with reliable R&D expenditure data. In turn, the two-year patent application-grant lag renders 1981 our first year of estimation of innovative efficiency that is normalized by five-year R&D capital. Thus, our final sample of patent data includes 1,314,962 patents granted by the USPTO from 1981 to 2010, yielding 30 intersectoral citation networks across 370 industries (of which there are 306 non-ICT industries), identified by their fourdigit SIC codes.

## 5. Variable Measures

## 5.1. Innovation Network Space

Prior studies (Scherer 1982, Jaffe et al. 1993, Verspagen 1996, Kortum and Putnam 2006) have estimated technological relatedness between a pair of industries using direct relationships, including (a) extent of overlap in product fields between the industries (Verspagen 1996), (b) rates of cross-citations of patents between the industries (Jaffe et al. 1993, Verspagen 1996), or (c) cross-classification of patents by industry of production and industry of use (Scherer 1982, Kortum and Putnam 2006). We complement this literature by devel oping a network-based conceptualization of technological relatedness. We posit that the ICT industry shares innovation linkages with non-ICT industries, not only through direct applications in the industry but also through indirect linkages via their producing industries. Dyadic measures fail to capture these economy wide effects of ICT (effected through direct and indirect relationships with application sectors) (Rahmati et al. 2021). Hence, we construct an intersectoral citation network that spans all sectors in the economy.

The first step in constructing such a network of intersectoral technological relationships involves the assignment of patents to an industry. Hence, we assign each patent in our sample to the primary four-digit SIC code of the assignee and construct intersectoral citation networks for each year in our sample period. Specifically, for each year, we construct a directed and weighted network where the nodes or vertices are identified by their four-digit SICs. The edges that connect these industries are directed and weighted: The direction of the edge is from the cited to citing industry, representing the direction of technology impact (can be read as cited industry making a technological impact on the citing industry); the weight of the edge is the “citation intensity.” We define citation intensity for pair of industries A and B as the number of citations made by the patents of industry A to the patents granted over the past five years in industry B. Specifically, say, in Year y, the number of citations made by Industry A to patents granted to Industry B during Years $( y - 5 )$ to (y � 1) is p. If $p > 0 ,$ , we create an edge from Industry B to Industry A with a weight of p for measurement Year y; $p = 0$ implies no edge between industries A and B. The five-year period used in computing citation intensity is broadly consistent with prior research, which suggests that technology cycles measured by the duration of rents extracted from R&D are approximately five years in most industries (Lev and Sougiannis 1996, Gulati et al. 2005). Going forward, we will use the words industries, sectors, nodes, and vertices synonymously to refer to industries; we will use the words ties, links, or edges to refer to connections between industries in our citation network.

## 5.2. ICT-Closeness

Our objective is to estimate the impact of technological relatedness of a non-ICT industry to the ICT industry on the former’s innovation outcomes and competitive dynamics. Following Rahmati et al. (2021), we define the technological relatedness as the proximity or closeness of a non-ICT industry to the ICT industry in the innovation network space. We measure closeness between two nodes in a network as the inverse of the shortest path distance between them. If we directly use the number of citations as an edge weight, the shortest path between two industries will be the set of links with the lowest number of citations. This, in turn, would capture the least technologically related nodes. Hence, we compute the shortest path using the inverse of the number of citations as edge weights, so that the shortest path captures the strongest technological relation between a pair of nodes or industries in the network. Thereafter, the inverse of the shortest path length provides us the closeness measure between any two industries in the intersectoral citation network.

We illustrate this approach further using Figure 1, (a) and (b), where A, $B , { \overset { \cdot } { C } } ,$ and D are the four nodes or industries in the network. The edge weights in Figure 1(a) represent the number of citations between the patents of the industry as is, and in Figure 1(b), it represents the inverse of the number of citations. Consider Figure 1(a), the shortest path from A to D is through B with a length of 11; however, this represents the path of the weaker technological relation (compared with the path through C). When we take the inverse of citation count as represented in Figure 1(b), we find that the shortest path from A to D is through C with a length of 0.2, which in turn represents the path of strongest relation. Taking inverse of 0.2 gives us the closeness between A and D as five.<sup>8</sup>

Following the previous reasoning, we estimate the closeness of a non-ICT industry to the ICT industries, ICT-closeness, as the average closeness of that industry to the set of ICT industries. We also assess the face validity of ICT-closeness by examining its impact on the proportion of ICT technologies, and complementarity between ICT and non-ICT technologies in the patent portfolio of the focal industry. We discuss these two measures here.

5.2.1. ICT Patent Ratio. We first assess whether ICTcloseness of an industry relates to greater proportion of ICT patents in the patent portfolio of the citing industry. Toward this end, we divide our sample of patents into two categories: ICT patents and non-ICT patents. We use the IPC classes identified in Corrocher et al. (2007) to classify patents as ICT patents. Corrocher et al. (2007) use patent abstracts to detect important applications in the ICT field by selecting the most frequent sequential triples of words that identify technological applications in the field. Their method yields a set of IPC classes related to these applications that are broader than those usually considered. We consider a patent as an ICT patent when at least one of the IPC classes assigned to the patent identifies ICT patents as defined in Corrocher et al. (2007). Subsequently, we cal culate the proportion of ICT patents for each firm in a given year. We then compute the average of this proportion to obtain ICT patent ratio of the industry.

5.2.2. Complementarity. Prior research studies measure complementarity between two patents based on the co-occurrence of technology classes (Petralia 2020, Goldfarb et al. 2022) in them. Following Goldfarb et al. (2022), we measure the complementarity between ICT and non-ICT technologies using the co-occurrence of technology classes in granted patents. Specifically, for each firm, for patents granted in year y, we initially identify the total number of IPC class combinations, where at least one of the IPC classes pertains to ICT technologies.<sup>9</sup> We then normalize this number by the total number of IPC class combinations across all patents granted in year y for the firm. We use this proportion as a measure of complementarity between ICT and non-ICT technologies in a given firm. Furthermore, we use the average of this measure across all firms in a given industry as the degree of complementarity between ICT and non-ICT technologies in that particular industry.

## 5.3. Dependent Variables

We aim to assess the economic significance of ICTcloseness of non-ICT industries in the intersectoral citation network. Specifically, our hypotheses posit the positive impact of ICT-closeness of an industry on its innovation outcomes (innovation efficiency, recombinant indices, and new business methods, products, and services) and competitive dynamics (industry concentration, cash flow volatility, and cash flow spread). ## 5.3.1. Innovation Performance Measures

5.3.1.1. Innovative Efficiency. The first innovation outcome that we estimate is innovative efficiency, operationalized as patents granted per unit of R&D investment. We estimate the innovative efficiency of an industry as the mean innovative efficiency across its constituent firms. Following prior research (Cohen et al. 2013, Hirshleifer et al. 2013), we define the innovative efficiency of a firm in measurement year y as the ratio of the number of patents granted to the firm in year y to the R&D capital, five-year cumulative R&D expenditures assuming an annual depreciation rate of 20%, corresponding to year (y � 2). We allow a twoyear gap between R&D capital and patents granted as it takes, on average, two years for the USPTO to grant a patent (Hall et al. 2001).

5.3.1.2. Recombinant Innovation Indices. As noted earlier, an important element of innovativeness of firms is their ability to recombine existing technologies (Henderson and Clark 1990, Yayavaram and Ahuja 2008). Following prior research (Galunic and Rodan 1998,

Yayavaram and Ahuja 2008, Carnabuci and Opert 2013), we posit two measures of recombinant capabilities, notably, recombinant creation and reuse.

5.3.1.3. Recombinant Reuse. Following Carnabuci and Operti (2013), recombinant reuse captures the extent to which an industry innovates by reusing existing combi nations of technologies to solve new problems, find new applications, and identify new contexts where previous knowledge can be used. Accordingly, we count the number of times each pair of technology classes used in patents granted to firm f in year y had already been used by the firm in the previous five years. Our choice of a five-year window is consistent with prior studies (Katila and Ahuja 2002, Carnabuci and Operti 2013) as knowledge depreciates sharply within approximately five years. We then normalize this count of repeated technology class combinations by the total number of technology class combinations present in the patents granted to firm f in year y. Thus, we calculate the recombinant reuse index of a firm f in year y as follows:

$$
R e u s e _ {f, y} = \frac {\sum_ {t = y - 5} ^ {y - 1} R e p e a t e d C o m b i n a t i o n s _ {f , t}}{T o t a l C o m b i n a t i o n s _ {f , y}},
$$

where RepeatedCombinations<sub>f,t</sub> is the number of times a combination present in year y is also repeated in year t (t goes from $y - 1$ to y � 5). The denominator, $T o t a l C o m b i n a t i o n s _ { f , y } ,$ is the total number of pairs of technology classes used by the firm f in year y. The average value of this measure across all firms in an industry yields the recombinant reuse index for that industry.

5.3.1.4. Recombinant Creation. Following Carnabuci and Operti (2013), recombinant creation captures the extent to which the industry innovates by creating new technological combinations. Initially, we identify the total number of new pairs of technology class (fourdigit IPC class assigned to the patent) combinations present in patents that are granted to firm $f$ in year y that are absent in patents granted to firm f in the previous five years (y � 1 to y � 5). We then normalize this count by the total number of technology class combinations present in the patents granted to firm f in year y. Thus, we calculate the recombinant creation index of a firm f in year y as follows:

$$
C r e a t i o n _ {f, y} = \frac {\text {NewCombinations} _ {f , y}}{\text {TotalCombinations} _ {f , y}},
$$

where NewCombinations<sub>f,y</sub> represents the number of pairs of technology class combinations that were not used by the firm in the prior five years; TotalCombinations<sub>f,y</sub> represents the total number of pairs of technology class combinations used by the firm in year y. Our choice of five-year windows is consistent with prior studies (Katila and Ahuja 2002, Carnabuci and Operti 2013) as knowledge depreciates sharply within approximately five years. The average value of this measure across all firms in an industry yields the recombinant creation index for that industry.

5.3.1.5. New Business Methods. Business models refer to the design or architecture of mechanisms of value creation, delivery, and capture used by a firm (Teece 2010, Desyllas and Sako 2013). However, such innovation is difficult to measure because new business models are unlikely to qualify for a patent. Therefore, management scholars (Teece 2010, Amit and Zott 2012, Desyllas and Sako 2013) define business model innovation as changes to individual components of a firm’s business model (rather than one that focuses solely on changes to the overall architecture of a business model). In turn, they posit that business method patents are a clear measure of business model innovation because they disclose activities that could underlie components of a business model. Following Chan et al.(2021), we identify patents belonging to USPC Class 705 (Data Processing: Financial, Business Practice, Management, or Cost/Price determination) as business method patents.<sup>10</sup> These inventions, whose primary novel contribution is in creating or improving business operations, fall into two categories: those related to customer service (including and not limited to those in the area of incentive programs, coupons, electronic shopping, and cost/price determination, and reservations) and those related to operations/administration (such as operations research, healthcare management, inventory management, accounting, and shipping) (USPTO 2017).

5.3.1.6. New Products and Services. Following Rahmati et al. (2021), we obtain the information on product segments of each firm in our sample from Compustat Historical Segments data set. These data extend beyond a firm’s primary industry classification and show how each firm’s portfolio of sales revenue is segmented among different product lines reported by the firm, named business segments. We compare the business segments of a firm in the current year with its business segments in the previous year to identify the firms new business segments. We then compute the average number of new business segments across all the firms in the industry to obtain the number of new business segments of an industry, which is used as a proxy for the number of new products and services offered by the industry.

5.3.2. Competitive Dynamics. McAfee and Brynjolfsson (2008) note that, although productivity assessments involve an estimation of level changes in fundamentals, assessment of competitive dynamics involves an estimation of variance in fundamentals within an industry. Consistent with this research, we argue that industries closer to the ICT industry potentially experience Schumpeterian competition (Schumpeter 1939, 1947), characterized by higher concentration, performance turbulence, and spread. We use cash flow volatility to measure turbulence and cash flow spread and industry concentration levels to determine whether the market demonstrates “winner-take-all” dynamics. We use Sales Herfindahl-Hirschman index (HHI) to measure industry concentration.

Our use of cash flow as a key performance fundamental is aligned with prior research in management, especially that on innovation. For example, it is widely acknowledged in the marketing literature (Srivastava et al. 1998, Krasnikov et al. 2009, Srinivasan et al. 2009, Fornell et al. 2018) that product innovations and customer satisfaction increase firm value by improving growth or outlook on cash flows. Specifically, this body of research suggests that innovations accelerate or increase cash flows, reduce vulnerability in cash flows and “increase the volatility of cash flows in the short run, but can eventually lead to stable cash flows” (Srinivasan et al. 2009, p. 28). Volatility is induced more by pioneering innovations than sustaining ones. More recently, the information systems (IS) literature (Nandkumar et al. 2018) notes that markets and investors value high-tech firms more for future growth than for current period profits or profitability: They find that the “intangible” returns of these firms, or the component of valuation that is explained by future growth, is much higher than their “tangible” returns, or the component of valuation that is explained by current period profits. Finally, the use of cash flow as a key fundamental is consistent with the assertion of prior research (Rappaport 1986, Srivastava et al. 1998) that not only is cash flow a preferred capital market measure, but it also is less susceptible to the problems associated with accrual accounting measures, notably that they reflect prior performance and are not forward looking, they are not adjusted for risk, and they cannot be distorted by accounting laws and conventions.

Following prior research (McAfee and Brynjolfsson 2008, Huang 2009, Keefe and Yaghoubi 2016), we estimate industry concentration as the HHI of the sales revenue of the constituent firms of the industry, cash flow volatility as the standard deviation of cash flow across a rolling window of three years, and cash flow spread as the difference in the average cash flows of the firms in the industry performing below the 25th percentile and above the 75th percentile.

## 6.1. Identification Strategy

The estimates of closeness in the above specification may be biased by endogeneity of network tie formation. Endogeneity concerns may arise from (a) unobserved variables that impact both citations and innovation performance and (b) reverse causality, whereby in the expectation of superior innovation outcomes, firms may systematically create innovations that bring them closer to the ICT industries. We address these concerns using an instrumented network approach, where we use examiner characteristics as instruments for tie formation in the network. We detail the instrumented network approach and our choice of instrument here.

6.1.1. Instrumented Network Approach. The endogenous regressor in our context is a network statistic, namely ICT-closeness, estimated for a non-ICT industry as the average shortest path between that industry and the set of ICT industries. This shortest path is derived from links between multiple industries that are, in turn, an outcome of several interdependent social processes (such as popularity of nodes or in the intersectoral citation network and knowledge exposure of an industry) that simultaneously drive network formation (Lusher et al. 2012, Kim et al. 2016a). For exam ple, in the intersectoral citation network, knowledge exposure of an industry would drive its innovation activity and citations to other industries that increase the knowledge exposure of other industries, leading to increased innovation and citation activity across industries over time. Hence, the basis of link formation between a pair of industries is not only driven by the characteristics of the focal pair but also the characteristics of other industry pairs and the likelihood of other industries to cite or get cited. Furthermore, these factors that affect the likelihood of citation across industries could be unobservable to the econometrician. Hence, the processes that drive network formation are both nonlinear and endogenous.

To address these endogeneity concerns, we use an instrumented network approach based on ERGMs. ERGMs, by design, account for cross-dependencies across nodes and other interdependent processes that underlie network formation. That is, ERGMs not only model covariates included in traditional regressions but also variables that provide insights into processes underlying network formation. In addition, ERGMs provide standard errors for each attribute used to model the network, which can be used to confirm the identification properties of our instrument for tie formation, examiner experience. Prior research in management has extensively used ERGM to understand network formation (see Kim et al. (2016a) for a review of this literature; Wasserman and Pattison 1996, Snij ders et al. 2006, Robins et al. 2009, Lusher et al. 2012, Wang et al. 2013). Kim et al. (2016a) articulate studies in other disciplines of social sciences (Goodreau et al. 2009, Atouba and Shumate 2010, Wimmer and Lewis 2010, Cranmer and Desmarais 2011) that have adopted this technique.

Using ERGM, in the intersectoral citation network, we first estimate the likelihood of link formation as a nonlinear function of network measures (notably, the network’s in-degree and out-degree distribution) and industry characteristics (notably, examiner experience that we use as an instrument and report exogeneity checks for). Subsequently, we simulate or predict the network using Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method. This predicted/simulated network is called the instrumented network and is analogous to the first stage of the two-stage least squares (2SLS) model. For each year, we estimate closeness of the non-ICT industries to the ICT industries in this instrumented network and use it as a regressor in our models of impact assessment. This last step is equivalent to the second stage in a traditional 2SLS model. The instantiation of this approach in our context is briefly enumerated in Table 1 and explained in detail in Section A of the Online Appendix.

6.1.2.1. Patent Assignment to Examiners. An incoming patent application to the USPTO is received by a central office that assigns it an application number, patent class, and subclass codes that detail the technology type incorporated in the application.<sup>11</sup> The patent is then allocated accordingly to one of the art units in charge of the examination process. Once in the art unit, a supervisory patent examiner (SPE) assigns the application to a specific examiner. Each art unit has discretion over how applications are allocated to examiners by SPEs. Interviews with patent examiners, conducted by Lemley and Sampat (2012), reveal that the assignment is per arbitrary rules (such as the last digit of the application number), docket management needs, or familiarity with the technology. That is, the assignment is functionally equivalent to random assignment. The interviews provide no evidence that SPEs engage in any substantive evaluation of applications that could inform about their potential forward citations. To the contrary, Merges (1999) notes that given informational barriers and the limited attention accorded to patents prior to their assignment to a specific examiner, there is low probability of systematic sorting.<sup>12</sup> Therefore, it is unlikely that applications are assigned to examiners according to their potential citations. The empirical evidence is consistent with these observations. For example, Lemley and Sampat (2012), Righi and Simcoe (2017), and Sampat and Williams (2019) show that patent applications assigned to lenient and strict patent examiners within an art unit have similar observable characteristics at the time of application. In line with our observation about the patent assignment process and the findings of prior research, we contend that patent applications assigned to more and less experienced examiners have similar observable characteristics at the time of application.

More precisely, we compute the time-varying measure of examiner experience at the application level as follows:

$$

The difference between $E _ { j k a t }$ and $U _ { a t }$ represents the relative experience of the examiner at the time of review of patent application j filed in year t. For a single patent application, the corresponding examiner’s relative experience, $E _ { j k a t } - U _ { a t } ,$ is a suitable instrument for its citations to the prior art. However, we are interested in obtaining an instrument for the citations made by all patents granted in the industry at a given point in time. Accordingly, we average $E _ { j k a t } - U _ { a t }$ across all patents, $n _ { i , t } ,$ , in the industry i in year t to obtain the industry level average examiner experience as follows:

$$
E x p _ {i, t} = \frac {\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n _ {i , t}} (E _ {j k a t} - U _ {a t})}{n _ {i , t}}.
$$

## 7.2. Exogeneity Checks

As noted earlier, we adopt the instrumented network approach to estimate ICT-closeness and assess its content and impact on industry innovation outcomes and competitive dynamics. The consistency of these results obtained from the instrumented network approach depends on the validity of average examiner experience as an instrument for the industry’s citations to other patents (that is the basis of tie formation in our innovation network). Therefore, we provide a series of supporting exogeneity tests.

We first test whether the patent application portfolios of industries assigned to more and less experienced examiners are similar in terms of observable characteristics that predict citations to prior art at the time of filing. Following Sampat and Williams (2019) and Melero et al. (2020), we use the variables, patent family size and number of claims, that are listed in the patent application to predict citation count. ## 7.3. ICT-Closeness of Non-ICT Industries

To assess ICT-biased shifts in technological development, we examine whether there is an increase in the technological relatedness of non-ICT industries to the ICT industry, as measured by ICT-closeness, over time. Second, we assess the content of ICT-closeness by relating it to two attributes of the patent portfolio of the citing industry: (i) proportion of ICT technologies and (ii) complementarity between ICT and non-ICT technologies.

## 8. Robustness Checks

In this section, we present the results of several additional tests that confirm the robustness of our main findings. We provide details of each of these analyses in Sections B–F in the Online Appendix. We briefly summarize these here.

8.1. Alternate Methods of Endogeneity Correction In this section, we provide details of additional analyses conducted to complement the instrumented network approach and address concerns of endogeneity of tie formation. We discuss these additional analyses here.

8.1.1. 2SLS Model with Alternate Instruments. We test the robustness of our results and support for Hypotheses 1 and 2 to the use of a 2SLS model that uses an alternate instrumental variable. Specifically, following Rahmati et al. (2021), in the first-stage regression, we use ICT-closeness of direct neighbors of the focal industry as an instrumental variable for ICT-closeness of the focal industry. We then use the predicted value of ICT-closeness from the first stage as a regressor in a seemingly unrelated regression model to estimate the impact of ICT-closeness on the performance metrics of the industry. We find that these results are qualitatively similar to our main results, thereby establishing the robustness of our results. We provide more details of these analyses in Section C.1 in the Online Appendix.

8.1.2. Dynamic Panel Data Model. To control for the potential endogeneity caused due to reverse causality, we conduct additional analyses using a dynamic panel data model with generalized method of moments (GMM) estimator. Specifically, following prior research in the innovation literature (Liu et al. 2010, Bardhan et al. 2013), we estimate the relation between the ICTcloseness of an industry and its performance outcomes (innovation outcomes and competitive intensity) using the Arellano-Bover/Blundell-Bond system GMM estimator (Arellano and Bond 1991, Arellano and Bover 1995, Blundell and Bond 1998). We find that these results are also consistent with our main results, confirming the robustness of our findings. Section C.2 in the Online Appendix presents more details of these analyses.

8.1.3. Alternate Networks Using Examiner-Specified Citations Alone. In our main analyses, we use the instrumented network approach with examiner experience as an instrument. We additionally test the robustness of our results to endogeneity concerns in network formation by constructing the citation network using citations added by examiners alone in their reviews of the focal patents (see details of these analyses in the Online Appendix in Section C.3). The results of these analyses are similar to our main results, thereby establishing the robustness of our results to endogeneity concerns.

## 8.2. Alternate Industry Classification

Thus far, we identified the industry of a firm based on its four-digit SIC code, which in turn is used to classify industries as ICT and non-ICT. This classification may lead to mismeasurement of closeness to the ICT industry arising from ambiguity in the classification of technology companies with a sectoral focus. For instance, Amazon being an e-commerce platform is categorized as a retail company based on its SIC code (5961). However, one may argue it to be a technology company based on its venture into the cloud market. Therefore, we further validate our findings using an alternate textbased approach to classifying firms following Hoberg and Phillips (2016). We present more details of these analyses in Section D in the Online Appendix.

## 8.3. Alternate Measures of Key Variables

In this section, we demonstrate the robustness of our results using alternative measures of key variables used in our main analysis. Specifically, we conduct additional analysis with alternate measure of dependent variables, innovation outcomes and competitive intensity, and the main variable of interest: ICT-closeness. We discuss these analyses here.

8.3.1. Alternate Measures of Dependent Variables. In our main analysis, we define innovation efficiency as the ratio of number of patents per R&D capital, calculated as the five-year cumulative R&D expenditures assuming an annual depreciation rate of 20%. Given that all patents may not have the same purpose, in our additional analyses, we construct two alternative measures of innovation efficiency based on patent citations: (i) adjusted patent citations used in Hirshleifer et al. (2013) and (ii) number of forward citations scaled by R&D capital as in Zhong (2018). We also assess the robustness of results to the estimation of innovative efficiency as number of patents normalized by twoyear lagged R&D expenses instead of R&D capital. Furthermore, recent literature in IS (Ravichandran et al. 2017) measures innovation outcomes based on application year instead of grant year, as the application date helps in overcoming the issues arising from variation in time to grant the patent (Wadhwa and Kotha 2006). Hence, we estimate the impact of closeness of innovation outcomes, innovation efficiency, recombi nant indices, and business methods, by computing these measures basis the application year instead of grant year. We present these analyses in Section E in the Online Appendix.

In addition, we also test the robustness of our results for competitive intensity using alternate measures of cash flow spread and cash flow volatility. We define cash flow spread of an industry as the difference in performance between firms below the 25th percentile and those above the 75th percentile. As a robustness check, we compute cash flow spread based on the 20th and 80th percentiles and test the robustness of our results to these thresholds. In our main analysis, we compute cash flow volatility over a rolling window of three years. We perform additional analyses as robustness checks by computing volatility over a window of five years. Please refer to Section E.2 in the Online Appendix for more details.

8.3.2. Alternate Measure of Independent Variables. In our main analyses, we use the average of the shortest path of a given non-ICT industry to the set of ICT industries in computing ICT-closeness of the non-ICT industry. In our robustness checks, we validate our hypotheses by computing closeness based on the minimum of the shortest paths to the ICT industry. Section F in the Online Appendix presents details of these analyses.

The results for alternate measures of key variables are consistent with our main findings, thereby establishing the robustness of our main findings.

## 9. Discussion and Conclusion 9.1. Theoretical Contribution

Anecdotal evidence of the digitally biased shift in the content of innovation abounds in the form of electric vehicles and driverless cars in the automobile industry, jet engines that produce terabytes of data per flight to facilitate pre-emptive maintenance and repair, and the emergence of bioinformatics and computational biology as key tools in drug development. Yet, large-scale empirical evidence of the pervasiveness, magnitude, and nature of technological impact of the ICT industry is limited. Our study adopts a network perspective to document and demonstrate this widespread shift in innovation and its impacts. Specifically, our analyses lead to two important observations about innovation in modern industries. First, we find a significant increase over time in the relatedness of technologies created in an array of industries and fields to the ICT industry. Underlying the increase in technological relatedness of the non-ICT industries to the ICT industry are the significant increase in proportion of ICT patents of the industry and complementarity of non-ICT patents with ICT patents.

In addition, we find that closeness of a non-ICT industry to the ICT industries in the innovation network space positively relates to its innovation outcomes. Specifically, a unit standard deviation increase in closeness to the ICT industry yields a 10.2% improvement in innovative efficiency, 48.39% increase in recombinant reuse, 9.09% increase in recombinant creation, 2.7% increase in the average number of business method patents, and 1.3% increase in the average number of new product segments. More important, we find that digital technologies help reduce the trade-off between two critical recombinant capabilities, creation, and reuse.

Indeed, Carnabuci and Operti (2013, p. 1608) argue that “the factors that enhance one type of recombinant capability generally hinder the other type.” For example, they find that integrative mechanisms in knowledge creation that provide inventors opportunities for collaboration, reciprocal knowledge sharing, joint problem solving, and in turn, continual redefinition of tasks through interaction increase the firm’s ability to innovate through recombinant reuse but reduce recombinant creation. Conversely, the diversity of knowledge base across inventors increase recombinant creation but reduces reuse. The authors conclude that concurrently building both capabilities is challenging and that firms may decide to invest in either type of capability depending on their initial conditions: technological advantage, industry maturity, competition, and uncertainty, among others. Our results are parti cularly salient in the context of this finding. The nature of digital technologies, reprogrammability, homogenized data, and self-referential nature, facilitates a common language for codification and representation of problem structures and potential solutions, thereby easing the transfer of recombination related knowledge from inventors who possess it to those who need it while also increasing individuals’ ability to absorb such knowledge. Therefore, the likelihood of collaborative integration and reusing technologies is significantly improved. At the same time, digital technologies also enable broader and more flexible cause-effect under standings that lead to novel associations and linkages between technologies. Therefore, our study contributes to this stream of research by highlighting the increasingly critical role played by digital technologies in enhancing the capacity of a firm’s knowledge base for change.

We also find that an increase in closeness of a non-ICT industry to the ICT industries alters competitive dynamics of the industry. Specifically, a unit standard deviation increase in closeness to the ICT industry increases the industry concentration by 1.64%, cash flow spread by 11.11%, and cash flow volatility by 5% for the industry. These demonstrated impacts are consistent with the observation that digital innovations create a complex or Schumpeterian competitive environment (McAfee and Brynjolfsson 2008, Rahmati et al. 2021), where successful IT exploiters pull away to increase performance spread and industry concentration, successive competitors use IT to leapfrog one another over time to increase volatility, and thus, rivalry is fast-paced, intense, and dynamic.

## 9.2. Managerial Implications

Our research points to important implications for managers. First, we emphasize the systematic and pervasive shift in the content of technological change, notably the centrality of the ICT industry to technological innovation and development of new products and processes across a wide range of industries. Fostering talent, structures and processes that enable this shift emerge as key executive responsibilities in the digital economy.

Furthermore, a paradigm shift to ICT-intensive R&D implies a wider competitive landscape for firms in the digital economy. Specifically, IT companies are likely to transcend the boundaries of the ICT industry to emerge as formidable competitors with business models that represent discontinuous change for incumbents. For example, in the automobile industry, technology companies like Uber, Google, and Tesla are competing with traditional car manufacturers on multiple fronts and compelling the latter to reinvent their operating and business models. Similarly, in the pharmaceutical industry that engages in ICT-intensive R&D, technology companies such as Apple, IBM, and Qualcomm Technologies are exploiting shifts in patient engagement to extend sources of patient data beyond medical records to include apps, wearable devices, and online communities that yield critical analytical insights for clinical decision support. They represent a shift in business models from blockbuster medicine to personalized medicine. Industry incumbents can use their position in innovation networks to identify potential competitors and design responses thereof.

Finally, our measure of closeness to the ICT industry in the citation network provide policymakers with a new tool to measure extent of digitization of an industry, competitors that transcend traditional industry boundaries, and externalities from legislation that benefits conventionally defined IT industries.

## 9.3. Directions for Future Research

Our study can be extended in several ways. It is valuable to ask why, if the move toward incorporation of ICT technologies is associated with superior performance in all of these industries, what prevents certain industries from moving in that direction? Tushman and Anderson (1986) point out that radical technological shifts might be competence destroying in nature and incumbents need to acquire new knowledge and capabilities that are consistent with this shift (Tushman and Anderson 1986, Gatignon et al. 2002, Lavie 2006). However, extant resources, processes and values of the firm engender inertia and resistance to change with core competencies acting as core rigidities during such shifts (Leonard Barton 1992). Resource allocation processes in firms too may be biased toward addressing the needs of existing stakeholders (McElheran 2015, Nandkumar et al. 2018). The advancement of this work through a greater understanding of the micromechanisms that enable and constrain digital innovation represents an important direction for future research. It would also be invaluable to explore shifts to products enabled through more ICT-centric R&D such as greater personalization engagement, and data-driven product design. Future research could also examine shifts in competition in industries that engage in ICT-centric R&D and allied shifts in sources of competitive advantage. Such research provides improved understanding of systematic differences in competitive dynamics, mar ket valuations and productivity between industries that drive ICT-centric innovations and others.

## 9.4. Limitations

Our study is subject to certain limitations. First, our data are limited in time because they only include patents granted through 2010. We expect that the technological trends of mobility and connectivity observed across industries reflect an amplification of the shifts in innovation documented in this study. Empirical analyses of patents granted after 2010 will help us demonstrate the continuity, if not strengthening, of the centrality of the ICT industry in modern R&D in recent years. Second, our data are also limited to public firms in the United States. Prior research (Kerr and Lincoln 2010, Arora et al. 2013) has emphasized a sizeable human resource advantage of the United States in the technology domain, and therefore, it is unclear whether the ICT-centricity of R&D documented in this study is idiosyncratic to the United States. Future research could extend the current analyses to other countries to determine whether the findings of our study extend to other countries.

Finally, our sample is restricted to patentable innovations alone. However, as Griliches (1990) summarized, “not all inventions are patentable, not all inventions are patented, and the inventions that are patented differ greatly in … the magnitude of inventive output associated with them” (p. 1669). ICT technologies may not be patented for one of three reasons. First, given the significant costs of obtaining and enforcing the patent, firms typically tradeoff intellectual property (IP) protection offered by patents with other options such as trademarks, industrial designs, trade secret protection, etc. Second, certain ICT technologies, albeit valuable, may not be eligible for patent protection because of the difficulty involved in estimating the inventiveness of the patent over the existing state of the art. Software patents are especially susceptible to these challenges. Finally, the widespread and free use of ICT innovations, exemplified through open-source software, suggests that ICT technologies may exist and be used without any IP protection at all. Hence, we recognize that our patent data do not encompass all innovations. In turn, our estimates of the centrality of the ICT industry and the impact of the latter on innovation outcomes in using industries may be biased downward.

In addition, the innovation trajectory in the ICT industry may not be exogenous, and it is possible that the innovations can diffuse from non-ICT industries to the ICT industry. However, our closeness measure captures the non-ICT industries’ consumption of innovations produced by the ICT industry. In addition, we control for endogeneity concerns arising from omitted variables that may be correlated with ICT closeness and innovation performance of the industry. For this reason, we believe that our results may not be affected by the diffusion of innovations from the non-ICT industries to the ICT industry.

Despite these limitations, our study makes an important contribution in documenting and demonstrating significant impact of the technological relatedness to the ICT industry and the shifts engendered in the R&D function across a wide spectrum of industries. These shifts are blurring industry boundaries and engendering fundamental changes in competitive strategy. These emerging patterns further suggest that any changes to legislation and policy that impact innovation in the ICT industry, including proposed reforms in immigration laws, will impact the competitiveness of not just the ICT industry but rather a range of industries traditionally considered unrelated to the ICT industry.
