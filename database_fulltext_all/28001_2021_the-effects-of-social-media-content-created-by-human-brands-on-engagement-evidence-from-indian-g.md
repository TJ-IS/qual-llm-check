Individual celebrities or <sup>human</sup> <sup>brands</sup> in fields ranging from sports to art to <sup>Abstract.</sup>politics use social media platforms to connect and engage with their audience. We analyze the effects of content generated by human brands (referred to as <sup>human</sup> <sup>brand</sup> <sup>generated</sup> <sup>content</sup>) on a popular social media platform, Twitter, on audience engagement. The firs objective of this study is to examine the effects of positive and negative tone of human generated content on social media engagement. Next, we study the impact of popularity of human brands on the effect of tone on audience’s engagement. Building on the arguments of perception spillover effects, we also investigate the effects of content generated by related human brands on the engagement of the focal human brand. Set in the context of the 2014 Indian general election, we find that both positive-toned content and negativetoned content have a positive effect on social media engagement. We find that the popularity of a human brand negatively moderates the relationship between positive toned content and engagement. Our findings indicate interesting spillover effects of content created by related brands on the effectiveness of content created by a focal human brand. In particular, we find evidence for presence of the reinforcement effect, that is, the effect of negative tone on engagement is greater when the tone of related human brands is negative and the engagement is higher when the tones of both the focal human brand and related brands are positive. However, we find that the effect of negative-toned tweets of a focal human brand is lower when the tone of related human brands is positive. Our analyses account for endogeneity issues, correlated unobservables, and reflection problem. We supplement our core results with additional analyses that include alternative mode specifications, alternative samples, estimation strategies, and engagement metrics. We present implications of this study to practice and the literature on social media content.

## 1. Introduction

1. Introduction<sub>Human brands or personal brands refer to individ-</sub> uals who desire to market and promote themselves to their target audience.<sup>1</sup> <sup>Human brands</sup>, including politicians, chief executive officers (CEOs), top management executives, musicians, artists, and athletes, use social media to directly connect, communicate, and engage with their online fans and followers. Human brands are considered the most influential users on social media, and firms are increasingly trying to adopt human brands’ strategies to build their brands on social media (<sup>Wall Street Journal</sup> 2015). Some human brands have a larger social media following, in terms of number of followers, compared with traditional brands (i.e., firms and organizations). For example, although Coca-Cola has around 3.33 million followers on Twitter, Katy Perry and the former

President of the United States, Barack Obama, have more than 107 million followers on Twitter, and professional footballer Cristiano Ronaldo’s Facebook page has more than 120 million followers.<sup>2</sup>

The recent \$1 billion Nike deal with Cristiano Ronaldo highlights the economic value of a human brand on social media. Badenhausen (2017, p.1) argues that “Cristiano is one of the top influencers on the planet who has effectively leveraged his social following and engagement into a media powerhouse to drive tremendous value for his sponsor,” which suggests that the high brand valuation of Ronaldo is mainly attributed to his ability to generate engagement with his followers and his visibility on social media. Despite the vast popularity of human brands, research on the social media strategy of human brands is limited. Our study seeks to fill this gap.

Previous literature in the area of social media has studied the effects of social media content generated by individual users (commonly referred to as UGC <sub>or</sub> user-generated content<sub>)</sub> <sub>and</sub> <sub>by firms</sub> <sub>(FGC</sub> <sub>or fi</sub>rm-<sup>generated</sup> <sup>content</sup>). UGC refers to content created by individual users based on their interaction as consumers of firms (e.g., communication related to consumer complaints on social media), and between consumers (e.g., word of mouth, communication on forums, and review boards). FGC refers to firm-initiated marketing content on its official social media pages (Kumar et al. 2016). Customers create UGC to convey their opinion and connect with other customers online, and firms rely on FGC to engage with their customers and promote their products. Similar to firms and brands, human brands are increasingly using social media as a platform to communicate and develop a <sup>one-on-one</sup> relationship with their fans and followers.

We refer to human brand generated content (HGC) as communication and content created by human brands on their social media pages that help them to directly engage with fans, followers, and other social media users. As human brands attempt to build their image online, HGC allows them to actively participate on social media and engage with their followers, which strengthens the connection between human brands and their followers. An important characteristic of HGC is that it facilitates followers or fans to develop a strong interpersonal emotional attachment with human brands (Thomson 2006), and the relationship between human brands and their followers is that of fandom, idolatry, stardom, and worship (Saboo et al. 2016). Furthermore, unlike firms, human brands are independent and can often post provocative content in social media with varying tone, which can elicit a differential response from their fans and followers. Although human brands increasingly (and successfully) use social media to engage with their followers (<sup>Wall</sup> <sup>Street Journal</sup> 2015), little is known about the effects of tone of HGC. Our research seeks to understand the effect of the tone of HGC on online audience engagement.

## 1.1. Research Questions

More formally, the first research question that we <sub>examine is (1)</sub> What is the impact of the tone of HGC on social media audience engagement? <sub>First, we analyze the</sub> impact of positive tone of HGC on social media engagement. On the one hand, one could argue that the audience is more likely to engage with positive-toned HGC as it reflects the positive attitude of the person engaging with the article. On the other hand, based on the work of Fiske (1980), one can make a competing argument that positive content can be uninteresting and may not entice the curiosity of the audience. Thus, the audience may not engage with positivetoned HGC. Next, we examine the effect of negativetoned HGC on social media engagement. Previous literature in psychology suggests that negative-toned messages are likely to be perceived as engaging and may evoke more interest (Fiske 1980). Thus, one can argue that the social media audience is more likely to interact or engage with a human brand’s negativetoned tweets. However, the negative-toned content may not resonate with a human brand’s fans and followers as the audience may not want to be associated with negative-toned content for fear of unintended backlash. Against this background, we seek to answer the first research question: Is the audience likely to engage more with positive-toned content or with negative-toned content? Whereas the focus of the past literature has been on analyzing the effects of tone of content generated by users and firms (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006, Dellarocas et al. 2007, Aggarwal et al. 2012), our contribution lies in understanding the effects of the tone of the content generated by the human brands.

Human brands with a large number of followers will likely have higher engagement levels and thus the effect of the tone of HGC on the audience may get amplified depending on the brand’s popularity. Given that the audience has different expectations from a popular human brand compared with a less popular human brand, understanding the interplay between popularity and HGC tone becomes important as human brands pursue strategies to increase their engagement online. We argue that the audience engages differently with content created by popular human brands in contrast to less popular human brands. Although prior literature has examined the impact of negative publicity on a brand’s popularity, the impact of social media popularity on the relationship between HGC tone and engagement is not clear. Therefore, our second research question is (2) <sup>Does</sup> social media popularity moderate the relationship between the content tone (positive and negative) and engagement? Analyzing this question will provide important insights on the interplay among human brand popularity, HGC tone, and engagement, and the differen strategies that human brands can adopt with respect to creating content with a certain tone. In addition, our findings will contribute to the literature on the role of brand popularity on social media engagement.

A unique aspect of human brands is that they often belong to a group or a community. For instance, athletes and politicians are associated with teams and political parties, respectively. We define related brands as individual brands belonging to the same umbrella brand. Literature in psychology suggests that when forming an opinion about a person or a focal brand, the audience may look for traits in common between the focal brand and other related or similar brands (Bazerman and Moore 2008). In a similar vein, if audience were to perceive some human brands as related to each other, we argue that the tone of HGC of related brands can moderate the relationship between the HGC and engagement of a focal human brand. We refer to this phenomenon as <sup>perception</sup> <sup>spillover</sup> <sup>effects</sup> between brands. Although spillover effects have been documented in the context of traditional brands in the marketing literature (Janakiraman et al. 2009), no study, to the best of our knowledge, has examined the role of perception spillover effects related to HGC tone on social media platforms. Our findings on spillover effects provide important implications for the role of content created by related brands on the engagement of social media content created by a focal brand. Accordingly, the third key <sub>research question is (3)</sub> What are the spillover effects of the tone of related human brands on a focal human brand<sub>’</sub>s engagement level? In particular, does the tone of related brands<sub>’</sub> HGC moderate the relationship between focal human brand<sub>’</sub>s HGC and its engagement levels on social <sup>media</sup> <sup>platforms?</sup> By providing an empirically driven answer to the third research question, our study sheds light on perception spillovers among human brands.

## 1.2. Research Context

<sup>1.2. Research Context</sup>To empirically answer our research questions, we seek to identify a research setting with the following characteristics. First, we require a context where human brands actively use social media platforms to communicate their ideas and messages to the target audience to increase their engagement levels. Second, we need a context where human brands are likely to use both positive- and negative-toned content on social media to engage with their audience. Third, we require a context where human brands belong to a group and the tone of HGC of human brands connected to a focal human brand is likely to affect the engagement of the focal human brands. Finally, because we are investigating the effect of popularity, we need human brands with varying levels of popularity among the social media audience.

To accomplish our objectives, we leverage a novel data set in the context of the Indian general election of 2014. The context of elections in which individual political candidates serve as human brands and political parties are umbrella brands satisfies the previous critical requirements and aids us in examining our research questions. Although brands are typically associated with products and organizations, the notion of branding is popular in political science (Smith and French 2009). In recent years, like corporate brands, politicians and political parties have increasingly embraced social media platforms to promote their brands and to engage with their target audience or constituents (Murse 2019). Several popular news outlets and industry studies have also emphasized the role of social media in election campaigns. For example, a recent report found that 100% of U.S. senators and 99% of members of the House of Representatives use Twitter to engage and communicate with their constituents.<sup>3</sup> Furthermore, 66% of online users have used social media to engage in political activities (Duggan and Smith 2016). In addition, Pew Research Center’s report has shown that about 44% of adult Americans relied on social media to gather new information about the 2016 U.S. presidential elections.<sup>4</sup> The trend of widespread use of social media during elections is prevalent across the world. For instance, our context, the 2014 Indian general election was famously <sub>labeled</sub> <sub>as</sub> India<sub>’</sub>s <sub>fi</sub>rst social media election <sub>(Khullar</sub> <sub>and</sub> Haridasani 2014) because of widespread use of social media by the political candidates.

Political candidates post both positive-toned content to promote their manifesto, agenda, and ideas on various topics to their constituents and negativetoned content to attack the reputation of their opponents and policies offered by their opponents. Furthermore, political candidates, in the case of the parliamentary system, are not only concerned about their own victory but also need to make sure that they do not hurt other members of their political party (or related candidates/related brands). This is because political candidates have an incentive to ensure that the related candidates are also elected. In the case of the parliamentary system that is followed in India, where the elected members determine the Prime Minister, it is likely that social media content of candidates who belong to the same political party as that of a focal candidate affects how the audience reacts to the social media content posted by the focal candidate. Furthermore, in the context of politics and politicians, we have human brands with varying levels of popularity on social media, which allows us to analyze the impact of popularity on engagement. These characteristics of political candidates and elections provide us with an ideal empirical setting to examine our research questions.

Set in the context of Indian general election held in 2014, we assemble a novel data set collected from a popular social media platform (Twitter.com) and the Election Commission of India (ECI). Our data consist of detailed information on each political candidate’s tweets, number of retweets (i.e., shares), and the popularity of each candidate on Twitter. We combine this information with other offline characteristics of political candidates and their respective political parties obtained from ECI. We use the Na¨ıve Bayes sentiment classification algorithm (Antweiler and Frank 2004) to categorize the tone of candidates tweets into positive tone and negative tone. In our context of social media platform, we face the issue of endogeneity of HGC tone and social interaction effects.

To account for the endogeneity and social interaction effects, we follow the prescriptions from recent literature on social networks (Kumar et al. 2018, Park et al. 2018) and propose a control function approach. In addition, we perform a battery of robustness checks to rule out alternative explanations for our results. Specifically, we supplement our main analysis with different model specifications, estimation strategies, and metrics for measuring engagement levels.

## 1.3. Key Findings and Contributions

<sup>1.3. Key Findings and Contributions</sup>We report the following set of results. First, we find that positive- and negative-toned HGC by human brands/candidates are associated with higher levels of engagement as measured by number of retweets. We find that the effect of positive tone on engagement reduces as popularity of human brands increases. We also find evidence of reinforcement effect between HGC created by brands related to a focal brand and the focal brand’s HGC. In particular, we find that the positive effect of negative-toned HGC is greater when the tone of related candidates is negative and the positive effect of positive-toned HGC is greater when the tone of related brands is positive. We find that the effect of negative-toned HGC is lower when the tone of related brands is positive.

Our work makes several important contributions to the academic literature and practice. Building on prior studies that have examined the effects of tone of social media content (Kumar et al. 2016), we extend the literature by examining the tone of HGC. Whereas the focus of most literature has been on the content generated by consumers and firms, our contribution lies in understanding the effects of tone of social media content created by human brands. By demonstrating that the engagement levels are higher when the tone of HGC is positive and negative, our study contributes to the current literature on the role of tone of social media. We also show that positive tone may not always be effective as we find evidence for negative moderating effect of popularity on the relationship between positive-toned HGC and engagement. Understanding how HGC affects engagement by considering the moderating effects of tone of related brands and popularity differentiates our research from other studies that examine the effect of social media content and helps us advance our knowledge of the effects of social media content in general. Furthermore, our research is among the first to establish the spillover effects of social media content in the context of human brands and social media. In particular, we find a statistically significant reinforcement effect, which advocates the need for a coordinated social media strategy between the focal human brands and related brands.

From the practice perspective, we demonstrate the importance of a comprehensive social media strategy that takes into account the tone of social media content, popularity of human brands, and the spillover effects from related brands. Although the current study is in the context of human brands from the political domain, our findings may be applicable to human brands in the corporate world. Top management executives of businesses are also under constant pressure to engage with the social media audience in an effort to influence the perception of their busi nesses (Saboo et al. 2016). Industry studies suggest that top management executives who engage on social media are found to be better equipped than their peers who are not active on social media in mitigating risks, enhancing the credibility of the business, and handling crisis (BrandFrog.com 2016). Our findings provide important insights to executives on strategies to improve their social media engagement.

## 2. Conceptual Framework

2. Conceptual Framework<sub>Given the context of social media content and our</sub> focus on behavioral effects related to the tone of content, the role of brands, and content sharing, we draw on the diverse and rich literature of impression management (Fiske 1980) and the spillover affects across brands (Janakiraman et al. 2009) to build our framework.

2.1. Social Media Content, Tone, and Engagement <sup>2.1. Social Media Content, Tone, and Engagement</sup>Engagement refers to “customer’s behavioral manifestation towards a brand” (Van Doorn et al. 2010, p. 254). In the context of social media, engagement in the form of sharing or retweeting (on Facebook and Twitter platforms, respectively) captures the interactions between a brand and its followers that allow the brand to build a personal one-on-one relationship with its fans and followers. Users can demonstrate their affinity to human brands by liking, sharing, or replying to their social media posts, thereby creating a more meaningful connection between the human brands and their followers. Among other measures, brand marketers acknowledge that content sharing is the most appropriate measure of audience engagement on social media as each share facilitates transmission of the brand’s content to a new audience, which amplifies the impact of the original content (Long 2015). Furthermore, content sharing (or retweeting) can be viewed as a form of endorsement from the audience (Oestreicher-Singer and Zalmanson 2013, Rishika and Ramaprasad 2019). Consequently, consistent with more recent studies (such as Lambrecht et al. 2018) and practice, we operationalize engagement by the number of retweets. As part of the robustness checks, we use other measures such as the number of replies and number of mentions to operationalize engagement. 5

Researchers in psychology and marketing have long been interested in understanding why the audience engages or shares information (e.g., a news article or advertisement content) with others (Berger and Milkman 2012). Research in the area of content sharing suggests that the emotional aspect of content is a critical driving force of content sharing (Heath et al. 2001) and that users within a social network are more likely to share emotional content as it deepens their social bonding (Parkinson et al. 2005). We thus propose that the tone of social media content created by human brands can play a vital role in determining audience engagement. In our context of political candidates creating social media content, tone of the content can drive emotions of fans and followers and their engagement with the content. For instance, expression of disappointment or anger against political opposition in the form of a negative-toned message or a more positive-toned content that captures a politician’s enthusiasm can drive engagement among the politician’s followers and fans in his or her social media platform. Analyzing the differential effects of positive versus negative tone on sharing is important (Godes et al. 2005), and our study extends the literature on transmission of social media content by analyzing the effects of positive- and negative-toned social media content created by human brands on engagement.

Positive-toned social media content, in the context of elections, serves to communicate the achievement, policies, and plans of a political candidate and his/ her political party to the online audience in addition to emphasizing the strengths of the candidate. Cialdini (1984) suggests that people who express positive emotion are perceived more positively and hence considered more likable than people who express negative mood. Furthermore, Berger and Milkman (2012) argue that most individuals want to be known as someone who endorses optimistic, positive messages or messages from likable people rather than someone who shares pessimistic negative information. Thus, we expect that the audience is likely to engage more with positive-toned content of political candidates by sharing the content with their friends on social media.

Negative-toned content is common in politics and is used to attack opponents by highlighting their weaknesses. The impression formation literature in psychology, which studies how consumers process and integrate information, explains one of the rationales behind the effect of negative marketing communication. Within the impression formation literature, the novelty theory proposed by Fiske (1980) argues that negative information is novel and highly revealing as it distinguishes itself from other more common information and thus requires greater attention. As a result, individuals pay more attention to negative information and value it more compared with positive information (Hamilton and Zanna 1972, Baumeister et al. 2001). Furthermore, negative information is deemed to be more diagnostic and hence likely to be more contagious (Maheswaran and Meyers-Levy 1990, Rozin and Royzman 2001). Prior literature in political communications has examined the role of negative advertisements and negative campaigns on different performance measures, and the consensus is that negative ad campaigns resonate with audience (Soroka and McAdams 2015). Furthermore, metaanalysis–based findings suggest that both positiveand negative-toned ad campaigns are effective, and there is no evidence of detrimental effects of negativetoned content (Lau et al. 1999). Based on these arguments, we argue that a human brand’s negative-toned HGC will evoke the interest of the online community, and therefore online users will engage more with negative-toned HGC. In summary, we argue that both positive- and negative-toned HGC can have a positive effect on audience engagement on social media platforms.

## 2.2. Moderating Role of Popularity

<sup>2.2. Moderating Role of Popularity</sup>In the previous section, we argued for the effects of positive- and negative-toned HGC on audience engagement. We now posit that the level of popularity of human brands (operationalized by the number of followers on Twitter) can influence how tone affects audience engagement. More specifically, we argue that the positive- and negative-toned content could have a larger impact on engagement for less popular human brands compared with human brands that are more popular. The rationale behind this postulation is that positive- and negative-toned content will likely draw more attention to less-known brands compared with well-known brands.

Previous literature in psychology and marketing has argued and shown that publicity through emotional content will more likely have a greater positive effect for products with lower existing product awareness compared with products with higher awareness (Reinstein and Snyder 2005, Berger et al. 2010). This is because publicity through emotional content can stimulate greater awareness for less-known brands compared with well-known brands. Well-known brands, given their fame, already have high levels of awareness among the audience to start with, and hence advertising may not necessarily further increase their brand awareness (Kent and Allen 1994). Furthermore, literature suggests that publicity derived from negativetoned content is shown to be more effective when used by less-known brands because it invokes greater brand attitude than when used to promote a well-known brand (Iyer 1988, James and Hensel 1991, Chattopadhyay 1998). Similarly, in the context of our study, we posit that positive- and negative-toned HGC, which are deemed to be more interesting and diagnostic, will likely increase the awareness of less-known human brands among a social media audience. In contrast, compared with less-known candidates, audience is more likely to be already familiar with the popular candidates, and therefore the popular candidate’s positive- or negative-toned content will not necessarily amplify the candidate’s awareness (or the increase in awareness will be marginal) among the audience. Thus, when less-known candidates post a positive- or negative-toned content, the candidates will likely attract the attention from the audience who may pay more attention to the content, which wil increase the likelihood of audience engaging with the content. On the other hand, as the audience is likely to be more familiar with popular human brands, positive- and negative-toned HGC may not necessarily help the popular candidates stand out. Based on this reasoning, we expect a more pronounced effect of HGC tone on engagement for less popular human brands, as positive- or negative-toned HGC will draw the attention of a new audience, thereby increasing awareness and engagement of less popular human brands. Therefore, we expect a higher marginal effect of the tone of content on engagement for less popular human brands compared with recognized human brands.

## 2.3. Perception Spillover Effect: Focal Brands and Perception Spil

<sup>Related Brands</sup>Prior studies in the context of corporate or product brands have shown that information and perception about related or even competing brands can influence the beliefs and opinions of a focal brand (Roehm and Tybout 2006, Janakiraman et al. 2009). Analogous to umbrella corporate brands, political parties represent an umbrella organization with individual members of a political party serving as individual brands (Singer 2002). We argue that the perceptions of members related to the same political party as the focal candidate (i.e., related brands) can influence how the audience responds to the focal candidate’s social media content. In the context of our study, we postulate that the audience will process all the available information that they obtain from the focal candidate and related brands, before making a decision to engage with the focal candidate. The associative network model argues that information about brands and their characteristics exist in customers’ knowledge network as network nodes and network linkages between such nodes facilitate memory recall (Collins and Loftus 1975, Anderson 1983, Janakiraman et al. 2009). When a user views a tweet from a focal candidate, the node of the foca candidate and all the associated nodes (i.e., related brands) are activated as well, leading to retrieval of all relevant information in the user’s memory. As a result, the user is likely to evaluate the HGC of the focal and related brands when making a choice to engage with the social media content of a focal human brand.

We propose that the tone of related brands will moderate the relationship between the focal brand’s tone and audience engagement, which we refer to as perception spillover effects within human brands. Given that the tone of a focal brand’s and the related brands’ HGC can be positive and negative, we have four possible moderating effects: (i) the positive tone of related brands moderating the positive tone of the focal brand, (ii) the negative tone of related brands moderating the negative tone of the focal brand, (iii) the positive tone of related brands moderating the negative tone of the focal brand, and (iv) the negative tone of related brands moderating the positive tone of the focal brand.

The multiple source effect proposed by Harkins and Petty (1981) suggests that when the audience is exposed to a similar information from multiple sources, they are more likely to process the information, and thus a consensus is formed as the opinion is corroborated by multiple information sources. More specifically, they argue that information from multiple sources can lead to a conclusion that the information can be trusted Furthermore, prior studies have argued that consensus in opinion is associated with higher persuasiveness (West and Broniarczyk 1998, Yan and Tan 2017). In our context, when both a focal candidate and other members of his/her political party use a similar toned content (e.g., all the members of a political party use similar negative-toned content to attack the policies of opposing political party or use positive-toned content to highlight their policies and accomplishments), a consensus is likely to be formed. We posit that the audience will perceive this information to be trustworthy, which results in a higher likelihood of the audience engaging with the content of a focal brand. We refer to this as the <sup>reinforcement effect</sup>,<sup>6</sup> whereby the engagement levels are likely to be greater when the tones of the focal brand and the related brands are similar. In a similar vein, we expect increased engagement levels when the tones of both the focal and the related brands are negative. To summarize, we argue that the engagement levels will be greater when the tones of both the focal and the related brands are either positive or negative.

In contrast, when the tones of focal human brands and the related brands are different, we argue that the opposite toned messages may work against each other. The opposite tones of focal and related brands can lead to a lack of credibility of their content. A differing set of tones of HGC of the focal and related brands can also result in crowding out and lower validation of information from multiple sources. As a result, we expect the engagement levels to be lower when the tones of the focal brand and related brands contrast with each other. Specifically, we posit that engagement levels will lessen when (i) the tone of related brands is positive and the tone of focal brand is negative or (ii) the tone of related brands is negative and the tone of focal brand is positive. ## 3. Data

3. Data<sub>The</sub> <sub>context</sub> <sub>of</sub> <sub>our</sub> <sub>study</sub> <sub>is</sub> <sub>the</sub> <sub>Indian</sub> <sub>general</sub> <sub>election</sub> held in 2014. In Section 1.2, we argued for the suitability of the context of 2014 general election in India for our study. India is the largest democracy in the world (D’Ambrogio 2014), and elections in India are widely followed around the world, because the results have a widespread impact on global economy (Agrawal 2017). India follows a parliamentary form of government where the parliament is the supreme legislative body comprising two houses: Rajya Sabha (or the Upper House) and Lok Sabha (or the Lower House). In the parliamentary structure, citizens from a predefined geographical region (or a constituency) elect one of many politicians representing multiple political parties<sup>7</sup> from that region. The elected member, called a Member of Parliament (MP), represents the people of his or her constituency in the Lok Sabha. The members of the Lok Sabha in turn elect the

Prime Minister (PM) of India. The PM is generally a member of the majority political party in the Lok Sabha. The MPs not only elect the PM of India but also act as representatives of the people of the constituency. Lok Sabha consists of 543 elected representatives, and elections are held every five years. The 2014 general election in India was held from April 7, 2014 to May 12, 2014. Parvatiyar (2014) notes that that the campaign style of a top political party had several similarities to U.S.-style election campaigns, including engagement through political discourse and creating political awareness of their policies. As is the case with U.S. elections, several political parties and politicians in India have taken advantage of the enormous size of various social media platforms to reach out to their target constituents.

Our main data come from the popular microblogging website, Twitter, and consist of individual candidate level tweets related to the 2014 Indian general election. We collected tweets using various keywords, such as the names of political parties, politicians, and other terms related to the Indian elections from December 1, 2013 to May 31, 2014 (i.e., a time period that spans before, during, and after elections). Our data contain individual tweets posted by political candidates (i.e., human brands), prominent political party members, and the official Twitter accounts of political parties (i.e., related brands). We collect the content of tweets generated by 297 Twitter accounts belonging to the candidates, important political party members (who are not running in the elections), and political parties. ## 3.1. Dependent Variable: Number of Retweets

<sup>3.1. Dependent Variable: Number of Retweets</sup>The unit of our analysis is at the candidate-weekly level. The dependent variable in our study is the number of retweets (denoted by $R T _ { i t } )$ received in week <sup>t</sup> by candidate <sup>i</sup>. When a human brand posts a message on Twitter, the message appears on the timeline of the brand’s followers. When the followers log in to access their Twitter account, they see the recent tweets by the human brands they follow. However, if the followers are not very active on Twitter (e.g., they open Twitter once a month), they will generally see only the most recent tweets. Furthermore, evidence from prior studies suggests that engagement decays to zero in a few days (Lee et al. 2018). Accordingly, we assume that followers may engage with only recent tweets made by a human brand (hence the unit of analysis is at the weekly level). We identify the number of retweets for each of the 11,109 original tweets posted by 63 candidates running in the elections.

## 3.2. Main Independent Variables

In addition to classifying the tweets into positive tone and negative tone, the sentiment classification algorithm categorizes tweets into mixed or neutral tone. Although a randomized experiment is the most robust way to establish a causal effect of HGC tone on engagement, such an approach is not feasible with observational data in a context such as ours. We identify and address multiple potential challenges in estimating unbiased effect of HGC tone on social media engagement using observational data. Because our primary goal is to analyze how the audience engages with the content generated by human brands, one challenge we face is differentiating the audience engagement effect from social interaction effects. Social interaction effects exist when a user directly influences the choices/outcomes of other users in a social network (Hartmann et al. 2008). In our context, a follower’s decision to engage with the content created by a human brand could depend on the actions of other followers of the focal human brand or the focal political party. Likewise, a human brand’s decision to tweet with a specific tone may depend on other unobserved factors. Prior literature has identified two primary confounding factors that could hinder <sup>uncov-</sup> <sup>ering causal effects</sup> accurately in social networks: (i) the reflection problem and (ii) correlated unobservables (Manski 1993, Rishika and Ramaprasad 2019). In addition to these two confounding factors, we need to consider the possible endogeneity of the HGC tone. Before presenting our model and estimation methods, we discuss how we overcome these identification challenges.

## 4.1. Reflection Problem

<sup>4.1. Re ection Problem</sup>The first problem that often arises when dealing with observational data is the issue of simultaneity or what is referred to as the reflection problem in social networks literature (Manski 1993). This problem arises when individuals belonging to the same group affect each other’s behavior simultaneously. For example, in our context, number of party retweets and party level mentions could simultaneously influence the number of retweets of the focal candidates. In other words, audience behavior is not fixed, but varies depending on the behavior of other group members. To alleviate this concern, we use lagged values for number of replies, mentions, party mentions, and party retweets. This method of using lagged values is often used in the social networks literature to overcome the concern of simultaneity (Hartmann et al. 2008, Park et al. 2018). Hence, in our model, we use lagged values for the following variables: <sup>Replies</sup>, Mentions<sub>,</sub> PartyMentions<sub>, and</sub> PartyRetweets<sub>.</sub>

## 4.2. Correlated Unobservables

<sup>4.2. Correlated Unobservables</sup>The second challenge associated with proper identification of HGC effects is the issue of correlated unobservables. This problem arises when the users behavior is influenced by unobserved characteristics that may be correlated (Durlauf and Young 2004). In the context of our study, major political events in the form of large political meetings, release of manifesto, and major news related to elections could influence the decision of the audience to engage with the human brand. If ignored, this increase in engagement levels may be mistaken for the actual effect of HGC tone on engagement. To account for this effect, we include <sup>time-period</sup> fi<sup>xed</sup> <sup>effects</sup> by including week dummies to account for any unobserved exogenous time specific shocks (and to capture important events) such as announcement of election notification, dates, and enforcement of election poll conduct. In line with existing literature (Ghose and Han 2011), we also account for location fixed effects to control for any region specific effects (through region-specific dummy variables), allowing us to control for time invariant spatially correlated unobservables.

## 4.3 Endogeneity of HGC Tone

<sup>4.3 Endogeneity of HGC Tone</sup>Despite controlling for various candidate-level and party-level factors, the actual decision by the human brand to either post a positive-toned tweet or a negativetoned tweet is not completely observed in the data set. These unobserved factors may be correlated with the error term in Equation (1), which could result in endogeneity of positive and negative tone of HGC (i.e., <sup>PosT</sup> and <sup>NegT</sup>). To address these endogeneity concerns, we use the control function approach, also referred to as the Heckman two-step process (Cameron and Trivedi 2005, Wooldridge 2015, Kumar et al. 2018).<sup>18</sup>

In the control function approach (hereafter, also referred to as the CF approach), control function variables are added to the main regression model (in Equation (1)) to account for endogeneity (Wooldridge 2015). The control function variables are generated via instrument variables that explain the unobserved variation in the endogenous variables. Instrumental variables should satisfy two conditions. First, the instruments should be relevant and explain variation of the endogenous variables (i.e., <sup>PosT</sup> and <sup>NegT</sup>). Second, the instruments should not directly affect the outcome in the main model (i.e., RT) other than through <sup>PosT</sup> and <sup>NegT</sup>; that is, the instruments should be unrelated to the errors of the main model $( \mathrm { i . e . , } e _ { i t } )$

In the context of elections, when creating content, the focal brand is likely to post tweets as a reply to the rhetoric of his/her competitors. We thus argue that a human brand’s social media behavior may depend on the content created by his/her competitors. In particular, we posit that the tone of candidates belonging to competing political parties (or opposition parties) in the previous period could explain the process of how a focal candidate selects his/her HGC tone. For example, if the competing human brands are attacking the policies of a focal candidate on social media (i.e., the tone is negative), the focal human brand is likely to respond back with appropriate rhetoric (i.e., in a negative tone to attack the competitor in return or a positive tone to counter the competitor’s argument) on social media. This implies that the tone of competing human brands could likely explain the variation of the tone of the focal human brand.

Our argument is in line with Germann et al. (2015), who propose that the actions of competing firms could explain the variation, at least in part, in the endogenous actions of a focal firm. They argue that using the actions of competing firms as an instrumental variable for the actions of a focal firm is relevant because of similar market expectations of both the focal firm and competing firms. Therefore, we use the positive and negative tones of a focal candidate’s competitors in the previous period as our instrumental variables. We further validate our argument by examining the first-stage results, which provide evidence of a statistically significant association between the tones of human brands and their competitors’ tones (See Table A1 in the online appendix).

The tone of human brands from opposition parties also meets the exclusion restriction; that is, the instruments should not be correlated to the errors (or the omitted variables) in the main model. We believe that the content created in the <sup>previous</sup> period by the opposition brands is unlikely to directly affect how the audience engages with the focal brand’s tone. Although not a formal test, following Acemoglu et al. (2001) and Kumar et al. (2018), we check the statistical relationship between our instruments (i.e., OppPosT <sub>and</sub> $O p p N e g T )$ by including them in our main model $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ Equation (1)). We find that the coefficients of the instruments (tone of the opposition candidates) that we use are not statistically significant after controlling for the tone of the focal candidates. This suggests that there is no evidence for a direct relationship between the instruments and the focal candidate’s engagement, likely because the instruments affect the focal candidate’s engagement only through the tone of focal candidates and therefore the tone of opposition candidates is insignificant in Equation (1).

To summarize, we argued that the tones of opposing brands’ content created in the previous time period are valid instruments. $$
\begin{array}{r l} N e g T _ {i t} \sim P o i s s o n (\phi_ {i t}), \phi_ {i t} & = \exp (\pi_ {2 0} + \pi_ {2 1} O p p N e g T _ {i t - 1} \\ & \quad + \pi_ {2 2} O p p P o s T _ {i t - 1} + \Psi_ {2} Z _ {i t} \\ & \quad + v _ {2 i t}). \end{array} \tag {3}
$$

The predicted residuals $( \hat { v } _ { 1 i t } a n d \hat { v } _ { 2 i t } )$ that we obtain from Equations (2) and(3) effectively serve as control functions to alleviate endogeneity concerns. After correcting for endogeneity and addressing the concerns related to correlated unobservables and simultaneity, the full model is as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & R T _ {i t} \sim P o i s s o n (\mu_ {i t}), \\ & \mu_ {i t} = \exp \bigl (\beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} P o s T _ {i t} + \beta_ {2} N e g T _ {i t} \\ & \qquad + \beta_ {3} M i x T _ {i t} + \beta_ {4} P a r t y P o s T _ {i t} \\ & \qquad + \beta_ {5} P a r t y N e g T _ {i t} + \beta_ {6} P a r t y M i x T _ {i t} \\ & \qquad + \beta_ {7} P a r t y P o s T _ {i t} \times P o s T _ {i t} \\ & \qquad + \beta_ {8} P a r t y N e g T _ {i t} \times P o s T _ {i t} \\ & \qquad + \beta_ {9} P a r t y P o s T _ {i t} \times N e g T _ {i t} \\ & \qquad + \beta_ {1 0} P a r t y N e g T _ {i t} \times N e g T _ {i t} \\ & \qquad + \beta_ {9} P a r t y P o s T _ {i t} \times N e g T _ {i t} \\ & \qquad + \beta_ {1 0} P a r t y N e g T _ {i t} \times N e g T _ {i t} \\ & \qquad + \beta_ {1 1} f o l _ {i t} + \beta_ {1 2} P o s T _ {i t} \times f o l _ {i t} \\ & \qquad + \beta_ {1 3} N e g T _ {i t} \times f o l _ {i t} + \mathbb {Z C} _ {i t} \\ & \qquad + \rho_ {1 1 i t} \hat {v} _ {1 i t} + \rho_ {2} \hat {v} _ {2 i t} + \xi_ {1 i t} + \xi_ {2 i t}), \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

where $\hat { v } _ { 1 i t }$ and $\hat { v } _ { 2 i t }$ are control functions for correcting endogeneity concerns. We follow a two-step procedure prescribed by Terza et al. (2008) and Kumar et al. (2018) to first estimate Equations (2) and (3) to generate control functions required to estimate Equation (4). To summarize, we addressed the challenges related to endogenous explanatory variables, correlated unobservables, and simultaneity associated with establishing the effects of content tone on engagement by relying on the solutions established in the social networks and panel data econometrics literature. In the next section, we discuss the results of our proposed model.

## 7.1. Additional Instruments

<sup>7.1. Additional Instruments</sup>The first concern relates to using appropriate instruments to generate control functions to correct the endogeneity of <sup>PosT</sup> and <sup>NegT</sup>. In the main analysis, we argued and used the lagged number of positiveand negative-toned tweets of competitors’ tweets $( \mathrm { i . e . , } O p \bar { p } P o s T _ { i t - }$ and $O p p N e g T _ { i t - 1 . }$ , respectively) as the instruments for the number of positive-toned tweets of focal candidate $( \mathrm { i . e . , } P o s T _ { i t } )$ . For the first alternative set of instruments, we use $O p p P o s T _ { i t - 1 }$ as the only instrument for $P o s T _ { i t }$ (rather than using both $O p p P o s T _ { i t - 1 }$ and $O p p N e g T _ { i t - 1 }$ as instrumental variables) and <sup>Opp</sup> $N e g T _ { i t - 1 }$ as the only instrument for $N e g T _ { i t }$ . For the second set of alternative instruments, in addition to using $O p p P o s T _ { i t - 1 }$ and $O p p N e g T _ { i t - 1 }$ as instruments for both the tones of focal candidate, we also use the lagged number of mixed tone $( \mathrm { i . e . , } O p p M i x T _ { i t - 1 } )$ of the competing human brands as an additional instrument (results of this model are presented in column 2 of Table A2 in the online appendix). ## 7.2. Accounting for Heterogeneity at Party and Accounting for

Candidate Level <sub>fi In the</sub> <sup>7.2.1. Correlated Random Coef cients Model.</sup>main model, we addressed the issue of endogenous explanatory variables taking a control function approach. However, one could argue that candidates and their related brands who belong to the same party tend to be similar to each other. Hence, the impact of tone varies across candidates depending on the party to which they belong. A random coefficients model can be used to account for heterogeneity at party level when there are no concerns for endogeneity. However, to account for heterogeneity at party level (random coefficients across party) in addition to the endogeneity of tone of the candidates, Heckman and Vytlacil (1998) propose a correlated random coefficients (CRCs) estimation approach. Following Heckman and Vytlacil (1998), to allow for random coefficients, we add the following interaction terms $\hat { v } _ { 1 i t } \times P o s T _ { i t }$ and $\hat { v } _ { 2 i t } \times N e g T _ { i t }$ to Equation (4). These interaction terms control for the random slopes of $P o s T _ { i t }$ and $N e g T _ { i t } .$ . The estimates are presented in column 1 of Table A3 (in the online appendix). The results from the CRC model (in terms of direction and significance) are similar to the findings in our main model.

## 7.4. Panel Internal Instruments Model

<sup>7.4. Panel Internal Instruments Model</sup>In the main model, we used a control function approach using instruments to account for the endogeneity of the content tone. An alternative way to account for endogeneity is to rely on internal instruments instead of external instruments (Germann et al. 2015). Specifically, we supplement our results obtained by the CF approach with a dynamic panel model proposed by Blundell and Bond (1998). In the dynamic panel model, the lag terms of dependent variable and endogenous explanatory variables (i.e., <sup>PosT</sup> and <sup>NegT</sup>) are exogenous and therefore can be used as instruments. Following prior studies, we use system generalized method of moments (GMMs) approach to estimate the dynamic panel model (Roodman 2009). The system GMM approach estimates the following first differences and the levels equation simultaneously:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \Delta r t _ {i t} = & \alpha \Delta r t _ {i t - 1} + \beta \Delta X _ {i t} + \phi \Delta W _ {i t} + \Delta \xi_ {i t} (f i r s t \\ & - d i f f e r e n c e s e q u a t i o n), \end{array}\tag{6}
$$

$$
r t _ {i t} = \alpha r t _ {i t - 1} + \beta X _ {i t} + \phi W _ {i t} + \xi_ {i t} (l e v e l s e q u a t i o n),\tag{7}
$$

where $r t _ { i t }$ denotes log $( R T _ { i t } ) , X _ { i t }$ is a vector of endogenous predictors and their interactions, and $W _ { i t }$ denotes the vector of exogenous and control variables. In the first-differences equation (i.e., Equation (6)), the lags of endogenous explanatory variables and the dependent variable are used as instruments for their differences. In the levels equation (i.e., Equation (7)), the past differences of the variables are used as instruments for the levels of the endogenous explor atory variables and the dependent variable.

The estimates of the system GMM are reported in Table A5 (in the online appendix). The coefficient for the lagged dependent variable is less than one, which suggests that the estimates converged (Blundell and Bond 1998). The coefficients for <sup>PosT</sup> and <sup>NegT</sup> are positive at $p < 0 . 0 5$ and $p < 0 . 1 2 5$ significance, respectively. We find that the popularity of the candidate negatively moderates the positive effect of <sup>PosT</sup>. Furthermore, consistent with earlier findings, we find evidence for spillover effects.

## 8. Discussion

8. Discussion<sub>With human brands favoring social media over other</sub> traditional media modes to communicate and engage with the audience, it is critical to analyze how the content generated by human brands and their related brands affects audience engagement. Although recent literature has analyzed the relationship between the content generated by the users/firms and engagement (Dellarocas et al. 2007, Kumar et al. 2016), there is a scarcity of research in information systems that systematically studies how human brands, who are widely followed on social media, generate engagement in social media.

Prior studies have argued that the interaction between the audience and human brands is based on fandom and worship, which is different from how audience associate with corporate brands (Saboo et al. 2016). Our research provides insights into the impact of tones of content generated by human brands and their related brands on engagement. Based on Twitter data and set in the context of general election in India, we examine the effects of the tone of human brands social media content on audience engagement levels measured by the number of retweets. Moreover, with industry experts suggesting that firms emulate human brands’ social media strategy, our results could provide insights not only to human brands but also to traditional or corporate brands (i.e., firms) on how social media content can affect audience engagement. In the following, we first summarize our key findings and the intuition behind these findings and then present theoretical contributions of our study. Finally, we discuss the insights that human brands, with respect to their social media strategy, can glean from our study.

## 8.1. Summary of Findings

Our empirical findings indicate that both positive and negative tone of HGC positively affects engagement. In particular, we find that a unit increase in the number of positive (negative) toned HGC increases engagement by 33.51% (31.69%).<sup>23</sup> However, we find that the popularity of human brand negatively moderates the effect of positive tone on engagement. This finding suggests that popular human brands should consider keeping their content objective and rational rather than getting into emotional discourse. For example, emotionally charged tweets of Elon Musk, cofounder and CEO of Tesla, were found to have hurt his and Tesla’s reputation (Molla 2018). Our empirical findings, in line with evidence from industry, suggest that audience expect popular human brands to post content with less emotional tone.

With respect to perception spillover effects, we find that the tone of HGC of related human brands affects the engagement of content generated by human brands. In particular, we find evidence for reinforcement effect; that is, engagement levels are higher when the tones of both the focal brand and the related brands are similar (either positive or negative). This indicates that frequent exposure to positive- or negative-toned HGC from multiple sources increases the likelihood of the audience engaging with the human brand. When the tone of both the human brand and the related brands is similar, the audience may find the content of all the brands belonging to the same group reinforcing and therefore engage with all the brands.

The principle of social proof could provide an alternative reasoning for high engagement levels when both the focal and related brands’ HGC tones are negative (or positive). Cialdini (2009) argues that the audience tends to determine whether a piece of information is consistent by examining information from other sources. In particular, the audience looks for consistency of the information and as the audience sees more news, either positive or negative, they may choose to engage with the content. In the context of elections, it is likely that members of a political party (i.e., human brands and related brands) post content pertaining to a similar topic in a given week. For example, they could be talking about a failed policy of an opposition candidate or political party, and hence the tone of both the focal brand and the related brands is negative. In such a scenario, the audience perceives the negative-toned HGC of related brands as an endorsement to the negative-toned HGC of the focal human brand and thus are more likely to engage with the post of focal human brand. Furthermore, our results suggest that the effect of negative tone of focal candidate is lower when the tone of related brands is positive.

Finally, we extend our analysis to empirically analyze the significance of social media engagement from a human brand’s perspective. We find that social media engagement is positively associated with visibility on social media platforms. This result indicates that higher engagement is indeed an important metric for human brands to evaluate their social media strategy. To summarize the findings of our work: (i) the tone of HGC affects engagement; (ii) the effect of HGC on engagement is lower for more popular human brands as compared with less popular human brands; (iii) tone of related brands’ HGC can affect social media engagement; and (iv) higher engagement is associated to increased visibility on social media. Overall, these findings provide important takeaways and implications for human brands (or their managers) on their social media strategy.

## 8.2. Implications

<sup>8.2. Implications</sup>Our findings have several important implications for academic literature and practice. The findings from earlier studies on the tone of UGC consistently show that emotionally charged content can affect various outcomes such as book sales, movie revenues, and venture capital funding (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006, Dellarocas et al. 2007, Aggarwal et al. 2012). However, the results from the prior work on UGC may not extend to HGC, as HGC is primarily for self-promotion of human brands, whereas the primary focus of earlier studies on UGC has been on product reviews and consumers’ interaction with firms. Thus, from a theoretical perspective, our study takes the lead in understanding the consequences of the content created by human brands.

Although Berger et al. (2010) have documented the impact of brand popularity on the relationship between negative publicity and product sales, our study is one of the first to document the moderating role of social media popularity on the effect of HGC. The findings of our study indicate that the popularity of the human brand plays a vital role in moderating the effect of HGC tone on engagement. This implies that failing to account for popularity will likely result in overestimation of the effects of tone on engagement. These novel findings on how popularity affects social media engagement contribute to understanding how and why tone affects engagement.

Our research is among the first to establish perception spillover effects of social media content in the context of human brands, whereas previous literature has demonstrated the presence of the spillover effects of content generated by other firms on engagement (Janakiraman et al. 2009). Our work contributes to the literature by extending the understanding of the effects of content tone on different domains and identifying the mechanisms (i.e., moderators) through which content affects engagement. Although most previous studies in the context of social media have focused on the effect on sales or other similar constructs, we focus on a more behavioral construct of engagement, as captured by the number of retweets that social media content generates. Furthermore, we also empirically demonstrate that engagement on social media is associated with increased social media visibility of the content creator.

Our work also has several significant managerial implications for the social media managers of human brands who seek to increase engagement with the audience. Although we have shown the impact of HGC content on engagement in the context of politics and Twitter, the implications of this study are potentially relevant to other contexts (e.g., top management executives of corporations and sports personalities) and other platforms (such as Facebook and LinkedIn). As human brands from different domains and fields are increasingly using social media to engage with the online community (e.g., CEOs), the question of how to generate social media engagement is of importance. Given that in certain industries, social media engagement of human brands can have direct economic implications (Saboo et al. 2016 demonstrate that the social media activities of musicians have a positive impact on product sales), our findings can provide guidance to human brands from different domains on how to design successful social me dia campaigns.

8.2.1. Popular Brands Should Actively Use a More 8.2.1. Popular Brands Should Actively Use a More Rational Tone, Whereas Less Popular Brands Should<sub>An important insight that we</sub> <sup>Use an Emotional Tone.</sup>glean from our study is that less popular human brands should focus on creating emotionally charged content (either positive or negative) to generate higher engagement and greater visibility on social media platforms. We believe that our findings may be extended to the context of traditional brands and human brands from other domains. In particular, our findings suggest that less popular brands (such as small businesses and startups) could increase their audience engagement (and thus visibility) by increasing either positive- or negative-toned content. It is often argued that smaller brands need to create their social media content so that they can stand out from the crowd. Our results indicate that one way of standing out is by creating emotionally toned content, which could give the visibility needed for a smaller firm. On the other hand, in case of bigger brands (or more popular brands), emotional tone on social media could backfire and lower audience engagement.

8.2.2. Have a Comprehensive Social Media Strategy. Our study empirically establishes the presence of reinforcement effect, which advocates the need for a coordinated social media strategy between focal human brands and related brands so that the tone of one human brand does not hurt the engagement of other human brands. This effect should also be generalized to other contexts where human brands extensively use social media (e.g., top management executives and CEOs). Furthermore, given the similarities of human brands and traditional brands, we speculate that our findings may be applied to traditional brands as well. For example, the negative advertising strategy of one car make and model (e.g., Toyota Corolla) might increase its engagement levels. However, this could have a negative impact on its related brands (e.g., Toyota Camry, Rav4, and Highlander). Thus, as a first step, brands should have a comprehensive social media strategy across all products. In summary, our research highlights the importance of social media tone, related brands’ social media tone, and popularity on online engagement and visibility. Unlike traditional marketing strategies, social media allows human brands to connect with their audience instantaneously. Therefore, it is important for brands to have an effective social media strategy that accounts for the tone of its social media content, the tone of its related brands content, and its popularity.

## 8.3. Conclusion and Future Research Directions

<sup>8.3. Conclusion and Future Research Directions</sup>In this study, we develop a robust empirical model to examine the effects of tone of a human brand’s social media content on engagement with the audience. To answer our research questions, we assemble a novel data set comprising candidate-level Twitter data collected during the 2014 Indian general election. Our findings provide guidance to human brands on an all-inclusive social media strategy while contributing to the literature.

Although the findings of our study provide several interesting and important insights on the impact of HGC, we acknowledge that our work has limitations.

First, we recognize the fact that our study is limited to examining the impact on only one particular social media platform. Future research can explore and revalidate our findings on other platforms such as Facebook, Instagram, and Snapchat. Second, we restricted our focus to the context of elections and political candidates. Future studies can investigate the generalizability of our results with human brands from different contexts such as corporate firms, fashion, sports, and cinema. Although there are several similarities among political candidates across the world, it would be interesting to determine whether our insights hold for candidates in other countries and account for the differences in cultures. Whereas our research focuses on only one aspect of HGC content (tone), we hope that future research can build upon our study to examine the impact of other aspects (such as including emotion and humor). Although the focus of this study is the effect of the tone of content, as a future research direction, it will be interesting to study the impact of the topics on the relationship between the tone and engagement, using techniques such as Latent Dirichlet allocation or joint sentiment topic modeling. Finally, future research can build on our study to explore other issues related to the content generated by human brands on social media.
