Crowdfunding aims to collect small investments from a large number of backers to support a project by fundraisers. Although uncertainty has been an important issue in the reward-based crowdfunding literature, there is limited research on how backers respond to probabilistic uncertain rewards. This study examines how backers respond to the <sup>lottery</sup>—a type of uncertain reward—and how their motivations may shape their responses to probabilistic uncertain rewards. We hypothesize (1) how backers respond to probabilistic uncertain rewards, (2) how these effects vary over time, and (3) how backer motivations moderate their response to probabilistic uncertain rewards. We empirically test our set of hypotheses using data collected from a reward-based crowdfunding platform in China. First, theresultsfromproject-levelanalysisshowthatalthoughthelotterydoeshelptoattractahigher number of backers on a crowdfundingproject, the lottery also reduces the total money raised by the project—and thus the probability of reaching its funding goal. Specifically, although the lottery incentivizes individuals who would otherwise not fund the project to become (small) lottery backers, it also cannibalizes regular prospective rewardees and donors by encouraging them to opt for the (cheaper)lotteryoption. Second, both theincentivizing and thecannibalizing effects of the lottery are stronger in the earlier stages of a project’s fundraising cycle. Third, our individual backer-level analysis shows that the lottery has larger effects on backers with higher prior tendencies to be donors than rewardees. Our study theoretically contributes to three streams of literature—crowdfunding, decision making under uncertainty, and the lottery. From a practical standpoint, our study informs (1) fundraisers on how to design strategies to attract the right type of backers and (2) crowdfunding platform owners on how to leverage the lottery as a type of uncertain reward in crowdfunding platforms.

## 1. Introduction

1. Introduction<sub>How</sub> <sub>to</sub> <sub>acquire</sub> <sub>funds</sub> <sub>to</sub> <sub>launch</sub> <sub>businesses</sub> <sub>has</sub> <sub>long</sub> been a challenging issue for entrepreneurs. Rewardbased crowdfunding allows fundraisers to collect small amounts of money from the crowd by describing their ideas and offering early access to their products or services (Mollick 2014, Courtney et al. 2017). Attracted by the innovative ideas or the new prototype products, potential backers then either back a prespecified amount for a reward or donate without receiving a reward. Reward-based crowdfunding has been rather successful in raising funds for new products. Notably, Kickstarter, the largest reward-based crowdfunding platform in the United States, had successfully raised nearly \$5 billion for more than 182,000 fundraisers by May 2020.<sup>1</sup> Besides raising funds, reward-based crowdfunding has become an increasingly major means for fundraisers to get feedback from the public (Belleflamme et al. 2013, Gerber and Hui 2013) and increase visibility and product consumption (Burtch et al. 2013, Mollick and Kuppuswamy 2014). Despite the popularity of reward-based crowdfunding, many entrepreneurs find it challenging to attract a sufficient number of backers and raise sufficient funds to reach their funding goals. As Mollick (2014, p. 6) described, “Among crowdfunded projects, failures happen by large amounts, successes by small amounts.” Thus, encouraging backers to contribute to crowdfunding projects is of theoretical and practical interest.

One objective of this study is to examine how <sub>backers respond to</sub> probabilistic uncertain rewards<sub>—</sub> specifically the lottery. Prior studies in economics and marketing often distinguish between two types of rewards, certain and uncertain. Certain rewards have fixed and known values, whereas uncertain rewards are probabilistic and involve several potential values with either known or unknown probabilities (Shen et al. 2015). We herein consider a specific type of uncertain reward, the lottery, which is currently used by reward-based crowdfunding platforms such as Zhongchou, JD Finance, and Suning Zhongchou. The lottery allows backers to pay a small lottery price in order to win a reward with a certain probability. The probabilistic nature of the lottery allows us to examine how backers respond to uncertain rewards in the context of reward-based crowdfunding. Prior crowdfunding literature mostly focuses on the uncertainty of receiving the promised rewards from fundraisers (e.g., because of the fundraisers’ inability to secure sufficient funds or deliver the promised rewards), which is often unobservable or difficult to measure. Extending the literature, our study examines a different type of uncertainty—the uncertainty because of the probabilistic nature of the lottery. Although it is difficult for researchers to directly measure the extent of uncertainty that comes from fundraisers’ inability to secure sufficient funds or deliver the promised rewards, the lottery mechanism provides an opportunity to measure the extent of uncertainty about the probability of receiving the reward, which allows us to examine how backers may respond to such uncertainty.

Our study is closely related to prior research on decision making under uncertainty, which has examined how individuals respond to uncertainty and choose between certain versus uncertain rewards. One the one hand, people may prefer certain rewards because of risk aversion (Gneezy et al. 2006, Iyengar et al. 2008). On the other hand, uncertain rewards may also be attractive under certain circumstances. Prior literature suggests that individuals who are risk-seeking (Friedman and Savage 1948, Nyqvist et al. 2018), overweigh small probabilities (Kahneman and Tversky 1979), or have a limited budget (Kwang 1965) may find the lottery appealing. The lottery also attracts those who derive pleasure from uncertainty (Conlisk 1993, Miyazaki et al. 1999) because it allows people to win an uncertain reward with a generally low probability.

In addition, we would like to empirically investigate how backers’ motivation moderates their response to reward uncertainty. In comparison with traditional purchasing, where consumers are typically extrinsically motivated by the focal product, backers in rewardbased crowdfunding can be both extrinsically and intrinsically motivated (Gerber and Hui 2013, Ryu et al. 2020). Prior literature on uncertainty posits that the effectiveness of uncertain rewards depends on backers initial motivations (Kivetz 2003, Shen et al. 2015). Intrinsically motivated individuals have a lower preference for the reward and are less sensitive to uncertainty because they are more likely to attribute their activity to the excitement of the activity itself and less likely to make the attribution that they are engaging in the process to seek the reward.

In this study, we collected a three-year data set from Zhongchou, one of the largest reward-based crowd funding platforms in China, and we took advantage of a feature change applied on November 12, 2015 Before the feature change, when posting a reward-based crowdfunding project, fundraisers would prespecify a menu of rewards with different prices, and then backers would choose which reward to back. Potential backers could either (1) back a relatively large amount as <sup>rewardees</sup> to get the reward or (2) back a relatively small amount as <sup>donors</sup> without obtaining any extrinsic rewards in return. After November 12, 2015, a new feature became available, and fundraisers could employ a type of probabilistic uncertain reward—the lottery—by specifying the price and probability of receiving the reward. Accordingly, in addition to the existing reward and donation backing options, backers can now back a crowdfunding project as <sup>lottery backers</sup>. By design, the lottery has a much lower price but imposes higher uncertainty in obtaining the reward, because the probability of winning the lottery is often as low as 5% or less. The unique context allows us to examine how the introduction of the lottery as a type of uncertain reward influences backers’ backing decisions. Specifically, we ask the following two research questions: (1) how do backers respond to the introduction of the lottery as a type of uncertain reward? and (2) how does the backers’ response to the introduction of the lottery vary by backer motivation (i.e., intrinsic versus extrinsic motivation)?

At the project level, results show that projects with a lottery attract, on average, 18% more backers than those without a lottery, providing evidence of an incentivizing effect of the lottery. When decomposing this effect, however, the lottery is associated with a 41% decrease in the number of rewardees and a 21% decrease in the number of donors, providing evidence of a cannibalizing effect. Together these two competing effects make a project with a lottery 11% less likely to reach its funding goal as a result of a 64% decrease in the total money raised. These findings are robust across model specifications, including models with and without fundraiser fixed effects, coarsened exact matching (CEM) to address fundraisers’ selfselection of the lottery, a difference-in-differences model with a matched sample, and instrumental variables. Finally, although prior studies have consistently found evidence of herding among backers (e.g., backers are more likely to back a project with higher prior cumulative funding; see, e.g., Zhang and Liu 2012, Burtch et al. 2018), we find that the lottery attracts backers who rely less on cumulative funding by prior backers as a signal for project quality (the ability to attract backers and obtain funds to pursue its objectives), implying that lottery backers tend to be relatively insensitive to quality uncertainty. At the individual backer level, results show that the lottery has larger incentivizing and cannibalizing effects on backers with higher prior tendencies to be donors than rewardees, suggesting that intrinsically motivated individuals are more likely to choose the uncertain reward than extrinsically motivated individuals given backer heterogeneity.

Our study makes several contributions. First, although scholars have recognized reward uncertainty for backers as a critical issue in reward-based crowdfunding (e.g., Mollick 2014, Courtney et al. 2017), the primary focus has been the uncertainty of receiving the promised rewards from fundraisers. Because it is difficult for researchers to directly measure the extent of uncertainty that comes from fundraisers’ ability to deliver the promised rewards, there is limited research on rewards under uncertainty. This study contributes to the reward-based crowdfunding literature by focusing on a different type of uncertainty by utilizing the introduction of the lottery as a type of probabilistic uncertain reward in a reward-based crowdfunding platform to understand how backers respond to uncertainty about the probability of receiving the reward. Second, whereas the literature showed that backer behavior in reward-based crowdfunding is driven by both extrinsic and intrinsic motivations (Gerber and Hui 2013, Mollick 2014), our findings suggest that intrinsically motivated individuals are more likely to be attracted by uncertain rewards because they tend to be less sensitive to uncertainty than extrinsically motivated individuals. Third, although the literature has explored various factors (e.g., Burtch et al. 2013, Mollick 2014) and incentive tools (e.g., Cai et al. 2017, Burtch et al. 2018) that affect crowdfunding outcomes, our study contributes to this literature by examining the role of a novel and unique feature—the lottery—in affecting crowdfunding outcomes.

Finally, this study has managerial and policy implications. Our findings suggest that intrinsically motivated backers tend to be less sensitive to the uncertainty about receiving a reward, which provides implications for projects with higher uncertainty to allocate more effort or design campaign pitches to attract intrinsically motivated backers (e.g., donors) rather than extrinsically motivated backers who are driven by the reward. Furthermore, our results indicate that the lottery incentivizes backers, but it also reduces the probability of project success. If fundraisers’ primary goal is to increase awareness of their products or work, the lottery may be an effective approach by attracting a large number of backers. However, fundraisers should be cautious about the negative role of the lottery in reducing total money raised, given its cannibalization effect, if their primary goal is to secure enough funds to reach their funding goal.

## 2. Background and Background and Hypothesis Developmen

<sup>2.1. Background: Crowdfunding</sup>As crowdfunding has become an increasingly popular source of financing for entrepreneurs, backers uncertainty about receiving the promised rewards has become prevalent (Mollick 2014, Courtney et al. 2017). Different from consumers in standard e-commerce who purchase mature products from sellers, backers in reward-based crowdfunding generally face greater uncertainty about receiving the reward (Hauge and Chimahusky 2016). Often the product of a rewardbased crowdfunding project is in early stages of development and is not available on the market yet (Mollick 2014). Therefore, to decide whether to support a crowdfunding project, backers mainly rely on the information provided by the fundraiser, whose credibility is difficult to ascertain (Courtney et al. 2017), and thus face much uncertainty about receiving the focal reward. Besides, most reward-based crowdfunding platforms apply an all-or-nothing model, where backers do not get the reward if the project fails to reach its funding goal. Even if a project reaches its funding goal, it is still uncertain whether the fundraiser will deliver the product as promised, particularly because those products are usually at early stages (Hauge and Chimahusky 2016), and there is no legal obligation for the fundraisers to deliver the promised product (Mollick 2014). Mollick (2014) reports that more than 75% of successful projects delivered their products later than initially promised, whereas Mollick and Kuppuswamy (2014) report that 37% of the funded projects on Kickstarter went over their initial budget. Although uncertainty has been an important issue in rewardbased crowdfunding, there has been limited research on how backers respond to uncertainty, which is often unobservable or difficult to measure in practice.

Prior literature has examined several factors that may signal the quality of crowdfunding projects and influence crowdfunding outcomes, such as <sup>project</sup> <sup>characteristics</sup> (e.g., project type, goal, and duration) and <sup>fundraiser</sup> <sup>characteristics</sup> (e.g., experience, attestation, and social capital; see, e.g., Mollick 2014, Agrawal et al. 2015, Gong et al. 2020). Recent studies find that cumulative backing behavior by prior backers plays an important role in influencing the behavior of subsequent backers, thereby providing evidence of herding behavior (Zhang and Liu 2012, Agrawal et al. 2015 Burtch et al. 2018). A small body of work examines incentive designs, such as incorporating donations in reward-based crowdfunding (Cai et al. 2017), and the comparison between all-or-nothing and voluntary contribution mechanisms (Burtch et al. 2018).

Understanding what motivates backers to contribute to crowdfunding projects has been an important focus of the literature. Backer motivations are broadly divided into two categories: (1) <sup>extrinsic</sup> motivations <sub>and</sub> <sub>(2)</sub> intrinsic motivations <sub>(Gerber</sub> <sub>and</sub> Hui 2013, Ryu et al. 2020). First, in reward-based crowdfunding platforms, backers can be extrinsically motivated by the tangible rewards, such as receiving a product (Gerber and Hui 2013, Mollick 2014). Backers in reward-based crowdfunding platforms are often considered early consumers because crowdfunding, as a preselling process (Mollick 2014), gives early access to new products (Agrawal et al. 2014). Second, backers can be intrinsically motivated, regardless of whether backing is rewarded. Intrinsic motivations include the desire to help others (Gerber and Hui 2013), help a new product or a new idea (Agrawal et al. 2014), support a cause (Gerber and Hui 2013), or make a meaningful impact (Gerber and Hui 2013, Kuppuswamy and Bayus 2017). Prior work shows that intrinsic motivations are the primary drivers of backer behavior in donation-based crowdfunding (e.g., Burtch et al. 2013, Tan et al. 2016).

Recent studies show that intrinsic motivations are also prevalent in reward-based crowdfunding (e.g., Gerber and Hui 2013, Kuppuswamy and Bayus 2017, Ryu et al. 2020, Dai and Zhang 2019). For example, interviews confirm that backers in reward-based crowdfunding are motivated not only by the rewards but also by the desire to help others in an altruistic fashion (e.g., Gerber and Hui 2013). Kuppuswamy and Bayus (2017) analyzed data from Kickstarter to find that backer contributions were highest as projects approached their funding goals but significantly dropped after they reached their funding goal, implying that backers want to help fundraisers if their contribution makes an impact on helping fundraisers reach their funding goal.

Backer behavior in reward-based crowdfunding can be driven by both intrinsic and extrinsic motivations, and ignoring either of them would be incomplete (Mollick 2014, Ryu et al. 2020). Donors are mainly intrinsically motivated, because they back an amount without receiving an extrinsic reward as a return. The motivation of rewardees can be purely extrinsic or both extrinsic and intrinsic (Dai and Zhang 2019). Rewardees may be purely extrinsically motivated if they are interested in the reward itself and are willing to pay for the reward. Rewardees may also be intrinsically motivated by altruistic reasons, and they may simply appreciate the reward as a benefit. Given the fact that rewardees typically back a larger amount than donors and actually receive a reward, we argue that extrinsic motivation is the main motivation for rewardees. Finally, if a potential backer has both low intrinsic and extrinsic motivations, he or she would likely be a nonbacker.

## 2.2. Hypothesis Development: Lottery and Project-Level Crowdfunding Outcomes

<sup>Project-Level Crowdfunding Outcomes</sup>Prior studies often distinguish between two types of rewards, certain and uncertain. Certain rewards have fixed and known values, whereas uncertain rewards involve several potential values with either known or unknown probabilities (Shen et al. 2015). One popular example of uncertain rewards is the lottery (Rogers 1998). One the one hand, people may prefer certain rewards because of risk aversion (Gneezy et al. 2006, Iyengar et al. 2008). On the other hand, a small body of studies finds that uncertain rewards may be more effective. For example, Dhar et al. (1995) showed that low-probability lottery-like discounts can lead to higher purchase intentions than equally costly conventional discounts. Goldsmith and Amir (2010) showed that when the stakes are low and the choices do not demand much thinking, consumers prefer uncertain rewards almost as much as the more preferred outcome, often driven by innate optimism. It is also postulated that uncertain promotions offer customers hedonic benefits with experiential pleasure and emotions (Chandon et al. 2000). Finally, the motivatinguncertainty effect posits that people are willing to invest more effort, time, and money in pursuit of an uncertain than a certain reward of a higher expected value when focusing on the process of pursuing a reward rather than the reward itself (Shen et al. 2015) because people enjoy a positive experience, such as excitement, from uncertainty when pursuing a reward.

The literature on reward uncertainty and the lottery<sup>2</sup> has provided insights into motivations for choosing uncertain rewards and under what circumstances people prefer uncertain to certain rewards. Specifically, prior literature on reward uncertainty and the lottery suggests that the lottery as a type of probabilistic uncertain reward attracts people (1) who are willing to tolerate the risk (e.g., Friedman and Savage 1948, Markowitz 1952, Nyqvist et al. 2018) or disproportionally overweigh small probabilities (Kahneman and Tversky 1979), (2) who would like to allocate their budget to many products in which they are interested because of the indivisibility of spending (Kwang 1965), and (3) who derive pleasure from uncertainty (e.g., Conlisk 1993, Miyazaki et al. 1999). Extending the literature to reward-based crowdfunding platforms, we propose two potential theoretical mechanisms by which uncertain rewards affect the decisions of prospective backers and determine key crowdfunding outcomes (the number of backers and total money raised/probability of reaching the funding goal)—the cannibalizing effect <sub>and</sub> <sub>the</sub> incentivizing effect<sub>.</sub>

The Role of the Lottery in the Total Number of In reward-based crowdfunding, prospec-<sup>Rewardees.</sup>tive rewardees are primarily extrinsically motivated because of the reward (Gerber and Hui 2013). When the lottery as an uncertain reward becomes available, the lottery offers prospective backers a chance to win the reward with uncertainty. Although the probability of winning is low, the uncertain reward may be attractive to those who are willing to tolerate the risk of not winning the reward (Friedman and Savage 1948, Markowitz 1952) or disproportionally overweigh small probabilities (Kahneman and Tversky 1979). In addition, if the lottery is available, prospective backers could allocate their limited budget, which they would otherwise spend on a single certain reward (product), across several projects in an attempt to probabilistically win many rewards that interest them (Kwang 1965). Therefore, it is possible that some prospective backers may switch from backing as rewardees, absent the lottery, to backing as lottery backers when the lottery is available (cannibalizing effect). The Role of the Lottery in the Total Number of<sub>The second group of prospective backers</sub> <sup>Donors.</sup>consists of those who are mainly intrinsically motivated and do not seek to obtain the focal reward. Before the introduction of the lottery, intrinsically motivated backers may choose to become donors without receiving a reward. When the lottery is available, such prospective backers now have two options: back as donors without any reward (as before) or participate in the lottery as lottery backers to win the reward with uncertainty. The lottery may serve as a tangible incentive by offering additional benefits from contributing (Morgan 2000, Landry 2006). In addition, the lottery may attract backers who derive positive utility from uncertainty itself, particularly when the stakes are small (Conlisk 1993). People may be motivated to choose the uncertain reward because of the desire to win, impulse, curiosity, feeling lucky, and enjoyment (Miyazaki et al. 1999). This view could be relevant to the lottery in crowdfunding; besides the pleasure derived from uncertainty, the knowledge that the money raised helps fundraisers may make the lottery attractive (Clotfelter and Cook 1990). (Total Number of Donors) <sup>The</sup> <sup>lottery</sup> <sup>is</sup> negatively associated with the total number of donors a crowdfunding project attracts.

2.2.3. The Role of the Lottery in the Total Number of The lottery in crowdfunding provides prospective backers with a chance to win the reward with a much lower backing amount (the lottery price) than what is typically required to obtain the reward. By introducing the lottery, fundraisers may attract additional backers who would not otherwise back the project (incentivizing effect), thus increasing the total number of backers a project receives.

First, for those who are intrinsically motivated to contribute but do not because of the donation cost, the lottery serves as an incentive to offset the cost of participation, thus incentivizing these backers to become lottery backers (Morgan 2000, Landry et al. 2006). Specifically, Morgan (2000) posits that because the lottery provides an additional benefit from philanthropic giving, it generates higher levels of donations than does a voluntary donation mechanism. Empirical studies have also shown that when the lottery is used as an additional benefit without incurring additional costs of participation, the lottery helps charities raise more money than voluntary donations by inducing a higher participation rate (Landry et al. 2006). Second, uncertain rewards were shown to be mor appealing to risk-tolerant individuals (e.g., Nyqvist et al. 2018). When the stakes are small, people may prefer large rewards with uncertainty to small certain rewards (Friedman and Savage 1948, Markowitz 1952) Given its low price, the lottery may attract new backers who are interested in the focal reward of the project but who are not willing to pay the full price to get the reward with certainty. Third, the indivisibility-of-spending explanation for lottery participation posits that rational people with a limited budget who want to purchase multiple products may participate in the lottery when their limited resources do not allow them to purchase many products (Kwang 1965). The availability of the lottery may attract prospective backers with a limited budget to allocate their budget over several projects in an attempt to win multiple rewards. Finally, the lottery may attract additional backers who derive positive utility from uncertainty itself (Miyazaki et al. 1999), particularly when the stakes are small (Conlisk 1993).

We argue that the lottery has a positive effect on the total number of backers a project receives. It is worth noting that if the lottery only encourages prospective backers to switch from backing as donors and rewardees (absent the lottery) to backing as lottery backers instead, the total number of backers a project receives is not likely to change. Hence, the effect of the lottery on the total number of backers depends on the extent to which the lottery may incentivize additional backers who would otherwise <sup>not</sup> back the project. Accordingly, we propose the following hypothesis for testing.

(Total Number of Backers) <sup>The</sup> <sup>lottery</sup> <sup>is</sup> positively associated with the total number of backers a crowdfunding project attracts.

2.2.4. The Role of the Lottery in Total Money Raised and As discussed earlier, the lottery may inadvertently cannibalize potential rewardees who, absent the lottery, would otherwise back a large amount for the reward. Because lottery backers, by design, back a smaller amount than rewardees do, this may reduce the average contribution amount. The lottery may also cannibalize potential donors who would otherwise back a small amount without receiving the reward. Although the lottery price is not necessarily lower than the backing amount of donors, empirically, more than 50% of the projects with a lottery set the lottery price at RMB 1, the minimum donation amount suggested by the crowdfunding platform. Thus, the lottery is likely to decrease the average contribution amount because of the cannibalizing effect on likely donors. However, the proposed incentivizing effect suggests that the lottery may incentivize individuals who would otherwise not back at all to become lottery backers, thus increasing the total number of backers attracted to the project as well as the total money raised. Therefore, the role of the lottery in fundraising success depends on which is the dominant effect. If the incentivizing effect dominates the cannibalizing effect, we would observe that the lottery has a positive effect on the total money raised and the probability of reaching the funding goal. This happens if the lottery incentivizes enough new backers to offset the reduction in total raised money caused by the lower average contributions as a result of the cannibalizing effect. By contrast, if the lottery fails to incentivize many nonbackers, it will have a negative effect on the total money raised and the probability of reaching the funding goal. Because we cannot specify ex ante which effect (incentivizing or cannibalizing) is stronger, we propose a set of competing hypotheses.

(Total Money Raised) <sup>The lottery is</sup> (a) <sup>pos-</sup> <sup>Hypothesis 4a .</sup>itively or <sub>(b)</sub> negatively associated with the total money raised by a crowdfunding project.

(Project Success) <sup>The lottery is</sup> (a) <sup>pos-</sup> itively or <sub>(b)</sub> negatively associated with the probability of reaching the funding goal of a crowdfunding project <sub>(</sub>i.e., project success<sub>).</sub>

Prior work has examined subsequent backers’ herding behavior caused by observational learning from the cumulative funding a project receives from prior backers (e.g., Zhang and Liu 2012, Agrawal et al. 2015, Burtch et al. 2018). Studies have consistently shown a positive association between the cumulative funding from prior backers and subsequent backers’ behavior, suggesting that prior backings offer signals about the quality of the project and alleviate uncertainty about whether the project will secure sufficient funds to reach its funding goal. As a result, backers are more likely to back a project in later stages of its fundraising cycle, when a project is approaching its funding goal. However, if uncertain rewards attract risk-tolerant backers (Friedman and Savage 1948, Markowitz 1952 Nyqvist et al. 2018), these backers may rely less on the cumulative funding from prior backers as a signal to reduce their uncertainty. Instead, risk-tolerant individuals may be more likely to back the project earlier in the fundraising cycle when the project is at higher uncertainty of reaching its funding goal. Second, if the lottery attracts individuals who choose the lottery because they enjoy the hedonic pleasure of uncertainty (Conlisk 1993, Miyazaki et al. 1999), they may rely less on prior backings as a signal to reduce uncertainty. If this is the case, lottery backers are more likely to back the project in earlier stages of the fundraising cycle, when the cumulative funding that a project has received is relatively low, indicating that both the incentivizing and cannibalizing effects of the lottery are likely to be stronger in earlier stages of the fundraising cycle. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis.

<sub>(Timing Effects)</sub> The association between the lottery and <sub>(a)</sub> the number of rewardees, <sub>(b)</sub> the number of donors, and <sub>(c)</sub> the number of backers is likely to be stronger in the earlier stages of the fundraising cycle than in the later stages.

## 2.3. Hypothesis Development: Lottery and Hypothesis Development: Lotter

Next, we theorize which types of individual backers are more likely to find uncertain rewards appealing. Specifically, we characterize backers based on their prior backing tendency to be donors versus rewardees.

The effectiveness of uncertain rewards is likely to depend on backers’ initial motivations (Kivetz 2003) It has been well accepted in the literature that intrinsically motivated individuals tend to be more risk tolerant than extrinsically motivated ones (Lepper and Greene 1978, Deci and Ryan 1985, Kivetz 2003, Dewett 2007). The motivating-uncertainty effect posits that individuals express higher motivation toward an uncertain reward than a certain reward of a higher expected value when they focus on the <sup>process</sup> of getting the reward (in our case, supporting the fundraiser) but not the <sup>reward</sup> <sup>itself</sup> (Shen et al. 2015). The reason is that when individuals focus on the process (i.e., supporting the fundraiser), uncertainty generates a positive experience, and such consummatory and affect-rich aspects during the reward pursuit process tend to increase motivation (Deci and Ryan 1985, Sansone et al. 1992, Kivetz 2003, Choi and Fishbach 2011). Intrinsically motivated individuals are less likely to make the attribution that they are engaging in the process of seeking the reward. Instead, they are more likely to attribute their activity (i.e., to support the fundraiser) to the excitement of the activity itself; thus, they would have a lower preference for the reward (Kivetz 2003).

Because the lottery as an uncertain reward is associated with a low winning probability, intrinsically motivated backers, whose focus is to support the fundraiser rather than obtaining the reward, may be more likely to tolerate the uncertainty of the lottery. Therefore, intrinsically motivated backers may be more attracted to the lottery. By contrast, extrinsically motivated backers, whose focus is to obtain the reward, may be less attracted to the lottery because of the motivating-uncertainty effect (Shen et al. 2015). Thus, we expect that the effectiveness of the lottery should vary with the different motivations of the backers, as indicated by their prior tendency to back as rewardees versus donors. We propose the following hypotheses.

(Prior Backing Tendency and Likelihood to Back Projects with a Lottery) <sup>Prospective</sup> <sup>backers</sup> <sup>with</sup> <sup>a</sup> higher prior tendency to back as donors are more likely to back projects with a lottery <sub>(</sub>than those with a higher prior tendency to back as rewardees<sub>).</sub>

## 3. Data and Research Context

3. Data and Research Context<sub>We collected data from Zhongchou, one of the largest</sub> reward-based crowdfunding platforms in China, launched in 2013. Similar to other reward-based crowdfunding platforms, such as Kickstarter, Zhongchou specifies an all-or-nothing mechanism wherein fundraisers must reach their funding goal to collect the amount raised. Another similarity is that both platforms allow backers to either back a prespecified amount for a reward (as rewardees) or back any amount without receiving the reward (as donors). To initiate a project, fundraisers must prespecify the backing amount needed to receive different reward tiers. However, the crowdfunding platform prespecifies the donation options—RMB 1, RMB 5, and RMB 10—in addition to any other self-selected amount. Potential backers can observe the current funding status of each project, such as the cumulative funding received and the number of backers who have chosen each backing option.

The platform introduced the lottery, a type of uncertain reward, on November 12, 2015, allowing fundraisers to set up a lottery for their projects. When posting a project with a lottery, fundraisers need to specify the backing amount needed for backers to participate in the lottery (lottery price; e.g., RMB 2),<sup>3</sup> the winning probability (e.g., one out of 20), and the winning prize (which is typically the focal reward of the project), all of which are prespecified and remain unchanged throughout the duration of a project. In sum, for projects with a lottery, potential backers have three ways of backing the project: (1) back the prespecified amount for a reward as rewardees, (2) back without receiving the reward as donors, or (3) back the lottery with a prespecified small amount and small winning probability as lottery backers.

In total, we collected data on 15,252 projects spanning March 2013–September 2016, among which 6,033 projects started after the introduction of the lottery. We excluded projects that had not completed the fundraising process by the time we initially collected the data, projects that did not receive any backing at all, and projects that were removed from the platform’s website. We are left with 5,442 projects, among which 38.9% (3,327) adopted the lottery (treatment group) and 61.1% (2,115) did not (control group). The definition of each variable and the summary statistics are shown in Online Appendix B.

Although the lottery is increasingly used by crowdfunding platforms in China, online gambling, including the lottery, is prohibited in the United States. In the United States, the Unlawful Internet Gambling Enforcement Act of 2006 makes it unlawful under federal or state law for gambling businesses to knowingly accept payments in connection with the participation of a person in a bet or wager that involves the use of the internet (Stewart and Gray 2011) This may prevent crowdfunding platforms from adopting the lottery as a potential means to incentivize backers,<sup>4</sup> although it is not clear whether the lottery in crowdfunding platforms is considered gambling from a legal standpoint. Worldwide, around 85 nations have legalized online gambling, which generated nearly \$30 billion in revenues in 2010 (Stewart and Gray 2011).

## 4.2. Results

<sup>4.2. Results</sup> To estimate the effect of the <sup>4.2.1. Main Model: CEM.</sup>lottery on crowdfunding outcomes, we compared the outcomes of projects with a lottery (treatment group) to those of projects without a lottery (control group) posted after the introduction of the lottery. Ideally, we wanted the treatment and control groups to be statistically identical except for the treatment. In our empirical setting, however, fundraisers decide whether to adopt a lottery for a crowdfunding project.

The self-selection of the lottery may result in differences in observables between the treatment and the control groups. CEM, commonly used in information systems (e.g., Bapna et al. 2016, Burtch et al. 2018), is designed to match observations in the control group to observations in the treatment group by balancing observable characteristics, thus mimicking an ideal counterfactual for projects with a lottery.

Specifically, CEM was conducted in two steps. First, projects were stratified based on observable characteristics, including project type; funding goal; number of reward options; minimum, mean, and standard deviation of the reward prices; project duration; fundraisers’ funding and backing experience; whether there is a video; and project posting week. After this, each stratum contained identical (exact match) or similar (approximate match) projects based on these characteristics. Second, we discarded strata containing only treated or untreated projects, and we matched each treated project with a control project in the same stratum on a one-to-one basis. The CEM procedure produced 1,308 projects in the treatment group matched with 1,308 projects in the control group. To ensure that the CEM resulted in comparable matches, we ran a <sup>t</sup>-test to examine whether the means of the observable characteristics differed significantly between the treatment and control groups (Table B3 in Online Appendix B). Before matching, the treatment and control groups differed significantly in terms of their funding goal, number of reward options, minimum reward price, fundraising duration and fundraiser’s prior fundraising experience. After matching, the magnitude of the differences became statistically insignificant, indicating that the matching procedure reduced the imbalance between the treatment and control groups.

## 4.3. Timing Effects of the Lottery

<sup>4.3. Timing Effects of the Lottery</sup>To test Hypothesis 5, we constructed unbalanced panel data at the project-day level using the CEM sample. The dependent variables in our analyses are AddedBackers<sub>jt,</sub> AddedRewardees<sub>jt, and</sub> AddedDonors<sub>jt,</sub> which are the respective count variables of the number of backers, rewardees, and donors project <sup>j</sup> receives in day <sup>t</sup>.

Following Kuppuswamy and Bayus (2017) and Burtch et al. (2018), we used the lagged percentage of goal funded (<sup>%GoalFunded</sup>jt) to test the effect of prior cumulative funding. To allow for nonlinear effects, we defined six mutually exclusive dummy variables: %GoalFunded <sub>[0%, 25%),</sub> %GoalFunded <sub>[25%, 50%),</sub> % GoalFunded <sub>[50%, 75%),</sub> %GoalFunded <sub>[75%, 100%),</sub> % <sup>GoalFunded</sup> [100%, 125%), and <sup>%GoalFunded</sup> [125%, ...). The variable <sup>%GoalFunded</sup> [0%, 25%) is set as the reference category. We interacted the presence of the lottery with the dummy variables indicating the percentage of the goal funded to examine the moderating role of the lottery. We also include the days left, project characteristics, day-of-week fixed effects, month fixed effects, and location fixed effects to control for unobserved time trends and location effects. Because the data are at the project-day level and have a substantial number of zeros in the dependent variables, we estimated a Poisson model (Kuppuswamy and Bayus 2017).

Results are shown in Table 2. First, as seen in columns (1)–(3), we observe a similar pattern as reported in prior studies (e.g., Kuppuswamy and Bayus 2017, Burtch et al. 2018). That is, before the project reaches its funding goal, the percentage of goal funded is positively associated with subsequent backings, suggesting that prior cumulative funding that a project has received offers a signal about the quality of the project, thus attracting more backers to back the project. Immediately after the project reaches its funding goal, additional backer support is reduced, consistent with prior work positing that people back a project when they believe that their support has greater impact (Kuppuswamy and Bayus 2017). With respect to the interactions between the lottery and the dummies indicating the percentage of goal funded, we find that the lottery’s incentivizing effect (column (3)) and cannibalizing effect (columns (1) and (2)) are stronger in earlier stages of the funding cycle (supporting Hypothesis 5), consistent with the literature that risktolerant individuals and those who derive pleasure from the uncertainty provided by the gambling lottery experience are more attracted by the lottery, and they rely less on signals of prior cumulative funding by other backers.

Backer Behavior<sub>To test whether the proposed effects differ across</sub> backers with different prior tendencies to be donors versus rewardees (Hypothesis 6), we switched the unit of analysis to individual backers. Specifically, we construct a backer-level variable <sup>RewardeeIndex</sup> based on individual backers’ behavior prior to the introduction of the lottery. Therefore, only backers whose first backing took place before the introduction of the lottery were considered.

Empirically, it is challenging to identify a proxy for backer motivation. We propose that if a backer backed as a donor in all prior projects before the lottery was introduced, it signals that the backer may be generally intrinsically motivated. By contrast, if a backer backed as a rewardee in all prior projects, this behavior signals that the backer may be generally extrinsically motivated. Because a backer can back one project as a donor and another as a rewardee, we constructed a continuous measure, <sup>RewardeeIndex</sup>, as the percentage of prior backings (before the introduction of the lottery) by a backer as a rewardee out of the total number of prior backings. We also control for the backer’s prior backing frequency (per day) and the average backing amount per project prior to the introduction of the lottery.

## 5.2. Results

Results are reported in Tables 3 and 4. First, column (1) of Table 3 shows a positive coefficient on the lottery, consistent with the incentivizing effect of the lottery on backing a project. The negative coefficient for the interaction between the lottery and rewardee index suggests that the lottery has a larger incentivizing effect on prior donors than on rewardees, consistent with our theory that the lottery is more likely to attract backers who are intrinsically motivated (supporting Hypothesis 6a). Results are qualitatively similar after controlling for the interaction terms between the lottery and backing frequency and the average backing amount (column (2)).

Second, column (1) of Table 4 suggests that given that a backer chose to back a project with a lottery, a backer with a higher prior tendency to back as a rewardee is less likely to choose the lottery, supporting Hypothesis 6b. Results are qualitatively similar after controlling for backing frequency and average backing amount (column (2) of Table 4).<sup>8</sup> Taken together, the results in Tables 3 and 4 suggest that the backers’ motivation (as proxied by rewardee index) plays an important role in backers’ tendency to choose the lottery.

## 6. Conclusion

6. Conclusion<sub>Several crowdfunding platforms have introduced</sub> the lottery, a type of uncertain reward, besides the existing reward and donation backing options, to support crowdfunding efforts. First, we found that projects with the lottery in reward-based crowd funding do attract more backers (Hypothesis 3) by incentivizing additional backers who would otherwise not back the project (incentivizing effect). However, projects with the lottery are shown to raise less money (Hypothesis 4a) and have a lower probability of success (Hypothesis 4b); this is because the lottery switches prospective backers from backing as rewardees (Hypothesis 1) or donors (Hypothesis 2) to lottery backers (cannibalizing effect), thus decreasing their average contribution amount. Second, in terms of the timing effects of the lottery, we found that the lottery attracts backers who rely less on the cumulative funding by prior backers as a signal for project quality (Hypothesis 5). Third, our individual backer-level analyses suggest that the role of the lottery is highly heterogeneous. Specifically, the lottery is more likely to attract donors than rewardees (Hypotheses 6a and 6b). Online Appendix F summarizes our hypotheses’ testing results.

Our study makes several contributions to the literature. First, we contribute to the crowdfunding literature by utilizing the introduction of the lottery as a type of uncertain reward to understand how backers respond to uncertainty about receiving rewards in reward-based crowdfunding. Second, whereas prior studies have shown backer behavior in reward-based crowdfunding to be driven by both extrinsic and intrinsic motivations, our results interestingly suggest that intrinsically motivated backers are more likely to be attracted by the lottery. This is consistent with prior literature on decision making under uncertainty that intrinsically motivated individuals are less sensitive to reward uncertainty (Kivetz 2003, Shen et al. 2015). Third, our study contributes to the crowdfunding literature on incentive mechanisms (e.g., Cai et al. 2017, Burtch et al. 2018) by showing that the lottery helps crowdfunding projects to attract more backers by acting as an incentivizing mechanism—albeit reducing the total amount raised and impeding the chances of reaching the funding goal. Fourth, whereas the crowdfunding literature has consistently found evidence of herding among backers (e.g., Zhang and Liu 2012, Burtch et al. 2018), our results on the timing effects of the lottery suggest that the herding behavior is weaker among lottery backers than among rewardees and donors. Accordingly, the lottery may help fundraisers maintain early momentum in the fundraising process, which is critical to successful fundraising (e.g., Agrawal et al. 2014, Burtch et al. 2018). Finally, our findings that the incentivizing role of the lottery is stronger in earlier stages of a project’s funding cycle suggests that lottery backers rely less on prior cumulative funding as a quality signal, extending the lottery literature that showed that the lottery attracts risk-tolerant individuals and those who derive pleasure from the uncertainty. Extending the effects of the lottery in crowdfunding has implications for understanding the broader effects of the lottery in novel fundraising contexts (e.g., peer-to-peer lending and equity crowdfunding) as a novel type of probabilistic uncertain reward for fundraisers to raise funds with a probabilistic form of investing.

This study also has managerial implications for fundraisers to design strategies to attract backers. Reward-based crowdfunding faces uncertainty about receiving the reward because the products are usually at early stages. Our findings offer implications for fundraisers whose crowdfunding projects are relatively uncertain to design campaign or marketing pitches to attract intrinsically motivated backers because these backers tend to be less sensitive to the uncertainty of receiving the focal reward. This study also has implications for both fundraisers and crowdfunding platforms in terms of incentive design. As the lottery is increasingly used by crowdfunding platforms, our study shows two opposing effects of the lottery— that is, an incentivizing effect for engaging backers and a cannibalizing effect that results in lower probability of project success. If the fundraisers’ primary goal is to increase the publicity of their project, the lottery may be an effective tool. However, if their main goal is to secure enough funds to reach their funding goal, fundraisers should be aware of the generally negative role of the lottery in reducing total money raised.

From a policy perspective, although the lottery is currently prohibited in the United States, our findings are valuable for policymakers who are considering legalizing the lottery and gambling more generally. As U.S. lawmakers consider legalizing the online gambling industry (D’Angelo and Irwin 2012), our understanding of how people use the lottery is still limited. The issue of legalizing online gambling (including the lottery) has broader ramifications for both the gambling industry and other industries, such as crowdfunding.

This study has certain limitations. First, although we have conducted a number of identification strategies, including coarsened exact matching, a difference-indifferences model with a matched sample, fixed-effects models, and instrumental variable estimation, as well as multiple robustness checks, which may suggest a causal relationship between the lottery and the hypothesized outcomes, we cannot unequivocally ascertain causality because we use observational and not experimental data. We thus cautiously interpret our findings as associations rather than strong causal effects. Second, our analyses focused on the role of the lottery in shortterm crowdfunding outcomes and did not study the long-term effects of the lottery, such as the increase in publicity as a result of the increase in the total number of backers or the (un)favorable evaluation of the focal reward to the crowdfunding project or the crowdfunding platform. Third, in our empirical setting, the lottery is set as a third backing option displayed along with other backing options, and the prize of the lottery is usually the focal reward of the crowdfunding project. Thus, our findings should be cautiously generalized to other forms of lottery designs in crowdfunding platforms. These limitations create interesting opportunities for future research on the effects of the lottery in crowdfunding platforms and other contexts.
