Research questions surrounding phenomena in information systems (IS) are <sup>Abstract.</sup>growing more complex and less amenable to simple explanations. Although many encourage the use of multiple research methods, we go further, advocating for leveraging research methods, analytic approaches, and theoretical perspectives (coined MAP) to better examine complex IS phenomena. The use of multi-MAPs benefits from the complementarity between different theoretical perspectives, that is, variance, process and systems perspectives, and the various analytic approaches that support them in addition to multiple methods. While outlining the broader context of multi-MAP research, this re search note focuses on the complementarity between the variance and systems perspectives, associated analytic approaches, and supporting research methods. We use the variance and systems multi-MAPs approach to illustrate our reasoning and outline how the approach can be applied to other MAPs. In addition, we provide researchers with guidelines that they can use to state their choice of MAP based on their research questions. Finally, we demonstrate how researchers can expand their reasoning from deductive and inductive modes to an abductive reasoning process. Using this process, they can move between integrating multiple MAPs a priori within their research design to addressing emergent findings using an a posteriori addition of MAPs. An illustrative study is used to showcase the advocated multi-MAP abductive process. In this research note, we offer guidance for the study of complex phenomena using multiple MAPs through the process of abductive reasoning. In doing so, we extend the multiple methods research discourse. We also encourage researchers to be explicit and transparent in their MAP choices, whether using a single MAP or multiple MAPs.

## 1. Introduction

1. Introduction<sub>Information technology (IT) and information systems</sub> (IS) continuously evolve and are increasingly embedded in complex organizational and social contexts, making it challenging for researchers to clearly delineate IT’s role and impact on performance (El Sawy et al. 2010). Some IS researchers have advocated for the use of multiple research methods to better examine complex phenomena, as the knowledge we are able to gain as researchers is partly dependent on the type of research methods we use (Weber 2004). In contrast, we advocate for studying complex IT phenomena by focusing our attention on a combination of research <sup>methods</sup>, analytic <sup>approaches,</sup> and theoretical <sup>perspectives</sup> (coined MAP), rather than research methods only, when developing and answering research questions. That is, we advocate for multi-MAP approach to research, one that leverages the complementarity between theoretical perspectives. We adopt the Burton-Jones et al. (2015, p. 665) term theoretical perspective as the “concepts and relationships that researchers can choose when theorizing and how they can assemble these types of concepts and relationships to form a theory or theoretical model.” Consequently, we suggest that researchers explicitly review perspectives’ assumptions and complementarities. In turn, this guides the choice of approaches to analyze data collected through carefully chosen research methods.

Three main types of perspectives are variance, systems, and process (Burton-Jones et al. 2015). We focus this research note on combining variance and systems perspectives to illustrate the suggested multi-MAP approach.<sup>1</sup> Although variance and systems perspectives may appear different and irreconcilable, we see them as compensating for and complementing each other. For instance, the variance perspective focuses on changes in one set of variables caused by changes in another set of variables, with the objective of isolating and measuring the strength of individual relationships (Mohr 1982, Van de Ven and Poole 2005). In contrast, the systems perspective focuses on explaining complex wholes, with the objective of examining how a phenomenon may be the result of several conditions configured in specific ways (Greckhamer et al. 2013). By combining variance and systems perspectives and leveraging analytic approaches that espouse these perspectives’ assumptions, researchers can gain a better understanding of theoretical elements and their relationships (Burton-Jones et al. 2015) and address more complex research questions (Sabherwal and Robey 1995). The combined strength of the variance- and systems-based multi-MAP approach can be leveraged for specific types of research questions that ask about the form (i.e., how factors together are linked to a phenomenon of interest) and the strength of each relationship between factors and a phenomenon of interest.

Our intention with this research note is to provide researchers with knowledge needed to state clearly and explicitly their choice of MAP in light of their research questions. In addition, we argue that the process by which it is possible to leverage the strengths of the variance- and systems-based MAPs is iterative in nature and engages both variance and systems theoretical perspectives and data in turn. This type of reasoning, called abductive reasoning or retroduction, alternates between empirical findings and theory to address research. The use of abductive reasoning has been advocated in organizational (Bamberger 2018) and IS research (Tiwana and Kim 2019) to enhance knowledge of a specific phenomenon and foster theoretical developments.

In summary, our methodological contribution is a process by which researchers can explicitly integrate the multiple MAPs used a priori to develop and answer research questions in domains where cumulative results have been inconsistent or incomplete. Existing theoretical models can receive more empirical support (Grover and Lyytinen 2015). Equally, with the iterative nature of abductive reasoning, researchers are equipped to respond to emergent findings that challenge their preconceptions through retroduction and a posteriori addition of MAPs. This two-stage multi-

MAP approach allows researchers to minimize divergent findings and see more findings converge as they examine and report more complete, emerging views of phenomena

Grand, complex organizational, societal, and global challenges, in which IT permeates processes and systems, cannot be tackled with a single paradigmatic lens (King et al. 2010, Winter and Butler 2011, Beath et al. 2013, Majchrzak et al. 2016). However, by using a multi-MAP approach, we are better equipped to address complementary sets of research questions. We gain a more complete understanding of complex, far-reaching phenomena when we set out to use iterative multi-MAPs that highlight the multifaceted interplay between theory and data.

## 2.1. Theoretical Elements

Theories are, at their most basic level, structured representations of phenomena (Weber 2003, Gregor 2006). At their core, theories are defined by concepts and relationships (Burton-Jones et al. 2015) that can be evaluated based on their falsifiability and utility (Bacharach 1989). Although they may include assumptions (Gregor 2006) and boundaries (Weber 2003), it is the relationship between the concepts that is critical to the building of theoretical models. The assumptions of a particular theoretical perspective are ontological in nature, driving what those concepts are, how they are related, how they are represented, and, ultimately, how they are studied.

The variance perspective focuses on properties of entities (variables or factors) that are assumed to have varying values (Burton-Jones et al. 2015). The premises of the perspective are that values of the properties are related to an outcome of interest in a unidirectional and constant manner (Mohr 1982); emphasis is placed on covariation or correlation among properties within a system and their relationship with an outcome. Research questions associated with a correlational analytic approach benefit from a theoretical perspective that allows for highly varied arrangements of concepts and relationships, and the objective is to assess the strength of individual relationships between a variable and an outcome.

Configuration

The systems perspective focuses on understanding “wholes, parts, and emergent properties that arise from interactions among parts” (Burton-Jones et al. 2015, p. 668). Each element affects the whole, elements and effects on the whole are interconnected, and each subgroup of elements affects the whole; emphasis is placed on the overall system, emergence, and interaction (Ragin 1994). A configurational analytic approach to developing research questions benefits from a theoretical perspective that embraces equifinality and nonlinear relationships and seeks to examine underlying system mechanisms, that is, understand the form of the relationships among the elements and with the whole (Burton-Jones et al. 2015).

We argue that the theoretical elements of variance and systems perspectives are not irreconcilable, suggesting that having a multiple perspectives approach is possible. Specifically, variables in a variance perspective can also be viewed as the parts in a systems perspective. The relationship between both individual variables and the wholes (represented by the interactions between parts) is not contradictory, merely a matter of point of observation, where the former is assessed through correlation and the latter through configuration, illustrated in Figure 1.

## 2.2. Types of Relationships and Hypotheses

<sup>2.2. Types of Relationships and Hypotheses</sup>Theories are about the connections within and among phenomena (Sutton and Staw 1995). Theoretical perspectives provide the epistemological basis to define relationships between concepts within theories (Burton-Jones et al. 2015). Each perspective provides a set of rules and expectations, acting as a shorthand in which researchers lay out theories and by which they can be understood by others. Hypotheses, then, are theoretically grounded and testable explanations and predictions of the relationships between constructs describing a phenomenon (Gregor 2006).

Within the variance perspective, these relationships may be viewed as unidirectional, constant, and deterministic, where the focus is on individual relationships between parts of a case (i.e., variables) and the outcome, and the researcher examines variation among the values of properties of entities (Burton-Jones et al. 2015). Within a population, it is assumed that individual variables are both sufficient and necessary conditions for a dependent variable and that the independent variable makes a symmetrical contribution in both negative and positive directions (Schneider and Wagemann 2010). Thus, each independent variable is correlated with the dependent variable; but the correlation may vary in size, direction, and significance (Fiss et al. 2013). The analysis usually seeks to determine which independent variable has the greatest impact on the dependent variable, regardless of other variables’ effects, known as the net effects view (Ragin 2008). Correlation-based hypotheses are used in analytic approaches such as SEM to link a positive or negative regressor and a regressand that increases or decreases with a marginal rate of change over a plane (Thiem et al. 2016). These hypotheses isolate the effect of an independent variable on a dependent variable and are formulated in terms of positivity (the more of <sup>x</sup>, the more of <sup>y</sup>), negativity (the more of <sup>x</sup>, the less of <sup>y</sup>), or independence (the more or less of <sup>x</sup>, neither more nor less of <sup>y</sup>).

Within a systems perspective, relationships can be viewed in terms of interactions among parts of a whole and between the system and its environment, where the reciprocal and concomitant relationship are typical (Burton-Jones et al. 2015). With a systems perspective, an analytic approach like QCA seeks to identify “causal recipes” for the occurrence of an outcome, using both theory and method, by leveraging data to theorize, and vice versa (Fiss et al. 2013) Conditions may be necessary, sufficient, both, or INUS (Schneider and Wagemann 2010). Implication hypotheses are formulated in terms of necessity (if <sup>x</sup> then <sup>y</sup>), sufficiency (only if <sup>x</sup> then <sup>y</sup>), and necessity and sufficiency (<sup>x</sup> if, and only if, <sup>y</sup>) and are used in configurational approaches to link the presence or absence of a condition to an outcome across a range of combinations (Thiem et al. 2016).

The shared conceptualization of necessity and sufficiency is a path to an integrated view. Both interac tion and conjunctions can occur simultaneously for both parts and wholes; it is merely the position of the researcher in examining the phenomenon that changes Necessity is an underlying precept of the relationship between a variable and an outcome in correlationa analytic approaches; testing for necessity of a condition is equally a key step in configurational approaches, either implicitly (e.g., typologies) or more explicitly (e.g., QCA) Hypotheses should be formulated with concepts of necessity and sufficiency in mind, specifically positing whether a condition is necessary, sufficient, both, or INUS using concepts such as contingency to define relationships (see Bell et al. 2014). In developing a hypothesis, the researcher should consider how the conditions interact both with each other and with the outcome. From a variance perspective, this hypothesis translates into interaction effect hypotheses; from a systems perspective, we expect that the combination of some conditions will be associated with the presence of an outcome.

## 2.3. Analytic Approaches

<sup>2.3. Analytic Approaches</sup>Moving from conceptualization of theory and its representation in constructs and relationships to methods of developing and testing hypotheses through analytic approaches further differentiates between the variance and systems perspectives. In general, different analytic approaches have been developed to address different types of problems. As illustrated in Figure 2, from single-case descriptions and single-variable classifications to large-N and large-K cases and variables, researchers select analytic tools that suit their research needs.

The focus of the variance perspective is on variables and their relationships in terms of correlations (Marx et al. 2013). As correlation is generally crosssectional, causality is not inferred from correlation and time ordering of variables is immaterial (Mohr 1982). Because of their reliance on parametric statistics, large sample sizes of cases are needed to increase the probability of detecting effects, particularly as the number of variables increases. Examples of variance approaches include regressions, SEM, and econometric models. A systems perspective looks for commonalities among cases resulting in similar outcomes using concepts of sufficiency and necessity (Ragin 2000). Examples of analytic approaches include QCA and soft systems methodology (Checkland 1981, 2011; Checkland and Haynes 1994). Although QCA<sup>2</sup> was originally intended for small samples, recent methodological developments have facilitated its use with large and very large samples (Greckhamer et al. 2013).

## 2.4. Model Building and Analysis

<sup>2.4. Model Building and Analysis</sup>Variance analytic approaches such as SEM are suited for the testing of the impact of individual independent variables on the dependent variables. Systemsbased approaches such as QCA better identify patterns that exist across a subset of cases, thus allowing for complex causal relationships to surface (Vis 2012). Models built in each tradition can be compared based on the underlying algebra used, the complexity of relationships posited, the causal logic applied, and the concept of additivity and symmetry employed, as illustrated in Figure 3.

SEM relies on linear algebra, whereas QCA is grounded in Boolean algebra. Although they may often resemble each other syntactically, they employ semantically incommensurable languages that can lead to problems of interpretation if researchers are not proficient in and conscious of the application of each (Thiem et al. 2016). Laws of commutativity and associativity are identical between algebras, but other laws such as distributivity are different. For example, the expression <sup>x +</sup> (<sup>−x</sup>) = 1 is demonstrably untrue if interpreted using linear algebra, which would be read as <sup>x</sup> plus negative <sup>x</sup> equals one (when the correct answer is zero). However, in Boolean algebra, this would be read as the union of <sup>x</sup> and not <sup>x</sup> yields the universal set (1). It is therefore critical to understand the basic mathematical functions of each approach and apply them appropriately.

In SEM and other correlational analytic approaches, the interaction between two variables is often modeled by creating a latent product variable of the two main effects (Gefen et al. 2000). Because of the linearity and additivity assumptions of these approaches, determining interaction effects requires more data as the number of hypothesized interactions increases (Schumacker and Lomax 2010). For example, two main effects (A and B) create one latent product variable (AB), whereas three (A, B, and C) create four (AB, AC, BC, and ABC) and four create 10. This makes it challenging to assess multiple causal recipes or combinations of independent variables associated with a dependent variable (Ragin 2008). In addition, the overall objective in correlation is still to identify the individual net effect from each variable (including the new latent product variable or variables) on the dependent variable, where in contrast, identifying multiple conjunctions of conditions is the overall objective of QCA design (Thiem et al. 2016).

In SEM and other correlational analytic approaches, correlation is both directional and proportional; that is, the relationship is monotonically increasing or decreasing where the degree of change is variable based on the correlation coefficient. In terms of symmetry, a positive correlation assumption is that (1) more of <sup>x</sup> leads to more of <sup>y</sup> and (2) less of <sup>x</sup> should lead to less of <sup>y</sup>. Theoretically then, adding more cases that exhibit the relationship—whether higher <sup>x</sup> with higher <sup>y</sup> or lower <sup>x</sup> with lower <sup>y</sup>—increases the observed correlation between <sup>x</sup> and <sup>y</sup>.<sup>3</sup> There is no additivity in QCA and other configurational approaches. Each condition is not assumed to have a unique impact on the outcome; rather, it acts in combination with all other conditions (Berg-Schlolsser and De Meur 2009). Conditions do not have uniform impacts; rather, a condition may contribute to a positive outcome in one configuration and a negative outcome in another. Asymmetry is assumed in that positive and negative outcomes are described by different configurations (Fiss 2007).

In SEM and other correlational analytic approaches, variables are usually sampled and are centered toward the mean. Testing is based on probabilistic causal reasoning, using correlations of variables as a proxy for causality, and relies on probability statements (significance) to express confidence for generalizability (Ragin and Rihoux 2004, Schneider and Wagemann 2010). A single equation is generated with the best fit proposed to cover the widest number of cases. With configurational approaches, it is assumed that no single condition is either necessary or sufficient for the outcome, a situation known as causal complexity. Equifinality, implied in causal complexity, suggests multiple combinations may produce the outcome rather than a single “optimum” solution. Conditions do not compete to explain variation in the outcome, as independent variables do, but act as “potential collaborators in the production of outcomes” (Ragin 2008, p. 114).

As some operations in linear and Boolean algebra have certain irreconcilable concepts, integration at an arithmetic level is not possible. However, complementarity is possible between approaches centering around interaction, causal logic, additivity, and symmetry by accepting different assumptions and considering variables and configurations sequen tially or simultaneously. Specifically, configurations can be seen to aggregate to net effects and net effects to decompose into configurations. A variance-systems multi-MAP can move between these two viewpoints.

## 3.1. Applying Deductive, Inductive, and Applying Deductive,

<sup>Abductive Reasoning</sup>Empirical results emerge from the interplay between the theories we generate or test and the data that we collect and analyze. We embrace three types of reasoning in examining theory, data, and results— deductive, inductive, and abductive. The difference has been succinctly defined thus: “Deduction proves that something must be; induction shows that something actually is operative; abduction merely suggests that something may be” (Peirce 1931, CP 5.171). See Table 2.

Deductive reasoning employs syllogism to logically deduce specific conclusions from general true premises, where a general statement is followed by a specific example and then a conclusion—it goes from theory through data to results. A statement (a premise or conclusion) is true if it is accurate and correct, whereas an argument is valid if the conclusion follows from the premises.

Although deductive reasoning starts with the theory and investigates data to derive results to test theory inductive reasoning starts with data itself and infers propositions from the results to develop theory. That is, inductive reasoning logically deduces general propositions from observed cases, where there are a series of confirming antecedent observations leading to a consequent conclusion.

In both deductive and inductive forms of reasoning, researchers end with the results as they seek to interpret them. Abductive reasoning starts with the results and works back through data and theory. Complementary to deductive reasoning, abductive reasoning, often termed retroduction, can be used deliberately to identify potential explanations for unexpected findings from a hypothetico-deductive model, then returning to deductive reasoning to test them (Bamberger 2018, Behfar and Okhuysen 2018). Abductive reasoning can take many forms; be triggered by novelty or anomaly; and result in various outcomes, such as facts, rules, or new theories (Aliseda, 2006).

Abductive reasoning has been introduced into the IS literature in several contexts and approaches. For instance, it plays a key part in the iterative approach taken in design science research in IS (Fischer and Gregor 2011). It is also a key tenet of QCA where configurational results are compared back to the source cases to increase understanding of the results (see Park et al. 2017). Retroduction can also be viewed as a backward loop in the research process where results are fed back into the working theory as alternate hypotheses are generated and evaluated (Tiwana and Kim 2019). ## 3.2. Varying Research Methods, Analytic

## Varying Research Methods, Analytic

<sup>Approaches, and Theoretical Perspectives</sup>Mingers (2001) differentiated between pluralism (combining multiple methods from potentially different paradigms), isolationism (mutually exclusive and contradictory paradigms), and complementarism (respect for different paradigms) in examining the ontological and epistemological consistency of different research methods. This differentiation can equally be applied to analytic approaches and perspectives. Pluralism implies that the underlying assumptions of the multiple MAPs are congruent, isolationism that they are contradictory, and complementarism that they are compatible. Compatibility does not imply identical results but rather triangulation onto the phenomenon of interest as it is the differences between methods, analytic approaches, and perspectives that make their combined application valuable to researchers (Sabherwal and Robey 1995). In discussing how to combine each of the methods, analytic approaches, and perspectives below, we explore the space between complementarism (a weak form of integration) and pluralism (a strong form) to achieve different research outcomes.

There have been multiple calls for the use of multimethods in management (Jick 1979) and the IS discipline (Kaplan and Duchon 1988; Mingers 2001, 2003; Venkatesh et al. 2013, 2016). Research methods are well-defined sequences of activities that if carried out properly will yield predictable and replicable research outcomes; well-known research methods include laboratory experiments, case studies, and surveys (Mingers 2001). Researchers purposefully select research methods based on their theorizing to capture and structure data on the phenomena of interest.

Multimethods have generally been described as using both quantitative and qualitative methods (mixed methods), though there is great diversity in the manner in which methods can be mixed including by type of data collected, ontological positioning of the researcher, sequencing of both studies and events in time, etc. (Venkatesh et al. 2013, 2016). It is that sequencing that is important to consider in multimethods, as research is not usually a single event but rather a longer-term, phased activity consisting of several triangulating studies, where certain methods may be of greater value than others at different times. A research method is the set of decisions on the source, sampling and form of data along with the tools and techniques to collect it (Mingers 2001), and one research method’s strengths can be selected to compensate for the other’s limitations, together giving a more complete, holistic view of a phenomenon (Venkatesh et al. 2013, Pascal et al. 2018). The use of multiple research methods is especially helpful to answer research questions that focus on complementarity (i.e., research questions to gain complementary views of a phenomenon), completeness (i.e., research questions to gain as complete as possible a view of a phenomenon), and/or diversity (i.e., research questions to obtain divergent views of the phenomenon; Venkatesh et al. 2013). Triangulation between methods increases confidence in results and can lead to the synthesis or integration of theory (Jick 1979). It can also uncover errors in analysis in one method and lead to new insights not likely to be found using a single method (Kaplan and Duchon 1988).

Mingers (2001) identified five different types of multimethod research: (1) sequential—methods are employed in sequence with results from one feeding into the later one; (2) parallel—methods are carried out in parallel with results feeding into each other; (3) dominant—one methodology is seen as the main approach with contributions from the others; (4) multimethodology—a combination of methods, embodying different paradigms, developed specifically for the task; and (5) pluralist—research conducted simultaneously at different levels of an organization and using different methods (Mingers 2001). All five types are underpinned by the consistency of complementarism, but only the last two embrace pluralism. In their meta-analysis of over 20 studies of IS research methodologies, Riedl and Rueckel (2011) identified 50 different methodologies; divided them into classical empirical, design science, mathematical, and conceptual methods; and noted that the most commonly applied were classical empirical methods of surveys, case studies, laboratory experiments, and field experiments. Given the diversity of multimethods, the multitude of ways to combine methods, and the variety of reasons for mixing them, general guidelines are given preference over specific procedures for each possible combination or type (Venkatesh et al. 2016).

Analytic approaches both drive the analysis of data and frame the presentation of results. Analytic approaches can be differentiated from research methods as the latter focus on the collection of data, whereas the former focus on their analysis. Best practice for each different analytic approach is found throughout the IS and management literatures (see SEM: Gefen et al. 2000; regression and time-series analysis: Shook et al. 2003; QCA: Fiss 2011; qualitative analysis: Tracy 2010). The types of combinations of analytic approaches can also fall into Mingers’ (2001) classification for methods— sequential, parallel, dominant, multiapproach, and pluralist—where the analytic approach is selected to be appropriate to the research method used and data produced.<sup>4</sup> In applying multiple approaches, it is important to respect and adhere to the underlying ontological and epistemological assumptions as well as the best practices and rigorous applications of each approach

Based on our ontological and epistemological assumptions of the phenomenon of interest, perspectives both frame our theorizing and help us interpret our results. As with methods and approaches, it is possible to employ multiple perspectives in the same study. For instance,

Sabherwal and Robey (1995) suggest that the integration of perspectives (in their case variance and process) relies on understanding the underlying ontological and epistemological assumptions of each and how they complement or reinforce each other. This can again be framed in terms of complementarism or pluralism. These authors identify four means of integration that, using Mingers’ (2001) classification, include sequential (i.e., contingency), multiperspective (i.e., stage), pluralist (i.e., causal ordering), and parallel (i.e., joint application). In contrast, Ortiz de Guinea and Webster (2017) classify degrees of integration of different perspectives as being the key differentiator: (1) parallel—both perspectives are used independently in the same or different studies; (2) weakly blended— both perspectives are combined in the same study, incorporating mainly elements from one perspective with a few elements from the other; and (3) strongly blended—both perspectives are combined in the same study, incorporating a set of elements from each. These applications map onto Mingers’ (2001) classification of parallel, multiperspective, and pluralist, respectively.

Drawing these threads together, our theoretical perspective drives the selection of our research method, which organizes the collection of our data, whereas our analytic approach drives the analysis of our data but also frames the presentation of the results, which are then informed by our theoretical perspective. Overlaying the linkages between perspective, approach, and method with our theory, data, and results and our three types of reasoning, we can develop an overarching view of a multi-MAP research process (Figure 5). From this point of view, we can gain insights into both how the varying of MAPs can influence the conduct of research and how the adoption of a multi-MAP research design can increase our triangulation on the phenomenon of interest. The research questions will drive researchers to an initial MAP and research design, and so we start our discussion of the multi-MAP research process here.

## 3.3. Multi-MAP Research Questions Multi-MAP Rese

We approach decisions on the application of multi-MAP research by using and extending the Venkatesh et al. (2013) general guidelines for mixed method research, with Tiwana and Kim’s (2019) postobservational backward loop of retroduction between results and both data and theory. As such, we recognize that researchers make both a priori and a posteriori decisions in evaluating the appropriateness of multi-MAP research.

In a priori applications, researchers must consider their research question to determine if it drives dif ferent research methods, analytic approaches, or theoretical perspectives by examining the assumptions inherent in the question. This starts at the perspective level, where an element focused on parts within wholes would lead the researcher to consider a systems perspective and a related set of methods and analytic approaches. In contrast, an element of valence, preeminence, or strength may prompt the researcher to choose the variance perspective and potentially a different set of methods and analytic approaches. Once the appropriate perspective or perspectives to meet research objectives are identified, a research design—using either inductive or deductive reasoning—to encompass the various MAPs can be developed, recognizing the data needed and the methods to gather it alongside the analytic approaches and tools required to examine that data. Once the MAPs are identified, their degree of consistency will lead to decisions on multi-MAP research design based on the consistency of the underlying assumptions. That is, principles of complementarism or pluralism are followed to determine the sequencing of MAPs. Once the methods are executed to collect data and analyses conducted, the researcher can draw meta-inferences or conclusions drawn from the integration of findings from multiple MAPs (Venkatesh et al. 2013).

In a posteriori application, researchers move retroductively from results back into the theory and vice versa using abductive reasoning. Examining their originally postulated theory, researchers may ask what initially hypothesized relationships warrant revision and what additional hypotheses might address inconsistencies or novelty in results (Tiwana and Kim 2019). This can then lead to a supplemental reexamination of the data or initiate a search for supplemental data through the same or a new MAP in order to address the emergent findings. The decision whether more data are required will drive decisions regarding the existing method’s appropriateness. Whether it is or not, a decision will also be made to determine if the analytic approach is still appropriate or needs to be complemented. Finally, these decisions will need to be assessed if they challenge the original perspective or perspectives taken for the original research questions and, ultimately, whether the research questions require revision.

Multi-MAP research questions most amenable to the integration of different perspectives and related analytic approaches seek to answer questions about a phenomenon of interest where past research might have found incomplete or inconsistent findings, for instance, diverging views of the same phenomenon. There are benefits of integrating different perspectives both at the conceptual and relational levels (Burton-Jones et al. 2015). For example, the variance perspective when paired with a systems perspective can lead to deeper understanding of whether the individual properties of a system’s parts affect the emergent properties of the system or whether interactions between parts of a system depend on the properties of those individual parts. The use of analytic approaches such as SEM and QCA from the variance and systems perspectives, respectively, allows a researcher to leverage the strengths of both perspectives and move toward a converging view by creating a more holistic view of the phenomenon of interest.

## 4. Multi-MAP Research Illustration

4. Multi-MAP Research Illustration<sub>To illustrate both a priori and a posteriori decision</sub> making, we use one of our studies (Levallet and Chan 2016) as an exemplar of a research design grounded in pluralism as a multi-MAP application of two MAPs anchored in variance and systems perspectives. The study examines the role played by IT capabilities for strategic improvisation capability (SIC).<sup>5</sup> IT capabilities are challenging to study because they are pervasive in organizations, their effects are combined with other capabilities, and they affect performance in multiple and often unexpected ways. Previous research shows that the ability to improvise is facilitated when managers have access to real-time information and can instantly communicate (Crossan et al. 2005). IT capabilities and resources facilitate the development of dynamic capabilities, such as SIC (Pavlou and El Sawy 2010, Levallet and Chan 2018). For this study, two IT capabilities, namely, IT infrastructure flexibility and information management (IM) capability, as well as organizational memory, were examined. Although the role of IT capabilities was examined in only a few IS studies with mainly positive results (Pavlou and El Sawy 2010, Chatterjee et al. 2015, Levallet and Chan 2018), management studies have found mixed results for the impact of organizational memory on SIC.<sup>6</sup>

Given these factors, we recognized we were operating in a complex and divergent research field and were interested in both the importance of the individual elements leading to SIC and the emergent interactions on those same elements. Following principles in Online Appendix A to guide a priori choices of MAP approaches, our focus on both the strength and the form of these relationships led to a research question suitable to both variance and systems per-<sub>spectives:</sub> How do organizational, information, and knowledge capabilities in<sub>fl</sub>uence strategic improvisation? <sub>The</sub> <sub>principal</sub> hypothesis therefore is stated in a manner that allows for both individual direct effects and configurational conjunctural effects by focusing on our theoretically grounded belief that each of the three concepts has a positive (though unspecified) relationship with SIC.

The selection of method and analytic approach starts after the development of hypotheses by the researchers. In this example, we follow a deductive reasoning path taken in many IS studies that requires the testing of hypotheses. In keeping with the selected variance perspective, we decided on using an analytic approach such as partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) to analyze the data. Given that approach, we decided that a survey method would be appropriate to gather the data from a natural setting with sufficient observations to meet the underlying assumptions regarding sample size required by SEM. The correlational approach allows researchers to evaluate the strength of the relationship between each independent variable and the outcome, which was important for us to determine, based on our research question. Similarly, in keeping with the systems perspective we also selected, we decided on using a configurational analytic approach such as fuzzy-set QCA (fsQCA) to analyze the data, as it was equally important to investigate the form(s) of the relationships between elements. Given fsQCA’s ability to handle similar quantities and formats of continuous data to PLS-SEM, and that the number of conditions was small enough to meet fsQCA’s assumptions of limited diversity, we were confident in our ability to analyze the survey data with both approaches.

As part of a larger study, we piloted and conducted a survey among 88 top IS executives in small and medium North American organizations. Starting with the variance perspective, the data were analyzed using PLS-SEM (Chin 1995). An overview of the procedure followed for the measurement and structural checks is provided in Online Appendix B. Results are provided in Figure 7.

The structural main effects model results indicated that only IT infrastructure flexibility positively affected SIC and that IM capability and organizational memory did not. The total variance explained was 0.31, which is a moderate $R ^ { 2 }$ for such a model (Hair Jr et al. 2014).

The next step in the analytic process was to confirm the form of the relationship and compare with the correlational results using a configurational approach. We followed the accepted methodology to analyze the data from a QCA perspective (Fiss 2007, 2011) and used notations commonly used in IS QCA research (see Dawson et al. 2016a, Park et al. 2017). See Online Appendix C for full details.<sup>7</sup> Table 3 summarizes results for high- and low-SIC solutions. Two high-SIC solutions are presented in order of solution coverage and are followed by a low-SIC solution.

In both high-SIC solutions (High 1 and High 2), IT infrastructure flexibility is present and combined with the presence of one condition and the absence of another condition. In the low-SIC solution (Low 1), the presence of organizational memory combined with the absence of IT infrastructure flexibility and the absence of IM capability are associated with low SIC. Examining the different elements, IT infrastructure flexibility can be seen to be highly symmetrical, where high IT infrastructure flexibility is consistently linked to high SIC and low is linked to low. Conversely, both IM capability and organizational memory display asymmetry, where combinations of both high and low levels of each lead to high SIC.

Comparing SEM and QCA results and drawing out meta-inferences, we note that the highest path weight and greatest significance in the SEM analysis is also the condition with the greatest symmetry in a QCA analysis, whereas, conversely, the low path-weight, nonsignificant relationships in the SEM analysis are also the asymmetrical conditions in the QCA. This is an explainable result as SEM effectively aggregates the configurations generated by QCA, so the individual configurations teased out by QCA are subsumed in the net-effects of SEM. It is also a convergent result as QCA supports the dominant effect of IT infrastructure flexibility while identifying different equifinal paths to the high and low SIC. As such, both sets of results are viewed as accurate and adhering to the analytic approach’s underlying assumptions, when viewed from each perspective in turn.

These solutions display good levels of consistency compared with the generally accepted standard of 80% for data sets of more than 50 cases (Greckhamer et al. 2013). However, coverage of the solution space is reasonable but by no means complete (as only 32 of 87 cases appeared in either the high- or low-SIC solutions), and so we deduced that the three conditions studied may not allow us to fully explain SIC and that other factors may be at play for the development of SIC. This conclusion was equally supported by the moderate $R ^ { 2 }$ from the SEM analysis. Considering the underlying complexity of the relationships between IT capabilities, organizational memory, and SIC, it seemed reasonable to expect that other phenomena might influence these relationships. Best practice in QCA research (see Ragin 2008, Rihoux and Ragin 2009) recommends identifying any additional conditions that might help resolve the preceding contradictions resulting in less-than-perfect coverage. Including more conditions has been used in past IS research to help resolve contradictory cases (e.g., Rivard and Lapointe 2012), equating to a retroductive approach in nature, alternating between theory and data (Behfar and Okhuysen 2018, Tiwana and Kim 2019). As such, we decided to proceed with an a posteriori iteration of our multi-MAP design.

During the Levallet and Chan (2016) study, we collected data on the type of IS strategy followed by the surveyed organizations, as the type of IS strategy may affect the different types of capabilities developed by an organization (Chen et al. 2010). As such, we determined that we did not have to go into a supplementary data collection phase and that the original survey method was still appropriate. Questions in the survey were asked to assess whether these organizations mainly followed an innovative, conservative, or undefined IS strategy (Chen et al. 2010, Leidner et al. 2011). By reviewing the results from the 88 cases in our data set, we identified that an innovative IS strategy might serve as a catalyst for IT infrastructure flexibility and IM capability in SIC. In addition, a cursory analysis of the correlations between the innovative IS strategy, conservative IS strategy, and undefined IS strategy constructs and SIC revealed that an innovative IS strategy was most strongly linked to SIC than other types of IS strategy (0.41 versus 0.09 and <sup>−</sup>0.20, respectively). Consequently, we conducted further analyses including an innovative IS strategy.

As such, we expected to see a positive relationship between the new variable and the outcome. In correlational terms, this means we should expect a strong relationship between an innovative IS strategy and SIC, whereas in configurational terms, it means we should expect the presence or absence of an innovative IS strategy in many configurations. We revise our hypothesis as follows:

<sub>(Revised)</sub> Strategic improvisation is contingent on the development of IM capability, IT infrastructure <sub>fl</sub>exibility, and organizational memory within an innovative IS strategy context.

Results for the direct effects model with an innovative IS strategy as a fourth independent variable are provided in Figure 8. Results from the PLS analysis indicate that a positive and slightly significant relationship exists between an innovative IS strategy and SIC, supporting our revised hypothesis of a higher $R ^ { 2 }$ than the original model (from 0.31 to 0.35). However, the other three relationships are not significant. The addition of an innovative IS strategy independent variable appears to affect the impact of the other constructs.

A further fsQCA analysis was conducted with results presented in Table 4. When comparing with results in the first fsQCA analysis (Table 3), we note that the innovative IS strategy condition is present in all high-SIC configurations and that it is a combination of the presence of two of the three original components that is key to the generation of high SIC. We also note that overall coverage increases from 60% to 70% for high SIC and from 73% to 77% for low SIC, indicating that we captured more of the phenomenon of interest by adding innovative IS strategy (a total of 62 of 87 cases appeared in either the high- or low-SIC solution).

Based on the refined fsQCA analysis, which almost doubled the number of cases included in solutions, we conclude that an innovative IS strategy is an important ingredient in the causal recipes for SIC, confirming our post hoc observations of the data set and the subsequent SEM analysis. Our meta-inferences focus on the importance of first and foremost developing an innovative IS strategy and then deploying supporting capabilities, such as IT infrastructure flexibility, IM capability, and organizational memory. Based on the integration of our results from the <sup>a priori</sup> and <sup>a</sup> <sup>posteriori</sup> iterations of multi-MAP applications, rather than only IT infrastructure flexibility being important, we conclude that it is a capability primus inter pares (i.e., first among equals) with the others, but still works most effectively when paired with them.

The above example illustrates how a multi-MAP approach, through a priori and a posteriori decision making, may be leveraged to explore a phenomenon of interest more fully. Our initial research question sought to determine both the strength and the form of the relationships among three concepts (IM capability, IT infrastructure flexibility, organizational memory) and SIC. From the use of the first MAP (variance-SEMsurvey), we learned that only IT infrastructure has a positive and significant link to SIC. The second step of the a priori process led us to identify the form of these relationships using the second MAP (systems-QCAsurvey). This step was crucial in identifying alternative paths to SIC that separately combine IT infrastructure flexibility with IM capability and organizational memory but also in conjecturing that another factor, in this case innovative IS strategy, may be at play. By adopting an a posteriori approach through abductive reasoning, we were able to demonstrate the key role of innovative IS strategy. Without this iterative, abductive process of the multi-MAP approach, we would only have had a partial view into the role of these factors for SIC. In doing so, the abductive process resulted in models with greater explanatory power, increasing the $R ^ { 2 }$ of the SEM analysis and the coverage of the fsQCA analysis.

## 5. Discussion

5. Discussion<sub>In</sub> <sub>this</sub> <sub>research</sub> <sub>note,</sub> <sub>we</sub> <sub>argue</sub> <sub>for</sub> <sub>a</sub> <sub>different</sub> <sub>ap-</sub> proach to the study of complex phenomena where, for example, IT-related constructs are so enmeshed with other organizational constructs that it is challenging to examine IT’s impact using single methods (El Sawy et al. 2010). Specifically, rather than using multimethods combining research methods and data collection (e.g., qualitative and quantitative methods), we encourage researchers to explore a multi-MAP approach for their research.

We do not suggest that a multi-MAP approach be applied in all research projects or that all papers incorporate a multi-MAP approach. However, should the research question lend itself to ${ \mathrm { i t } } ,$ a multi-MAP approach is worth exploring, either a priori or a posteriori. Online Appendix A provides additional guidelines on how to develop research questions for a multi-MAP approach as well as criteria to assess them. With this research note, we hope that researchers will make the process of developing research questions iteratively with the choice of MAP an explicit one and that they will also differentiate their choice from multimethods by expanding their consideration to both analytic approaches and theoretical perspectives.

In contrast with multimethods, such as mixed methods research that focuses on the qualitative/ quantitative divide for the choice of appropriate research methods, we suggest that the choice of two research methods for multimethod research should align with assumptions underlying the different perspectives. We highlight the similarities and differences between variance and systems to demonstrate the advantages of using a variance-systems multi-MAP approach. We further suggest that the full benefits of the multi-MAP approach may reside in the researchers’ ability to follow an a posteriori, iterative, and retroductive process.

The multi-MAP design leverages the benefits of multimethod research in triangulation of findings but also addresses some of its weaknesses, including multiple data collection. Gaining access to data is often a key challenge for researchers in terms of time commitment, financial constraints, or access to participants; multiple methods compound that challenge by requiring the collection of multiple data sets (Venkatesh et al. 2013). In contrast to multimethods and the analysis of <sup>two</sup> data sets with a <sup>single</sup> analytic approach for each data set, we suggest there may be much to gain by analyzing a <sup>single</sup> data set using <sup>two</sup> analytic approaches that are anchored within the same or different theoretical perspectives.

We note some limitations to the use of multi-MAP approaches. First, the use of analytic approaches from different perspectives requires that researchers be experts in multiple analytic approaches stemming from different perspectives. We demonstrated that the variance and systems perspectives’ assumptions are mostly reconcilable, but not perfectly. A boundary condition of the use of this approach is encountered when assumptions of the individual research method, analytic approach, or theoretical perspective are determined to be incommensurate, conflicting, or violated by combining MAPs. Researchers who want to undertake a variance- and systems-based MAP approach or any other multi-MAP approach in their research should have developed worldviews that are amenable to perspective shifting. As such, researchers whose worldviews are more bounded toward a single perspective should either consider multimethods within their preferred perspective or partner with researchers well versed in other perspectives. Second, we focused on a specific multi-MAP approach, the variance- and systems-based MAP, to build our arguments in favor of multi-MAP approaches. In doing so, we discussed how research questions and consequently results within a variance- and systems-based MAP approach would seek to examine the strength and the form of relationships between key phenomena. Although we believe these arguments should hold for other multi-MAP approaches (e.g., variance and process based, process and systems based), future research should develop these as well as the type of questions lending themselves to other MAP designs. See Online Appendix A for an example using the digital innovation ecosystem research field.

Similarly, to illustrate the multi-MAP approach, we used two analytic approaches—SEM and QCA—that operationalize the variance and system perspectives. We contend that our proposed integrated approach can be applied to other analytic approaches under the variance and systems perspectives. However, researchers would need to ensure that these analytic approaches reflect the variance perspective or the systems perspective and leverage their combined strengths to address their research questions. Other types of configurational approaches and methods could be used with SEM to operationalize the variance and systems perspectives, for instance, typologies using the case study method (e.g., Chong et al. 2019), or cluster analysis using survey data (e.g., Ferratt et al. 2005), or even variations within approaches such as csQCA versus fsQCA (as we considered in the illustrative example). In practice, this would mean identifying the key characteristics of operationalization of each perspective through the analytic ap proach and the research method chosen. As such, care should be taken to ensure that the strengths of both perspectives are respected, preserved, and integrated with the two approaches and methods.

## 6. Conclusions

6. Conclusions<sub>A key contribution of this research note is to offer a</sub> guided approach to examining complex phenomena using multiple perspectives, approaches, and methods. In addition to the guidelines, we provide criteria to define and evaluate multi-MAP research questions, whether a priori or a posteriori. The multi-MAP approach goes beyond the focus on methods that has been the center of multimethod discussion. We enhance the benefits of multimethod research by leveraging the discourse on plurality and complementarity existing within theoretical perspectives and analytic approaches (Mingers 2001) and the retroductive process (Tiwana and Kim 2019).

Our recommendations go beyond the multi-MAP approach and the decisions made by researchers to leverage it for complex phenomena. We contend that researchers should explicitly state their theoretical perspective, analytic approach, and research method in an integrative manner when developing research questions, even if the approach uses a single MAP. Because there can be multiple analytic approachmethod combinations for each perspective, making the decision process transparent is valuable to any empirical paper’s methodology section. By leveraging and explicitly stating MAP choices and linking them to their research questions, researchers would enhance the rigor of their study and more clearly situate their findings in the context of their chosen MAP.

Another contribution is related to differentiating our proposed multi-MAP approach from multimethod approaches like mixed methods research. The multi-MAP approach is in contrast with the use of multiple methods in that it can leverage key tenets of different theoretical perspectives to analyze a single data set. The focus is on the research questions and the best MAPs to address them. The iterative process between research questions and MAPs ensures that the assumptions of the perspective(s) chosen fit with the research questions. At the same time, researchers explicitly identify their choice of analytic approach in the context of their related theoretical perspective to address research questions, thereby gaining a stronger theoretical view of theoretical elements and their relationships (Burton-Jones et al. 2015). The differentiation between analytic approach and research method brings additional clarity that might benefit the multimethod literature, where method and analytic approaches are sometimes amalgamated (e.g., Sabherwal and Robey 1995, Mingers 2001).

A final contribution of this research note is to demonstrate how retroduction and abductive reasoning can be used as a complement to deductive reasoning, a dominant paradigm in IS research. The use of abductive reasoning hinges on the dialogue between theory and empirical data to offer plausible explanations for surprising findings, but it does not validate these explanations (Bamberger 2018). Deductive reasoning and its use of hypotheses to validate explanations is still needed and should rightly remain a dominant form of reasoning; however, there are benefits to alternating between deductive and abductive modes, leveraging the strengths of both (Behfar and Okhuysen 2018). The use of retroduction as a complement to hypothetical-deduction is especially useful for theory development in IS research (Fisher and Aguinis 2017, Behfar and Okhuysen 2018, Tiwana and Kim 2019). The complementary deductive– abductive process inherent in a multi-MAP design holds promise for theory development in IS research as a way to move away from looking at theory and data “too tightly coupled and linearly sequenced”

(Grover and Lyytinen 2015, p. 272) and toward a more expansive and complete view of complex phenomena.
