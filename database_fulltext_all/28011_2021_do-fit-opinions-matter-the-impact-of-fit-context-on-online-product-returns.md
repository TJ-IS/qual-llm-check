Product-fit uncertainty is cited as one of the top reasons for high online product <sup>Abstract.</sup>return rates. Fit describes how well a product suits a consumer’s needs. The value of a product drops sharply when it deviates from a customer’s ideal fit. In this study, we focus on ordinal fit, a type of fit attribute that can be ordered on a scale—for example, the size of apparel or the difficulty level of courses. By leveraging a change in the product-review system at an online retailer, we examine the impacts of two types of fit information—fi <sup>valence</sup> (an overall evaluation of a product’s ordinal-fit attribute) and fi<sup>t reference</sup> (a re viewer’s ordinal-fit attribute and her choice of the product’s fit attribute)—on returns of apparel goods. Using the lens of advice taking, we reveal the important role of the context of fit opinions (i.e., fit reference) in facilitating shoppers to better interpret fit valence b enabling effective ordinal-fit adjustment and, consequently, reducing product returns. We employ a predictive analytics framework for counterfactual prediction via the Generalized Synthetic Control method to address endogeneity issues and shed light on the dynamic treatment effect. Our findings indicate that fit valence alone can lower product returns onl in a limited situation—when the majority of reviewers agree on the fit valence. In other cases—when either the fit valences are inconsistent or few and far between—it is the combination of fit valence and fit reference that lowers product returns. With the avail ability of both types of fit information, similar reviewers play an important role in helping improve the accuracy in ordinal-fit adjustments. Yet, albeit less effective, information from reviewers with dissimilar body sizes can also help make useful ordinal-fit adjustments Besides, shoppers appear to benefit from both positive and negative fit valences, as long as they are aided by fit reference. Our empirical insights are relevant to many situations where ordinal-fit attributes dominate consumers’ product-evaluation process. Accordingly, we provide useful implications for online sellers grappling with high product return rates.

## 1. Introduction

1. Introduction<sub>Product returns remain a top concern for online re-</sub> tailers. According to <sup>eMarketer</sup>, the average online return rate is between 20% and \~30% (Garcia 2018). This high rate, in contrast to 8%\~10% at brick-andmortar stores, is commonly regarded as the plague of e-commerce (Orendorff 2019). The problem is especially exacerbated for apparel products. Nearly 60% of customers worldwide reported buying clothing online in 2018 (Clement 2020). Yet, these flourishing sales statistics are blemished by the troublingly high rate of returns for apparel—which averages around 35% and peaks as high as 50% (Rudolph 2016). Lack of product fit is cited as one of the top reasons driving those numbers (Mulpuru 2017). The focus of this study is on examining how product-fit uncertainties faced by online shoppers and the availability of fit information in product reviews together impact product return rates, using the apparel category as a backdrop.

Fit describes how well a product suits a customer’s needs. When products are differentiated by fit attributes,<sup>1</sup> there is a unique product variety that offers the best value to each customer; any movement of a product away from this level of the fit attribute sharply lowers the value of the product (Ulrich 2011). Although there are many types of fit that are pertinent, we focus on <sup>ordinal</sup> fi<sup>t</sup>—where the levels of the fit attribute can be ordered on a scale. Examples of ordinal-fit attributes include the size of apparel, the torso length and waistband of backpacks, and the level of course difficulty, among others.

Our interest in fit in general, and ordinal fit in particular, stems from the fact that although it is relatively easy to determine fit prior to purchase in the offline channel due to the ability to physically investigate products, it is rather challenging to do so online. Dimoka et al. (2012) aptly frame online product uncertainty as an information-asymmetry problem and liken it to description uncertainty—where an online shopper has difficulty in assessing product features—specifically, whether a product will suit her needs.

It is well known that online consumers today turn to product reviews, especially ratings, to determine the best products to purchase. A single rating, however, may conflate product fit and quality and can mislead consumers who place different weights on them (Sun 2012, Chen et al. 2018). For example, a high-quality jacket that fits a tall, slim customer may not look as dapper on a short or well-built customer. Shoes that fit a runner with high arches may not fit another with a different foot shape, even if the shoes are well-made. When fit is an important driver of utility, ill-fitting products provide little value to customers, even if the quality is acceptable.

Although people generally agree on what is good versus poor quality, they have heterogeneous valuations of product-fit attributes, thus making the context of fit important. We argue that past customers’ ordinalfit evaluations may be less meaningful for future customers without additional information about the context of fit. In realization of the unique problems posed by fit uncertainty, retailers are seeking to address the lack of fit information online. One approach has been to introduce separate review functions for fit in order to tease it out from other product attributes. The goal of this study is to examine the role of ordinalfit opinions in product reviews on consumers’ purchase decisions and the conditions under which different elements of fit opinions could help customers reduce fit uncertainty and product returns.

We analyze two elements of ordinal-fit opinions. The first, fi<sup>t valence,</sup> is a reviewer’s summary evaluation or rating of a product’s ordinal-fit attribute. The second, fi<sup>t reference</sup>, which describes a reviewer’s ordinal-fit attribute and her choice of the product’s fit attribute, provides the context for the reviewer’s fit valence. For the focal category in our study—apparel— the valence of fit regarding size is specified as <sup>true</sup> to size<sub>,</sub> runs large<sub>, or</sub> runs small<sub>, while fit reference</sub> includes the reviewer’s body size and the size of the product purchased. We leverage a change in the review system that took place at a leading online retailer to examine the impacts of ordinal-fit opinions on product returns.<sup>2</sup> The retailer had earlier allowed reviewers to report only fit-valence opinions, separately from the standard reviews. Later, it introduced a new feature that allowed reviewers to also provide fit-reference information. This unique setting allows us to estimate the effect of fit valence, by itself, and when presented with fit-reference information, on product returns.

We apply the lens of the advice-taking literature to theorize the mechanism of opinion adjustment that underlies the utilization of others’ fit opinions by shoppers. Our results suggest that fit valence alone does not always reduce product returns. Rather it is the combination of fit valence and fit reference that lowers product returns in situations that are devoid of (1) a sufficient number of fit valences from reviewers, or (2) consistent fit valences from heterogeneous sources. In these scenarios that are much more prevalent than their counterpart in reality, the presence of fit reference allows shoppers to make appropriate ordinal-fit attribute adjustments in selecting a better-fitting product, thereby reducing product returns.

Ordinal-fit attributes and the uncertainty stemming from lack of fit information are important not only for apparel. They play a crucial role in a wide array of frequently purchased online product and service categories, including tools, toys, furniture, video, games, sporting goods (e.g., bowling balls, skis, racquets), and both in-person and online lessons. Our study contributes to the literature on online re views and product-fit uncertainty by examining situations where ordinal fit matters—a heretofore understudied area. Although much of the existing literature focuses on matters of fact and matters of taste, our study is one of the few to investigate when and how adjusting opinions from heterogeneous sources could benefit consumer decision making in <sup>matters of</sup> fi<sup>t</sup>. The empirical findings not only further our knowledge about the differential roles of fit valence and fit reference in fit-uncertainty reduction, but also extend our theoretical understanding of the role of advice taking from multiple advisors Our findings offer key actionable insights that can guide retailers and service providers that struggle with online returns due to ordinal-fit uncertainty.

In the rest of the paper, we review related literature in Section 2 and present the theoretical framework for ordinal-fit opinion adjustment in Section 3. Section 4 describes the research setting and data, while Section 5 presents the model formulations and implementations for the main analysis. Section 6 reports the main results and robustness checks, and Section 7 tests several threats to validity. In Section 8, we further explore the theoretical mechanism. Finally, we discuss the contributions and limitations of our study in Section 9.

## 2. Related Literature

2. Related Literature<sub>The early literature in online product reviews ex-</sub> amined the effect of overall rating summaries, measured as valence, volume, and variance, on sales performance and found significant impacts (cf. a meta-analytic study by Babic Rosario et al. ´ 2016). These effects have been documented across categories, including books (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006, Forman et al. 2008, Li and Hitt 2008, Sun 2012), electronics (Archak et al. 2011, Ghose and Ipeirotis 2011), and movies (Duan et al. 2008, Chintagunta et al. 2010). Yet, this volume of work did not usually distinguish the variety of product attributes that a rating represents. Rather, overall ratings were implicitly assumed to represent product quality.

A handful of studies go beyond overall ratings and analyze the sales impact of product attributes found in review text. Archak et al. (2011) studied reviews for electronics, which are vertically differentiated products, and, therefore, the features they extracted were about “quality” (e.g., picture quality, battery life, etc.). The valence of these attributes was found to positively affect demand, which is not surprising, given the shared evaluation criteria for quality across consumers. Ghose et al. (2012), studied hotel reviews and considered the suitability of a hotel for a particular travel purpose (e.g., romance or business). Our paper is similar in spirit to these research works in the recognition of multidimensional product preferences, but we also consider the additional impact of multiattribute review functions that enable reviewers to separately offer attribute-specific evaluations. Chen et al. (2018) is one such work that investigated the role of a multidimensional rating system in the context of restaurants and found that it improves decision accuracy, measured as ex post ratings, by allowing consumers to find restaurants that matched their preferences. Yet, their focus was on taste attributes, which differ from the ordinal-fit attributes that we study.

Finally, although most studies analyze the effects of online product reviews on sales, only a couple that we know of specifically examine their impacts on product returns. Minnema et al. (2016) found that overly positive ratings increase product returns due to greater expectation disconfirmation. Sahoo et al. (2018) studied the role of summary product-rating features in reducing product uncertainty and product returns. However, both studies regarded the overall rating measures as quality signals. Although Sahoo et al. (2018) used features like review length to proxy the information about product fit, they did not consider the context of product fit—which we will show is important, given that consumers hold idiosyncratic fit preferences. Our paper contributes to this stream of work by explicitly examining the unique roles of two types of ordinal-fit information—fit valence and fit reference.

## 3. Theoretical Background

3. Theoretical Background The advice-taking literature studies how decision makers use opinions from one or more advisors to make decisions. The information environment faced by an online shopper in the presence of reviews is one where the advice-taking model is potentially useful. This body of work primarily focuses on <sup>matters of fact</sup> and is conducted exclusively in laboratory settings. Matters of fact are decisions where there is an objec tive target to be inferred (e.g., estimating a mountain’s height) or a single ideal point of preference across consumers (e.g., identifying the best quality). This literature found that decision makers can increase their judgment accuracy by relying on the majority opinion, which converges to the true forecast when advisors’ judgments are uncorrelated (e.g., Yaniv 2004 and Soll and Larrick 2009).

Recent works have explored the role of advice in <sup>matters</sup> <sup>of</sup> <sup>taste</sup>. Taste is defined as a type of product variety in addition to quality and fit (Ulrich 2011) Taste attributes are those for which consumers have multimodal preferences—for example, a customer may be satisfied with blue or green color, matte or polished finishes. Matters of taste resemble <sup>matters of</sup> fi<sup>t</sup> in some ways. Both taste and fit are horizontal attributes in that one’s needs for fit and preferences for taste are heterogeneous. Different from matters of fact, advisors use subjective criterion to decide how much they value taste attributes (Yaniv et al. 2011). It is, therefore, not a foregone conclusion that opinions of taste from majority others would be relevant for personal decisions. Laboratory studies of choices about music and movies found that subjects made better self-predictions by using the judgment of ad visors that displayed similar past choices as themselves than they did by using average opinions (Müller-Trede et al. 2018). Hence, it is likely that similar others will play an important role in matters of fit as well.

Fit attributes, however, also differ from taste attributes in important ways that motivate our study Taste is a dimension that is hard to quantify, while ordinal-fit attributes can be. For example, the size of a piece of apparel, or the level of a technical course, can be presented on an ordinal scale. Such a unique feature of ordinal-fit attributes suggests that, in the absence of a similar other, decision makers might still be able to accurately infer fit by estimating the distance between their and an advisor’s fit needs and adequately adjusting the advisor’s opinion, which is difficult to do for taste attributes. Hence, although fit and taste pose somewhat similar informational challenges, they differ sufficiently in the value of others’ opinions for customers. Our focus is specifically on matters of ordinal fit, and we examine whether and how online shoppers utilize ordinal-fit information from (dis)similar advisors to make better purchase decisions.

One experimental study on taste by He and Bond (2013) is pertinent to our work. Unlike most studies on matters of taste that define similarity between a decision maker and an advisor in terms of overlap in past rating behaviors, the authors allowed a decision maker to determine similarity by reading textual commentary from an advisor. They found that the availability of commentary led to more accurate forecasts, even if the advisor and decision maker had dissimilar taste preferences. Our study differs by examining matters of ordinal fit in a real-world online shopping context, where incorrect decisions have real costs to both retailers and customers. Also, shoppers in our study read opinions from multiple reviewers.

## 3.2. Ordinal-Fit Information and Fit Uncertainty

<sup>3.2. Ordinal-Fit Information and Fit Uncertainty</sup>Ordinal-fit criteria are often accessible from product or service providers’ information pages. For instance, a size chart describes the mapping from a range of body sizes to the specific sizes of likely fit; a course prerequisite offers information about the skills required to complete the course. Yet, uncertainty about these fit attributes may still remain high due to the possible use of idiosyncratic reference points by providers and consumers, which makes it challenging to evaluate a product’s fit prior to a purchase (Dimoka et al. 2012). When shopping for apparel, a customer may find that different sizes fit better for two products from the same brand because of different product designs. Similarly, two lecturers teaching the same course might have different emphases, so that a student may be successful in one, but fail in another. To reduce fit uncertainty, online shoppers search for and utilize fit opinions provided by past consumers (Hong and Pavlou 2014). In Table 1, we summarize the uncertainty levels of different scenarios of fi<sup>t valence</sup> and whether and <sup>how</sup> fi<sup>t reference</sup> would be valuable for ordinal-fit adjustment and uncertainty reduction.

When is fit reference likely to be useful? Prior research on advice taking has revealed that when the volume of fit valence is sufficiently large and the reviewers show high agreement in their fit opinions, fit valence alone was sufficient to help decision makers make better decisions (e.g., Larrick and Soll 2006 and Müller-Trede et al. 2018). Such a finding sheds light on when the wisdom of the crowd is useful. In the case of online apparel shopping, we expect that when a reasonable number of reviewers share similar fit-valence opinions, fit valence alone will be diagnostic, and fit reference will not offer additionally useful information. For example, when most reviewers of an apparel item indicate that fit runs large, it is likely that the design of this product is larger than its typical fit as given by the size chart. A shopper can simply follow the majority fit valence and select a size smaller than what the size chart suggests. Thus, fit reference would not be necessary to make such an adjustment, and the fit-uncertainty level the shopper faces would not be significantly different between the situation when fit reference is available and the situation when it is not. We group these two cases into one scenario—“<sup>Agreeing Crowd on Fit</sup>” in Table 1 and denote it by A<sup>W,</sup> <sup>WO</sup>, where the superscripts—W and WO—represent “With fit reference” and “Without fit reference.”

Scenario A<sup>W,</sup> <sup>WO</sup> is rare in reality, as we will show in our empirical probe. The more popular cases are either a crowd with inconsistent fit-valence opinions or an absence of crowd. In both situations, we posit that the mere presentation of fit valence from reviewers is unlikely to offer sufficiently diagnostic cues to a shopper. In the first case, when the fit valences are highly dispersed—for example, the number of reviewers indicating <sup>true to size</sup> is about the same as the number of reviewers indicating <sup>runs large</sup> and/ or <sup>runs</sup> <sup>small</sup>—shoppers do not converge on a consistent fit opinion. In the second case, the availability of only a small number of fit-valence opinions (e.g., two <sup>runs large</sup> or one <sup>runs small</sup>) is not sufficient for the shoppers to infer the true majority valence. The absence of a sufficiently large crowd decreases the confidence in the displayed “majority” fit valence.

3.3. Ordinal-Fit Adjustment Based on Fit Reference <sup>3.3. Ordinal-Fit Adjustment Based on Fit Reference</sup>When the fit reference of a fit valence is available in reviews, it allows a shopper to examine the extent of (mis)match between reviewers’ needs and the ordinal-fit attribute of products that they purchased. The shopper can then adjust reviewers’ ordinal-fit valence to determine the fit valence that would best apply to herself. This adjustment process may occur in the following steps. To contextualize a reviewer’s fit valence about an apparel product, for example, the shopper first hypothesizes the size of the product that would best fit the reviewer, based on the fit reference (i.e., the body size) provided by the reviewer. Next, she assesses the deviation of the reviewer’s purchased size from this hypothesized size, and subsequently adjusts the reviewer’s fit valence if a deviation exists. We posit that this adjusted fit valence is likely to be a more accurate basis for decision making by the shopper, thus reducing the likelihood of an incorrect purchase.

Consider, for example, a shopper who, based on her knowledge about the product size (e.g., through a size chart or past experiences) and a reviewer’s body measurements, identifies that the size purchased by the reviewer is larger than the hypothesized size. If the reviewer reported her fit valence as <sup>true to size</sup>, it would be misleading to the shopper had the fit reference not been reported, because the reality is that the reviewer had sized up. If the reviewer had selected the hypothesized size, the true valence would have been <sup>runs small.</sup> On the other hand, if the reviewer’s purchased size was smaller than the hypothesized size, and she reported a <sup>true</sup> <sup>to</sup> <sup>size</sup> (or <sup>runs</sup> <sup>small</sup>), the adjusted valence of the hypothesized size for the reviewer would accordingly be <sup>runs large</sup> (or true to size<sub>).</sub>

We expect that similarity between a shopper and the reviewer(s) in terms of fit reference will play a pivotal role in conducting ordinal-fit opinion adjustments, in a manner similar to advice taking on taste (Yaniv et al. 2011), as adjusting opinions from similar others is relatively straightforward. By comparison, because a shopper is typically less familiar with how the product sizes (mis)match the body size of a dissimilar reviewer, the accuracy of her hypothesis of the size that fits such a reviewer and the subsequent opinion adjustment could suffer to some extent. Hence, we expect that the similarity of fitrelated needs between the reviewer(s) and a shopper would moderate the effect of the availability of combined fit-valence and fit-reference information on the shopper’s decision accuracy. Yet, the utility of having both fit reference and fit valence, even from a dissimilar reviewer in Scenario $\mathtt { B } ^ { W }$ , would still be greater than that of fit valence alone in Scenario $\mathrm { B } ^ { \mathrm { W O } }$ as the former case allows for opinion adjustments, whereas the latter does not.

In our empirical analyses, we will analyze the effect of the combined fit-valence and fit-reference information on the product return outcome in the main analysis (Section 6) and conduct several tests of validity (Section 7). In mechanism exploration (Section 8), we will show that <sup>Agreeing</sup> <sup>Crowd</sup> <sup>on</sup> <sup>Fit</sup> (i.e., Scenario $\mathrm { A } ^ { W , \ W \mathrm { O } } )$ is a special, but rare, case where fit reference is not necessary to reduce shoppers fit uncertainty and demonstrate the moderating effect of reviewer–shopper body size (i.e., fit reference) similarity.

## 4. Research Setting

4. Research Setting<sub>We</sub> <sub>leverage</sub> <sub>an</sub> <sub>introduction</sub> <sub>of</sub> <sub>a</sub> <sub>new</sub> <sub>fit-review</sub> function to the rating system at an online retailer’s website to examine the impact of fit information on product returns. Our data provider is one of the largest online specialty retailers of outdoor goods located in the western United States. The product category we focus on is apparel, which includes clothing such as jackets, pants, shorts, vests, dresses, etc. These products are vulnerable to prepurchase ordinal-fit uncertainty and, thus, provide an apt context for the main research questions.

## 4.1. Research Design

Since June 2013, the retailer has allowed reviewers to provide summary fi<sup>t-valence</sup> evaluations, ranging <sub>from</sub> runs small <sub>to</sub> true to size <sub>to</sub> runs large<sub>, of the</sub> apparel products they purchased, in addition to overall ratings and textual reviews. On February 18, 2015, the retailer introduced a new review function that allows reviewers to reveal fi<sup>t-reference</sup> information on their body sizes (i.e., height and weight) and the sizes of the apparel purchased. Figure 1 shows an actual review containing fit valence and fit reference.

Because it was not mandatory to use either of the two fit-related functions, a product may receive four types of reviews with fit information after the system update: those with only fit valence, those with only fit reference, those with fit valence and fit reference, and those with no fit information. The treatment of interest to us is a review with both types of fit information—called <sup>treatment</sup> <sup>review</sup>. A product that had received at least one such review before the end of our data collection is a <sup>treated</sup> product; otherwise, it is a <sup>control</sup> product. Figure 2 illustrates the research setting. Before the fit-reference function was introduced, neither product A nor product B received treatment reviews. After the system update, product A received treatment reviews and is, thus, a treated unit, whereas product B did not and is, hence, a control unit. By utilizing this variation across products, we can analyze the impact of treatment reviews on product returns.

## 4.2. Preliminary Data Exploration

Our initial data consist of 4,240 products that had sales both before and after the system update and cover a two-year time range between October 2013 and September 2015. Among them, 1,487 products received at least one treatment review. We use this set of data to explore the treatment-review evolution since January 2015 and plot the graphs in Figure 3. The solid line depicts the monthly adoption rate of the fit-reference function by newly posted reviews, indicating that this new function was quickly adopted. Between March and September 2015, the adoption rate was rather stable around its mean value of 73.35%. The dashed line shows cumulative adoption rate by products, which increased steadily. Overall, the plots seem to suggest no exogenous shocks that bump up or slam down the adoption rate of the fitreference function.

Next, we compare our main outcome of interest, the average monthly product return rate, between the treatment and control groups, which is calculated as the percentage of orders placed in each month that are returned by the end of November 2015—two months past the end of the sales data. To make the results comparable, we removed products that (1) did not receive any reviews by the end of the sales data, and (2) have less than 10 months of observations. The first truncation is applied due to the concern that products with no reviews are likely to qualitatively differ from those that eventually received reviews. The second criterion is set to ensure that we observe products for sufficient periods. The resulting sample consists of 1,881 products: 370 treated products and 1,511 control units. Figure 4 plots product return rates across time.

We observe that, after the system update in February 2015, a sharp difference in the product return rates between the two groups began. To determine whether the treatment could have causally contributed to the change in product returns, we would need to address several issues. First, the return rates of treated and control products do not seem to follow a parallel trend during the pretreatment periods. Second, in our study, the treatment reviews start appearing in different months across products. We need to take this variation into account to eliminate the concern of nonrandomness in treatment timing. Third, even if the two groups of products are comparable, there may exist other factors that potentially drive both product return rates and appearance of treatment reviews. We proceed to the formal analysis.

## 5.1. The Generalized Synthetic Control Method

<sup>5.1. The Generalized Synthetic Control Method</sup>The GSC method marries the Interactive Fixed Effects (IFE) model (Bai 2009), which accounts for latent factors whose effects may vary across time and units, with the Synthetic Control (SC) method (Abadie et al. 2015), which constructs synthetic control units to serve as counterfactuals for the treated units. The integration of these two techniques allows the GSC estimation to (1) relax the assumption of pretreatment parallel trends, and (2) account for potential unobserved factors that are time-variant at the unit level. Meanwhile, in contrast to the SC method, which only works for one single treated unit, the GSC method is designed for multiple treated units. Unlike the IFE model that imposes a constant treatment effect to all units, the GSC method incorporates treatment heterogeneity across units (Xu 2017).

5.1.1. Latent Factor and Factor Loading. First and <sup>5.1.1. Latent Factor and Factor Loading.</sup>foremost, the GSC method adopts a linear IFE framework to model the latent factors. In our study, the outcome $Y _ { i t }$ —that is, the monthly return rate for product <sup>i</sup> at time <sup>t</sup>—can be expressed as:

$$
Y _ {i t} = \delta_ {i t} T R _ {i t} + X _ {i t} ^ {'} \beta + \lambda_ {i} ^ {'} f _ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t}.\tag{1}
$$

In Equation (1), $T R _ { i t }$ is the treatment—that is, it equals one if product <sup>i</sup> has received treatment reviews prior to time <sup>t</sup> and zero otherwise. $\delta _ { i t }$ is the heterogeneous treatment effect—its subscripts <sup>i</sup> and <sup>t</sup> indicate that the estimates vary across units and across time. $X _ { i t } ^ { ' }$ is a (1×<sup>k</sup>) vector of observed covariates (i.e., <sup>k</sup> is the number of control variables), including product-review features and transaction features detailed in Section 5.2, and $\beta$ is a (<sup>k</sup>×1) vector of the corresponding estimates. Let <sup>r</sup> be the number of latent factors; then $, f _ { t }$ is an (<sup>r</sup>×1) vector of unobserved common factors, and $\lambda _ { i } ^ { ' }$ is a (1×<sup>r</sup>) vector of unknown factor loadings. The factor component can be expressed as $\lambda _ { i } ^ { ' } f _ { t } = \breve { \lambda _ { i 1 } } f _ { 1 t } + \lambda _ { i 2 } f _ { 2 t } +$ $\cdots + \lambda _ { i r } f _ { r t }$ . A two-way fixed-effects specification is a special case of the factor component, where $r = 2 .$ $f _ { 1 t } = 1 .$ , and $\lambda _ { i 2 } = 1$ , so that $\lambda _ { i } ^ { ' } f _ { t } = \hat { \lambda _ { i 1 } } + f _ { 2 t }$ t. Here, $\lambda _ { i 1 }$ can be regarded as the product fixed effects, while $f _ { 2 t }$ is the time fixed effects. In general, $\lambda _ { i } ^ { ' } f _ { t }$ is estimated by an iterative factor analysis of the residuals from the model. Xu (2017) illustrates the detailed latent factor analysis in the supplementary materials. Lastly, $\varepsilon _ { i t }$ is the unobserved error term for product <sup>i</sup> at time <sup>t</sup>.

The effect of a latent factor can be time-variant at the unit level. In our research setting, for example, a product consumed by opinion leaders during an early stage may attract more treatment reviews than a product not consumed by such reviewers during the same period. The professionalism and the credibility of the reviewers on the platform, which are unobserved in our study, may affect both the treatment and the outcome. The GSC method handles such unobserved factors. Hence, in contrast to the fixed-effects model, it has a weaker exogeneity assumption—that ${ \mathrm { i } } \mathbf { s } ,$ it requires the error term to be independent of treatment assignment, observed covariates, and the latent factors, but allows the treatment to be correlated with the covariates and the latent factors for any unit at any time period.

Besides, the GSC approach assumes that the error terms are cross-sectionally independent and homoscedastic, but allows weak serial dependence of the error term within the unit and heteroscedasticity of errors across time. Lastly, the GSC method handles imbalanced data, which is crucial to our study because the products do not have sales every month. 5.1.2. A Predictive Analytics Framework for Counter-An important task in applying the IFE framework is to determine the number of latent factors, <sup>r</sup>. Xu (2017) proposes a predictive analytics procedure for this purpose. For better illustration, we split the total number of products into two groups: <sup>Tr</sup> and <sup>Co</sup>, where <sup>Tr</sup> represents the set of treated products and <sup>Co</sup> represents the set of control products. The latent-factor selection algorithm first applies Equation (1) to the data that belong to control units only, across all pretreatment and posttreatment time periods, using the following equation:

$$
Y _ {i t} = X _ {i t} ^ {\prime} \beta + \lambda_ {i} ^ {\prime} f _ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t}, \forall i \in C o.\tag{2}
$$

Because the control products never received treatment during the data-collection period, $\delta _ { i t } T R _ { i t }$ is dropped in Equation (2). <sup>r</sup> varies over a range of candidate values specified by the researchers. For each given value of $r ,$ the algorithm runs Equation (2) and obtains the estimates $\mathit { \hat { \beta } }$ and $\hat { \ b { f } } _ { t }$ . Next, for all the treated products, a cross-validation procedure is performed. In particular, let <sup>s</sup> be the index of a pretreatment period, ranging from one (the first pretreatment period) to $t ^ { 0 }$ (the last pretreatment period), then Equation (3) is applied to each of the pretreatment periods, beginning with $s = 1$ , for all the products in the treatment group by taking one period out and using the remaining periods to estimate the factor loadings.

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & {\hat {\lambda} _ {i, - s} = (\hat {f} _ {- s} ^ {0} \hat {f} _ {- s} ^ {0}) ^ {- 1} \hat {f} _ {- s} ^ {0 ^ {\prime}} (Y _ {i, - s} ^ {0} - X _ {i, - s} ^ {0 ^ {\prime}} \hat {\beta}), i \in T r,} \\ & {\quad s = 1, \ldots , t ^ {0}.} \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

In Equation (3), $\hat { \boldsymbol f } _ { - s } ^ { 0 }$ and $\hat { \boldsymbol { \beta } }$ are the estimates from (2). The superscript <sup>0</sup> means the periods prior to the introduction of the treatment, and the subscript <sup>−s</sup> represents all periods except for <sup>s</sup>. Based on the estimates in Equation (3), the algorithm predicts the outcome for treated unit <sup>i</sup> in period <sup>s</sup> using $\hat { Y } _ { i s } ( 0 ) = X _ { i s } ^ { ' } \hat { \beta }$ $+ \lambda _ { i , - s } ^ { ' } \hat { f } _ { s }$ and records the out-of-sample error $e _ { i s } =$ $Y _ { i s } ( 0 ) - \hat { Y } _ { i s } ( 0 )$ for all $i \in T r$ . In the last step, the algorithm calculates the <sup>Mean</sup> <sup>Squared</sup> <sup>Error</sup> (MSE) of the prediction, summed over all pretreatment periods $s ,$ for each candidate value of <sup>r</sup>. Out of all MSE values, the one that minimizes this prediction error is selected, and the corresponding set of <sup>r</sup> latent factors will be used in the causal inference process.

The GSC algorithm uses the estimated function to predict the counterfactuals for treated units—that ${ \mathrm { i } } \mathbf { s } ,$ the outcome of treated products—had they not received treatment in the posttreatment periods, labeled $\hat { Y } _ { i t } ( 0 )$ . Mathematically, the causal effect of the treatment is calculated as the <sup>Average</sup> <sup>Treatment</sup> <sup>effect</sup> <sup>on</sup> the Treated unit <sub>(ATT), where</sub> $\begin{array} { r } { \widehat { A T T } _ { t } = \frac { 1 } { N _ { t r } } \sum _ { i \in T r } \big [ Y _ { i t } ( 1 ) - } \end{array}$ $\hat { Y } _ { i t } ( 0 ) ]$ for $t > t ^ { 0 } . ~ N _ { t r }$ is the number of treated units, and $Y _ { i t } ( 1 )$ ) is the observed outcome for treated product <sup>i</sup> at time <sup>t</sup>. The gist of GSC estimation is that, if the predicted outcomes for the treated products during the pretreatment periods is accurate, the algorithm can produce a valid counterfactual for each treated unit during the posttreatment periods. Lastly, the GSC method applies bootstrapping to estimate confidence intervals and standard errors (Xu 2017).

Unlike in typical econometric modeling, the latentfactor selection algorithm in GSC chooses the mode with the lowest out-of-sample prediction error, so as to accurately predict the counterfactuals, which differs from selecting the model with the best fit (e.g., in terms of information criterion). As such, models with more latent factors may not be selected, even if they account for more confounding factors. Besides, a GSC estimation with no latent factor still produces a more valid estimate than a difference in differences (DID) model unless (1) the treatment is randomly assigned, of which the parallel trend is one necessary condition; and (2) no treatment heterogeneity exists. The DID estimate is likely to be invalid if either condition does not hold.

## 5.2. Sample Selection and Variable Generation

5.2. Sample Selection and Variable Generation<sub>The GSC method works under important data con-</sub> ditions that guide the extraction of our estimation sample. First, it requires more observations than fixedeffects models. Xu (2017) suggests $t ^ { 0 } = 1 0$ and $N _ { c o } = 4 0 $ at a minimum. When following these guidelines to extract the aforementioned sample of 370 treated and 1,511 control products, we used month as the time unit to reduce the occurrences of missing value due to the sparsity of sales at the week or day level for majority products in our data.

As described in Section 5.1, our treatment measure, Presence of Rev. with Fit Val. and Fit Ref.<sub>, is binary. In</sub> the main estimation, we include the following productreview features: <sup>Absence of Reviews</sup> (whether a product has not received a review yet), <sup>Avg.</sup> <sup>Rating</sup>, <sup>Rating</sup> <sup>Volume</sup>, <sup>Rating</sup> <sup>Stdev.</sup> (standard deviation of the ratings), <sup>Avg.</sup> <sup>Rev.</sup> <sup>Length</sup> (average number of words in the reviews), and, more importantly, two fit-valence measures: <sup>Volume</sup> <sup>of</sup> <sup>Fit</sup> <sup>Val.</sup> <sup>Only</sup> (number of reviews with fit valence only)<sup>3</sup> and <sup>Net Fit Val.</sup> (the overall valence of fit). We set a one-month lag between those measures and the outcome. For transaction features <sub>including</sub> Avg. Price<sub>,</sub> Avg. Discount<sub>,</sub> Avg. Orders on <sup>Holidays</sup>, <sup>Avg.</sup> <sup>Re-Orders</sup> (i.e., whether the shopper has bought the same product before), <sup>Avg.</sup> <sup>Customers</sup>’ <sup>Past</sup> <sup>Returns</sup> (number of previous returns from the customers), and <sup>Avg. Product Age</sup> (number of days between the first sales date of the product and the date of the transaction), we average the values across purchases of a product in each month. All control variables have been found to affect product returns in past literature (e.g., De et al. 2013 and Sahoo et al. 2018).

The second data condition of the GSC method is that the treated units and the control units share common support in factor loadings. Violating this assumption may lead to erroneous inferences because the approach imposes the same factor loadings from the control units on the treated ones (Xu 2017). To mitigate this concern, we follow Ho et al. (2007) and apply nonparametric matching to preprocess the data. In particular, we use the observations up until the last pretreatment period for each of the 1,811 products to derive propensity scores and apply a logit model that includes all the aforementioned covariates as its inputs. We adopt the original values of product-review features in the last month before treatment because they are accumulative until this month, and the average value of each transaction covariate across all pretreatment periods. Next, we use the nearest-neighbor function with a value of one without replacement for matching. This reduces the loss of control units that may otherwise be discarded when there are significant differences between the two groups in terms of some variables (e.g., <sup>Rating</sup> <sup>Volume</sup>). Such a process results in 333 treated prod ucts and 333 control products, dropping 37 units in the treatment group. A comparison between the matched and unmatched treated products implies no statistically significant difference. $$

$$

$$
S B _ {A f t e r} = | M _ {T r M} - M _ {C o M} | / \sqrt {0 . 5 \times (s _ {T r M} ^ {2} + s _ {C o M} ^ {2})}\tag{5}
$$

In Equation (4), $M _ { T r U }$ is the average value for each covariate of the unmatched treated units, while $M _ { C o U }$ is the average value for each covariate of the unmatched control units; $s _ { T r U } ^ { 2 }$ is the variance of each covariate of the unmatched treated products, whereas $s _ { C o U } ^ { 2 }$ is the variance of each covariate of the unmatched control products. Equation (5) is the same as (4), except that the statistics are calculated for the matched sample.

As illustrated, all variables have an improvement in the mean difference and the standardized bias. After matching, the mean difference of each covariate be tween the two groups is below a quarter of a standard deviation of this covariate, which indicates good matching (Cochran 1968). Hence, we are confident of the presence of common support between treated and control products in this matched sample because they share similar observable characteristics. We will use this sample for most analyses in our study.

## 6.2. Robustness Checks

<sup>6.2. Robustness Checks</sup>We conduct several important robustness checks, as presented in Table 6. First, we control for more factors that might confound the treatment effect. One concern is that unobserved review quality, rather than treatment reviews, may be responsible for lowering product returns. To mitigate this concern, we add a few proxies of review quality to our analyses. Specifically, we include the number of reviews posted by vendors and employees, because such reviews come from sources likely to be more knowledgeable about the products. Following Ghose and Ipeirotis (2011), we include review-readability score (Simple Measure of Gobbledygook Index) and the number of spelling errors. Next, we apply text analysis to extract and include the numbers of comments on product quality, geometric fit, and aesthetic features from the review text. Online Appendix A details the text-mining procedure. We also control for transaction features that may impact consumers’ decisions to return products, such as quantity ordered (of the same stock keeping unit (SKU)), orders with multiple SKUs, and orders placed on weekends, all of which are calculated as monthly averages for a product. In addition, we account for customer characteristics, such as the average customer tenure (number of days since a customer’s first order till the order date on the website) and the proportion of female customers that month. Online Appendix B presents the descriptive statistics of these additional covariates. Because the matching procedure in Section 5.2 also improved the balance of these additional variables with mean difference < 0.25 standard deviation for each covariate (see Online Appendix C), we utilize the matched sample in Section 5.2 to enable comparison.

We also address the issue of incidental truncation— that is, we can only observe returns for products that had purchases. This is problematic if the product return outcome is nonrandom—for example, consumers purchase products they are less likely to return. Following Wooldridge (2002), we apply a Tobit selection model using sales volume as the outcome in the first stage to a balanced panel in which each product has 24 periods. In periods with no sales, the outcome is coded as zero. For each product, transaction-related covariates, such as price and discount in the zero-sales periods, are calculated as the averages of the periods that have sales. The product age in a month is the number of months since the first sales date of the product. Next, we include the residuals from the Tobit regression in the linear GSC estimation.

Columns (1) and (2) in Table 6 present the results of the two-stage analyses that include additional covariates. The estimate of the residuals from the first stage is not significant, indicating the independence between the two stages. The treatment effect remains significant, and its magnitude is even higher (<sup>−</sup>0.0554 versus <sup>−</sup>0.0530 in column (3) of Table 4). Among the review quality measures in the product returns model, only reviews from vendors have an impact on the outcome. It is surprisingly positive, perhaps indicative of overly positive information in those reviews that might lead to incorrect purchase decisions. One of the more interesting results among the productreview controls is that textual comments on geometric fit, which are closely related to the treatment measure, have no significant impact on the outcome. To assess whether treatment effects might merely be masking the effect of textual-fit comments, we analyze data of purchases where no structured fit information was available and find the same result—insignificant estimates of the textual comments about fit (reported in Online Appendix D). This highlights the important role of fit information presented in a structured for mat, i.e., shoppers are able to utilize it better than similar information embedded in review text. Among transaction features, we find that orders with multiple SKUs are positively correlated with returns, implying a potential “try-on” decision (Sahoo et al. 2018). Also, female customers tend to return products more, which is consistent with the findings in Anderson et al. (2009). We continue to use this extended set of controls, but drop the two-stage specification in all remaining analyses.

Our main GSC estimations select the model with no latent factors (among the candidates of <sup>r</sup> from zero to five) because it generates the best out-of-sample prediction of the product return rate. To ensure that the estimate is not affected by omitting the latent factors, we impose one latent factor in the model. The results are reported in the third column of Table 6. Although the treatment effect is slightly lower (<sup>−</sup>0.0535), it is still significant. We also test a model that imposes two latent factors and obtain a qualitatively similar result.

Next, we apply a more restrictive criterion to select products that have at least 15 pretreatment periods and replicate the matching process in Section 5.2, which results in a sample of 368 products (184 in each group). As reported in column (4) of Table 6, the treatment effect is still significant, and its magnitude increases (<sup>−</sup>0.0575). Another concern is that using one nearest neighbor causes a significant drop in control units used, which might increase estimation bias (Ho et al. 2007). To address this issue, we increase the ratio of matching to two and obtain a sample of 897 products, where 333 are treated products and 564 are control products. In column (5) of Table 6, we see that the treatment effect (<sup>−</sup>0.0583) is still significant.

Lastly, we adopt a different matching method, Look-Ahead PSM (LA-PSM) (Bapna et al. 2018, Jung et al. 2019), which restricts the control group to units that have not yet received but would eventually receive treatment. Those products, by virtue of ultimately receiving treatment, are more likely to share unobserved characteristics with already treated products than products that never receive treatment. Because we obtained the product-review data in March 2016, we use the reviews posted after September 2015 to find 298 control products that are eventually treated. The PSM procedure detailed in Online Appendix E is similar to that of Section 5.2. Because the number of control units is fewer than that of the treated products, we set the matching ratio as two with replacement (i.e., a control unit can be matched with multiple treated units). The final sample consists of 343 treated and 196 control products. We apply the GSC method to this LA-PSM sample and present the results in column (6) of Table 6. The treatment estimate (<sup>−</sup>0.0516) is consistent with the main finding. All in all, the robustness checks strengthen our confidence in the main result that ordinal-fit information can lower product return rates to the tune of 5.35\~5.83%.<sup>4</sup>

## 7.1. Falsification Checks

<sup>7.1. Falsi cation Checks</sup>First, we check whether reviews with fit valence only were also able to lower the return rate by estimating a model on data in the time period prior to introduction of the fit-reference function. The time range of such data in the matched sample runs from January 2013 (timestamped by the earliest sales) to December 2014. The fit-valence function was introduced in June 2013.

We adopt GSC estimation and use a binary measure of reviews with fit valence only as the treatment. We report the results in column (1) of Table 7. In line with our theoretical framework, <sup>Presence</sup> <sup>of</sup> <sup>Fit</sup> <sup>Val.</sup> <sup>Only</sup> does not affect product return rate (0.1099, <sup>p</sup> > 0.1).

Next, we shift the appearance of treatment reviews for each treated product to eight months earlier and remove the observations after February 2015 in the matched sample. Because the treatment did not exist in these alternate periods, we should not observe a significant estimate in this analysis. The coefficient of the manipulated treatment (<sup>−</sup>0.0173, <sup>p</sup> > 0.1), as reported in column (2) of Table 7, is consistent with our expectation. Overall, the two falsification checks demonstrate no significant decrease of product return rates when the treatment is not truly present.

## 7.2. Treatment Heterogeneity

<sup>7.2. Finally, we analyze the treatment effects across categories and brands (see Online Appendix F). All the findings above suggest that the treatment effect is stronger when fit uncertainty is higher, supporting our theory and the validity of the treatment.

## 7.3. The Sales Impact of Treatment Reviews

<sup>7.3. The Sales Impact of Treatment Reviews</sup>If reviews with both fit valence and fit reference affect return rate by helping reduce customers’ prepurchase fit uncertainty, we should also expect a positive relationship between treatment reviews and sales. A negative estimate in this analysis would indicate that treatment reviews do not help reduce the product-fit uncertainty, but, rather, screen out uncertain buyers. This has important implications for firms, as factors that also increase sales and not merely reduce returns would have stronger impacts on net profits.

We estimate the effect of the treatment reviews on sales volume by applying the GSC method (to account for nonrandom treatment assignments) in columns (7) and (8) of Table 7. In column (7), we use the same sample as that in column (1) of Table 6. In column (8), we include sales from the previous period, <sup>Lag Sales</sup>, as a covariate. As a result, each product has one less observation (i.e., 23 periods) in this estimation. In both analyses, treatment reviews have a significant positive effect on the sales volume (6.7390 and 4.7440, respectively), indicating that treatment reviews also increase sales by lowering prepurchase product-fit uncertainty. Regarding the covariates, the absence of reviews has a negative impact, whereas average rating is positively correlated with sales, both of which are consistent with the findings in the extant literature (e.g., Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006). Rating volume has a negative estimate, probably due to its high correlation with <sup>Volume of Fit Val. Only</sup> (their Pearson correlation is 0.85). Lastly, price has a negative effect on sales, whereas discount and product age appear to positively affect sales, which are supported by the literature as well (e.g., Minnema et al. 2016 and Sahoo et al. 2018).

## 8. Mechanism Exploration

8. Mechanism Exploration<sub>To</sub> <sub>further</sub> <sub>explore</sub> <sub>the</sub> <sub>mechanism</sub> <sub>described</sub> <sub>in</sub> <sub>Sec-</sub> tion 3, we analyze transaction-level data. In particular, we extract 182,678 observations for the 666 products in the matched sample, where the unit of analysis is a combination of an order identification (ID) and a product ID. The dependent variable is whether a purchased product is returned or not, whereas transaction features from the order are used as controls. The treatment is measured as a count of the number of treatment reviews, which allows us to estimate the value of an additional treatment review.

Because the GSC method is not applicable to this new setting, we apply DID estimation with the logit specification, after applying static matching. We use cluster-robust standard errors at the order level in these analyses to account for the lack of independence among purchases that belong to the same order. Our focus here is not on unveiling causal relationships. Rather, we aim to provide empirical evidences to support the proposed mechanism of ordinal-fit adjustment.

## 8.1. The Role of Negative Fit Valence in The Role of Negati

<sup>Treatment Reviews</sup>We first examine the role of negative fit information in product reviews, which encompasses the two fit valences <sup>runs</sup> <sup>small</sup> and <sup>runs</sup> <sup>large</sup>. If a shopper engages in ordinal-fit adjustment according to the framework in Section 3, then negative fit valence can also be valuable in reducing this shopper’s fit uncertainty. For example, a valence of <sup>runs</sup> <sup>large</sup> from a reviewer similar in body size and size choice implies that the shopper can size down to find the size of likely fit.

To test this conjecture, we split the fit-valence measures into four groups: treatment reviews with positive fit valence, treatment reviews with negative fit valence, reviews with positive fit valence only, and reviews with negative fit valence only. Each of them is measured as a count variable. To avoid multicollinearity, we remove <sup>Net Fit Val</sup>. Because our model includes a control for absence of reviews, each estimate should be interpreted as the impact of positive/negative fit valence compared with reviews with no fit information. The results are presented in column (1) of Table 8. Albeit only marginally significant $( p < 0 . 1 )$ , both positive and negative fit valences reduce returns when they are combined with fit reference in treatment reviews. In contrast, the mere presence of positive or negative fit valence without reference has no effect on product returns.

## 8.2. Agreement of Fit Valence Among Reviewers

Our theoretical framework suggests that fit reference may not be valuable in the scenario of <sup>Agreeing</sup> <sup>Crowd</sup> <sup>on</sup> <sup>Fit</sup> (i.e., scenario A in Table 1). In such a situation, a shopper can simply follow the majority valence to select an appropriate size by sizing up if the majority valence is <sup>runs</sup> <sup>small</sup>, sizing down if it is <sup>runs</sup> <sup>large</sup>, or adopting their usual size if it is <sup>true</sup> <sup>to</sup> <sup>size</sup>. To test this, we calculate fit dispersion using an informationentropy measure, where each fit valence (i.e., <sup>true to</sup> <sup>size</sup>, <sup>runs</sup> <sup>small</sup>, and <sup>runs</sup> <sup>large</sup>) is treated as a category (Provost and Fawcett 2013). A low (high) value of this measure implies high (low) agreement among the reviewers. To make the results easily interpretable, we create a binary variable based on the mean value of fit dispersion—0.26. If the value is below 0.26, we classify it as having <sup>Low</sup> <sup>Fit</sup> <sup>Dispersion</sup>. We then interact it with the mean-centered <sup>Volume of Rev. with Fit</sup> <sup>Val</sup>. Column (2) in Table 8 reports the estimates of the main measures of interest. The treatment effect remains negative and significant, while <sup>Low Fit Dis-</sup> <sup>persion</sup> does not affect the outcome. More interestingly, the interaction between <sup>Volume of Rev. with Fit</sup> <sup>Val.</sup> and <sup>Low</sup> <sup>Fit</sup> <sup>Dispersion</sup> has a negative and significant coefficient (<sup>−</sup>0.0627), indicating that product return probability tends to be lower when a crowd (of reviewers) is consistent in their fit-valence opinions.

To probe further, we split the sample into three parts: <sup>Agreeing Crowd</sup> (fit dispersion < 0.26 with at least five reviews with fit valence), <sup>Disagreeing Crowd</sup> (fit dispersion 0.26 with at least five reviews with fit valence), and <sup>Absence of Crowd</sup> (number of reviews with fit valences is less than five). The results are reported in columns (3)–(5) in Table 8. We find that the treatment effect is insignificant in the subsample of <sup>Agreeing Crowd</sup> (0.1268, <sup>p</sup> > 0.1) and significant in the other two subsamples, which is in accordance with our conjecture—that is, when the crowds mostly agree on fit valence, treatment reviews offer no additional value. Because the <sup>Agreeing Crowd</sup> subsample represents only 18.76% (34,270 observations) of the full sample, the majority of purchases would benefit from ordinal-fit adjustments utilizing fit reference to reduce their prepurchase fit uncertainty—when either reviewers have dispersed fit opinions or when sufficient fit opinions are lacking. On the other hand, the treatment effect is stronger in the subsample of <sup>Absence of Crowd</sup> (<sup>−</sup>0.4422) than that in the subsample <sub>of</sub> Disagreeing Crowd <sub>(</sub>−<sub>0.1916). The</sub> Absence of Crowd condition represents nonpopular or new products that haven’t accumulated sufficient reviews yet. This finding implies that, for such products, having reviews with both types of fit information is essential to help customers make better decisions.

Testing this conjecture requires a group of customers (1) each of whom revealed her body size in a treatment review, and (2) at least one treatment review from another customer had been available prior to her purchase. Based on reviewers’ and customers’ body-size information (i.e., height and weight), we compute the pairwise reviewer–customer similarity by applying the <sup>Manhattan</sup> distance formulation. ## 9. Business Implications, Limitations, Business Implic

## and Conclusion

We leverage an introduction of a new fit-review function at an online retailer’s website to examine the impact of ordinal-fit valence and fit-reference information found in product reviews on apparelproduct returns and to further probe into the mechanism of ordinal-fit adjustment afforded by the context provided by fit-reference information. The results suggest that the presence of fi<sup>t valence</sup> alone is diagnostic only when a sufficient number of reviewers indicate consistent opinions about fit, which typically is a rare scenario in reality on online review platforms. When fit valences are either inconsistent or few and far between, fi<sup>t reference</sup> about reviewer body sizes and the sizes they selected endows online shoppers with contextual cues necessary to effectively adjust fitvalence opinions and determine the best fit for their needs. Consequently, product returns are reduced. Although similar reviewers play an important role in improving the accuracy of ordinal-fit adjustments, fit information from dissimilar reviewers can also help customers make such adjustments when both types of fit information are available. Furthermore, shoppers can benefit from both positive and negative fit valences, when fit reference is provided as well.

The estimation of heterogeneous treatment effects allows us to assess when such fit information is likely to be more valuable for consumers. We find that because customers have lower ordinal-fit uncertainty for popular brands and product categories with more forgiving size ranges (e.g., base layers), they benefit less from fit reference than for products of less-known brands and for outerwear. Also, the treatment effect is stronger for first-time buyers (versus all purchasers) and women’s apparel (versus men’s apparel)—situations where customers face greater fit uncertainty than their counterparts.

Our study makes valuable contributions to the literatures on online fit uncertainty and product reviews. Past research works have primarily focused on conceptualizing product-fit uncertainty and its role in online markets (e.g., Hong and Pavlou 2014, Kwark et al. 2014) or on product-quality information in online reviews (e.g., Sahoo et al. 2018). This study, instead, examines whether and how a different kind of information in online reviews—regarding ordinal fit—can reduce product-fit uncertainty and, subsequently, affect online product returns. Ordinal-fit uncertainty impacts a wide range of products and services $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , recreational goods, online classes, and furniture). As retailers increasingly find ways to incorporate fit information in reviews, it is useful to understand its economic impact. By examining the role of fit-related opinions from multiple advisors in the field, we extend prior works on advice taking that have mainly focused on matters of fact or matters of taste with a single advisor in laboratory settings $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ He and Bond 2013). Our theoretical framing and empirical results also offer new insights into the value of both similar and dissimilar reviewers in fit-related decision-making contexts, as well as the role of a crowd’s agreement on fit. As such, the findings are relevant to the streams of research on the role of reviewer sim ilarity, as well as on the wisdom of the crowd.

In terms of methodology, this is one of the early papers on causal inference in the information systems literature to apply the newly developed GSC method (Xu 2017) to address the possible violation of pretreatment parallel trends. An advantage of this approach is the use of a predictive-analytics framework to produce synthetic units for treatment-effect estimation, thereby mitigating the issue of poorly matched controls.

## 9.2. Practical Implications

<sup>9.2. Practical Implications</sup>This study offers several important implications for firms. First, it provides guidance to companies looking to improve their online review system. For products that are highly sensitive to fit, we suggest that online retailers implement product-review functions that allow customers to provide both a summary fit valence and their fit context (i.e., fit reference). Our findings could be generalized to ordinal-fit attributes other than product size. For instance, a student deciding on the appropriate level of an English course to take can benefit from a former student’s ordinal-fit valence $( \mathrm { e . g . } ,$ too difficult, too easy, or the right level) along with the fit context (e.g., Test of English as a Foreign Language score that reflects the student’s pre-enrollment English skills). As another example, a beginner photographer may benefit from fit-contextual information (e.g., long-distance shots, close-ups, etc.) to understand the fit (valence) of the specific design of a digital single-lens reflex lens. In both cases, fit valence without fit context would not be as meaningful to a decision maker.

Second, unlike information about quality attributes, where positive opinions are desirable, negative fit valences are valuable as well. Customers should thereby be encouraged to honestly report their fit evaluations. Third, the same type of fit information presented in review text does not decrease product return rates, which indicates that displaying structured fit information can reduce shoppers’ information search cost. Lastly, fit opinions from reviewers with similar fit reference benefit the customers more. Thus, it would be useful to develop a personalized recommender to offer the best set of reviews ranked by fit-related attributes to further reduce fit-related search cost.

## 9.3. Limitations and Future Directions

<sup>9.3. Limitations and Future Directions</sup>We acknowledge several limitations of our study and offer avenues for future research. First, although we verify some aspects of the ordinal-fit adjustment mechanism, it is far from thorough. Laboratory experiments could extend our study to examine the mechanism more directly. Second, we limit our focus to ordinal fit, whereas other types of fit, such as the fit of a software to an operating system and the fit of a drug to a symptom, are also important. Further studies are warranted to analyze the process of review adjustment in those scenarios. Besides, fit to one’s needs is important for both products and services. This study only analyzes products that consumers can return upon consumption. The investigation of the issues of fit uncertainty and the impact of fit opinions in service contexts (e.g., apartment rental, food delivery, or medical service) could be a valuable research endeavor. Through this study, we hope to raise awareness of the importance of ordinal-fit uncertainty and of the unique role of ordinal-fit opinions in online markets and to pave the way for more related studies in the future.
