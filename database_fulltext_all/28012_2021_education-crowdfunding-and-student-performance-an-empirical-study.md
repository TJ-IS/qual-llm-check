Despite the growing popularity of online public funding for education (i.e., <sup>Abstract.</sup>crowdfunding), controversy persists about whether teachers’ efforts to use this channel are justified and whether donations thus received can actually make a difference in students academic performance. We present the first empirical evidence of the positive influence of online education crowdfunding after teachers successfully raise funds. Using data from California public school teachers and their students, as well as the teachers’ fundraising activities on DonorsChoose.org, we find significant academic performance improvements among students after their teachers receive donations through this platform. Such im provements in students’ academic performance are stronger when more local donors participate in teachers’ fundraising campaigns. Notably, teachers who attempt to raise funds online but are unsuccessful are still able to improve their students’ performance. These findings not only document the positive impact of online education crowdfunding on students’ academic performance, but also demonstrate that such impacts are at least partially attributable to the nonfinancial motivational benefits on teachers from receiving such donations. Our study therefore contributes to the literature on crowdfunding, as well as the ongoing debates surrounding the financing of public education.

## 1. Introduction

1. Introduction<sub>Public education is critical for the long-term economic</sub> growth and functioning of civil society (Gradstein and Justman 2002, Blankenau et al. 2007). Unfortunately, public funding for education, especially funding for kindergarten through 12th grade (K–12) education, has continuously declined during the past several decades (Leachman et al. 2016). Nevertheless, most teachers perform their jobs with great pride and inspiration. Although their salaries have been largely stagnant (Barnum 2018)—in fact, decreasing, thanks to the rising cost of living—a recent study released by the National Center for Education Statistics (NCES) shows that public school teachers still spend an average of \$480 per year out of their own pockets on school supplies (Spiegelman 2018). Within this educational and financial context, and enabled by the increasing popularity of the internet and internet-based financial technologies (fintech), online education crowdfundingplatformssuchasDonorsChoose.org,GoFundMe .com, and Support-a-School.org have emerged to provide alternative funding sources for K–12 teachers. These online crowdfunding platforms allow teachers to ask online donors to contribute to various classroom projects or other educational efforts. DonorsChoose .org, in particular, has often been recognized as an innovator in educational funding reform and has been endorsed by an impressive list of supporters, including Bill and Melinda Gates, Sheryl Sandberg, and Stephen Colbert. In fact, former President Barack Obama characterized DonorsChoose.org as “strengthening America’s leadership in the 21st century by improving education in science, technology, engineering, and math” (Candice 2010, paragraph 3).

The funding that teachers receive from donors through online education crowdfunding platforms is very different from the public funding that their schools receive from federal, state, and local governments. First and foremost, when attempting to raise funds through these platforms, teachers must actively “make a case” to potential donors by describing the purpose and intended use of the funds requested. In contrast, traditional school funding allocations are typically (a) the result of negotiations and compromises between school district administrators and government officials, (b) subject to budgetary conditions, and (c) the result of competing priorities (Poterba 1997). The funding amount that a teacher receives from the traditional budgeting process is almost entirely based on the number of students that they teach. The funding process, therefore, is much more distant and disconnected from the individual needs and observations of teachers, despite the fact that they know their students and their needs much better. Second, unlike traditional public education funding, which is almost always disbursed with stringent school accountability and reporting requirements (Hanushek and Raymond 2004), donations that teachers receive from online education crowdfunding carry very few restrictions. Typically, platforms ask only that teachers post public updates regarding the use of those funds. For this reason, even though the teachers may have the best intentions, it is unclear ex ante whether the educational projects and activities funded through this new channel should be considered as mere novelties, classroom enrichment, and extra resources that are nice to have, or whether the donations actually contribute to higher academic performance of the students.

Perhaps also because of its novelty, online education crowdfunding is, in fact, not yet fully embraced by all public education administrators. For example, while the Austin Independent School District in Texas and the Chandler Unified School District in Arizona openly encourage their teachers to seek donations online,<sup>1</sup> 67 school districts in Ohio (Cachero 2019) as well as Nashville’s public school system (Del Valle 2019) explicitly forbid their teachers to do so. Therefore, despite the apparent popularity of education crowdfunding, there remains a strong need for a systematic empirical investigation into its impacts.

In this paper, we study whether funding that teachers receive through online education crowdfunding produces empirically detectable effects on students’ academic performance. As of the time of our writing, minimal research has been conducted in this area despite its apparent political, social, and educational significance. If donations to teachers through education crowdfunding provide little or no positive influence, then donors should be aware of this fact, so that they have clear expectations about the educational outcomes that their funds can (or cannot) produce. In contrast, evidence of a positive influence on students’ academic performance will provide these fintech platforms further credibility and contribute substantially to their long-term effectiveness. The success of these platforms also may inspire us to think more deeply about how to improve educational financing in general. More specifically, if education crowdfunding donations contribute significantly to student achieve ment, and traditional methods of funding education struggle to deliver the results that stakeholders, citizens, and taxpayers expect, then perhaps legislators, school administrators, and parents can accommodate a crowdfunding model as a supplement to the current education funding processes.

If crowdfunding donations exert a measurable influence on students’ academic performance, then a natural follow-up question is <sup>how</sup> this influence is achieved. Such influence must necessarily be channeled through teachers, because they are the conduit through which the resources and other benefits that result from successful online fundraising efforts ultimately reach students. Teachers must first make a case to potential donors; then, after successful fundraising campaigns, the teachers must implement the activities and projects that they proposed. In other words, the influence of online education crowdfunding on students’ academic performance will inevitably be the result of the teachers’ effort and behavior.

These online donations, in turn, might influence teacher behavior through either a financial mechanism (e.g., acquiring additional resources that were unavailable elsewhere) or through a nonfinancial mechanism (e.g., reciprocating the trust offered from socially connected donors; Granovetter 1973), or both. Whereas several previous education research studies suggest that additional resources do not necessarily translate into higher academic performance, the current consensus is that they do (Baker 2017). In other words, if online education crowdfunding improves students’ academic performance, at least part of this improvement should be attributed to the additional funding that teachers receive. Hence, a more important and meaningful question is whether improvements in academic performance among students are solely the result of financial factors or whether these improvements can be at least partially explained by nonfinancial factors. If nonfinancial factors are also responsible, then perhaps the same amount of money could lead to a <sup>higher</sup> margin of improvement when distributed through online education crowdfunding

To summarize, our goal is to determine whether online education crowdfunding has a measurable influence on students’ academic performance and, if so, whether this influence is attributable, at least in part, to the nonfinancial effects of these funds on the behavior of teachers

We explore these questions by compiling and studying a novel longitudinal data set consisting of information about public schools in California. Our data set was derived from multiple sources, including DonorsChoose.org and the California Department of Education (CDE). Specifically, we obtained information about student cohorts, teachers, schools, and the fundraising activities of teachers on DonorsChoose.org. Through a series of statistical analyses and robustness tests, as well as taking into account potential selection and endogeneity issues, we find that, all else being equal, receiving funding from DonorsChoose.org increases students’ average scores on the California Standards Tests (CSTs). In addition, we find that (a) the positive influence on students’ academic performance is higher when more local donors contribute to a project, and, quite notably, (b) even students whose teachers seek but fail to secure online crowdfunding donations experience performance improvements. These analyses provide convincing evidence that the positive influence of education crowdfunding is not derived exclusively from the monetary value of the donations. The nonfinancial factors related to these donations contribute, at least partially, to the improvement of students’ academic performance.

Our study draws on and contributes primarily to two streams of literature. The first stream draws on the rich literature related to the economics of education. Within this research stream, one of the most persistent and challenging questions is how to improve students’ academic performance. Our study is among the first to demonstrate empirically that students’ academic performance is improved in a nontrivial way when teachers engage in online fundraising activities. More notably, we demonstrate that the positive influence on students’ academic performance is partially attributable to nonfinancial factors. Specifically, this study is the first to provide evidence of the existence of “planning effect”; in other words, students’ academic performance improves even when their teachers attempt to elicit online crowdfunding donations but are ultimately unsuccessful in raising funds. Moreover, whereas in an offline setting, social bonds established through parental support and community involvement are shown in the literature to be important factors that contribute to student achievement (e.g., Henderson and Mapp 2002), our study is the first to document evidence that such community effects remain highly relevant for online crowdfunding.

This paper also contributes to a second stream of literature—that is, the more recent and fast-growing research on online crowdfunding. Online crowdfunding is an excellent example of the ways in which information technologies have transformed the ancient practice of fundraising. Information systems scholars have examined, in great depth, various domains of crowdfunding, including reward-based crowdfunding (e.g., KickStarter.com; Burtch et al. 2015, Kim and Hann 2019), debt-based crowdfunding (e.g., Prosper .com; Lin et al. 2013, Liu et al. 2015), equity-based crowdfunding (e.g., Seedrs.com; Bapna 2019), and donation-based crowdfunding (e.g., DonorsChoose.org). Whereas many early studies in each of these domains typically focused on the transactional efficiency of crowdfunding, recent studies have begun to examine its indirect and offline influences (e.g., Roma et al. 2018, Signori and Vismara 2018, Burtch and Chan 2019). Our study extends this line of research into the domain of online education crowdfunding. We are among the first to document its influence on the academic performance of students who are not directly involved in the fundraising process. More importantly, the magnifying effect of local donors on students’ academic performance further highlights the potential synergy between online and offline activities, which is also novel in the crowdfunding research literature.

## 2. Related Literature and Related Literature and Theoretical Developm

<sup>2.1. Economics of Education</sup>Long-standing debates have focused on whether additional school resources can influence students’ academic achievement. This debate is especially pertinent given the limited state and local support provided for public education (Kreisman and Steinberg 2019). A vast number of studies on this topic have been published. Specifically, Coleman et al. (1966) provided the first large-scale U.S. report linking educational resources to students’ academic performance; however, Coleman et al. (1966) failed to identify any definitive positive influence associated with these educational resources. Since then, numerous studies have been conducted, but unfortunately these studies have reached two generally opposing conclusions.

On the one hand, Hanushek and his coauthors conducted a series of studies, many of which were meta-analyses (e.g., Hanushek 1989, 1997, 2003), and their conclusion was somewhat pessimistic: “Providing more funding or a different distribution of funding is unlikely to improve student achievement” (Hanushek 1997, p. 153). However, other researchers using the same meta-analysis methods (e.g., Greenwald et al. 1996) found a small but positive relationship between school resources and student achievement The major differences between these two lines of meta-analysis are the synthesis methods and samples included in the analyses. The former approach (represented by Hanushek and his coauthors) tallied the number of studies that reported different results (i.e., positive relationships, insignificant relationships, and negative relationships) and focused on the direction and significance of the relationships between variables. In contrast, the latter approach focused on the actual values by measuring the differences between school resources and students’ academic performance. Meta-analyses using the latter approach also included only studies that controlled for socioeconomic factors and students’ prior academic performance.

As a result, the conclusions from these two lines of meta-analysis appear to be largely inconclusive.

A wave of recent studies, however, provides consistent, convincing evidence that an increase in school resources can improve students’ academic performance (Abott et al. 2020). Using exogenous shocks from reforms in state educational financing, these studies (e.g., Jackson et al. 2016, Hyman 2017) conclude that providing additional resources does in fact improve students’ academic performance. However, providing these additional resources does not always yield the same benefits. For example, resources that directly affect students have been shown to provide more tangible improvements in students’ academic performance, such as resources that directly decrease the teacher–student ratio (Kreisman and Steinberg 2019), decrease the cost per student (Gigliotti and Sorensen 2018), and increase instructional materials, such as textbooks (Holden 2016). Financial resources are less effective if spent on capital investments, such as constructions (Martorell et al. 2016). From the perspective of students, additional resources are more likely to improve academic outcomes among students from low-income families than students from wealthy families (e.g., Hyman 2017). A common theme in these studies is that they all focus on school resources (a) allocated through traditional channels, (b) provided by governments, and (c) administered in school districts. We contribute to this literature by examining the influence of school resources requested by public school teachers and obtained through online education crowdfunding, a new and uncharted approach to public school financing.

## 2.2. Crowdfunding and Its Impacts

<sup>2.2. Crowdfunding and Its Impacts</sup>Crowdfunding enables individuals and organizations to raise funds from online crowds (i.e., contributors) for various purposes (Mollick 2014), and it is one of the prime examples of how information and internet technologies have transformed the finance industry. In fact, crowdfunding is one of the key areas of fintech research. Not surprisingly, crowdfunding has received significant attention from researchers during the past decade. An abundance of studies, especially empirical studies, have been conducted related to various types of crowdfunding (Agrawal et al. 2014). In particular, a majority of the existing studies focus on factors or platform features that determine the success probability of fundraising efforts. These platform features include social connections (Lin et al. 2013, Liu et al. 2015), spatial proximity (Agrawal et al. 2011, Lin and Viswanathan 2015), and information disclosure (Burtch et al. 2015).

More recently, some researchers have begun to examine what happens after entrepreneurs successfully raise funds through online crowdfunding platforms (Signori and Vismara 2018). The motivation of investors, backers, or donors in these studies is to obtain financial and/or nonfinancial benefits by contributing funds, and these benefits cannot be realized if the campaigns to which they contribute do not deliver on their promises (Vanacker et al. 2019). Many of these studies focus on equity-based crowdfunding and examine whether firms that are able to raise funds successfully through online crowdfunding platforms fare better than those that are unable to do so in terms of various performance metrics, such as survival, future fundraising efforts, innovation capacity, and financial indicators. The results are mixed. For example, firms that successfully raise funds through online crowdfunding platforms tend to have a higher likelihood of failure (Signori and Vismara 2018) and are not always able to secure future fundraising. The influence on the innovation capacities of firms that successfully raise funds through online crowdfunding platforms (e.g., patent applications and patents granted) is also mixed. For example, Walthoff-Borm et al. (2018) presented evidence in support of the positive influence of online crowdfunding on innovation, but Eldridge et al. (2019) did not find such evidence. The mixed results in this literature stream are likely attributable to the fact that (a) equity-based crowdfunding is relatively new and (b) the number of successful exits is small, which has resulted in insufficient data regarding this highly regulated market (Vanacker et al. 2019).

Studies on debt-based crowdfunding (also known as peer-to-peer lending) also have examined postfunding performance. For example, whether borrowers repay their loans is undoubtedly an important aspect of their business models and an important factor in lender behavior. Naturally, many studies of this type of crowdfunding have long paid attention to postfunding performance (e.g., Lin et al. 2013, Iyer et al. 2015, Hildebrand et al. 2016). As expected, a nontrivial proportion of loans default, although some of these risky loans can still be funded because of the higher interest rates that they offer up front.

Studies on reward-based crowdfunding also have examined postfunding performance, that is, whether fundraisers deliver on their promised rewards. Despite several high-profile cases involving failures or outright fraudulent activities (e.g., the iBackPack scam; see Federal Trade Commission 2019), the vast majority of fundraisers seem to fulfill their obligations despite some delays (Mollick 2014, Mollick and Kuppuswamy 2014).

To the best of our knowledge, our study is one of the first<sup>2</sup> to examine what happens after successful funding using an online donation-based crowdfunding platform within the important context of public education. In another context featuring online donation-based crowdfunding, Burtch and Chan (2019) are among the first to try and establish a link between online medical crowdfunding and offline bankruptcy rates using county-level bankruptcy filing data. The significance of this particular donation-based context is that in other types of crowdfunding, such as debt crowdfunding and equity crowdfunding, fundraisers assume significant and explicit obligations to the individuals who contribute funds, and fundraisers face severe consequences if they do not perform. For example, if fundraisers seek funds in the form of a loan, their credit score will be affected by whether they repay the loan. Likewise, the future funding of entrepreneurs is affected by whether they are able to realize or exceed performance metrics that they claimed when they requested funds. However, in online donationbased education crowdfunding, contributors of funds are generally well meaning (e.g., they have no expectation of financial returns), and the pressure to deliver on the promises that teachers make seems superficially low. In other words, the level of trust between the donors and the teachers is very different from the relationship between the supply-and-demand aspects of funds in other types of crowdfunding, which renders this form of crowdfunding, in a sense, even more vulnerable to opportunistic behaviors on the part of fundraisers. These dangers further highlight the importance of the empirical investigations that we are conducting.

## 2.3. Education Crowdfunding and Education Crowdfun

<sup>Student Performance</sup>For public education in the United States, typically, school funding decisions are made by government officials and school district administrators (Kreisman and Steinberg 2019). After school districts receive their funding, they maintain considerable control and decision-making power over the use and allotment of these funds. However, even with the best intentions, these decision makers may not possess first-hand knowledge of the needs in each classroom, for example, the specific materials and activities necessary to effectively motivate student learning. The inefficiency of these rigid allocation methods is partly due to the inefficient distribution of funding across administrative units (Dodson and Garrett 2004). In fact, because teachers can only passively rely on the funds allocated to them, this method actually undermines teaching efficiency, which in turn leads to adverse educational outcomes. This inefficiency may partially explain the absence of a positive influence of funding on students’ academic performance represented in the earlier literature.

Online education crowdfunding differs dramatically from this top-down approach. Two important differences between these types of funding include the <sup>speci</sup>fi<sup>city</sup> of the funding request as well as the amount of <sup>planning</sup> required from the teachers. Teachers must initially draw on first-hand, “front-line” experience derived from their interactions with students. Based on this experience, teachers are then able to identify the resources required to improve students learning experience and therefore increase their academic performance (Pearson and Moomaw 2006). Then, just as when entrepreneurs seek funding, the teachers must think from the point of view of donors and carefully make a case to convince potential donors to contribute. For example, teachers must describe specific activities that they intend to carry out and how such activities fit into their course curriculum. These planning and persuasive efforts must be made if teachers want to succeed in raising funds, and even then, there is still no guarantee that teachers will receive the funds they request. Nevertheless, through this planning process, teachers are able to develop a clearer understanding of their students’ needs and develop strategies that motivate their students to learn. Even if the fundraising campaigns are unsuccessful, the students of teachers who attempt to raise funds may still benefit from the teachers’ planning efforts and thus experience some academic performance improvement.

The second notable feature of this fundraising process, compared with traditional education funding, is the <sup>validation</sup> and <sup>motivational</sup> effects that come with successful funding. Not all crowdfunding campaigns initiated by teachers are successful. Receiving funding in this process is a clear validation that donors believe in the teachers’ visions for those educational activities, which likely will result in increased motivation among teachers (McClelland 1978).

The third feature of education crowdfunding is the <sup>personal connection</sup> it affords between the donors and the teachers they support. Many donors contribute financially to specific teachers based on personal connections; these donors can be the parents of students or simply local residents who care about students’ academic performance (Posey-Maddox 2013) Teachers are more likely to feel an obligation to reciprocate and honor the trust placed in them by exerting stronger effort, which is likely to lead to higher academic performance among students. Teachers are also more likely to be held accountable because of the close proximity of these donors and, perhaps more importantly, the fact that the teachers themselves are personally associated with the campaigns.

For these reasons, online education crowdfunding has great potential to correctly and effectively allocate funds to the most promising projects championed by teachers. As a result, it is more likely to help improve students’ academic performance. In other words, our working hypothesis is that students’ academic performance should improve after their teachers receive donations through online education crowdfunding.

## 3. Empirical Context, Data, and Variables 3. Empirical Context, Data, and Variable

The context of our study is DonorsChoose.org, one of the largest and most well-known online education crowdfunding platforms in the United States. This platform allows K–12 educators to request donations in order to purchase classroom materials (e.g., books and technology) or pay for school activities (e.g., school field trips or special events). (Online Appendix A provides an example of an authentic project for which a teacher requested donations.) A typical fundraising process unfolds in several steps. First, a teacher must register with DonorsChoose.org by providing personal and employment information, including the name of his or her school, contact information for the school’s principal, and contact information for colleagues who can serve as references. Second, after the registration is approved by the site administrators at DonorsChoose.org, the teacher is able to submit a request for donations to a project. The request should describe the motivation for the project, the amount of money required, and a detailed list of materials needed for the project. The request also should include student demographic information, such as the grade level, the poverty level (if applicable), and the number of students that the donations will support. After the request is submitted and verified (most importantly, to ensure that it originated from a teacher), it will appear on the platform.

Potential donors are able to browse the projects on the platform and select those to which they would like to donate. Like many crowdfunding platforms, DonorsChoose.org uses an all-or-nothing funding rule: If the total amount raised does not reach the requested amount by the deadline (normally within four months of the posted date), the donors receive refunds. If a project is fully funded and the total amount of money requested is raised, the platform uses the donations to purchase the requested materials and delivers them to the teacher’s school; the teachers have no direct access to the funds raised. Finally, the site sends emails to a randomly selected subset of the teacher’s colleagues to publicize the success of the teacher’s campaign and to the school principal to announce the arrival of the purchased materials.

Established in 2000, DonorsChoose.org expanded to all 50 states in the United States in 2007. As of April 2019, the platform had raised \$812 million from 3.6 million donors at the request of approximately 500,000 teachers across 80,000 schools (DonorsChoose.org 2019). In addition, 81% of public schools in the United States employ at least one teacher who has posted a request on the site. Approximately 60% of projects are fully funded, and among them, 36% requested classroom supplies, 30% requested technology, and 21% requested books.

## 3.2. Students' Academic Performance Data from the Students Academic Performance D

We compiled a detailed student performance data set from K–12 schools in California from several different sources. Although DonorsChoose.org operates in all U.S. states, California is uniquely appropriate for this study for two reasons. First, California is one of the few states where we can obtain data on student performance and link it to individual teachers.<sup>3</sup> Second, California provides longitudinal data that reflect public school student performance based on a standardized test: the CSTs. The ability to link these longitudinal data to individual teachers is critical because it allows us to identify changes in student cohort performance (if any) before and after teachers receive donations from DonorsChoose.org, where a student cohort is defined and measured using a quadruple label consisting of teacher, grade, subject, and academic year. Because DonorsChoose.org began its operations in California on October 16, 2004, this date marks the beginning of our sample period. Our sample period lasts for 10 years and ends in July 2013.

We collected data on teachers and the student cohorts that they taught (including their academic performance) from the start of the 2003–2004 academic year (August 2003) to the end of the 2012–2013 academic year (May 2013). We provide a brief descrip tion of our data compilation process here, and pres ent details in Online Appendix B. The first two data sets consist of (a) staff demographics and (b) staff assignments from the CDE. Staff demographic data consist of rich information about teachers for each year, such as the names of schools where they teach, the numbers of years they have taught, and the names of the subjects they teach (CST subjects). The staff assignment data contain information about instructors’ teaching each year, such as the names of the schools where they teach, subjects and grades they teach, and how many students they teach Matching these two data sets allowed us to generate the entire teaching histories of individual teachers. We omitted teachers who were not full time because DonorsChoose.org does not allow them to raise funds. We also omitted teachers who taught for only one year because our empirical strategy requires a panel data set. This process yielded a teaching history data set consisting of 666,798 teachers.

The second data source is students’ academic performance data from the CSTs, which were required of all California public school students in grades 2 to 11 between 1998 and 2013. Students are tested at the end of each spring semester, in four subject areas: math, English, science, and history–social science. Among these subjects, we focus on math and English, grades 2 through 8, because these are the two subjects in which students were consistently tested. Test results for the other two subjects (science and history–social science) were available only for students in grades 9 through 11 and therefore excluded. The CSTs data set includes test scores, grade levels, subjects, the numbers of students who completed the tests, and the number and percentage of students at each achievement level (e.g., advanced, proficient, and basic). As is common, and to protect the privacy of students, these test data are captured at the student cohort level (i.e., a group of students at a school who take a CST in a subject in a grade in a given year), not at the individual student level. We matched this data set with the previous teaching history data set via grade level, subject, and year to create a teacher-grade-subject-performance data set over time. Furthermore, because our empirical analysis requires test scores from the prior year, we exclude teachers of students in the second grade. This is the lowest grade level at which CSTs are administered, so no information for the prior year is available. We also excluded student cohorts in the sixth grade because this is the first year of middle school, and because of the lack of individual student data, we cannot track students to the elementary schools in which they were previously enrolled. In the end, we retain test scores of students who are taught by teachers of math and English in grades 3 through 5 and grades 7 and 8. These exclusions bring the total number of teachers in our sample to 55,086.

We then extract information about school finances and students’ performance from annual financial information, student poverty (free or reduced-price meals) data, and Academic Performance Index data provided by the CDE, respectively. These data sets contain information about the average expense per student per year, the percentage of students who applied for a free or reduced-price lunch, and the ratio of students to teachers. We merge this data set with the student performance data set via California’s countydistrict-school (CDS) codes.

We subsequently link this education data set to the DonorsChoose.org data set by first converting the CDS codes in the education data set to the NCES school IDs. Then we extract grade information for campaigns in the DonorsChoose.org data set. The DonorsChoose.org project data uses a range of grades (e.g., grades 4–6). We implement a combination of manual and automated text analytic techniques to extract the grade information for each project from a variety of sections on DonorsChoose.org—for example, project descriptions, notes on needs, letters of appreciation, and completion notes. Third, we focus on projects related to math and English for grades 2 through 8. We omit the small number of projects completed between May and June because most standardized testing in California occurs between mid-May and June each year.<sup>4</sup> Finally, we match these projects to the education data set according to schools, grade levels, and CST subjects.

Our final data set includes project information, such as the amount of funding requested and raised; funding status (e.g., whether the project was fully funded); the number of students supported; donation information, such as the amount of each donation, the location of each donor (e.g., ZIP code), and the time of each donation; school information; teacher information; and most importantly, student performance information. The final data set includes 55,086 teachers at 4,007 schools in California. Of these 55,086 teachers, 8,309 posted 28,259 CST-related projects and received donations for 18,590 projects.

We use this data set to compare (a) the academic performance of student cohorts when teachers applied for and received donations from DonorsChoose.org with (b) the academic performance of student cohorts when teachers applied for but did not receive donations In a later analysis (Subsection 5.2), we further compare (a) the academic performance of student cohorts when teachers applied for but did not receive donations with (b) the academic performance of student cohorts when teachers did not apply for funding at all.

## 3.3. Key Outcome and Independent Variables

Our unit of analysis is at the student cohort level (teacher-grade-subject-academic year). A student cohort, denoted by the subscript <sup>tgsy</sup>, is taught by teacher <sup>t</sup> in grade <sup>g</sup> in subject <sup>s</sup> in academic year <sup>y</sup> (hereafter, year). We measure academic performance $( S c o r e _ { t g s y } )$ using the average CST score received by a student cohort. Such a cohort analysis allows us to link the academic performance of a student cohort to a particular teacher. CST scores range from 150 to 600. Naturally, a higher score indicates better academic performance. Scores on the CSTs are widely used in previous education research (California Department of Education 2016).

The main independent variable for our study is <sup>ReceivingDonation</sup>tgsy, a binary variable that equals one if teacher <sup>t</sup> applied and received donations from DonorsChoose.org to support projects for his or her cohort (in grade <sup>g</sup> in subject <sup>s</sup> in year <sup>y</sup>), and zero if this teacher applied for but did not receive funding. In other words, the main treatment that we are inter ested in is applying for and successfully receiving donations (i.e., using “applied and got funding” as the treatment group). We compare these cases to cases in which teachers applied for funding but were unsuccessful (i.e., using “applied but did not get funding” as the control group). For some tests reported later in the paper, we also use two independent variables related to the source of the donations. One of these variables, GivingPageDonation $P c t _ { t g s y } ,$ , is the percentage of donations obtained through a “giving page,” which is essentially a referral mechanism through which enthusiastic donors are able to invite their friends to pitch in and help provide teachers with donations. The second variable, <sup>LocalDonors</sup>tgsy, captures the number of local donors for teachers, that is, donors who report the same ZIP code as that of the school where the fundraising teacher teaches. We also include an exhaustive list of control variables to account for the heterogeneity among teachers, student cohorts, schools, and school districts. Definitions of variables and descriptive statistics are provided in Table 1.

The main comparison that we are interested in is whether students of teachers who applied for and received online crowdfunding donations achieve higher academic performance. Within the literature on the economics of education (Duncan and Murnane 2011), a common approach of modeling students’ academic performance is to treat academic performance as a cumulative process in which current achievement is built on students’ initial endowment $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ , inherent ability) as well as the influence of individual, family, and school factors $( \mathrm { c f . \ ^ { \prime \prime } i n p u t ^ { \prime \prime } }$ in a production process; Harris and Sass 2011). This academic performance, in turn, is typically operationalized as a linear additive educational production function of student ability, teacher characteristics, family background, school features, and school district features (Rivkin et al. 2005) through a value-added modeling (VAM) approach. A large number of studies show that the VAM approach produces estimates that are very close to those produced by experiments (e.g., Abdulkadiroglu˘ et al. 2011, Deming et al. 2014). We therefore follow this well-established approach. Specifically, we model student achievement as a function of lagged student achievement metric (Todd and Wolpin 2003, Rivkin et al. 2005) and the contemporaneous input of family background, teacher characteristics, and school and school district information.

In addition to academic performance in the prior year, we further control for several other student cohort characteristics that the literature has shown to influence student performance. These student cohort characteristics include the percentage of new learners of English, that is, those who are new (less than 12 months) to the English language and therefore less likely to do well on tests (Jepsen and De Alth 2005); the average education level of students’ parents (Guryan et al. 2008); and the percentage of students who applied for a free or reduced-price lunch, a variable that reflects their family’s financial background (Van der Klaauw 2008).

For teachers, we control for their teaching experience $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ number of years teaching, measured for each academic year), because teaching experience naturally influences students’ academic performance (Rockoff 2004). We additionally include the number of assignments given by the teachers, which has been used in the literature as a proxy for teacher motivation (Rønning 2011). We also control for class size and cost per student, which reflect school and school district resources and policies (Hoxby 2000, Marlow 2000).

We represent contemporaneous input for a given cohort by a vector of time-varying covariates capturing information about the student cohort, their family background (<sup>Free</sup> $u n c h P c t _ { t g s y } ,$ <sub>,</sub> NewEngLearner $P c t _ { t g s y } ,$ , and $\overline { { P a r e n t E d u L e v e l _ { t g s y } } } ) _ { . }$ , teacher characteristics $( T e a c h i n g Y e a r s _ { t y }$ and <sup>Assignments</sup>tgsy), and school and school district characteristics (<sup>Expense</sup> $P e r S t u d e n t _ { t y }$ and $C l a s s S i z e _ { t y } )$ . ## 4.3. Addressing Identification Concerns

<sup>4.3. Addressing Identi cation Concerns</sup>Establishing causality and ruling out alternative explanations can be challenging with archival data. The approach we use so far has been well established in the literature, and our estimates should be close to those obtained through experimental studies (e.g., Abdulkadiroglu et al.˘ 2011, Deming et al. 2014). Nevertheless, we conduct additional tests to address potential endogeneity concerns. Our results are remarkably consistent and robust. For brevity, we report here the details of the instrumental variable and the propensity score matching (PSM) approaches. Additional tests, including a regression discontinuity design, are available in the online appendix.

Not all teachers seek donations online, and teachers must apply before they can receive online crowdfunding donations. Teachers who receive these online crowdfunding donations may also be different in unobservable ways from teachers who do not receive donations. To simulta neously address the potential concerns associated with unobservable factors (e.g., inherent motivations) and selection, we implement a Heckman (1979) selection correction model with an instrumental variable. The instrumental variable we use was inspired by Dranove et al. (2014), who used the number of same-alliance hospitals that had adopted electronic medical records (EMRs) as an instrument for a foca hospital’s EMR adoption. We use the number of teachers who already received donations, taught the same subject as the focal teacher at other schools in the same school district, and are within two miles of the focal teacher’s school (<sup>LocalPeers</sup>) as an instrument for our main independent variable, $R e c e i v i n g D o n a t i o n _ { t g s y } .$ . This instrument is conceptually valid because donations received by teachers at nearby schools increase the likelihood that a focal teacher will become aware of this funding channel and therefore increase the likelihood of the teacher receiving such donations; meanwhile, the funding activities of teachers at other schools should not have any first-order impacts on this teacher’s students. We also verify that this instrument is statistically strong, with an <sup>F</sup>-statistic higher than 10 (Stock and Yogo 2002).

We follow the two-stage least squares procedure recommended by Wooldridge (2010) and use the “xtheckman” package in Stata 16, where the first stage selection includes a teacher random effects model.<sup>5</sup> The results are reported in Table 3. The coefficient of <sup>ReceivingDonation</sup>tgsy is positive and statistically significant $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . This result indicates that receiving donations increases students’ cohort test scores by 1.274 points, even after we control for selection bias. This result is highly consistent with the results from the main analysis reported earlier. The effects of control variables are also consistent with our previous estimates. Results of the first-stage estimations are provided in Table C-1 of Online Appendix C.

The instrumental variable approach provides reassuring evidence. Nevertheless, to be cautious, we also use an alternative approach based on PSM to further address endogeneity concerns. The PSM method is widely used in the information systems literature (e.g., Chan and Ghose 2014, Burtch and Chan 2019). It is based on the idea that biases can be reduced when comparing outcomes between similar treated and controlled observations (Rosenbaum and Rubin 1983). The similarity is measured using a propensity score, that is, the probability of receiving treatment (in our case, receiving donations). The underlying logic is to create matched pairs of treated and nontreated observations with similar observed characteristics and very similar propensities of receiving treatment, which facilitates causal inference (Sun and Zhu 2013).

We first compute the propensity score using the same set of variables (Section 4.1) for receiving donations (Online Appendix C, Table C-2). Next, we use the nearest neighbor matching without replacement, based on the estimated propensity scores. We use the standard caliper size (0.25 of the standard deviation of propensity scores; Rosenbaum and Rubin 1985). To evaluate whether our PSM procedure achieves our goal of matching the treatment group to a similar control group, we check the balance of variables in matched pairs (Online Appendix C, Table C-3). Finally, we compare the average treatment effect of receiving crowdfunding donations to not receiving crowdfunding donations on the academic performance of students within the common support samples. The results suggest that receiving crowdfunding donations will increase test scores by 2.83 points compared with the control group (applied but did not get funding). This result further confirms our finding that receiving donations from education crowdfunding does exert a positive influence on students’ academic performance. Following Sun et al. ## 4.4. Robustness and Additional Tests

<sup>4.4. Robustness and Additional Tests</sup>We conduct many additional tests to check the robustness of our findings and to rule out alternative explanations. For brevity, we report several of our primary findings in the next subsection and additional findings in Online Appendix D. Our main results are highly robust.

4.4.1. Alternative Independent Variable: Donation Amount. The main dependent variable in our previous analyses is a binary variable (i.e $. , R e c e i v i n g \bar { D o } n a t i o n _ { t g s y } )$ . A natural question is whether the donation amount plays a role in improving students’ academic performance: Does students’ academic achievement improve more when teachers receive a larger amount of donations? To answer this question, we use the same data set and rerun all model specifications in our main analyses, but this time we replace $R e c e i v i n g D o n a t i o n _ { t g s y }$ with the variable <sup>DonationAmount</sup>tgsy, the amount of donations that teacher <sup>t</sup> received for his or her students. For ease of interpretation, we scaled this variable by 1,000 (i.e., divided by 1,000). We set DonationAmoun $\xi _ { t g s y } = 0$ for our control group (applied but did not get funding).

We report the results in Table D-1 of Online $\mathrm { A p \mathrm { - } }$ pendix D. All coefficient estimates of <sup>DonationAmount</sup>tgsy are statistically significant $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ and positive, suggesting that larger donations lead to more academic performance gains. The estimates suggest that an additional \$1,000 donation will increase the average CST score of a student cohort by around 1.7 points.

These effects are nontrivial, according to our discussions in Section 4.2.

4.4.2. Alternative Measurement for Academic Performance. So far, we use the average CST scores of student cohorts to measure student performance. CSTs also provide a less-detailed but perhaps easier-to-digest performance metric for each student cohort, that is, the percentage of students in each cohort whose CST score reached the following levels: (a) advanced (the best), (b) proficient, (c) basic, (d) below basic, and (e) far below basic (the worst). We test whether our results are consistent with this alternative performance metric.

We first use the percentage of a student cohort whose CST performance is at or above “proficient” as the main dependent variable; this is because California’s stated target goal is for all students to achieve this level of academic performance. To ensure robustness, we create two additional performance measurements by varying the threshold. One is the percentage of students within a cohort who achieved “advanced,” the highest level, and the other is the percentage of students who achieved at least “basic.” The rest of the model specification remains the same. Results (Online Appendix D, Table D-2) consistently show that receiving donations increases students’ academic performance regardless of the thresholds we use. For example, receiving crowdfunding donations increases the percentage of students who achieve an “advanced” level by 1.117 $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ ) percentage points.

All our analyses reported above, as well as in the online appendix, demonstrate consistently that the academic performance of students increases after their teachers receive crowdfunding donations. A natural follow-up question, then, is the mechanism behind this effect.

## 5. Behind the Impact: The Role of Behind the Impact: T<sub>fi</sub>

Non nancial Factors<sub>The influence of crowdfunding donations on the ac-</sub> ademic performance of students that we have identified so far can arise for (a) financial reasons (because donations provide financial resources), (b) nonfinancial reasons that drive behavioral changes, or (c) a combination of both. Given the fact that donations first and foremost provide financial resources, it is natural and intuitive that financial reasons play a role: if crowdfunding donations had no financial value, they simply could not satisfy the needs identified by the teachers. To understand the mechanism, therefore, is to understand whether the positive impact that we observe is entirely due to the financial value of these donations. If not, it would suggest that nonfinancial factors also contribute to the positive influence on students’ academic performance. 、6

Donations from well-meaning donors cannot translate directly into an increase in students’ academic performance. Teachers are the most important link in this process because they initiate the entire process by first identifying the specific needs of their students and building cases to convince potential donors. Furthermore, after the funds are raised and converted into the resources that the teachers requested, the teachers again use the materials to implement the activities that they envisioned. Therefore, to understand the mechanism behind the influence of online education crowdfunding on students’ academic performance, it is important to understand how crowdfunding donations influence the behavior of teachers.

We discussed some of these possible mechanisms in Section 2.3 when hypothesizing the influence of crowdfunding donations on students’ academic performance. These possible mechanisms include (a) the validation and motivational effect of completing a successful crowdfunding campaign, (b) the specificity of the requests, (c) the requirement of creating and communicating a persuasive plan, and (d) the social connections between donors and teachers. These aspects are not necessarily orthogonal to each other, and our goal is not to empirically distinguish one from another (see Endnote 5); rather, they represent various aspects of nonfinancial influences on teachers that may help explain students’ performance improvements. Our goal in this section is to design tests to verify the presence of these nonfinancial factors. If such nonfinancial factors do exist, it would suggest that there is a potential multiplier effect that we should recognize in the power of online education crowdfunding.

## 5.1. Community Effect: Funding Sources That Community Effect: Funding Sources That<sub>fl</sub> Re ect Community Involvem

<sup>Positive Impact of Donations</sup>If the positive influence on student performance that we have documented so far is attributable only to the dollar value and the financial nature of the online donations, then the source of funds should not matter. Therefore, the first test that we conduct is to check whether different sources of donations affect student performance differently. We focus on donation sources that reflect the social bonds between the teachers and their donors because, as discussed earlier, such connections can generate nonfinancial motivations for teachers to make the most efficient use of the funds even though there is no explicit obligation. Some of these donors are among teachers’ social connections, such as the parents of students, neighbors, or local residents. Because these donors are highly visible to the teachers, and vice versa, the presence of such social bonds in the fundraising campaigns should encourage (if not oblige) the teachers to reciprocate the trust (Sobel 2005, Leider et al. 2009) and exert extra efforts to improve students’ academic performance.

We test this conjecture in two complementary ways. The first is related to local donors, that is, donors who share the same ZIP code as that of the school where the teachers teach. Even when the dollar amount of crowdfunding donations is the same, a teacher who receives donations from more local donors will be more likely to exert extra efforts in teaching. That, in turn, should lead to better academic performance of students, all else being equal. To test this, we revise our main model and add an interaction term between $R e c e i v i n g D o n a t i o n _ { t g s y }$ and the number of local donors $( L o c a l D o n o r s _ { t g s y } )$ as the key independent variable, while retaining the same dependent variable, <sup>Score</sup>tgsy.

In our second test, we leverage the “giving pages” feature of DonorsChoose.org.<sup>7</sup> Created by passionate donors to encourage their friends and social connections to support specific campaigns, the giving pages are designed to drive traffic and donations for teachers. Donors who donate through these giving pages are either first- or second-order social connections of the teachers—that is, they are connected to a teacher either directly or through the creator of a giving page. If donations from DonorsChoose.org merely serve a financial role, then regardless of whether funds originate through a giving page, they should have the same influence on students’ academic performance. However, if donations raised through the giving pages result in higher academic performance among students, this result would lend support to our argument that nonfinancial mechanisms play a role in the main effect that we identified.

## 5.2. Planning Effect: Student Performance

Planning Effect: Student Performance <sup>Improves Even When Fundraising Attempts Fai</sup>If the positive influence of education crowdfunding is attributable only to the financial value of the donations, then teachers who attempt to raise funds online but ultimately are not successful (the control group, applied but did not get funding) should not see their students’ academic performance improve because they do not receive any funding. However, compared with teachers who do not attempt to raise funds online, those teachers who invest their time and effort in the fundraising campaigns still could benefit from the exercise, because they are required to think about how to make cases for their requests. They must think carefully about the strengths, weaknesses, and needs of their students as well as which resources will be most cost-effective in improving the student experience. This type of thinking and planning is beneficial for teachers (and their students) regardless of whether the fundraising efforts ultimately succeed or not. Such a planning effect is another nonfinancial mechanism that can lead to the positive impact on student performance.

We empirically test this planning effect by comparing the academic performance of student cohorts across different years for the same teacher $( \mathrm { i . e . , } w i t h i n \mathrm { - }$ <sup>teacher</sup> comparison). Specifically, we compare student cohort performance for the years when a teacher applied but did not receive funding (i.e., using an indicator $\bar { \boldsymbol { A } } t t e m p t _ { t g s y } = 1 )$ against years when the same teacher did not apply for funding $( { \mathrm { i . e . , } } A t t e m p t _ { t g s y } = 0 )$ . To be conservative, we exclude observations of years after the teacher applied and received funding (i.e., successful attempts). This process results in a data set that includes ${ \dot { 1 } } 2 , 6 1 2$ cases from 3,245 teachers. The outcome variable of interest is still the average CST score for each cohort. The main independent variable is $A t t e m p t _ { t g s y } ,$ an indicator of “made unsuccessful attempts” versus “made no attempts” for a given teacher’s cohort in year <sup>y</sup>. We estimate a model with the teacher-, subject-, and grade-fixed effects, focusing on teachers who had unsuccessful attempts. In other words, we compare the performance of their student cohorts in years when they made these unsuccessful attempts against the years when they did not, given the same grade level and the same subject. The model includes the same set of control variables as before.

Table 6 reports the estimates. The coefficient of $A t t e m p t _ { t g s y }$ is positive and statistically significant, suggesting that student cohorts achieved higher average test scores by 1.012 points $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ during years in which the teachers attempted fundraising (albeit unsuccessfully) than years in which the teachers did not attempt to raise funds. This lends further support to our argument that a nonfinancial mechanism—that is, the planning effect—is in part responsible for the positive impact that we identified. In alternative tests across teachers (<sup>between-teacher</sup> comparison, not reported here because of space constraints), we find that the same results hold when we compare teachers who attempted but failed to raise funds against those who never attempted to raise funds.

## 6. Discussions and Conclusions

6. Discussions and Conclusions<sub>Online crowdfunding represents a novel method of</sub> raising funds enabled by the growth of information and internet technologies. Since its emergence, it has been gradually recognized and embraced by various stakeholders, on both the demand and supply sides of capital, as well as researchers and regulators. This method of fundraising has been used in widespread applications and enjoyed extraordinary growth in debt, equity, rewards, and donation contexts. Many papers have been published on various aspects of the funding process, such as factors that lead to a higher likelihood of fundraising success, as well as rational and emotional influences in the decision-making process of the supply side of funds (e.g., investors). However, studies that focus on what happens after fundraising campaigns remain a minority, especially in donation-based crowdfunding for public education. One reason is that donors do not expect a financial return. Therefore, the outcomes that occur after fundraising success have not received the same level of attention as those of other forms of crowdfunding (e.g., the default in debt-based crowdfunding, or exits in equity-based crowdfunding).

However, understanding how donations from online crowdfunding are used is by no means a trivial matter. It has implications for the long-term viability of education crowdfunding in general. In addition, if there is no empirically detectable impact on key metrics of student performance, then donors or even these platforms may be well advised to explore alternative means of soliciting and using these funds. Teachers efforts in crafting fundraising documents may also have been misplaced. It is therefore critical to fill this gap in the literature.

We address this gap by compiling a highly detailed and comprehensive data set from multiple sources related to K–12 public schools in California. We link teachers’ successful fundraising campaigns in support of students to improvements in the students academic performance. We use methods commonly accepted in the economics of education literature and further subject our findings to a wide range of robustness checks, including tests to address selection and endogeneity concerns. All tests consistently show that when teachers successfully raise funds through DonorsChoose.org, their students enjoy an increase in the average test scores.

We then extend our analysis further to investigate factors that play a role in translating these donations into higher academic performance among students. Although financial factors are to be expected to play a role—ostensibly because donations represent additional resources for identified needs that convince a crowd—the nonfinancial factors that influence students’ academic performance are much less obvious. We design and conduct several tests that verify the presence of these nonfinancial factors. First, even though from a financial point of view the source of online donations is largely irrelevant, we find that the positive influence on student performance is greater when a higher number of local donors participate in a teacher’s crowdfunding campaign. The positive influence is also higher when a greater percentage of the funds are donated through DonorsChoose.org’s giving pages, that is, referrals made by enthusiastic supporters of specific teachers’ campaigns. Second, and more interestingly, students’ academic performance improves even if the teachers attempt but fail to raise funds. This finding suggests that benefits arise for students when teachers are required to plan specific pedagogical activities as part of their fundraising efforts. All in all, our findings suggest that nonfinancial factors play an important role in the positive impact of education crowdfunding on students’ academic performance.

These results and findings are important for several reasons. First, as we discussed earlier, we fill a gap in the literature by focusing on the effective use of funds raised through donation-based education crowdfunding. The identification of the role of nonfinancial factors is also novel in this literature.

Second, our study documents empirical evidence for the long-term value of these fintech platforms. We show that these platforms not only help provide financial resources by connecting teachers with donors, but also generate indirect, nonfinancial benefits that may very well spill over to students who are not directly supported by these fundraising campaigns.

These benefits have not been systematically investigated before and should be recognized by stakeholders.

Third, our study contributes to policy discussions surrounding teachers’ use of these platforms. As mentioned in the introduction, K–12 administrators across the United States have yet to reach a consensus about the value of this emerging form of school financing. For example, it is wholeheartedly embraced by some school districts but outlawed in others. Our study contributes to this debate by empirically verifying the positive influence of online education crowdfunding on students’ academic performance, as well as the role of nonfinancial factors in this process. Administrators of individual schools as well as entire school districts may find it useful to take these additional benefits into account when reviewing their policies on this new education funding channel.

Fourth, the unique features of online education crowdfunding (compared with traditional education financing) and the positive influence identified in this paper indicate some potential directions for the broader policy discussions on education financing. As mentioned at the beginning of this paper, high-quality public education is critical in every country, yet public education funding in the United States has experienced steady declines over time. Thus, it has become even more important for school administrators and government officials to seek ways to deploy these limited financial resources more effectively. On the one hand, it might be prudent to encourage teacher autonomy in identifying the best ways to deploy funds in addition to the regular curriculum management. In other words, rather than a strictly top-down approach, it may be beneficial to consider a bottom-up approach so that school districts are able to more effectively leverage teachers’ practical ingenuity as well as their first-hand knowledge of students in deciding how best to allocate limited education resources. On the other hand, one important feature of education crowdfunding is the role of the online “crowds” that help screen different education initiatives. Even though these online crowds cannot participate in school budget discussions, it shows that involving additional external stakeholders in the budgeting process, such as parents and local residents, may be worth exploring. Though crowds are not always wise, a budgeting process that takes into account more diverse opinions and options may very well generate better academic outcomes for students and other stakeholders.

Last but not least, our test on the unsuccessful fundraising attempts is one of the first of its kind in the broader crowdfunding literature. Most empirical investigations into the consequences of crowdfunding (Bernstein et al. 2017) are based on entities that are successful in such attempts. We are the first to empirically verify that benefits to students can arise even when such attempts fail to obtain funding.

Our study is subject to a few limitations that may provide opportunities for future research. First, we use education and crowdfunding data only from California because data from other states are far less detailed or far less accessible. Our empirical framework, however, can be readily implemented or replicated using data from other states, especially because the data set from DonorsChoose.org is public. That being said, we report in Online Appendix E an entirely separate set of analyses exploiting the entry of DonorsChoose.org into different states in the United States using academic performance data at the state level. We find that after DonorsChoose.org becomes available in each state, students’ academic performance increases. This lends support to the argument that the effects we report in this paper may not be limited to California. Second, individual student data (i.e., test scores and demographics) are unavailable, due to privacy and regulatory concerns. This is not a first-order limitation because the online crowdfunding campaigns are not for individual students but, rather, for individual teachers. However, access to student-level data would enable researchers to not only investigate a variety of additional outcome variables of interest to educators and regulators, such as student retention, but also better account for changes in the composition of student cohorts across time. Third, because of the archival nature of our data, we are unable to isolate and quantify different types of nonfinancial motivations. This calls for future studies that will most likely rely on experimental approaches.
