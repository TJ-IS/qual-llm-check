# News-Induced Dynamic Networks for Market Signaling: Understanding the Impact of News on Firm Equity Value

Kun Chen,<sup>a</sup> Xin Li,<sup>b</sup> Peng Luo,<sup>c</sup> J. Leon Zhao<sup>d</sup>

Abstract. Firm relations, which inform the competitive environments of firms, are critica to firm operations and are often factored into investor decisions. Previous studies and practices have considered relatively stable and long-term business relations as an indicator of firm market value. Public news often reports on business relations, especially dynamic and short-term opportunities and challenges involving different partners. Learning abou firm relations from news is commonly done by human investors but has not been studied systematically in previous research, leading to a research opportunity in market signaling via dynamic firm relations. In this study, we propose a new approach to market signaling b leveraging text-mining methods to extract cobenefit/counter-benefit networks based on firms’ mutual or conflicting interests in business events. We find that the resulting networks in the long term are partially aligned with firm cooperation and competition re lationships, confirming their semantic implications for investor perception and attention. Our empirical study further shows that dynamic networks formed in a short time period (measured by firm centrality in networks) have significant impacts on firm equity value, after controlling for market activities and other information from news, such as volume, sentiment, and comentions. We show that dynamic networks can provide additional value in predicting firm equity value over stable networks. Moreover, the negative effects of counter-benefit networks emerge rapidly and persist longer than the positive effects o cobenefit networks. This study provides new insights into investor perception of news and suggests new research directions for financial text mining. Our research findings on market signaling via news-induced networks also have an impact on financial practices, such as market analysis and automatic trading.

## 1. Introduction

The success of a business depends on its competitive environment, a major part of which can be depicted by firm relations. Firms may face competitions on similar products/services or suppliers and benefit from cooperative opportunities with suppliers, consumers, or complementors, and they generally need to manage different business relations carefully (Gnyawali et al. 2006). Accordingly, understanding the business relations and competitive environment of a firm contributes to the assessment of its market potential.

Previous studies often focus on the relatively stable and long-term cooperation and competition relations of firms. For example, in the management literature, firm alliances have been identified as factors influencing firm behaviors and outcomes (Asness et al. 2000, Madhavan et al. 2004). The recent finance literature also reports that stock risks and returns are transmitted through trading and supply chain relations (Acemoglu et al. 2012, Wu 2016). Although these studies show the value of understanding business relations, they suffer from significant limitations. On the one hand, stable relations require efforts to obtain and update, and thus are not easy for individual investors. On the other hand, the stability of these relations makes them incapable of predicting the dynamics of the financial market. In this regard, previous studies often leverage stable relations to collect information and make inferences between related firms.

Public news, however, provides investors with a channel to observe firm cooperation and competition. News has always been a major channel for people to understand the dynamics of the economy and the market. It complements the low-frequency official statistics and financial reports in driving the financial market. Prior studies have confirmed that the appearance of certain events (Mahajan et al. 2008) and sentiments (Das and Chen 2007) in news influences investor behaviors and causes changes in the stock market. However, they often focus on investor perceptions of individual firms or industries. There is a lack of studies that exploit firm relations from news and assess their impacts on the financial market.

Public news provides a more dynamic and recent view of particular firms’ opportunities and challenges in relation to other firms. Such news may report on the formation of or change in firm relations or highlight existing firm relations that play an important role in reported events. The dynamic nature of news enables us to induce dynamic firm relations, which may or may not be aligned with firms’ stable strategic relations. Nevertheless, such news does update investor perceptions of recent firm operations and relations and influence their short-term investment decisions. Therefore, identifying dynamic firm relations from news enables a deeper analysis of investor perception and provides a new market signal that facilitates automatic trading, where both aims contribute to research and practice in the emerging fintech (financial technology) domain.

Because of the complicated business relations among firms and the variety of news writing styles, automatically extracting firm relations from news is not a trivial task. Extending prior explorations (Jindal and Liu 2006, Ma et al. 2009), this study adopts a textmining approach to identify firms’ mutual or conflicting interests in business events (Madhavan et al. 2004), from which we define firm cobenefit and counterbenefit networks. After illustrating the effectiveness of the proposed relation extraction algorithm, we show the relevance of the extracted networks to firm stable relations in the long term. This relevance hints at the feasibility of using dynamic networks to proxy for firm competitive environments that are being impressed to investors on a daily basis. Finally, we conduct econometric analysis to show the value of dynamic networks as a market signal. We use the news of listed firms in the Shanghai–Shenzhen 300 (CSI 300) index in 2013 and show that firm centrality in dynamic networks has additional predictive power for abnormal stock returns after controlling for market activities and traditional news-based metrics. We find that the counter-benefit network has negative effects and the cobenefit network has positive effects, where the negative dynamic effects emerge faster and persist longer than the positive dynamic effects. We show that these dynamic networks have additional value over the stable networks used in previous research in predicting firm equity value.

This study makes several contributions. First, it offers a new way to decipher news for better understanding of the market. Although many studies on financial text mining have been conducted, there remains critical knowledge in news that has not been fully exploited. The study illustrates a new direction to discover market signals from news and understand the economy from news before official statistics/ reports are released. Second, the study extends the existing knowledge about the driving forces of firm equity value. The findings show that investors not only digest news sentiment but also consider firm relations when reading news articles. There is potential for offering new presentations of news to investors in financial information services. Third, the cobenefit/counter-benefit networks we develop provide a novel measurement. The proposed approach addresses some of the complications and heterogeneity involved in dealing with different types of business relations reported in news while providing additional predictive power beyond conventional news analysis, and thus has practical value in financial market analysis.

## 2. Related Work

## 2.1. Text Mining for Dynamic Market Signals

Public news and social media are major information sources that reflect company status and drive the financial market. Text-mining methods have the potential to reduce human efforts in processing news and generate market signals, and have thus attracted much attention. The approaches employed can be classified into three types: event analysis, sentiment analysis, and relation analysis.

The main task of event analysis is to categorize the events/topics reported in news. After event extraction, a traditional event analysis method can be used to assess how different events impact the market (Jiang et al. 2014). Several studies have explored the task of event extraction. For example, Cecchini et al. (2010) detected fraud and bankruptcy events from financial statements and annual reports using a lexicon approach. Employing more complex features, Wei et al. (2018) detected corporate fraud, leaked information, and rumors from social media. Huang et al. (2018) used topical analysis methods and illustrated the incremental information provided by analysts rather than conference calls.

For the application of text-mining methods in financial market analysis, a more popular approach is sentiment analysis. Sentiment analysis gauges the tone of news articles when reporting events (Schumaker et al. 2012), which implies the impacts of events on firms and the market. Tetlock (2010) identified a positive correlation between news sentiment and abnormal stock returns using lexicons to code sentiments. However, a lexicon is not always effective in capturing subtle signals in news content (Li 2010). The recent trend is to develop machine learning methods to extract sentiments (Das and Chen 2007). Financial text-mining studies have identified useful sentiment signals from online chats (Tirunillai and Tellis 2012), Twitter (Bartov et al. 2018), and microblogs (Deng et al. 2018).

Event and sentiment analysis focus on the direct relationship between events and firm performance. In practice, understanding firm relations is also important. Existing financial text-mining studies on firm relation identification are relatively primitive. A major method is comention analysis, in which two firms are considered relevant if they appear in the same news article. Bao et al. (2008) found that a company is more likely to co-occur with its competitors/partners in news, which indicates the business implications of comention relations. Netzer et al. (2012) employed cooccurrence analysis of products in customer discussions to identify competitor relationships. After extracting such relations, Jin et al. (2012) leveraged comention network evolution in predicting firm profit and revenue growth. In another study, Creamer et al. (2013) measured firm centrality in the comention network to predict stock returns and volatility. These studies show the potential use of extracting firm relations to understand the market.

Frequent news updates provide us with a more dynamic view of the market than official statistics. Text mining empowers people to better leverage this resource to derive dynamic market signals. However, existing studies focus more on event and sentiment analysis, and studies of news-induced firm relations are limited to comention relations. The potential for news-induced firm relations to be used as dynamic market signals has not been fully revealed.

## 2.2. Competitive Environment, Stable Firm

## Networks, and Their Economic Implications

Firm relations are critical to firm operations. In the literature, cooperative and competitive relations are considered in informing the firm competitive environment (Gnyawali and Madhavan 2001), defined as the context in which a business competes and functions. The firm competitive environment moderates the impact of technology strategy (Zahra 1996) and human capital (Prajogo and Oke 2016) on firm performance. Firm networks provide a straightforward approach to assess the firm competitive environment (Madhavan et al. 2004). For example, Gnyawal et al. (2006) found that firm centrality in a co-opetition network is positively related to firm competitive actions. Firm networks are thus often used to describe different aspects of a business and gauge the economic implications (Gnyawali and Madhavan 2001). The extensive studies in this field (Provan et al. 2007) are summarized in Table 1.

As shown in Table 1, one major type of network being studied is the alliance/supply/trading network, as alliance or supply relationships directly indicate economic relations between firms. For example, Koka and Prescott (2008) studied alliance networks in the steel industry and found that firms in a stronger entrepreneurial position (as measured by structure holes) perform better in sales than other firms. Wu (2016) found that stock shocks can impact the revenue of firms up to four connections away in a supply network. Alliance/supply relations also facilitate knowledge diffusion. For example, Gao (2013) showed that firms that are strongly connected in supply chain networks adopt more conservative financial policies. Moreover, Schilling and Phelps (2007) found that firms embedded in an alliance network with high clustering and high reach have greater innovative outputs than firms in other parts of the network. In addition to firm relations, studies of industry-level trading networks have shown that trading relations can result in risk transfer (Acemoglu et al. 2012). Aobdia et al. (2013) found that firms in the central position of industry trading networks were exposed more to systemic risks and realized greater returns than other firms.

As shown in Table 1, we consider the alliance/ supply/trading network to be a type of stable network, which is the major interest of the literature and features long-term and strategic cooperation and competition relationships. Obviously, such stable strategic relations have a longer and stronger impact on firms. However, capturing and clarifying such stable relations are complicated. According to Gnyawali et al. (2006), almost 50% of alliances are between competitors. Therefore, we observe that many stable networks are manually curated from financial reports, announcements, and economic databases, as shown in Table 1. In addition to the traditional alliance/supply networks, Larcker et al. (2013) examined firms’ coboard relationships and found that more centrally positioned firms earn superior returns and experience higher growth than other firms. Furthermore, Hochberg et al. (2007) examined the coinvestment networks of venture capital firms and found that more centrally positioned firms experience significantly better fund performance than other firms. These networks facilitate information and resource exchange among firms because of shared information sources.

Academic studies of stable firm networks have paved the path for industrial applications. In industry, a carefully curated ontology on firm relations has been used in market modeling. For example, Kensho leverages known firm connections to collect and infer news of one firm in order to answer questions about another firm. Refinitiv, a spinoff of Thomson Reuters, also manages large firm ontologies for reasoning and prediction. These successful applications integrate text mining, but their exploitation of firm networks generally relies on the stable relations documented in repositories.

## 2.3. Research Opportunities on News-Induced Dynamic Firm Networks

In addition to stable networks, Table 1 also lists studies of dynamic networks that are updated much more frequently. Some of these networks are defined based on activities in the financial or information market. For example, Leung et al. (2017) studied investors’ cosearch of firms in search engines and found that firms in cosearch clusters displayed comovement in stock price.

A more dominant venue from which to generate dynamic networks is news, which is the focus of this study. Public news provides a rich source to document dynamic cooperation and competition relations. However, capturing such relations remains a technical challenge. As explained in Section 2.1, previous studies mainly explored firm comention relations in news articles (Jin et al. 2012, Creamer et al. 2013). As shown in Table 1, Ma et al. (2009) used a directed cocitation network to predict company revenue. Jin et al. (2012) and Creamer et al. (2013) used comention networks in predicting stock performance.

Although previous studies have identified the value of relatively stable and long-term firm relations, the exploitation of firm dynamic networks is still limited and in an early stage. People are not clear about their characteristics or their impacts on the market. Particularly for dynamic networks induced from news, although comention networks were found to be relevant to firm market value, they cannot fully exploit the value of public news. In fact, comention relations are very coarse without knowing the semantics of firm relations; one can hardly use them to understand firm competitive environment, let alone to obtain trading signals from such relations and make investment decisions.

In summary, it is necessary to study dynamic firm networks in general and news-induced dynamic networks specifically to assess their value in predicting the financial market.

## 3. Capturing Dynamic Firm Networks from News

To address the void in the literature, this study aims to extract dynamic firm networks to model firm competitive environments for market analysis. Figure 1 illustrates the overall study design. First, we propose a cobenefit network and a counter-benefit network that are extracted by text-mining methods. Second, we investigate the semantics of the two networks by comparing cobenefit/counter-benefit relations aggregated in a one-year period with strategic cooperative/ competitive relations to show the potential of using them as proxies for a firm competitive environment. Third, we study the dynamic networks built on a daily basis and examine whether they have value in predicting firm equity value. Here, we use firm centrality in the two networks as indicators and control for the effects of market activities, traditional factors of news, and stable firm networks. Through these three steps, we show the feasibility of defining news-induced dynamic networks that can be used as market signals, whereas the algorithms to extract such networks and the measures to leverage such networks can be improved in future studies.

## 3.1. Firm Competitive Environments as Cobene<sup>fi</sup>t Counter-Bene<sup>fi</sup>t Relations

To derive more informative networks from news, we need to consider their semantics. A news article may discuss multiple companies and various firm relations, and companies may jointly cause a business event (such as a merger, acquisition, or lawsuit) or be affected by an event (such as government policy or natural disaster). It would be difficult to fully understand the semantics of these relations with existing text-mining technologies. Noting that the firm competitive environment is largely defined by firm cooperative and competitive relations with different economic implications (Gnyawali et al. 2006), we alternatively study firm cobenefit and counter-benefit relations according to the positive or negative implications of events for firms. We define the following.

De<sup>fi</sup>nition 1 (Cobene<sup>fi</sup>t Relationship and Counter-Bene<sup>fi</sup>t Relationship). Given two firms involved in one news event, if the event has impacts of the same direction (positive or negative) on the two firms, they have a cobenefit relationship. If the event has impacts of opposite directions (positive and negative) on the two firms, they have a counter-benefit relationship.

The online appendix, Section A1, includes an example article about Foxconn’s acquisition of Sharp for illustration purposes. This article discusses an event for Foxconn’s competitor Samsung and customer Apple. Foxconn and Apple benefit from this event, so we define them in a cobenefit relation.

Our design provides a simplification of relation semantics by focusing on only two types of relations: those where the impacts were in the same direction for pairs of firms and those where they were not. These relations are obviously not equivalent to alliance or competitor relations or acquisition relations, as discussed in the traditional competitive environment literature. However, such “inaccurate” relations may be statistically relevant to firm cooperative and competitive relations. When writing news articles, reporters tend to pull collaborators/partners and competitors into a story (Ma 2011). Firm cobenefit relations may be caused by alliance and supply relations or arise from cooperation on technology development and resource allocation (Dyer and Singh 1998). If an event causes opposite economic outcomes for two firms, they are likely tactically or strategically on two sides of a competitive relation (Bengtsson and Kock 1997). By aggregating multiple news items, one can derive a network that is statistically relevant to firm competitive environments.

Moreover, because news generally reports the recent activities of firms, the relations extracted from news cannot completely reflect all firm strategic relations. They largely reflect the dynamics of firm relations, such as alliance formation and competitor changes. However, such “incomplete” networks still have value because they can impact the financial market through investors, who update their knowledge on firms based on news reports (Tetlock 2010). These relations reported in news could draw investors’ short-term attention and update their memory. We expect such perceived information to complement official statistics and economic data and to affect investor decisions.

## 3.2. Text Mining for Cobene<sup>fi</sup>t/Counter-Bene<sup>fi</sup>t Networks

As shown in Figure 2, the proposed approach to build the two networks contains five steps: entity extraction, argument separation, sentiment classification, relation definition, and network construction. The pseudocode of the process is reported in the online appendix, Section A3.

3.2.1. Entity Extraction. First, we identify the firms that are mentioned in each news article. In the Fox conn example, we identify Foxconn, Sharp, Apple, and Samsung. Because this study mainly involves a Chinese context, we employ a name-matching method and use a list of company names/short names/aliases and product brands to identify firms. On the Chinese stock market, listed companies have abbreviations with two to four characters that are quite distinct from their regular names. In Chinese business news, it is a common practice to annotate stock codes and firm abbreviations when referring to listed firms. Using firm short names, aliases, and product brands is also common to convey to the audience what firm is being referred to. As a result, it is very effective to use a lexicon approach for entity extraction. We also employ the chunking method to avoid the misidentification of nested strings in firm names.

3.2.2. Argument Separation. Once firms in an article are identified, we pool the sentences related to each firm together as “arguments” to understand how the event impacts firms. In this step, we first segment news articles into sentences on the basis of punctuation, for example, full stop, exclamation mark, or question mark. We then compile all sentences related to a firm into one argument. If a sentence contains multiple firms, it is included in arguments of all mentioned firms. In the Foxconn example, “Foxconn” appears in the first, third, and fifth sentences, which together form the argument about Foxconn in the article.

3.2.3. Sentiment Classi<sup>fi</sup>cation. Next, we perform sentiment classification on the argument of each firm. We use a machine learning approach to classify each firm’s argument as positive, negative, or neutral, where positive (negative) indicates that the event releases a good (bad) signal regarding the firm. To improve the classification performance, we adopt target-dependent feature models. Different from the traditional bag-of-words model, these models weight word features in a sentence by their distances to the firm mentioned in a sentence. The distance could be measured by simple distance, depth difference, and path difference (Jiang et al. 2011, Liu 2015). We choose the simple distance approach after comparing the performance of different methods. As illustrated in the Foxconn example, the words closer to “Foxconn” (at the beginning of the sentence) are assigned a larger weight in classifying the sentence’s implication for Foxconn. Thus, if a sentence contains two firms, the model makes it possible to give different sentiment labels to these firms by considering words closer to a firm that are more likely to refer to the firm. (In the traditional bag-of-words model, a sentence’s implications for all mentioned firms are the same.) The details of our model are reported in the online appendix, Section A2. In the Foxconn example, the argument on Samsung is classified as negative, whereas the arguments on the other three firms are considered positive.

3.2.4. Relation De<sup>fi</sup>nition. Based on the classification results, we define the relationship between each pair of firms mentioned in an article. If two firms in a piece of news have the same sentiment (positive or negative), they are considered to have a cobenefit relation. If one piece of news expresses opposite opinions on two firms, the two firms are considered to have a counterbenefit relation. Neutral arguments are excluded from our analysis. As a result, in the Foxconn example, Samsung has counter-benefit relations with the other three companies, whereas, Apple, Sharp, and Foxconn have cobenefit relations.

3.2.5. Network Construction. After identifying the firm relations from multiple news articles, we aggregate the cobenefit/counter-benefit relations to two networks, in which the nodes are firms and the links are weighted by the frequency of two firms having certain relations within the sample. The networks are nondirectional because cobenefit and counter-benefit relations are mutual relations between firms.

## 3.3. Validity of the Network Extraction Algorithm

The proposed text-mining approach provides one way to generate cobenefit/counter-benefit networks. For the sake of research rigor, it is necessary to evaluate whether the method provides a reasonably accurate assessment of firm cobenefit/counter-benefit relations that would be sufficient for further analysis.

First, we conducted a small-scale experiment on the effectiveness of lexicon-based entity extraction. In the experiment, we randomly selected 50 news articles (from the data set to be elaborated in Section 4). We invited a coder and conducted the simple task of reading these news articles and identifying 39 firms in 212 places. We calculated the performance of the automatic method in identifying each firm’s name. Our algorithm achieved an average precision of 91.94%, an average recall of 92.31%, an average F-measure of 92.11%, and an average accuracy of 91.94%, which are quite high in general machine learning tasks.

Second, we evaluated the argument sentiment classification part by comparing with different targetdependent feature models under different machine learning algorithms. The experimental process is detailed in the online appendix, Section A2. We randomly selected 8,170 groups of arguments from different news articles for different firms. We recruited three coders to label the data for a gold standard, where all coders had sufficient knowledge of finance, received training on the argument separation process, and learned sentiment classification examples for a mutual understanding of the task. The three experts conducted labeling independently and delivered coding results with a 0.96 intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC; Cicchetti 1994) and 0.86–0.90 Cohen’s kappa coefficients (Landis and Koch 1977), indicating perfect intercoder agreement. To further improve the consistency of coding, we selected the argument groups that were mutually agreed upon by all coders: 7,311 groups of arguments, with 4,023 positive, 1,301 negative, and 1,987 neutral instances.

To address the target-dependent sentiment analysis problem, we adopted four feature generation methods, including simple distance, depth difference, path difference, and rule-based methods (Boiy and Moens 2009, Jiang et al. 2011, Liu 2015). We implemented three-category classifiers using support vector machine (SVM), decision tree, random forest, and na¨ıve Bayes approaches and ran 30 rounds of fivefold crossvalidation experiments. As shown in Table A1 in Section A2 of the online appendix, the features generated by the simple distance method combined with the SVM classifier perform significantly better (with an F-measure of 70%–80% for the positive and negative instances) than all other methods. The performance is sufficient for further analysis according to Deng et al. (2018); therefore, we chose this feature generation and classification setup for sentiment analysis.

Finally, we evaluated the effectiveness of the overall relation extraction process. We first randomly selected 150 news articles and asked the three coders to code cobenefit/counter-benefit relations based on their reading of news articles. The three coders identified 333, 343, and 359 co- and counter-benefit relations, respectively. Their coding results have an ICC of 0.95 and kappa coefficients between 0.67 and 0.75, indicating excellent/substantial agreement. We consider the 321 mutually agreed relations by all coders, including 283 cobenefit relations and 38 counter-benefit relations, as the gold standard. After being applied to the sampled news articles, the algorithm detected 357 relations. Compared with the gold standard with 321 relations, the algorithm has 95% accuracy (77% F-measure) in detecting counter-benefit relations and 73% accuracy (81% F-measure) in detecting cobenefit relations.

Compared with the literature that uses binary or trinary text classification for empirical research, Deng et al. (2018) suggest that a classification accuracy of 60%–70% is sufficient for trinary classification-based empirical analysis. Although there is room to further improve the relation identification algorithm, we think the current algorithm can support the following empirical analyses.

## 4. The Nature of Cobene<sup>fi</sup>t/ Counter-Bene<sup>fi</sup>t Networks

Although we show that the text-mining approach effectively identifies co- and counter-benefit relations from news, it is necessary to further understand the semantics of these networks. Here, we employ CSI 300 firms listed on the Chinese stock market in 2013 to illustrate the nature of these networks and justify their connections with firm competitive environments.

4.1. A Sample of Firm Networks Induced from News In this study, we inspect 300 firms listed in the CSI 300 index as a sample for dynamic networks. The 300 firms cover all sectors in the Chinese stock market and account for more than 60% of total market capitalization. Understanding the equity value of these firms is critical to understanding the entire market.

We collect Chinese news in 2013 using 300 firms names (or aliases) listed in the CSI 300 index from a general web crawler (TRS InfoRadar, http://www .trs.com.cn/) that searches 3,000+ online sources, including company websites, news wires, and blogs. To focus on financial or business news, we choose 230 financial/business channels from all news sources, including 219 news portals, four discussion boards, and seven blog websites. These sources cover China’s major financial media, including government media (e.g., People Daily and Xinhua Net) and major financial newspapers (e.g., Securities Daily, China Securities Journal and Shanghai Securities News). These sources also include popular financial portals, such as Sina.com, Eastmony.com, IFeng.com and Hexun.com. In total, we collect 567,352 pieces of news.

Because the 230 data sources often report on the same events, there is considerable overlap (repeated or reprinted news) across the sources. It is thus necessary to filter out duplicate news articles before analysis. We use cosine similarity (Singhal 2001) to compare the similarity of documents within a 10-day time window to detect reprints. Then, we use the earliest news article to represent the report of an event. To ensure good performance of similar news detection, we tweak the threshold of news filtering in a small scale experiment with 423 news articles. As reported in the online appendix, Section A4, the method reaches 98% accuracy and a 93% F-measure when the threshold is set to 0.85. Although cosine similarity is a very simple method, we consider its performance sufficient for this research. After we remove duplicate news items, our data set contains 211,297 unique news articles.

If too many firms are mentioned in an article, firm relations may be too complicated to assess and less informative to investors (Jin et al. 2012). To address this concern, we set a threshold for the number of firms mentioned in news. As shown in Figure A3 in Section A5 of the online appendix, only 6.07% of news articles in our data set mention more than five firms. Moreover, as shown in Table A2 in Section A5 of the online appendix, setting the threshold to five improves the model’s predictive power. Finally, our data contain 198,463 news articles that mention no more than five firms, including 64,282 news articles with more than one firm.

After applying the proposed approach in Section $^ { 3 , }$ we compile 177,264 groups of arguments related to the 300 firms based on 64,282 news articles. By applying the argument sentiment classifier, we obtain

94,595 positive groups, 23,556 negative groups, and 59,113 neutral groups. After removing the neutral arguments, we are able to define 88,119 cobenefit relations and 14,846 counter-benefit relations in the scope of 238 trading days.

## 4.2. Cobene<sup>fi</sup>t/Counter-Bene<sup>fi</sup>t Networks in the One-Year Period

The relations identified from news can be aggregated for different time periods to construct cobenefit and counter-benefit networks. After aggregation, these networks should better cover stable firm relations. Moreover, the highly frequent relations that appear in news should have a higher probability of being core relations between firms. We can filter out noise caused by relation extraction algorithms by setting a threshold of relation frequency. We compare these networks aggregated in an entire year with manually curated coindustry and supply networks to understand the semantics of co- and counter-benefit networks.

In the literature, coindustry relations are often considered to represent competitor relations (Gnyawali et al. 2006). Two firms in the same small industry sector tend to have similar products, consumers, and suppliers, resulting in market and resource competition. Following this rationale, we construct coindustry relations according to Wind’s 2013 third-level industry catalog, which provides us with 2,055 coindustry relations. We set different thresholds on relation frequency to filter out possible noise. As illustrated in Figure 3(a), if we set the threshold to be appearing five or more times in one-year news, the counter-benefit network has a much higher chance (from 44% when the threshold is 5 to 93% when the threshold is 30) of comprising coindustry relations. (Experiments on the second-level and fourth-level industry classifications yield similar results.) Although the coverage (recall) of the counter-benefit network in one year is not very high, we can still generally discern that the counterbenefit network is more aligned with coindustry relations than the cobenefit network. Notably, a cobenefit network also overlaps with coindustry relations when an event influences an entire industry and causes similar benefits for companies within the industry.

In the literature, supplier–customer relationships represent very strong cooperative relationships among firms (Wu 2016). To assess the semantic relationship between news-induced relations and cooperative relations, we identify supply chain relations from firm annual reports. The Chinese stock market requires listed firms to report their trading volume with the top five customers and suppliers. We obtain trading information from the 300 firms’ annual reports published in 2012 and 2013 from the China Stock Market & Accounting Research Database. Although most firms do not report the names of their customers/suppliers, we manage to identify 86 supply chain partners among the 300 firms as a small (core) subset of the supply network. Similar to the above analysis, we filter noise using relation frequency. As shown in Figure 3(b), the cobenefit network clearly has a better match with the supply network than the counter-benefit network does, covering 1.8%–2.0% of the supply network. In fact, no relations in the counter-benefit network can be matched with the supply relations if the noise-filtering threshold is higher than 15. Although the absolute matching is low because of the limited available data (most supplier– customer relations are not disclosed in financial reports), supply chain partnerships are clearly more likely to be cobenefit relations than counter-benefit relations.

From the comparison of cobenefit/counter-benefit networks aggregated from one year of news with manually curated coindustry/supply networks, we find that (1) counter-benefit firms are more likely to be coindustry (competitor) than cobenefit firms, (2) cobenefit firms have good coverage of supplier–customer partners, and (3) counter-benefit firms are very unlikely to be supplier–customer partners, whereas cobenefit firms could be supplier–customer partners. These observations match our expectations. Supplier– customer partners often have connected economic benefits and tend to have cobenefit relations (Menzly and Ozbas 2010). Coindustry relations may reflect competitors and thus counter-benefit relations. They may also match cobenefit relations under the influence of industrial events. Although machine-extracted relations have noise, after aggregating over a long time period and filtering out noise, they are aligned with longterm cooperative and competition relations to a certain extent. These findings confirm the semantic implications of the two proposed networks. The counter-benefit network may signal competitors. The cobenefit network may signal suppliers/customers, complementors, and coindustry peers, but is less likely to comprise competitors. The unmatched links also signal the potential of new information embedded in newsinduced networks.

In Section A6 of the online appendix, we report the topological characteristics of the two networks. The two networks each cover 299 firms but show significant topological differences. It is notable that news reports more cobenefit relations than counter-benefit relations. We further analyze the network topology in different industries, which shows the same advantage of cobenefit relations in different industries. We also find that the node degrees in the cobenefit network have a smaller variance than those in the counterbenefit network, indicating that more firm variances are captured by the counter-benefit network. Among different industries, the finance industry has the most intensive connections, and the public utility industry has the least intensive connections in both networks Investigations on example firms also show that semantics of cobenefit/counter-benefit relations are aligned with our analysis, as shown in the online appendix, Section A7.

## 5. The Impact of News-Induced Dynamic Networks on Firm Equity Value

Although dynamic networks better reflect news fluctuations, a remaining question is whether such networks have an impact on the stock market as market signals. To answer this question, we conduct an empirical study by employing firm centrality measures to convert daily dynamic networks to firm-level time series data to predict firm performance on the next trading day, as shown in Figure 4. In the empirical study, we control for various factors used in previous research.

## 5.1. Firm Centrality as Competitive Position in Cobene<sup>fi</sup>t/Counter-Bene<sup>fi</sup>t Networks

When the cobenefit/counter-benefit relations are aggregated in a short time period to be dynamic networks, we adopt two widely used network measures, degree and PageRank, to assess firm centrality in networks as market signals. The news-induced dynamic networks are weighted networks where the link weight indicates relation frequency, that is, relation strength. We take a weighted version of the two measures and leave the exploration of more advanced measures to future research. In general, centrality measures indicate the importance of a node in the network. Degree is a local measure, and PageRank is a global measure with similar implications. If a firm did not appear in news on a given day, then it had no relations with other firms and had zero degree and PageRank, thus attracting less attention from investors. In this study, because cobenefit and counterbenefit networks have different semantic implications, the measures will also have different market implications.

The cobenefit network indicates a semantics of cooperative relationships, which is similar to the friendship relation in social networks. In a cobenefit network, degree and PageRank reflect the extent to which nodes attract attention and receive support from other firms, and the implications are similar to those in many other domains. For example, in a web page network (Page et al. 1999), the degree represents the number of times a web page is cited, which indicates the importance of the web page. PageRank further measures whether a web page is cited by highly cited web pages, which provides a global assessment of web page importance. In a friendship net work (Baldwin et al. 1997), degree represents number of friends; PageRank further measures whether one’s friends are influential, which shows global importance.

The counter-benefit network indicates a semantics of competitor relationships, which is similar to adversarial relations in social networks (Yang et al. 2007) Degree refers to the number of firms that have direct interest conflicts with the focal firm, with a higher degree indicating a tougher competitive environment. PageRank further assesses whether a firm is involved in tough competition in terms of the number of competitors and the extent of pressure that each competitor exerts on the firm. Both degree and Page-Rank in the counter-benefit network indicate the toughness of a firm’s competitive environment.

## 5.2. Hypothesis Development

5.2.1. News-Induced Cobene<sup>fi</sup>t/Counter-Bene<sup>fi</sup>t Networks Bring Extra Predictive Power. In developing a new instrument to understand news, the first point of interest is whether the instrument brings additional value. Several studies have leveraged news sentimen to understand the market (Tetlock 2010, Boudoukh et al. 2013), and some studies have demonstrated the power of comention networks to predict firm equity value (Jin et al. 2012, Creamer et al. 2013). We want to assess whether differentiating the semantics of firm relations mentioned in news strengthens the ability to predict firm equity value.

In practice, business relations are heterogeneous, which leads to different implications of firm operations and profits (Bengtsson and Kock 1997). When reading news articles, in addition to perceiving news sentiment regarding the target firm, investors may also notice co- and counter-benefit relations. Such dynamic updates from news can highlight certain firm relations and affect investors’ cognitive maps (Colin 1988) and decisions. Differentiating the semantics of relations may provide a more accurate assessment of information that affects the market (Tetlock 2010). Merging different types of relations may cancel out important but opposite signals and result in the misinterpretation of investors’ understanding of news (Hagenau et al. 2013). Therefore, we expect that differentiating cobenefit/ counter-benefit relations can better describe the firm competitive environment that investors may perceive from news and use it to predict firm equity value.

Hypothesis 1. Differentiating cobenefit and counter-benefit relations from news articles yields a better prediction of firm equity value than using the comention relationship and conventional sentiment analysis methods.

5.2.2. Opposite Impacts of Competitive Positions in Cobene<sup>fi</sup>t/Counter-Bene<sup>fi</sup>t Networks. Whereas stable networks affect firm operations, the impacts of dynamic networks could be different. Because newsinduced dynamic networks mainly focus on the recent events highlighted by news, they hardly change firm operations on a day to day basis. However, dynamic networks may affect investors’ perceptions of firms and influence stock prices. Investors’ perceptions are formed based on their prior beliefs and external signals, and determine their trading behaviors (Mitroi and Ozbas 2014). Financial reports and news are well-known signals that may influence investor perception (Davis and Hollie 2008). Newsinduced cobenefit/counter-benefit networks lead to different perceptions according to their semantics.

A cobenefit network is semantically related to firm cooperation relations. Sanou et al. (2016) found that a firm’s position in a co-opetition network influences the extent of the firm’s competitive aggressiveness, which improves the market performance of more central firms. In the cobenefit network, a more central firm is perceived as receiving more support from partner companies or indirectly related companies, which positively affects its performance (Gnyawali and Madhavan 2001). Moreover, firm centrality in the alliance network was found to affect firm research and development and innovation (Zaheer and Bell 2005, Schilling and Phelps 2007), given its influence on receiving knowledge. A firm’s ability to innovate further increases its potential market performance. With these clues, investors who sense a company occupying a more central role in the cobenefit network may speculate positively regarding the firm’s operations, thus promoting the firm’s market performance. Thus, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 2a. A more central firm in a cobenefit network predicts higher firm equity value.

A counter-benefit network captures reverse economic outcomes between firms that are likely on both sides of the competition. Studies of competition found that severe competition with other firms leads to greater efforts in business operations (Skilton and Bernardes 2015). Stronger product competition also leads to lower firm value (Beiner et al. 2011). A more central firm in a counter-benefit network may prompt speculation about challenges and competition from directly or indirectly related companies. A counterbenefit network is an adversarial network, and studies of adversarial networks found that centrality in an adversarial network negatively affects an individual’s educational performance (Baldwin et al. 1997). In teamwork, degree centrality in a hindrance network negatively affects an individual’s work performance (Sparrowe et al. 2001). In line with these applications, investors would speculate negatively about firms in more central roles in news-induced counter-benefit networks, which would lead to underestimated firm performance on the stock market. Therefore, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 2b. A more central firm in a counter-benefit network predicts lower firm equity value.

5.2.3. Asymmetric Dynamic Impacts of Competitive Positions in Cobene<sup>fi</sup>t/Counter-Bene<sup>fi</sup>t Networks. In addition to the opposite directions, we are interested in the dynamic characteristics of impacts in these two networks, as reflected in the response and vanishing times on the market (Luo et al. 2013). The behavioral finance literature found that traditional asset pricing models have difficulty in explaining price dynamics, which may be caused by investors’ irrational behaviors. Investor attention theory emerged to address this problem in the last two decades (Gabaix et al. 2003, Peng and Xiong 2006, Andrei and Hasler 2015) to examine price dynamics by focusing on scarce resources, such as the attention, time, and effort, needed to collect and process market information.

According to investor attention theory, investors reactions to market signals depend on how they allocate their limited attention, and more attention leads to more informative market prices (Peng and Xiong 2006). Huberman and Regev (2001) pointed out that prices react to new information only when investors pay attention to it. Inattention slows down the pricing discovery process whereby new information becomes impounded in the market (Bacchetta and Van Wincoop 2005). In this study, we develop cobenefit/ counter-benefit networks that have different semantic and sentiment implications that attract different attention.

Investor attention theory rationalizes our research approach, particularly on the dynamic features of market signaling.

From a semantic perspective, cobenefit/counterbenefit relations require different efforts to process and may attract different attention. As shown in Section 4, counter-benefit relations have a clearer semantic, indicating two firms’ competition, whereas cobenefit relations contain both collaborators and competitors. The inconsistent semantics of cobenefit relations cause greater investor effort to digest and reason (Le Ny 1991), which may drive investors to first process counter-benefit relations. Previous research also shows that firm collaboration news, such as partnership news, barely affects market movements (Boudoukh et al. 2013), which may also lead experienced investors to pay more attention to counterbenefit relations.

From a sentiment perspective, cobenefit/counterbenefit networks have opposite sentiment implications. According to studies of investor attention (Andrei and Hasler 2015), investors try to acquire more information in bad times because they worry about their investments. Previous studies revealed that negative information generally captures more attention than positive information (Deng et al. 2018). Moreover, reactions to selling would be faster than reactions to buying because the former involves a smaller choice in decision making (Beber and Brandt 2010). Accordingly, Tirunillai and Tellis (2012) observed that negative online comments were reflected in faster trading. Luo et al. (2013) also showed that a negative blog has a smaller wear-in time to influence market return. These findings can be extended to dynamic network signals.

Overall, considering both semantics and sentiments, investors would pay more attention to counter-benefit relations, which may lead their impacts to appear earlier on the market. Thus, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 3a. The impact of counter-benefit networks on the market is realized faster than that of cobenefit networks.

According to investor attention theory, when investors allocate more attention to a firm and process more firm-specific information, there will be more pronounced overreaction (Peng and Xiong 2006). Such attention-grabbing signals tend to last longer on the market (Huynh and Smith 2017). From a semantic perspective, the number of counter-benefit relations is much fewer than that of cobenefit relations. The processing of counter-benefit relations is more firm specific and may lead to stronger reactions and longer influential time spans. Previous research also identified that news on collaborative relations, such as partnerships and acquisitions, has a shorter impact period than other types of news (Sprenger and Welpe 2011).

From a sentiment perspective, bad impressions are more resistant to disconfirmation (Baumeister et al. 2001), which could cause a longer period of avoidance in investors’ decisions. In addition, bad news is more likely to be shared (Stiegliz and Dang-Xuan 2014), especially in emotionally negative domains (Heath 1996); thus, bad news spreads to a larger population and lasts for a longer time period in social media Hornik et al. (2015). Previously, Tirunillai and Tellis (2012) observed that the effect of negative online comments had a longer vanishing time than that of positive comments. Joining the predictions from semantic and sentiment perspectives, the effects of the counterbenefit network may last for a longer period. Thus, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 3b. The impact of counter-benefit networks on the market lasts longer than that of cobenefit networks.

## 5.3. Econometric Model

5.3.1. Dependent Variable. In the empirical study, we use abnormal return to measure firm equity value following Luo et al. (2013). Abnormal return refers to a stock that is valued beyond expectations by the average stock market. To measure it, we first estimate the difference between the stock return and risk-free return using the Fama and French threefactor model:

$$
R _ {i t} - R _ {f t} = \alpha_ {i} + \beta_ {1 i} (R _ {m t} - R _ {f t}) + \beta_ {2 i} S M B _ {t} + \beta_ {3 i} H M L _ {t} + e _ {i t},\tag{1}
$$

where t is the time period; $R _ { i t }$ is the return of stock i in time t; $R _ { m t }$ is the average market return, as represented by the Shanghai composite index; $R _ { f t }$ is the risk-free rate of return; SMB is the effect of market size; HML is the effect of market value; $e _ { i t }$ is the model residual; and $\alpha _ { i }$ is the intercept. Next, we calculate the abnormal return of i in time $t , A R _ { i t } ,$ as the difference between the observed and expected returns:

$$
\begin{array}{r} A R _ {i t} = R _ {i t} - R _ {f t} - \big (\alpha_ {i} + \beta_ {1 i} \big (R _ {m t} - R _ {f t} \big) + \beta_ {2 i} S M B _ {t} \\ + \beta_ {3 i} H M L _ {t} \big). \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

5.3.2. Vector Autoregression Model. We adopt the VAR model to test the impacts of a firm’s competitive environments on abnormal returns. The VAR model allows us to account for biases of endogeneity and autocorrelations. In this research, firm networks are extracted from news. Stock performance may influence journalists’ attention and stock mentions in subsequent news reports, which may increase the comention of related firms. Abnormal stock return, therefore, may conversely influence the news-induced dynamic networks together with other news-related variables. In the model, we also control for market activity variables, which generally have significant autocorrelations that need to be addressed by VAR. Moreover, the VAR model can track both the shortterm and long-term effects of network metrics in predicting firm equity value, which supports the analysis of dynamic effects of networks. The model for firm i is

$$
\mathbf {V} _ {i, t} = \Pi_ {i} + \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {K} \Phi_ {i} ^ {k} \cdot \mathbf {V} _ {i, t - k} + \mathbf {E} _ {i t},\tag{3}
$$

where $\mathbf { V } _ { i , t } = [ A R _ { i t } , X _ { i t } , C _ { i t } ] ^ { T } , X _ { i t }$ is a vector of independent variables, and $C _ { i t }$ is a group of control variables; $\mathbf { E } _ { i t }$ is a vector of residuals; $\Pi _ { i }$ is the vector of intercepts; and $\Phi _ { i }$ are matrices of coefficients. In the model, we use the k lags of X to predict the dependent variables.

Using the VAR model, we can quantify the shortand long-term impacts of network metrics using elasticity results in an immediate/longer period through generalized impulse response functions (GIRFs) and quantify the influences of different network metrics with generalized forecast error variance decomposition (GFEVD). The analysis procedure is elaborated in the online appendix, Section A9.

5.3.3. Independent and Control Variables. As discussed in Section 5.1, we examine firm degree and PageRank centrality in the extracted cobenefit and counterbenefit networks as independent variables. Here, we use $d _ { i t } ^ { + }$ and $d _ { i t } ^ { - }$ to represent the degree of firm i at time t in the cobenefit and counter-benefit networks and use $p r _ { i t } ^ { + }$ and $p r _ { i t } ^ { - }$ to represent the PageRank centrality of firm i at time t in the cobenefit and counterbenefit networks, respectively.

To accurately assess the effects of dynamic networks, we control for other signals, including the news volume, news sentiment, and comention network extracted from news. To calculate the newsrelated variables, we follow previous practice and use all data (rather than the processed ones for network extraction). We calculate news volume for firm i at time $t , n v _ { i t } .$ . ## 6.2. Robustness Check

6.2.1. Including Additional Firm Centrality Measures. In addition to degree and PageRank, we check the effect of adding closeness centrality and betweenness centrality in the VAR model. These two metrics are commonly used in the network literature and represent how close a firm is to the center of a network or on the paths between other firms’ cooperation and competition. As shown in Table A6 in Section A10 of the online appendix, incorporating the two network metrics into model CM2 further improves $R ^ { 2 }$ from 0.1601 to 0.2001, and reduces the NRMSE from 0.1150 to 0.1123, where the degree and PageRank remain significant in both the cobenefit and counter-benefit networks. The direction and response time of shortand long-term market reactions are consistent with the findings in the main model, and our findings remain stable upon adding additional network centrality measures.

6.2.2. Enlarging Time Window of Network Construction. We choose to conduct daily analysis to test the network effect in the main model. Considering that news may last for a longer time in investors’ minds and contribute to the perception of the firm competitive environment, we further extend the network construction time window to the weekly level (i.e., seven days of news). We use the networks created during the week preceding a trading day to predict stock abnormal returns on the trading day. Table A7 in Section A11 of the online appendix reports the results. As shown, the effect of the model is consistent with our previous findings, which indicates the existence of a memory effect in news digestion.

6.2.3. Filtering Out News on Market Movement. In the main study, we employ all public news to extract the cobenefit/counter-benefit networks. Among these news articles, some articles simply summarize and report the daily market movement. Such market summary news is quite different from other business news, and the cobenefit/counter-benefit relations extracted from these articles essentially show stock price comovements. To address this issue, we conduct a robustness check by removing such news using keywords such as “stock,” “futures,” “stock market,” and “index” in news titles. This filtering process gives us 188,773 pieces of news for the experiment with firm names appearing no more than five times, including 59,830 articles with more than one firm. After news filtering, we repeat the analysis, as reported in the online appendix, Section A12. The findings are still consistent with those our main model.

6.2.4. The News Outbreak in December 2013. As shown in the online appendix, Section A8, there was a significant increase in news in December 2013, which may be due to the addition of news sources and the increase in news reports in each news source. To address concerns on this period of data, we conduct two robustness checks. First, we filter out December 2013 from our data set and repeat the empirical analysis. As shown in Section A13 of the online appendix, removing this period provides findings consistent with those of our main model. Second, we choose the news sources that were continuingly providing service in the entire scope of 2013 and repeat the network construction and empirical analysis. As shown in Appendix A14, the results of removing discontinuous news sources are still consistent with those of our main model. These robustness checks, combined with the discussions in Appendix A8, show that our method is robust to dramatic changes in news sources and volumes.

6.2.5. Alternative Econometric Models. In addition to using individual VAR models, we test our hypotheses using alternative models. First, we pool together the 16 banks’ time series data as panel data and estimate a panel VAR model, which allows us to control for unobserved individual heterogeneity. The analysis results are reported in the online appendix, Section A15. As shown, the estimation results are consistent with those in the VAR model and support our hypotheses.

Next, we conduct a classic panel model to predict abnormal returns in which we use 2SLS to control for the endogeneity issue. We use a two-way fixed-effect method to control both firm and time effects and use the lags of endogenous variables as instrument variables, similar to Sonnier et al. (2011). As shown in the online appendix, Section A16, the model results are also consistent with findings in the main model.

The annualized Sharpe ratio of the cobenefit/ counter-benefit network strategy is 1.4690, which is much higher than other indicators. The annualized alpha of the cobenefit/counter-benefit network strategy is 21.43%. Although more complex trading strategies exist to further improve trading performance, the results illustrate the practical value of the proposed networks, which should be exploited by investors.

## 7. Discussions

## 7.1. Theoretical Implications

This paper contributes to the literature in both the information systems and finance fields. First, our study proposes an innovative approach to deciphering firms competitive environment from news through co- and counter-benefit networks. In contrast to traditional financial textual analyses (Antweiler and Frank 2004, Schumaker et al. 2012), our study creatively focuses on firm relations rather than event topics or sentiments.

The extracted cobenefit/counter-benefit relations are semantically aligned with the coindustry/supply chain relations in the long term and offer dynamic signals to predict movement of firm equity value in the short term. Although there is room to refine the relevant techniques, the study demonstrates strong promise in improving understanding of financial news articles for market signaling

Second, this paper shows that news-induced networks have an impact on the financial market through investors’ perception and attention. Prior behavior finance studies found that investors’ perception and attention affect information processing and influence investor decisions (Peng and Xiong 2006, Davis and Hollie 2008, Andrei and Hasler 2015). Our study reveals that investors digest complex firm relations from news according to their semantics, and the process is reflected in the stock market; that is, our study enriches the behavior finance literature by illustrating investors’ perception of the firm competitive environment, which is relevant to equity price formation as found in this paper.

## 7.2. Practical Implications

This study has significant practical implications. First, it reveals that cobenefit/counter-benefit networks have predictive power for firm equity value. As shown in the trading simulation, the cobenefit/counter-benefit networks provide good buy and sell signals. Such signals enrich known indicators and support more complex trading strategies in investment and portfolio management.

Second, co- and counter-benefit networks may also be used to understand the structure of the economy. Prior literature often used financial reports and statistical data to capture changes in corporate strategy and industry position (Axelsson and Easton 1992, Morris and Tronnes 2018). This study suggests the possibility of assessing firm/industry changes using firm networks induced from news, which reflect the market in a timelier fashion.

## 8. Conclusions

In this study, we develop an approach to extract coand counter-benefit networks from news that are semantically relevant to firm’s competitive environment and show the impact of news-induced dynamic networks on firm equity value. We find that differentiating the two types of relations improves the prediction of firm equity value. By contrast, firm centrality in cobenefit networks, which reflects the support they receive, has positive impacts on firm equity value. Firm centrality in counter-benefit networks, aligned with the competition they face, has negative impacts on firm equity value. Moreover, the negative effects of counter-benefit networks emerge faster and persist longer than the positive effects of cobenefit networks. These findings have important implications for theory and practice, implying that investors digest competition information in addition to sentiment information from news. The work enriches the application of investor attention theory and shows that firm centrality in dynamic networks offers more precise investor perception.

This research also has its limitations. First, public news may be biased toward large companies, and small companies may not appear in news because of the limited attention of journalists. Such bias is a common disadvantage of news-based analysis. Moreover, news may also be biased by journalists’ writing styles. Future studies could consider more heterogeneous data, such as social media data, to examine firm competitive environments revealed from these channels.

Second, we propose an approach to extracting coand counter-benefit relations that is sufficient to show the impacts of news-induced dynamic networks on the stock market. However, to put this approach into practice, more advanced and effective text-mining methods could be developed. The design and comparison of different methods are beyond the scope of this paper and are left for future research.

Third, the experiments were conducted in the context of the Chinese stock market in 2013, which has unique characteristics. For example, this market has many individual investors who rely on news for investment decisions. By comparison, the U.S. market is dominated by institutional investors who have other tools to support their decisions. Chinese market characteristics also change over time. To generalize to other markets and periods, the relevance of findings should be tested before application. Nevertheless, we believe the proposed method is generic and can be adopted when calibrated with data from other financial markets.
