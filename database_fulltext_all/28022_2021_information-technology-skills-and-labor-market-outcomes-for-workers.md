# Information Technology Skills and Labor Market Outcomes for Workers

Hilal Atasoy,<sup>a</sup> Rajiv D. Banker,<sup>b</sup> Paul A. Pavlou<sup>c</sup>

Abstract. Job erosion is a major concern globally, especially given the COVID-19 pandemic. Unemployment and low wages remain pressing societal challenges in the wake of increased automation, more so for traditionally disadvantaged groups in the labor market, such as women, minorities, and the elderly. However, workers who possess relevan information technology (IT) skills might have an edge in an increasingly digital economy. In this study, we examine the role of IT skills in labor market outcomes for workers. We leverage a household IT use survey from an emerging economy that captures detailed, individual-level data on IT skills and IT use, which are also integrated with household labor force survey data on workers’ wages, occupations, and industries between 2007 and 2015. We find that basic IT skills increase employment probability, and these employment effects can be explained by both increased labor force participation and a higher probability of transitioning from unemployment to employment, after accounting for the decision to participate in the workforce. Advanced IT skills are not significantly associated with higher employment, conditioning on basic IT skills. However, having advanced IT skills helps workers to earn higher wages and incrementally increases the probability that they are employed in higher-paid jobs. Interestingly, the effects of basic IT skills on employment are significantly larger for the female and older workforce that typically has a higher pref erence for flexible work options. Additionally, IT skills complement occupations that have a higher share of nonroutine tasks that cannot be performed by predefined rules. These results emphasize the importance of providing necessary IT access and basic IT training to traditionally socially disadvantaged groups to reduce the IT skills gap and close the digital divide. We contribute to the literature by providing evidence on the role of different IT skills in the entire labor force across multiple occupations and demographics, going beyond IT professionals and employed workers who have predominantly been studied in the information systems literature. We discuss implications for the future of work and education and public policy for designing IT training policies for workers, students, and organizations to stimulate employment for workers with higher wages, particularly in developing economies and for traditionally disadvantaged segments of the workforce, such as women and the elderly, particularly after the COVID-19 pandemic.

## 1. Introduction

Information technology (IT) policies have been implemented around the world with the goal of eliminating the digital divide and stimulating employment.<sup>1</sup> These IT policies are premised on the notion that people who lack IT access and knowledge may become disconnected from the labor market. Workers with IT skills can participate in the labor force as well as transition from unemployment to employment easier in three key ways: First, IT skills are generalpurpose skills that can be transferred across organizations and can enable workers to achieve higher productivity in their jobs, thereby helping them to command higher wages (Kruger 1993). These IT skills are becoming an important component of human capital, and with the increased digitization across the economy, they are ubiquitously required in the labor market for different occupations and jobs. Second, the employment-matching process relies on information flows and information quality (Autor 2001). IT skills can help workers find job opportunities by making information readily accessible to them and by reducing the cost of job search via online tools and professional social networks, such as LinkedIn (Garg and Telang 2017). Third, IT skills can enable remote work settings and telecommuting, which may be particularly beneficial for workers with reduced mobility (e.g., elderly) or who prefer or need work flexibility (e.g., women with children).

In this paper, we examine how IT skills facilitate labor market outcomes, namely, employment and wages. This question has become even more important in early 2020 with the COVID-19 pandemic that significantly increased unemployment around the world and made telecommuting and flexible work options more relevant. Specifically, we focus on the role of basic and advanced IT skills across key demographics (gender and age) and task types in occupations (routine and nonroutine). To the degree that socioeconomically disadvantaged groups (e.g., women and the elderly) have been observed to increase their employment when work options are more flexible (Mincer 1962, Fuchs 1980), it is likely that having IT skills is encouraging these traditionally disadvantaged groups in the labor market to enter the workforce and seek employment. The skill-biased technical change literature shows that IT investments and IT use raise the relative wages in occupations that have more nonroutine tasks due to their strong complementarities with technology (Acemoglu 1998, Autor et al. 1998, 2003). Therefore, we analyze the heterogeneous effects of IT skills across demographics and labor task types.

IT skills are essential for both employee and firm success, and many information systems (IS) studies have analyzed their effects over time (Green 1989, Leitheiser 1992, Lee et al. 1995, Todd et al. 1995). The literature that has examined the relationship between IT skills and performance has thus far focused primarily on the IT workforce, IT careers, and organizational outcomes (Bassellier et al. 2003, Bassellier and Benbasat 2004, Slaughter et al. 2007, Langer et al. 2008, Mehra et al. 2014, Tambe 2014, Wiesche et al. 2019). Although these studies offer important insights into the effect of IT skills on the success of firms and the IT workforce, the role that IT skills play for non-IT professionals and the general labor population remains ambiguous.

Studies in the IS and economics literatures analyzed wage and performance benefits of IT skills for individual workers (Kruger 1993, Bassellier et al. 2003), and across occupations and industries (Autor et al. 1998, Autor and Dorn 2013). The literature studied both general-purpose IT skills that are transferable across firms (Tambe 2014) and also organizationspecific IT skills that are only valuable in a particular organization (Slaughter et al. 2007). These studies use data on computer use at work, IT skills, and IT experience for workers who are already employed, as the data sets are based on employee information. It is intuitive that IT skills are beneficial for workers productivity after they are employed; however, there is scarce evidence on whether IT skills affect employment opportunities and labor force participation for individual workers at the outset.

We contribute to the literature on the benefits of IT skills by offering a broader societal perspective on the role of general-purpose IT skills across the entire population, expanding the type of professions and labor market segments analyzed. We use governmentadministered and large-scale data sets from an emerging economy that are representative nationally and across demographic groups, occupations, and industries. These data allow us to assess the effects of IT skills on the extensive workforce besides IT labor, including multiple professions, as well as traditionally underserved segments of the general population, namely women and elderly workers. Moreover, our analyses expand to other segments of the labor force beyond employed workers (regardless of profession), because the IT skills questions in our data are also recorded by unemployed workers and those who are not in the labor force. This allows us to estimate the effects of possessing IT skills on labor force participation decision and employment status while accounting for the endogenous decision of being part of the labor force at the individual level. Overall, our contribution stems from focusing on IT skills (basic and advanced) that are measured for the entire population of workers with different occupations and different labor market statuses (employed, unemployed, not in the labor force), and providing empirical evidence on the societal value of IT skills across multiple segments of the workforce and demographic groups, including women and the elderly.

We leverage two individual-level data sets between 2007 and 2015 from the Turkish Statistical Institute (TurkStat) that are large-scale repeated cross-sectional surveys. The first data set is the Household IT Use (HITU) survey, which contains information on IT use and IT skills at the individual level. The IT skills vary from basic office skills, such as copying/pasting files and using spreadsheets, to more advanced IT skills such as troubleshooting computers and knowing a programming language. These are general-purpose IT skills that can be valuable in several occupations beyond traditional IT jobs, as they are typically lower-level skills than the ones that would be required for IT professionals. We also integrate the HITU survey with the Household Labor Force (HLF) survey, which includes data on individual workers’ wages, occupations, and industries. These government-administered surveys comprehensively cover workers across demographic groups and occupations.

The results show that, on average, basic IT skills increase individuals’ employment probability, which is driven by both higher labor force participation and a higher probability of transitioning from unemployment to employment, after accounting for the decision to participate in the workforce. Advanced IT skills do not provide a significant incremental effect on employment probability, on top of basic IT skills. However, having advanced IT skills helps workers to earn higher wages and incrementally increases the probability that they are employed in higher-paid jobs. These results indicate that basic IT skills can create an advantage in terms of competing for lowskilled jobs, which are targeted by a more heterogeneous candidate pool where IT skills can differentiate among workers. For high-skilled jobs with higher wages, however, having advanced IT skills might be a necessary albeit not sufficient condition to meet specialized job requirements in the smaller pool of candidates with more sophisticated qualifications. However, advanced IT skills can enable workers to compete for these high-skilled jobs that pay higher wages.<sup>2</sup> These effects are heterogeneous by demographic groups and occupation types, and the employment benefits of basic IT skills are significantly larger for women and older workers. Our findings are robust to several empirical specifications, such as the Heckman selection model, instrumental variables, seemingly unrelated regression, and other robustness checks included in the study.

The results offer insights into the impact of IT skills on labor markets, especially in developing economies. They have implications for organizations and governments and indicate that IT skills training can be designed as part of a targeted strategy to (a) develop at least basic IT skills for workers and students across demographics to help them to become competitive in the digital economy and benefit from flexible work settings, telecommuting, and distance education; (b) ensure that organizations enjoy a more productive workforce that can augment organizational IT systems; and (c) help nations to be more productive in the wave of increased automation.

## 2. Theoretical Development

## 2.1. Role of IT Skills in Labor Market Outcomes

IT skills can be defined as the knowledge and abilities needed to accomplish computer-related tasks. IT skills are an important part of human capital in the digital age, and extant literature in IS and labor economics has analyzed the effects of IT investments, use, and skills on employee and organization productivity. Interestingly, these literatures differ in terms of the population of workers analyzed and the IT measures considered. To date, however, the IS literature has predominantly focused on IT professionals and business managers, whose relevant IT skills include general and managerial skills, such as organizational and people skills, comprehensive IT skills including their IT competence and experience, and special technical IT skills critical for IT workers, such as Hadoop and Structured Query Language (SQL). In contrast, the labor economics literature analyzed wider labor pools besides the IT industry, and typically used a high-level, often single measure of IT, such as the level of IT investment in the industry and occupation, or whether an employee uses a computer at work.

We summarize the literature and argue that our contribution stems from two factors. First, we analyze the broader benefits of IT skills beyond IT labor with nationally representative data. Second, our measure of IT skills includes unemployed and people who are not in the labor force, in addition to employed workers, which enables us to identify how IT skills affect the labor force participation decision and labor market outcomes accounting for workforce participation at the individual level.

The IS literature has focused on how demand for IT skills changes over time and the impact of IT skills on IT workforce and firm performance (Benbasat et al. 1980, Cheney and Lyons 1980, Vitalari 1985, Green 1989, Leitheiser 1992, Lee et al. 1995, Todd et al. 1995, Tambe 2014). This segment of the literature uses IT professionals or business managers as focal points (shown in column 2 of Table 1 for the selected studies). In particular, the literature analyzes skills that are important for the IT workforce and careers, and the role they play in business outcomes (Wiesche et al. 2019). The skills of the IT workforce, project managers, and business managers were shown to have positive effects on project and firm success (Slaughter et al. 2007, Langer et al. 2008, Kim et al. 2014, Mehra et al. 2014). Additionally. IT skills affect both IS success and IT infrastructure flexibility (Byrd and Turner 2001). Tambe (2014) analyzed returns to the firm’s investments in Hadoop and SQL skills and found significant productivity gains from IT skills for firms with complementary resources. Bassellier et al. (2003) proposed a model for the nature and role of business managers’ IT competence, which significantly explained the variation in business managers intentions to champion IT. Business competence, which comprises of firm-specific knowledge that is not IT related, is also important for the development of partnerships between IT professionals and business managers (Bassellier and Benbasat 2004). Studies have expanded the view of the IT workforce beyond traditional employment settings, analyzing new contexts, such as online and open source knowledge communities (Howison and Crowston 2014, Huang and Zhang 2016).

The labor economics literature analyzeshow technology affects labor markets with different research questions, dependent variables, and unit of analyses (Table 1, columns 1 and 4) compared with the IS literature. Most of this literature focuses on the relationship between technological change and the relative wages for skilled versus unskilled labor at the industry and occupation levels (Acemoglu 1998; Autor et al. 1998, 2003; Bresnahan et al. 2002; Acemoglu and Autor 2011; Autor and Dorn 2013; Acemoglu and Restrepo 2020). These studies demonstrate that the relative wages of skilled versus unskilled labor increase in ITintensive industries and occupations; hence, IT complements high-skilled labor more than unskilled labor. The skill-biased technical change also accounts for job polarization where demand and wages for medium-skill jobs decreased the most (Goos et al. 2014, Michaels et al. 2014). Notably, Autor et al. (2003) proposed a theoretical mechanism for skill-biased technical change and show that the share of nonroutine tasks increases in more IT-intensive occupations and industries, as routine tasks are automated. At the individual level, Kruger (1993) found that the wages of workers who use a computer at work are higher, and these wage benefits are even stronger in less IT-intensive industries, indicating the importance of going beyond high-tech firms (Goss and Phillips 2002).

Overall, IT skills help to increase individual worker’s productivity, ceteris paribus; however, there is scarce evidence on whether IT skills help provide employment opportunities and affect the decision to participate in the labor force in the first place at the individual level, as the studies that analyze the broader employment impacts are usually at the industry, occupation, or regional levels (Table 1, column 4). One reason for this seems to be the measurement unit of the IT variable.

In these labor economics studies, the measurement of IT is at the employee level and does not include unemployed workers or people who are not in the labor force (Table 1, column 3). This is because the main source of IT measure comes from the Current Population Survey (CPS) supplement question on whether workers use a computer at work, which is only recorded for those workers who are already employed (Kruger 1993; Autor et al. 1998, 2003; Peng 2017). Autor and Dorn (2013) use a different measure of IT in a study that focuses on the impacts of computerization on job polarization and the shift in employment and wages across occupations to explain the high growth observed in the U.S. low-skill service sector. Based on private-sector surveys of computer inventories, their measure counts the number of personal computers per employee at the firm-level; thus, their IT measure also does not account for people who are unemployed or not in the labor force.

Our study builds on and extends the extant literature in two key ways: First, although the literature on the benefits of IT skills is focused on the professional IT workforce and organizational outcomes, we explore the role of IT skills in the entire labor market with workers of different professions. We use nationally representative data from a developing nation where the majority of workers are in non-IT nonspecialized, and mostly in low- and medium-level occupations. This also allows us to estimate heterogeneous effects for traditionally disadvantaged groups in the workforce, such as women and the elderly, which are largely understudied in the literature. Using data on workers across all professions enables us to examine the impacts of IT skills on the lives of the broader population of non-IT and nonspecialized workers who do not possess high-level IT or managerial positions.

Second, although the IT measures in the literature typically come from employee information, IT skills in our data are captured for people who have different labor market status, including employed, unemployed, and who are not in the labor force. A change in employment can be driven by an increase in labor force participation or a change in the probability of unemployed people finding jobs, and having data on individuals’ IT skills for different segments of the work force allows us to identify the effects of IT skills on labor force participation, which has not been studied in the literature, and employment status, while accounting for the endogenous decision to participate in the workforce at the individual level. Our study extends the literature by examining the role of IT skills, a type of human capital that is measured in the broader labor market at the national level, for people across different professions, labor market segments, and with different statuses in the labor market.

IT skills include general-purpose skills and firmspecific skills. General-purpose skills are readily transferable across all firms, whereas firm-specific skills are not (Mincer 1974, Jensen and Meckling 1992). Firm-specific skills require investment in knowledge that is unique to the firm, and therefore firms generally reward firm-specific skills more than general-purpose skills (Manning 2011). Similarly,

IT professionals in jobs that require more firm-specific human capital earn more than those in jobs requiring less firm-specific human capital (Bassellier and Benbasat 2004, Slaughter et al. 2007). Studies also analyze returns to firm-specific and industry-specific IT experience and tenure, which can be only gained in a specific setting (Kim et al. 2014).

There are two major differences between generalpurpose and firm-specific IT skills in our context. First, general-purpose skills can be gained through education, training, and self-study and thus they do not require employment, whereas firm-specific skills require employment at a firm. Second, general-purpose skills can be rewarded in any firm or facilitate job transition, while firm-specific skills are only rewarded in a specific firm, as this knowledge cannot be readily applied at other firms. Therefore, generalpurpose skills are more relevant for understanding the role of IT skills in employment outcomes for the entire population because employees with firm-specific skills are likely to be already employed at a focal firm that renders their firm-specific skills valuable.

People who are not actively seeking a job are not considered to be in the labor force, including discouraged workers who give up on their job search after failed attempts, whereas unemployed people who are actively seeking jobs are in the labor force. IT skills make it easier to participate in the labor force and to transition from unemployment to employment. First, IT skills are general-purpose skills, which are useful in different settings and can increase the probability of finding jobs with higher wages, as they increase one’s job productivity. IT skills are becoming an important component of human capital and have a high demand with the continuous increase of IT investments across multiple firms and industries (Atasoy et al. 2016). The findings in the IS and economics literatures on the effect of IT skills on workers’ wages and performance can be applicable to a larger pool of labor based on theories of general-purpose versus firm-specific knowledge and human capital formation. IT skills, in our context, are general-purpose skills, and these skills that are transferable to different settings are especially important in a worker’s long-term human capital, compared with firm-specific skills, which are costly to transfer across firms (Jensen and Meckling 1992). The labor economics literature has shown the role of education, training, and generalpurpose skills in labor market, offering a theoretical explanation on the relationship between various worker skills, employment, and wages (Becker 1962, Mincer 1974, Hamermesh 1986, Capelli 2004). There are positive gains from education and training, even after accounting for demographic characteristics, the direct and indirect costs of obtaining training or education, and the generally higher innate cognitive and intellectual abilities of people with more training and education (Becker 1994). IT skills that are not firm specific are valued in the market across firms and can improve workers’ productivity, helping them find jobs and earn higher wages throughout their careers.

Additionally, IT skills can be helpful in the job search process, which includes identifying and applying to jobs. IT skills lower the cost of job search by enabling easier access to job information and appli cations via the Internet (Kuhn and Skuterud 2004) Besides, online social and professional networks, such as LinkedIn, can yield job leads, interviews, and offers (Garg and Telang 2017). Finally, and increasingly more important recently, IT skills also enable the option of telecommuting and remote work settings (Staples et al. 1999, Gale et al. 2002), which can create flexible employment and part-time job opportunities for those workers with limited mobility, or preference for flexible work schedules. All these mechanisms can both increase labor force participation and employment possibilities. Therefore, we expect IT skills to lead to more employment opportunities for workers.

Summarizing these arguments, we propose that IT skills are associated with higher employment probability and higher wages. Nonetheless, this relationship may depend on the level and type of IT skills. Specifically, advanced IT skills are expected to have stronger complementarities with high-skilled jobs, and they can have a stronger positive role in help ing workers obtain higher wages (versus basic IT skills). This is because high-skilled occupations rely on knowledge that requires advanced IT skills to obtain and master in order to perform well in a job, thus commanding higher wages because of higher productivity. For example, programming skills might be required for an engineering occupation, whereas knowing more basic office skills could be useful in obtaining a clerical staff position. Thus, we expect advanced IT skills to be more crucial in higher-skill jobs and occupations with higher wages. This logic is consistent with skill-biased technical change theory indicating that IT intensity increases the relative wages of skilled to unskilled labor; therefore, higher IT skills are complementary to skilled labor (Acemoglu 1998, Autor et al. 1998). The theoretical mechanism behind skill-biased technical change theory can be further explained by task types involved in occupations (Autor et al. 2003), as we detail in Section 2.3. Combining these arguments, we propose the following hypotheses for testing.

Hypothesis 1. There is a positive relationship between IT skills and labor market outcomes (employment and wages).

Hypothesis 2. The positive relationship between IT skills and labor market outcomes is stronger for advanced IT skills compared with basic IT skills.

## 2.2. Role of IT Skills by Demographics (Gender and Age)

Next, we discuss the moderating role of sociodemographic characteristics, specifically gender and age. Low labor force participation and high unemployment rates among the underserved segments of the population are important public policy concerns. Gender and age are two key drivers of several labor market outcomes, such as job tenure and wages, and can moderate the relationship between IT skills and labor market outcomes. The literature has widely documented how work-family balance and job flexibility can play a role in women’s employment decisions and job satisfaction, thus creating disparities in employment across genders (Rousseau 1995, Scandura and Jankau 1997, Boden 1999). Similar gender issues in employment have been analyzed in the IS literature as well. Factors such as social expectations, gender roles, work-family conflict, and social networks are among the barriers for women in IT careers (Igbaria and Baroudi 1995, Ahuja 2002, Bapna and Funk 2021, Langer et al. 2020). Female IT workers prefer flexibility on the job market to improve work-family balance, and women are found to more likely to take on lower-status jobs and non-IT jobs, compared with male IT workers, consistent with stigmatization theory (Crocker et al. 1998, Joseph et al. 2015). The value of IT skills for non-IT jobs can also facilitate a preference for such jobs (Joseph et al. 2015).

Age is another important factor that has been debated in the IT industry since big companies faced legal action because of discrimination against older workers (Bloomberg Technology&Ideas 2018, Lexology 2019). Age can also shape interactions between IT skills and labor market outcomes. Opportunities to retire gradually with reduced commitment in terms of hours or workload are rated highly among older workers. Also, older workers often prefer more flexible employment as a means of bridge employment—taking up another job or work part-time after leaving one’s main career occupation (Loretto et al. 2005). Health problems and reduced mobility because of aging also create incentives for older workers to seek flexible work settings by working from home. Overall, socioeconomically marginalized groups (e.g., women and the elderly) have been shown to increase their labor force participation when employment options are more flexible (Mincer 1962, Fuchs 1980).

IT skills and basic computer knowledge are generally required for telecommuting and alternative work arrangements that involve remote work, and they are found to increase the use of flexible work organization practices and telecommunications technologies in addition to remote work self-efficacy (Staples et al 1999, Gale et al. 2002). Thus, even basic IT skills, such as the ability to use computers, send emails, download files, and use teleconferencing tools can afford workplace flexibility and enable those workers to telecommute and successfully engage in remote work practices. Such flexibility can encourage women and older workers to enter the workforce and pursue employment opportunities on their own terms. The marginal worker who decides whether to participate in the labor force or not, may be swayed by the availability of telecommuting and remote work, given the necessary IT skills. For example, a single mom who does not have childcare may not be able to work and could fall out of the labor force unless she has flexible job options enabled by her IT skills. Finally workers from underserved segments tend to possess fewer IT skills, on average, as they are less likely to adopt and accept new IT (Venkatesh et al. 2003, Niehaves and Plattfaut 2014), and a supply shortage of IT skills among their peers in the labor market can create a higher marginal impact on their IT skills. We, thus, propose the following.

Hypothesis 3. The positive relationship between IT skills and labor market outcomes is stronger for women.

Hypothesis 4. The positive relationship between IT skills and labor market outcomes is stronger for older workers.

## 2.3. The Role of IT Skills by Task Types (Routine or Nonroutine Tasks)

One mechanism behind the skill-biased technical change theory is attributed to how technology complements and substitutes different types of work tasks. Autor et al. (2003) formalized a simple theory of how technology affects the types of tasks needed across different occupations and shapes the demand for human skills. Tasks are broadly categorized as follows: (1) routine tasks with well-defined activities that can be executed in accordance with predefined rules (please see Table 2 for examples), and (2) nonroutine tasks, which are not well defined, and they involve problem-solving, complex communication, and visual or motor processing skills that cannot be carried out by following predefined rules.

We expect that IT skills complement certain types of occupations but are not equally useful for others, based on the types of labor tasks required by the given occupation, and whether the labor tasks can be performed by a well-defined set of rules. Routine labor tasks consist of well-defined repetitive activities that are readily programmable and may be substituted by IT (Bresnahan et al. 2002). Computerization has subsisted for repetitive information handling and customer service tasks, such as calculating, communicating, and coordinating functions of record keepers, cashiers, and telephone operators, as well as routine assembly (Bresnahan 1999).

On the other hand, IT skills can be more valuable for nonroutine tasks because they require collecting and interpreting data and information, which IT skills can complement (Levy and Murane 2004). Nonroutine cognitive tasks, such as managing people, making medical diagnoses, and offering legal support, involve human judgment, decision making, and complex communication skills; thus, these tasks are expected to have strong complementarities with IT skills. However, many nonroutine and manual tasks, such as janitorial services and truck driving, cannot be fully performed by predefined rules yet, and thus they have limited substitutability with technological advancements. These imply that IT substitutes routine tasks more than nonroutine tasks.

These arguments are consistent with the IS literature that analyzes how information intensity of tasks within occupations affects their disaggregation levels. These studies indicate that information-intensive, rulebased, and routine tasks, which do not require complex decision-making and communication, are more amenable to substitution because they can be easily codified and standardized. Accordingly, they are more likely to be moved to an offshore location within the value chain (Apte and Mason 1995, Mithas and Whitaker 2007).

Because IT substitutes routine tasks more than nonroutine tasks, IT skills would be a relative complement to workers performing nonroutine tasks. This is because workers with IT skills are likely to be more effective in handling nonroutine tasks. For example, a sonogram technician can use medical knowledge if he or she has the IT skills to retrieve, update, scan, or transmit sonogram images. This is also related to the fact that conducting routine information tasks is cheaper and easier with computerization and necessary IT skills, which, in turn, increases the productivity of workers performing nonroutine tasks. For example, ease of conducting comprehensive bibliographic searches can increase the productivity and work quality of employees who conduct legal research, and improvements in creating timely market and industry information help managers to make better decisions (Autor et al. 2003). Accordingly, we expect IT skills to be relatively more valuable in occupations that require more nonroutine tasks compared with routine tasks. Summarizing these arguments, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 5. The positive relationship between IT skills and labor market outcomes is relatively stronger in occupations that have a higher share of nonroutine tasks to routine tasks.

## 3. Data

## 3.1. Data Description

We used two major large-scale and individual-level data sets from the Turkish Statistical Institute (Turkstat) between 2007 and 2015 that are nationally representative: (1) the Household IT Use survey (HITU) and (2) the Household Labor Force survey (HLF). These are repeated cross-sectional surveys that cover all households across Turkey. Stratified clustered sampling method is used for data collection in both surveys that are designed to be representative nationally and across other selected criteria, such as demographics, regions, industries, and occupations for each year. The collection and compilation practices are in line with the European Commission (Eurostat) guidelines, which make the data reliable, credible, and comparable with the rest of Europe. Additional details on the data and the collection method can be found in Online Appendix A1.

The HITU extensively surveys national trends in IT use. This survey has around eight pages and 40 sections with questions on several topics, such as frequency and purpose of computer use for e-commerce, e-government, and personal purposes; mobile technology use; and security applications used. One section is on IT skills that we elaborate on later. The survey also provides information on individuals employment status and whether they participate in the labor force. There are three groups of individuals in the data set: (1) people who are employed, (2) people who are not employed but looking for a job (i.e., unemployed), and (3) people who are not employed and not looking for a job. The first two groups include individuals who are in the labor force; in other words, they are individuals who are willing to supply their labor, whereas people in the third group are not part of the labor force. Because our focus is on the role of IT skills in workers’ employment status, we restricted our sample to working-age adults between the ages of 16 and 65. We removed students and retirees who indicated that they were not seeking jobs, but we retained other people who were not seeking jobs because of a clear reason in analyzing employment outcomes because labor force participation is an endogenous decision, and IT skills can increase labor participation in the workforce by providing easier access to information and flexible work arrangements.<sup>3</sup> Finally, there was a significant change in IT skill questions in 2013, rendering them incomparable with those in other years; therefore, we removed 2013 from the data analysis. This led to a sample size of 121,485 observations for the individual level analysis based on the HITU data.

The HITU survey is a rich source of specific IT skills at the individual level; however, it does not include labor market data beyond employment and labor force participation status. We supplemented the HITU with the HLF survey, which also includes data on workers’ wages, occupations, and industries. The HLF survey data includes about 500,000 observations each year, making it the most comprehensive labor force data in Turkey. The survey covers a wide range of industries and occupations (see Online Appendix A1 for more details). The HLF survey is the main survey from which the national unemployment rates and labor statistics are calculated in the country.

We cannot directly match individual workers across these two data sets; however, because both data sets are representative at the national and socio-economic group levels and have a large number of observations, we are able to aggregate workers into labor cohorts, and we thus created a cohort-level panel data over time. We define each labor cohort as people of the same gender who are born in the same year. Cohort analysis is used in labor economics because people born in the same year are exposed to similar institutional and labor market conditions (Welch 1979, Kruger and Pischke 1992, Macunovich 1998, Card and Lemieux 2001, Juhn et al. 2005, Attanasio et al. 2008, Schwandt and Wachter 2019). In total, there are 103 labor cohorts for the working-age population, and a total of

766 cohort-year observations for cohorts that exist for more than one year between 2007 and 2015. We then calculated the ratio of people with IT skills for each cohort using data from the HITU survey. Moreover, the labor market variables from the HLF survey were aggregated for the same cohort. These variables include the ratio of people who are employed, part-time employed, in the labor force, average wages, and the ratio of employment in each major occupation and industry for each cohort. We then matched the cohorts across these two samples and created a cohortlevel panel. ## 4. Empirical Speci<sup>fi</sup>cation and Results 4.1. Identi<sup>fi</sup>cation Strategy

First, for the relation between IT skills and employment at the individual level, there can be omitted variables that are not observable. IT skills depend on an individual’s motivation and ability, both of which also affect the employment outcome. Therefore, unobserved individual characteristics can act as confounding factors. We used an instrumental variable (IV) approach to address potential unobserved heterogeneity by instrumenting the individual-level IT skills with the other individuals’ IT skills and IT use in the same labor cohort except for the focal individual and then except for the same location.

Second, there might be nonrandom self-selection in observing the outcome variable. That is, the omitted variable in this case is how people are selected in observing their employment status. An individual in the labor force can be employed or unemployed and looking for a job, whereas not being in the labor force indicates that the individual is not actively looking for a job. Employment status is only accurately observed for people who are in the labor force. If someone is not in the labor force, we cannot observe the counterfactual employment outcome of whether this individual would be employed, or not, assuming the individual were to look for a job. Simply removing individuals that are not part of the labor force would not address this problem because the decision to join the workforce is itself endogenous and can depend on the IT skills and other individual characteristics. Therefore, we used Heckman Selection models to account for this potential self-selection into the labor force (Heckman 1976, 1979; Mithas and Krishnan 2009; Aral et al. 2012; Goes at al. 2014).

Third, there may be contemporaneous cross-equation error correlations when empirically estimating the relationships across multiple variables. We estimated three equations for (a) IT skills, (b) employment status, and (c) labor force participation simultaneously at the individual level using seemingly unrelated regression (SUR) to account for the correlation in their error terms.

Fourth, reverse causality can take place if employment precedes acquiring IT skills; in other words, people can learn IT skills at their jobs after obtaining employment. We addressed this concern by using the questions on how the IT skills had been acquired, ultimately removing respondents who reported acquiring some IT skills at their jobs or at workshops requested by their employers at some point in time (around 7% of the sample). The results remained robust to this check, providing evidence that reverse causality is unlikely to be driving our results.

Besides the individual-level analyses, we also conduct cohort-level analyses by creating labor cohorts for workers of the same birth year and gender. The cohortlevel data enable analyzing other dependent variables besides employment, such as wages, ordered employment at different wage levels, and parttime employment, thus providing evidence for the underlying theoretical mechanisms through which IT skills can influence the proposed labor market outcomes. We track labor cohorts over time and use cohort-fixed effects, controlling for unobserved permanent heterogeneity across different cohorts. Therefore, the identification in the cohort-level analyses comes from within-cohort changes in employment rates, wages, and IT skills, which is not herein possible at the individual level. An important identifying assumption in the fixed effects model is that the trends in labor market outcomes for the cohorts with different levels of IT skills would be the same in the absence of IT skills. Therefore, we use cohort-specific time trends that estimate the effect of IT skills allowing each cohort to have its own employment and wage growth, and then measuring how the employment rate and average wages are different in each cohort with IT skill gains compared with what it would have been if each cohort continued its trend without IT skills (Angrist and Pischke 2008). Finally, we conducted a set of additional robustness tests that include removing IT occupations in which reverse causality and self-selection issues could be more of a concern, in addition to nonlinear models for fractional dependent variables.

4.2.3. IV Analysis. We used the IV approach to address unobservable individual characteristics, such as motivation and capability. We instrumented an individual’s IT skills by two sets of variables: (1) the average IT use and skills of the rest of his/her labor cohort, except for the focal individual; and (2) the average IT use and skills of the rest of his/her labor cohort except for everyone in the focal individual’s location (Table 9). The logic behind these IVs is that cohorts of the same birth year and gender can have a similar tendency to acquire IT skills and use IT because people of the same cohort face similar developments and observe similar market characteristics (Card and Lemieux 2001, Attanasio et al. 2008). On the other hand, IT skills and IT use of other people in the same labor cohort should not directly affect the focal worker’s employment outcomes, especially among individuals who do not live in the same geographical area because it is generally not feasible for them to directly compete for the same job, even if they have the same profession.

The assumption that the IVs satisfy the exclusion restriction, and thus are not correlated with the error term in the equation of interest, is not testable in exactly-identified models. If the model is overidentified (i.e., there are more instruments than endogenous variables), information becomes available to test this assumption. The most common test of these overidentifying restrictions is based on the observation that the residuals should be uncorrelated with the set of exogenous variables, if the IVs are truly exogenous (Sargan 1958). Accordingly, we used the overidentifying restrictions test for the validity of our IVs in column 1 where there are two instruments IT skill variable. The null hypothesis of this test is that the proposed IVs are valid. The resulting Sargan chisquared statistics for the test has a p value of 0.23. Therefore, we cannot reject the null hypothesis that our IVs are valid, even at the 10% significance level. We performed other tests for the validity of our IVs in this specification,<sup>7</sup> and we found no evidence that our model is underidentified or that the IVs are weak.

## 4.3. Cohort-Level Analysis

4.3.1. IT Skills and Cohort Employment Rate and Wages. As elaborated earlier, we integrated the HITU and HLF survey data to create labor cohorts to use additional labor market information (see Section 3.1 for details). We summarized the IT-related variables from the HITU survey, and the labor market outcomes from the HLF survey at the cohort-level by calculating ratios and averages. We estimated the following ordinary least squares specification with cohort-fixed effects:

$$
\begin{array}{c} \text { Employment   Ratio } _ {c, t} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \text { IT   Skills   Ratio } _ {c, t} + \delta X _ {c, t} \\ + \theta_ {c} + \alpha_ {t} + c _ {i t}. \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

The dependent variable is the employment ratio of cohort c at year t (number of people employed in the cohort/total number of people in the cohort), IT Skills $\mathrm { R a t i o } _ { c , t }$ is the ratio of people that have IT skills of cohort c at year t (number of people that has IT skills in the cohort/total number of people in the cohort), and $X _ { c , t }$ represents cohort characteristics at year $t ,$ such as the cohort’s average years of education and labor force participation ratio. We controlled for the employment ratios in different industries and occupations in each cohort. Cohort-fixed effects model (θ ) control for unobserved permanent heterogeneity among labor cohorts; accordingly, our identification comes from within-cohort changes in IT skills and labor market outcomes. Year-fixed effects (α ) account for potential shocks to the national labor market. Because the employment ratio is a continuous fraction between 0 and 1, we check the robustness of the results using a nonlinear model, Beta regression for fractional dependent variables (Table A8 in the online appendix).

There is a positive relationship between the cohortlevel employment ratio and the ratio of people who have IT skills in the cohort. If the ratio of IT skills of the cohort increases by one standard deviation (0.27), the employment rate increases by around 1.9 (0.27 × 0.07 = 0.0189) percentage points (column 1). Higher levels of basic IT skills are associated with a higher cohort employment rate, whereas higher levels of advanced IT skills do not have a statistically significant effect on the employment rate in the cohort, similar to the individual-level analysis.

Next, we analyzed the relationship between IT skills and labor wages. Columns 3 and 4 of Table 10 present the results where the log of average wage income of the cohort is the dependent variable. In column 3, there is no statistically significant relationship between the number of IT skills and the average income of the cohort. Column 4 indicates that basic IT skills do not increase average wages in the cohort, whereas more people with advanced IT skills are associated with higher cohort average income.

These results reveal an important distinction between the two focal labor market outcomes in this study—employment and wages. Although advanced IT skills do not affect employment rates incrementally over basic IT skills, they are associated with higher income levels, implying that advanced IT skills might help workers target and compete for higher-quality jobs. Basic IT skills are increasing employment rates; despite they do not affect the average income of the cohort. This may be due to basic IT skills facilitating different types of jobs including part-time and low-paid jobs for which having basic IT skills can create a competitive advantage.

We then calculate and examine an ordered probability of employment at different wage levels. This variable varies between 0 and 4, 1 being employed at the 25th percentile of the wage distribution or lower, 2 being employed between 25th and 50th percentile, 3 being employed between 50th and 75th percentile, and 4 being employed with a wage over the 75th percentile of the wage distribution, and then averaged for each cohort. The idea behind this variable is to reconcile the two dependent variables of employment and wages and to take into account not only being employed but also being employed at different income levels. We observe that advanced skills are associated with a higher probability of employment in a high wage bracket. Integrating the results, basic IT skills are shown to increase employment probability, whereas advanced IT skills incrementally increase the probability that workers are employed in a higher-paid job.

4.3.2. Results by Demographics. In Table 11, we estimated the relationship between cohort-level employment and IT skills that differ by gender and age. The results indicate that if the ratio of people who has basic IT skills increases by one standard deviation (0.27), the increase in employment rate is 3.2 percentage points higher for cohorts of women than men. Similarly, possessing basic IT skills is more valuable for cohorts of the older population. For a cohort that is one year older, the effect of IT skills on the employment rate is higher by 1.2 percentage points. These findings may be because of women and older workers’ preference for flexible work practices that can be facilitated by IT skills, such as telecommuting. To test this explanation, we used part-time employment as a dependent variable. There is no significant relationship between the number of IT skills and parttime employment, on average (not tabulated). This relationship is stronger for female and older workers (column 3), and this effect of IT skills on part-time employment for women and the elderly is driven by basic IT skills (column 4). These analyses offer evidence supporting the overarching theoretical logic that flexible job settings can be facilitated by basic IT skills.

## 4.4. Additional Robustness Checks

4.4.1. Seemingly Unrelated Regressions. As an alternative specification at the individual level, we estimate three probit regressions with maximum likelihood estimator using an SUR model. This model controls for the correlation among the error terms across the equations for different related dependent variables. The results are robust to this specification and presented in Table A4 in the online appendix.

4.4.2. Acquisition of IT Skills at Work. One of the endogeneity concerns at the individual level is reverse causality, because people can gain their IT skills at work. To address this, we used the additional questions in our database where people reported how they gained their IT skills that are available for 2007, 2008, and 2011. There are multiple mechanisms for acquiring IT skills (Table 5), one of which is attending a workshop or a class initiated by the employer or gaining IT skills at work, and 7% of people who have IT skills reported that they gained an IT skill with this mechanism. Although this does not necessarily indicate they received training at their current job, we removed all those workers who reported gaining an IT skill at work or via employer requested a workshop or a class. Table A5 in the online appendix presents the results following the Heckman two-step selection model (Table 8) after removing these workers. Our coefficients remain similar, providing evidence that the results are not likely driven by reverse causality.

4.4.3. Cohort-Speci<sup>fi</sup>c Time Trends. We checked the robustness of the cohort-level analysis by including cohort-specific time trends. This allowed us to address the issue that different cohorts might be on different trends in terms of their labor market outcomes and IT skills. The results are presented in Table A6 in the online appendix, and our main cohort level findings remain robust to the inclusion of cohort-specific time trends.

4.4.4. Removing IT Occupations. The IT skills reported in the HITU survey are general-purpose skills, we expect them to be beneficial for a wide variety of occupations and not only for IT occupations. The HLF survey covers several occupations, and this allows us to test whether the IT skills are valuable for all types of occupations or only for IT-related jobs. Additionally, reverse causality and self-selection into learning IT skills could be more severe for IT occupations (e.g., workers with IT jobs can learn that programming language at work). Thus, we removed occupation categories that include IT occupations and the results remained similar (Table A7 in the online appendix).<sup>9</sup> This offers further evidence that the focal IT skills are general purpose and valuable in most occupations beyond strictly IT jobs.

## 5. Discussion

## 5.1. Key Findings

This paper examines the relation between IT skills and labor market outcomes using individual-level data sets that are representative nationally and across demographic groups, occupations, and industries. On average, there is a positive relationship between IT skills and employment for workers who are not IT professionals, but there is no significant relationship between IT skills and wages. Instead, these relations depend on the type of IT skills one possesses: basic IT skills are associated with higher employment rates, whereas advanced IT skills are associated with higher wages. When employment rates at different wage levels are analyzed, we find that advanced IT skills increase employment in the higher wage groups. These results indicate that workers with basic IT skills could benefit more in terms of competing for low-skill jobs that attract a heterogeneous labor pool in which basic IT skills can differentiate workers. Advanced IT skills are a requirement for high-skilled jobs, among other specialized and sophisticated qualifications, and even though advanced IT skills might not differentiate workers in the smaller candidate pool for high-skilled jobs, they can enable workers to enter the competition for such positions with higher wages. Interestingly, the relationship between basic IT skills and employment is stronger for the traditionally underserved populations in the labor market, such as women and older workers. The key finding that basic IT skills increase part-time jobs for women and older workers supports our theoretical argumentation that flexible work settings can facilitate employment for these workers. There are also complementarities between IT skills and nonroutine tasks that require decision making, problem solving, and communication skills.

## 5.2. Contributions and Implications for Theory and Research

Our study contributes to the literature on IT skills. Many studies have found evidence of the effect of IT skills on the success of IT professionals and business managers (Bassellier et al. 2003, Langer et al. 2008). We extend these studies by analyzing a nationally representative pool of workers with different professions. We leveraged a government-administered individual-level IT use survey that includes information on stratified segments of the population and the labor force, including predominantly low-level and medium-level jobs. The representative nature of the data allowed us to infer the importance of IT skills, not only for IT professionals, high-skilled IT labor, or business managers, who have been studied extensively in IS literature, but also for the overall labor force that includes all types of workers, occupations, and industries. Thus, our study contributes to the literature on IT skills by using data on a large labor population going beyond the IT workforce and focusing on heterogeneous effects in underserved segments of society and virtually all occupations.

The IS and economics literatures focus on wage and performance effects of IT skills, firm-specific IT skills and experience, using data on employees. The measures of IT in the literature are driven by people who are already employed and do not extend to the unemployed or people who are part of the labor force. The IT skills variable in our data set accounts for a type of human capital, regardless of labor market status, and this survey question is recorded for the employed, unemployed, and people who are not in the workforce. This allowed us to analyze additional labor outcomes at the individual level that require the entire labor pool, such as labor force participation and employment status. We thus extend the extant literature by providing evidence on the effects of IT skills on broader labor market outcomes across many segments of the workforce. Finally, most of the prior literature has focused on the United States or other developed countries; however, studies on developing countries are still scarce. Critically, the role of IT skills may be more critical in developing countries where IT skills are not as prevalent in the population and the digital divide may be wider.

## 5.3. Implications for the Future of Work and Education

Our findings have implications for the future of work and education, which are digitized more every day, particularly after the COVID-19 crisis. As IT systems have proliferated rapidly in firms in the digital economy, virtually all workers will soon be expected to have general-purpose IT skills to effectively interact with IT systems. Besides IT professionals and business managers for whom IT skills are fundamental to their jobs, IT skills are likely to become valuable among all workers across multiple occupations throughout firms. IT skills are necessary for people to leverage employment opportunities, explore career paths, and engage in professional development and lifelong learning using online platforms. Our results should empower workers to continuously invest in their human capital and acquisition of IT skills to enhance the trajectory of their employment and remain productive in an increasingly IT-driven world that offers more work and educational opportunities via digital platforms.

The basic IT skills, which can be gained fairly easily with exposure to computers, not only offer valuable benefits for workers in terms of labor force participation and employment opportunities, but they are fast becoming fundamental in the workplace and in the classroom. Remote work and online education are turning into integral parts of our lives, especially after the COVID-19 pandemic that made having IT access and IT skills a necessity. Basic IT skills and computer knowledge are required for telecommuting and distance education, which emphasizes the importance of IT skills in the training of the increasingly IT-savvy workforce of the future.

Higher education is significantly changing as universities rethink and redesign how to provide more of their programs and student services online. This transformation can make higher education more accessible as well; however, this requires students to have the necessary IT skills. Thus, universities can provide basic IT training for all current and prospective students and educators to facilitate distance education settings and relevant modes of learning that take advantage of technology. In our data, we also see that most people gained their basic IT skills with self-exploration and trial and error (Table 5); thus, offering necessary resources and tutorials online can be a cost-effective and efficient way for basic IT skills training. Universities can also offer guidelines on the necessary IT skills to be able to successfully participate in open and distance education in order to clearly communicate expectations from students and enable them to self-assess their IT skills. Indeed, some higher education institutions provide lists for required IT skills needed in distance education that resonate with the basic IT skills covered in this study, such as using copy/paste functions and saving/moving documents. 10

Such educational programs for digital literacy and flexible learning methods can be incorporated in curricula besides higher education, such as high schools, vocational schools, and others. Tech companies who have domain knowledge can also give back to the community and contribute to education with IT skills training programs. Google’s Applied Digital Skills program launched in 2019 is a good example of such an initiative that aims to train teachers and students to develop their basic IT skills, such as file organization, document formatting, and spreadsheet design

Overall, these educational programs can help nations and societies to become more productive by enabling people to receive the necessary education and to complement the rapid sophistication of IT skills, preventing them from being automated out by technology and ensuring that school systems include relevant IT training in their curricula to furnish individual workers with relevant IT skills.

This study also has implications for practice. It is becoming essential for organizations to ensure their employees have the necessary IT skills to participate in remote work and flexible work settings when needed, thus, training programs for basic IT skills can be valuable in most organizations, particularly after COVID-19 that forced organizations to work from home. Besides, given the desire for diversity in today’s organizations, managers should be aware of the importance of employing traditionally disadvantaged groups, such as women and older workers, and organizations should provide occupation-relevant training for these marginalized groups to obtain IT skills. Managers should use our findings to help leverage qualified workers with specific IT skills to fill different labor needs in organizations. Furthermore, our findings can help organizations to enjoy a more productive workforce by training workers with relevant IT skills to augment IT systems with appropriate IT skills (as opposed to merely automating workers with artificial intelligence). Taken together, organizations can offer on-the-job IT training programs to build an ITsavvy workforce to both satisfy the increasing need for IT sophistication and flexible work settings in organizations and the need for workforce diversity.

## 5.4. Implications for Public Policy

Public policies that encourage IT access in rural and underserved areas are common in countries where computer and Internet penetration is low. However, such IT policies are not limited to developing countries. For example, in the United States, the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act of 2012 gave over \$18 billion to increase Internet access. The first stated goal of this policy was to increase employment and growth. Several public policy initiatives in Turkey, initially launched in 2003, aim to raise labor productivity and employment by improving IT infrastructure and IT access. Therefore, our results have public policy implications, especially for developing countries, and they provide guidance on how different IT skills would affect employment and wages differently across major socioeconomic groups. These results emphasize the importance of IT policies that promote computer access at home, in schools, and in communities through public libraries, because having access to computers and IT is critical to be connected to the labor market and gaining at least basic IT skills.

The digital divide is a serious societal concern across the globe because of the adverse socioeconomic costs for people who lack IT access. This topic is widely studied in the IS literature with a focus on determinants of individual-, regional-, and country-level IT adoption and across different socio-economic groups (Dewan and Riggins 2005, Kauffman and Techatassanasoontorn 2005, Barki et al. 2007, Agarwal et al. 2009). The digital divide is particularly significant for traditionally disadvantaged groups, such as women and the elderly, who generally have lower access to IT, lower levels of IT use, and lower IT acceptance (Venkatesh et al. 2003). Mitigating the digital divide is a key objective for policymakers, and empowering groups that have been underprivileged in labor markets would be a step in a societally beneficial direction. Thus, our findings should guide policymakers’ initiatives with regards to instituting appropriate legislation in terms of IT-related public policies that could aid disadvantaged groups in identifying and securing employment. Women and older workers are likely to benefit disproportionately more from possessing basic IT skills, and given their belonging to underprivileged groups in the labor market and the vastly positive effect obtaining IT skills would net them, IT skills would benefit women and older workers, not only in the labor market, but also in their professional development and overall welfare.

It should be noted that the policy recommendations arising from this study might be subject to general equilibrium effects. Gaining IT skills can increase the competitive advantage of workers; however, the returns to these IT skills may diminish as more workers gain IT skills over time. This may be less of a concern in developing countries where the prevalence of IT skills is still low, and our findings imply that a more targeted IT training of disadvantaged workers, specifically women and the elderly, could be more beneficial from a societal perspective.

## 5.5. Limitations and Suggestions for Future Research

This study has limitations that largely arise from inherent data constraints. First, our data set is yearlypooled cross-sectional data that do not allow us to track individuals over time. Thus, we cannot infer the longitudinal effects. Individual panel data can offer other opportunities to address causality, especially by observing the timing of changes in IT skills and related labor market outcomes. In terms of leveraging an exogenous shock, we could not identify a specific policy that could potentially serve as a natural experiment, since most policies expand over several years and also target education and healthcare, making it difficult to pinpoint the exact implementation.

Second, IT skills are self-reported by the survey respondents; thus, the measurement of IT skills might not be entirely accurate. Similarly, we are not able to observe the motivations of individuals as to why they decided to learn IT skills in the first place. We do sanity checks on the IT skills measures, and we tried to address potential self-selection biases with several analyses; however, this is a standard limitation of such large scale, nationally representative surveys conducted by government agencies. On the other hand, other ways of collecting data usually do not result in a large scale and representative sample. Our data set is designed to be representative of the entire country, which is useful in analyzing policy-relevant research questions, and similar large-scale surveys are typically used by governments to form nationwide public policies.

Third, we do not observe individuals’ experience levels with their IT skills. A specific IT skill was considered to be “present” if the individual reported that she had that particular skill at a given point in time, which does not measure the experience or level of knowledge. This could be especially important for advanced IT skills as for positions that require highlevel skills, the quality of the skill is more essential compared with basic IT skills that have a lower variation in quality. We expect our results to be stronger if we could observe the experience level instead of merely an IT skill’s presence since the potential effects of IT skills are likely to be driven by people who have greater experience with IT skills. If we assume that only a portion of people has extensive experience with IT skills, and if we still find a significant average effect when using all people who have IT skills (at any level), we should find an even stronger effect if we could measure the exact and objective level of experience of all people. Therefore, our findings can be interpreted as a lower bound of IT skill effects.

Finally, we used a unique data set from Turkey that enables the use of microlevel variables that are not usually available in public sources, but our findings are also grounded in the context of a developing country. Theoretically, the relationship between IT skills and labor market outcomes should hold both for developed and developing countries. However, in terms of empirical implications, they can diverge. There are significant differences between developed and developing countries with respect to technology diffusion and labor market conditions. Developing countries lag behind in terms of IT access and use, and they are characterized by higher unemployment rates and income inequality compared with developed countries. These factors could lead to two differences in empirical implications. First, we expect the current relevant IT skills to be more sophisticated in developed countries. Second, lower prevalence of IT skills and fewer job opportunities in the labor market might make the incremental effect of having IT skills higher in a devel oping country setting. One advantage of the HITU Survey of Turkstat is that it is designed and collected based on Eurostat standards; the data are directly comparable with that from other European countries Based on Eurostat data<sup>12</sup> on the digital economy, the levels and trends of Internet access and use are similar across Turkey and Eastern European countries such as Bulgaria, Romania, and Greece, whereas the rates in Western European countries are significantly higher. Besides, Eastern European countries such as Bulgaria and Romania have similar unemployment rates as Turkey.<sup>13</sup> Accordingly, we believe that our results might generalize to other developing countries, particularly in Europe, that have similar trends in terms of IT access and the labor market. Future research in different countries would be needed to empirically support these arguments and the generalizability of our findings to other nations.<sup>14</sup>

## 5.6. Concluding Remark

The role of IT on economic and social outcomes has been a key question in the IS and economics literatures. IT policies generally aim to increase employment and job opportunities by offering access to IT, reducing the digital divide, and enabling people to obtain IT skills. In this study, we attempt to untangle a small part of the multifaceted link between IT skills and labor market outcomes, particularly given the negative impact of COVID-19 on employment and the increased need for telecommuting, remote work, and virtual education. We hope our study encourages additional research on the theoretically challenging and practically relevant question of how IT skills shape labor market outcomes at multiple levels across socio-economic groups, occupations, and industries. Such research, particularly across nations, could develop a better understanding of the broader role of IT skills in employment and wages across the globe. This could help policymakers reduce the digital divide and help people obtain relevant IT skills to make them competitive in a global economy that increasingly relies on IT.
