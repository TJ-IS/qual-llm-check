# The Deterrent Effect of Ride-Sharing on Sexual Assault and Investigation of Situational Contingencies

Jiyong Park,<sup>a</sup> Min-Seok Pang,<sup>b</sup> Junetae Kim,<sup>c</sup> Byungtae Lee<sup>d</sup>

Abstract. Sexual assault is one of the most repellant and costly crimes, which inflicts irrecoverable harms on victims and society. This study examines the effect of information technology (IT)-enabled ride-sharing platforms on sexual assaults. Drawing upon routine activity theory from the criminology literature, we posit that ride-sharing can reduce a passenger’s risk of being a suitable target of sexual assault by providing a more reliable and timely transportation option for traveling to a safer place. By exploiting the nationwide quasi-experimental setting of Uber’s city-by-city rollouts in the United States during 2005–2017, we demonstrate that Uber’s entry into a city is negatively associated with the number of rape incidents. To zoom into the effects of ride-sharing at a more granular level, we employ precinct-hour–level data on Uber pickups and rape occurrences in New York City in 2015 and conduct spatiotemporal analyses. Our results from the spatiotemporal analyses corroborate those of the quasi-experiment and further reveal situational contingencies in the deterrent effect of ride-sharing. Specifically, ride-sharing contributes to a more significant reduction in the likelihood of rape occurrences in neighborhoods with limited transportation accessibility, and ride-sharing is more effective in deterring sexual crime in riskier circumstances, such as around alcohol-serving places on weekend nights or when the probability of crime occurrences increases. This study sheds new light on the potential of IT-enabled platforms to improve social well-being beyond their economic contributions and offers a new theoretical insight on the distinct role of digital platforms in public safety.

## Introduction

Crime is one of the most serious societal challenges, and it is paramount to deter malicious social conduct that threatens the property and safety of others in any civilized society. Among many types of crimes, sexual assault is considered to be among the most repellent and costly (McCollister et al. 2010, Chalfin 2015) as it entails substantial social and monetary costs to the victims and society. Such costs consist of tangible ones— including police responses, medical care, mental health services, and judicial processes to convict sexual offenders—and intangible ones—including a deteriorated quality of life, pain, and mental and physical suffering (Miller et al. 1996).

As information technology (IT)-enabled ride-sharing platforms, such as Uber and Lyft, have grown to an unprecedented scale, increasing attention of policymakers and researchers has been paid to the societal benefits and harms of ride-sharing beyond its economic impacts. In particular, many detractors of ride-sharing and regulators in major cities have raised safety concerns over ride-sharing platforms, including allegations of sexual crimes against passengers as well as assaults against drivers (e.g., Lafrance and Eveleth 2015). On the other hand, ride-sharing could be a viable transportation mode for a potential crime target to avoid dangerous and unfavorable situations (Weber 2019), an aspect that is largely neglected in the debate on ride-sharing and public safety. In many cases, potential offenders often attempt to prevent their targets from leaving the scene by controlling transportation modes. A case in point is a story that a woman shared on her social media in the midst of the #MeToo movement: “I was 20. I went to a restaurant with friends. My roommate and ride left me. My other friends’ boyfriend offered to give me a ride home. He did not. He took me to his place. He raped me. I had no choice. No way out . . .”<sup>1</sup> If she were able to leave the restaurant in any other way, she might not have been victimized. Although such anecdotes imply that ride availability can shape circumstances around crime occurrences, to the best of our knowledge, we have little understanding of how the emergence of ITenabled transportation systems, namely ride-sharing platforms, affect criminal offenses, particularly sexual assaults.

Given the enormous costs and damages from sexual assaults, this study aims to answer the following research question: can a ride-sharing platform provide a viable means to prevent sexual crimes? In answering this question, we focus on overall patterns of sexual assault in a given area beyond those committed by ride-sharing drivers. Routine activity theory explains how criminal activities are influenced by circumstances or environmental settings in which criminal offenders and targets are situated (Cohen and Felson 1979). Synthesizing this with the information systems (IS) literature on digital platforms, we theorize how the availability of IT-enabled ridesharing services reshapes a potential victim’s risk and exposure to sexual crimes. Specifically, we propose that ride-sharing deters sexual assaults by reducing a passenger’s propensity to become a suitable target as the passenger has access to more reliable and timely transportation options to evade a highly vulnerable situation.

To support our theoretical arguments, we exploit a quasi-experimental setting in which Uber has entered and operated in different locations at different times in the United States. This setting allows us to employ a difference-in-differences (DID) model to establish a causal link between ride-sharing availability and sexual assault incidents. By consolidating a nationwide, city-by-city data set from 2005–2017, we find that the entry of Uber into a city is significantly associated with a reduction in the number of rape incidents. An extensive range of robustness checks, including matching, placebo tests with randomly shuffled treatments, and falsification tests, allow us to rule out alternative explanations.

Moreover, to tease out underlying mechanisms and further investigate situational contingencies in the effect of ride-sharing, we conduct spatiotemporal analyses by using fine-grained precinct-hour–level data on Uber pickups and rape occurrences in New York City (NYC) in January–June 2015. To overcome a potential endogeneity problem, we use traffic-related air pollution as an instrumental variable (IV) for ridesharing transactions and estimate an IV linear probability model (LPM) with various levels of spatiotemporal fixed effects. The spatiotemporal analyses demonstrate that the frequency of Uber transactions is negatively associated with the likelihood of rape occurrences in a given neighborhood, which corroborates the finding from the quasi-experimental DID analysis. Intriguingly, this deterrent effect of ride-sharing is contingent upon spatiotemporal conditions and nuanced situational factors that could influence the risk of being a suitable target, which helps us to substantiate our theoretical arguments. Specifically, we find that ride-sharing is more effective in deterring sexual assaults in neighborhoods with limited transportation accessibility, particularly taxis, such as the outer boroughs in NYC and neigh borhoods with a higher percentage of non-White residents. These findings echo the distinct role of ITenabled ride-sharing platforms in providing more reliable and timely ride services. We delve deeper into risker situations with more suitable targets and potential perpetrators, in which ride-sharing could intervene. The results show that the deterrent effect of ride-sharing on sexual assault is accentuated in areas with a high density of places serving alcohol during Friday and Saturday nights and in crime-prone situations, such as when other crimes occur in a neighborhood or when street lights are reported to be broken. These findings are consistent with our theoretical hypothesis that ride-sharing helps potential targets of sexual assaults to avoid vulnerable situations in which the risk of being targeted escalates.

This research makes several key contributions to the IS literature. First, to the best of our knowledge, this is one of the few studies that demonstrate a significant role of IT-enabled platforms in crime deterrence. The IS literature has paid little attention to crimes and violence, two of the most chronic societal problems that are of concern to all, and particularly to sexual assault, which inflicts irrecoverable harms on victims and society. By integrating criminology theories with the IS literature on digital platforms, we propose a new theoretical model that explains the crime-prevention effect of IT-enabled platforms. Our research expands the theoretical body of knowledge on digital platforms by demonstrating that, owing to its ability to achieve a seamless matching of supply (drivers) and demand (passengers) in real time, ridesharing plays a distinct role in crime prevention compared with other traditional transportation modes, such as taxis and public transits, especially in the markets in which transport supply rarely meets transport demand. This interdisciplinary approach provides new theoretical and practical insights to the multiple disciplines of IS, criminology, and public/ urban economics. Second, we contribute to the literature on the societal impacts of IT (Chan and Ghose 2014, Chan et al. 2016, Greenwood and Agarwal 2016, Cheng et al. 2020), particularly in relation to sharing economy (Zervas et al. 2017) and ride-sharing platforms (Greenwood and Wattal 2017). By employing multiple identifications with different data sets to investigate the same phenomenon, this study goes beyond prior studies by uncovering a variety of situational contingencies with regard to the societal impacts of digital platforms.

Our study also has meaningful implications for policymakers and has the potential to reshape ongoing dialogues on the sharing economy. In reporting sexual assault incidents that have involved ridesharing platforms, the popular media tend to pay more attention to allegations of rape by ride-sharing drivers. However, to the best of our knowledge, there is little systematic evidence on the general relationship between ride-sharing availability and sexual assaults. For effective policy formulation in the sharing economy, this study provides an alternative perspective with a comprehensive and rigorous investigation into ride-sharing and public safety. Our findings suggest that ride-sharing provides an additional level of protection to potential victims, particularly in neighborhoods with inadequate transportation or in circumstances that are more prone to sexual crimes. Thus, to improve public safety, cities should facilitate urban transportation infrastructures that are seamless, convenient, and reliable so that potential vulnerable victims can avoid danger quickly and safely. In this respect, digital technologies can meet these needs to achieve public safety and improve crime prevention.

## Theoretical Development

## A Theoretical Framework for Sexual Assault

A large body of literature in criminology has extensively studied the relationship between socioeconomic factors and violent crimes, including sexual assault. Scholars have proposed various perspectives to explain the patterns in violent crimes, including conflict theory (Taylor et al. 1973), subcultural theory (Wolfgang et al. 1967), strain theory (Merton 1938), and social disorganization theory (Shaw and McKay 1942). Although these theories have successfully explained the spatial patterns of crime rates and the temporal patterns over a relatively longer term, they are not well suited for elucidating spatiotemporal variations in crimes over shorter time intervals, such as within a day, and for investigating environmental or situational factors that influence crime occurrence. In this regard, we find that routine activity theory (RAT) provides the most relevant perspective for our context and theoretical focus.

RAT explains how criminal activities are influenced by circumstances or environmental settings in which crime offenders and targets are situated (Cohen and Felson 1979, Cohen et al. 1981). According to this theory, for a crime to occur, it requires “the convergence in space and time of likely offenders, suitable targets, and the absence of capable guardians” (Cohen and Felson 1979, p. 589). It purports that a crime is most likely to be committed against a “suitable target,” or an individual who is more visible and accessible by a potential offender (Felson and Boba 2010). RAT identifies five risk factors that affect target suitability: exposure, guardianship, proximity to potential offenders, attractiveness of potential targets, and the definitional properties of specific crimes (Cohen et al. 1981). This theory has been widely adopted to understand spatiotemporal patterns in violent crimes (Cohen et al. 1981, Stahura and Sloan 1988, Fisher et al. 1998, Groff 2007).

By focusing on the environment in which an en counter between an offender and a target ensues and a subsequent crime takes place, RAT provides an appropriate theoretical lens to explain how ride-sharing platforms can reduce a passenger’s risk of becoming a suitable target of sexual assault in a neighborhood. As illustrated in Figure 1, the effect of ride-sharing on crime prevention is formulated as a combination of (i) its capability to change the situations or environments in which a suitable target of sexual assault encounters a potential offender and (ii) its availability in such situations.<sup>2</sup>

More formally, let $P ( A \mid B )$ be the probability of A given condition B. Then, the marginal effect of ridesharing on sexual assault, which is our main interest, can be expressed as follows:

Marginal effect of ride-sharing

$$
\begin{array}{l} = P (R a p e | w / r i d e s h a r i n g) - P (R a p e | w / o r i d e s h a r i n g) \\ = P (R a p e | w / r i d e s h a r i n g) - \frac {P (R a p e \cap w / o r i d e s h a r i n g)}{1 - P (w / r i d e s h a r i n g)} \\ = \frac {P (R a p e | w / r i d e s h a r i n g) - P (R a p e)}{1 - P (w / r i d e s h a r i n g)} \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

In this framework, the numerator indicates the difference between the probability of sexual assault with ride sharing (P<sub>(</sub>Rape | w/ ridesharing<sub>)</sub>) and the overall probability of sexual assault (P Rape ). The denominator indicates the availability of ride-sharing (P w/ridesharing ). Hence, as the probability of getting a ride through ride-sharing platforms increases, so does the marginal (negative) effect of ride-sharing on the likelihood of rape occurrence. This framework proposes that there are two main drivers of the effect of ridesharing on sexual assault: (i) the deterrent effect of ride-sharing (the numerator in Equation (1)) and (ii) its availability (the denominator in Equation (1)), which guide our theoretical developments. In the next few sections, we discuss the mechanisms of how and where ride-sharing as a transportation mode can alter situations in which crime targets are situated.

## Ride-Sharing and Routine Activity

Based on in-depth interviews with 69 sex offenders, Beauregard et al. (2007) propose a search process that sex offenders follow when looking for potential targets (or a “hunting process”), involving the following considerations: (i) choice of a hunting field, (ii) victim selection, (iii) a method of approach, (iv) an attack location choice, (v) a method to bring the victim to the crime scene, (vi) a crime scene choice, (vii) a method to commit the crime, and (viii) a victim release location choice. In such a process, we posit that ride-sharing can affect the first two phases by moving potential crime targets and thereby reducing their proximity to potential offenders and exposure to sexual assaults.

According to RAT, exposure to crime is associated with visibility, in which an offender knows of the existence of a target, and accessibility, in which it is easy for the offender to approach the target. Therefore, the more exposure the target has, the more suitable it is for a crime to take place against the target (Cohen and Felson 1979). According to Beauregard et al. (2007), “hunting fields” are most frequently identified by sex offenders based on local visibility, and victims are selected most often because of their location and availability. For example, social activities frequently happen away from the home, which brings a target closer to potential offenders (Franklin et al. 2012), and high visibility and accessibility on dangerous streets or areas after dark increase exposure to sexual violence (Miethe and Meier 1990, Rodgers and Roberts 1995). Additionally, participating in alcohol consumption or drug use is associated with an increased risk of being a victim of sexual violence because alcohol intoxication or drug use can lead to a loss of self-guardianship and increase exposure to potential offenders (Mustaine and Tewksbury 1999, Tewksbury and Mustaine 2001). Schwartz and Pitts (1995) provide empirical evidence that a lifestyle of going out drinking increases the suitability of targets of sexual assault.

Situational crime prevention strategy, rooted in RAT, aims to deter crime occurrence by changing routines and/or circumstances that foster opportunities for crimes (Clarke 1983). For example, Weber (2014) demonstrates that a university’s safe ride program helps move potential victims off the street, reducing crime occurrences in the service provision area. In this regard, ride-sharing services can change potential targets’ situation by safely and quickly transporting them from risky places (e.g., drinking districts or unfrequented streets) to ones with lower exposure to crime (e.g., home), thereby helping them to avoid hazardous situations in the wrong place at the wrong time.

Moreover, controlling transportation is a common way in which a potential offender tries to prevent a victim from leaving. Based on in-depth interviews with college students, Armstrong et al. (2006, p. 490) illustrate the situation in campus parties at which “transportation home was an uncertainty. . . Two women indignantly described a situation where fraternity men ‘wouldn’t give us a ride home. . .’ The men discouraged them from calling the cab and eventually found a designated driver.” In these situations, ridesharing services could provide an alternative transportation option and quickly transport a potential target away from a risky place to a safer place. It is worth noting that the transportation availability afforded by ride-sharing can help not only potential targets of sexual assaults by strangers, but by hostile acquaintances at “soluble” situations in which ridesharing could play a role, such as those related to alcohol consumption (see Online Appendix D for further discussion).<sup>3</sup>

Based on the foregoing arguments on the exposure and proximity to potential offenders, we put forth that P<sub>(</sub>Rape | w/ Ridesharing<sub>)</sub> < P<sub>(</sub>Rape<sub>)</sub> in Equation (1) and propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1. Ride-sharing availability is negatively associated with the likelihood of sexual assault occurrence in a given area.

## The Distinct Roles of Ride-Sharing vis-a-vis Other\` Transportation Modes

When it comes to the contingencies of the deterrent effect of ride-sharing, we propose that ride-sharing can limit exposure to sexual assaults in neighbor hoods with limited accessibility to traditional transportation modes, such as public transits and taxis (i.e., low accessibility neighborhoods) to a greater extent than in neighborhoods with increased accessibility to these modes of transport. Given that offering additional transportation options to a potential victim is not necessarily exclusive to ride-sharing, the marginal effect of ride-sharing availability is likely to be smaller in downtown areas where there are already sufficient alternative transportation options, such as taxis, buses, and subways, as compared with suburban areas. However, in transport markets in which supply barely meets demand, such as in suburbs and areas on the outskirts of a city, providing additional transportation options through ride-sharing platforms can be a more viable means of reducing the risk of sexual crimes attributable to lack of transportation; that is, P Rape | w/ Ridesharing  P Rape in Equation (1) is likely to have a greater negative value in low-accessibility neighborhoods.

Furthermore, our theoretical argument on the distinct role of ride-sharing in comparison with other transportation modes, such as taxis, is predicated upon its broader availability in neighborhoods with limited transportation accessibility, by which we expect that the effect of ride-sharing would be magnified in such areas. In the taxi market, search costs are substantial not only for riders as buyers, but also for drivers as sellers. Taxi drivers must travel around local areas to search for potential customers unless a ride is prearranged. As a result of such high search costs, taxi drivers tend to be spatially concentrated downtown (e.g., in Manhattan, NYC) and temporally concentrated during the day or in rush hours. On the other hand, the seamless driver–rider matching that occurs on ride-sharing platforms can reduce not only the riders’ wait times, but also the drivers’ search costs. This decline in search costs increases the drivers expected payoffs from driving to less-populated areas or traditionally underserved markets. At the same time, ride-sharing platforms’ flexible labor-supply model and dynamic pricing scheme allow drivers to respond to market demands instantly, which helps to match supply and demand closely. Cramer and Krueger (2016) show that the capacity utilization rate, or the fraction of time in which a driver transports a fare-paying passenger, is higher for Uber drivers than for taxi drivers.

The real-time matching and efficient resource allocations between supply and demand contribute to the broader service provision of ride-sharing throughout the day and across the city. Panel A of Online Figure A1 shows the percentage of Uber pickups relative to taxi pickups in NYC, highlighting that Uber expands transportation options outside of Manhattan. Accordingly, Shapiro (2018) shows that wait times for Uber rides are consistently shorter than those for taxis in Brooklyn but not in Manhattan, NYC (Panel B of Online Figure A1). Using detailed data on Uber and taxis in NYC, Lam et al. (2020) suggest that ridesharing platforms match supply and demand more efficiently thanks to rich and instantaneous market information in low-accessibility neighborhoods, and the lack of such information prevents individual taxi drivers from adequately serving these markets. In short, IT-enabled ride-sharing platforms fill the transportation demands that are not met by taxis and other public transportation in low-accessibility neighborhoods (i.e., P w/ Ridesharing > P w/ Taxi ).

Taking the higher marginal effect and the higher availability of ride-sharing in areas with limited transportation accessibility into account together, we argue that IT-enabled ride-sharing has a greater impact on sexual crime deterrence in areas where the transport demand has been unmet because of an insufficient supply of taxis and public transit whereby it reduces a potential victim’s exposure to sexual assaults to a greater extent. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2. The effect of ride-sharing availability on sexual assault occurrence is greater in areas with limited transportation accessibility.

## Overview of Empirical Approaches

The nature of our context involves crime, which renders a randomized experiment infeasible. Thus, this study employs a mixed-methods approach by synthesizing a macrolevel quasi-experiment with microlevel spatiotemporal analyses, which complement each other.

First, we exploit the nationwide quasi-experimental setting of the city-by-city rollouts of Uber services in the United States to identify the effects of Uber entry on the number of sexual assault incidents in a city. Given the nature of Uber’s staggered entries into U.S. cities, this approach has been adopted widely in prior IS studies to investigate the impacts of ridesharing on a variety of societal outcomes, such as entrepreneurial activities (Burtch et al. 2018), traffic congestion (Li et al. 2016), public transit use (Babar and Burtch 2020), alcohol-related motor vehicle accidents (Greenwood and Wattal 2017), and new car purchases (Guo et al. 2019). Thus, we expect that our quasi-experimental DID analysis establishes a causal relationship between ride-sharing availability and sexual crimes.

Second, we complement the quasi-experiment at the city-year level with spatiotemporal analyses at the neighborhood-hour level, which allows us to further investigate situational contingencies on the effects of ride-sharing (e.g., by location, time, or situation). Despite its advantage of clear identification, the citylevel quasi-experiment does not allow us to distinguish nuanced situational factors that influence the relationship between ride-sharing and sexual assault. Because this paper builds upon RAT, which emphasizes circumstances and environmental settings in crime occurrences, the spatiotemporal analysis using fine-grained precinct-hour–level data in a large metropolitan area (NYC) allows us to zoom into the phenomena of interest at a more granular level. In this regard, we expect that our spatiotemporal analyses help us tease out underlying mechanisms and provide unique insights into when and where ride-sharing is more effective in deterring sexual crimes. Furthermore, the spatiotemporal analyses based on IV provides additional evidence of a causal link between ride-sharing and sexual crimes by ruling out any possibilities of city-wide confounding factors.

## A Quasi-Experiment Data

For a geographical unit, we focus on 377 urbanized areas (UAs) in the United States, in which all variables from various sources are available over the period of 2005–2017.<sup>4</sup> We consolidate a longitudinal data set of the UAs at the yearly level because of data availability and consistency for our dependent and independent variables as well as a variety of control variables. As our main independent variable, we consider the Uberavailability indicator as a proxy for the availability of ride-sharing services in a city because Uber is the largest provider of ride-sharing services in the United States, and the entry of Uber services preceded Lyft’s entry in most U.S. cities (Babar and Burtch 2020).<sup>5</sup> We collect Uber’s dates of entry into different U.S. cities from Uber’s press blog and news media. Online Table A1 presents a list of UAs and the timing of Uber entry, and Online Figure A2 shows the trend for Uber entry across U.S. cities.

For dependent variables, we obtain annual crime data from the Uniform Crime Reporting (UCR) Program compiled by the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI), which is the most comprehensive nationwide data set of crime incidents in the United States. This data set provides the number of offenses reported to law enforcement agencies for major types of felonies: murder, rape, robbery, aggravated assault, burglary, and larceny-theft. We aggregate the police department-level crime counts to the UA level, which is our geographical unit. We also control for demographic and socioeconomic factors that influence criminal activities (Patterson 1991, Sampson et al. 1997, Glaeser and Sacerdote 1999, Kelly 2000, Lochner and Moretti 2004). To do so, we obtain annual data on population, education, median household income, poverty rates, and unemployment rates from the American Community Survey (ACS) conducted by the U.S. Census Bureau. For education, we use the percentage of the population over 25 years old with a high school diploma or higher. The poverty and unemployment rates are defined as the percentage of population below a poverty level and the percentage of unemployed individuals, respectively. To account for potential factors that could influence criminal activities, we control for the number of police officers, which is obtained from the Law Enforcement Officers Killed and Assaulted database.

In addition, we also consider three control variables that could be related to reporting of rape incidents: (i) female officer rates, (ii) clearance rates, and (iii) the gender pay gap (see Online Appendix E for a formal discussion on underreporting bias). Miller and Segal (2019) show that female representation among law enforcement officers significantly increases the reporting rate of violent crimes. Given that a belief that the police could not do anything is one of the main reasons for not reporting a rape (Felson and Pare 2005), clearance rates—the percentage of cleared rape incidents—may influence underreporting tendency. As gender equality is also related to rape incidents (Martin et al. 2006) and reporting behavior (Boyle et al. 2017), we control for gender equality proxied with the gender pay gap measured as the ratio of female-to-male median yearly earnings among fulltime, year-round workers from the ACS.

Our final data set includes 4,901 UA-year–level observations during 2005–2017. Online Tables A2 and A3 show the summary statistics and correlations of the variables, respectively.

## Difference-in-Differences Framework

Since its first launch in San Francisco in 2010, Uber has entered and operated in different U.S. cities at different times. The city-by-city rollout of Uber services allows us to exploit a quasi-experiment for clear identification based on a DID modeling framework. Conceptually, the DID model quantifies the net effect of the treatment as the difference in the change in the number of crime incidents after the treatment is applied (i.e., Uber has entered the city) as compared with the change in control group cities where Uber services are not available.

To examine the effect of Uber entry, we employ the DID specification as follows:

$$
y _ {i t} = \alpha_ {i} + \beta (U b e r E n t r y) _ {i t} + Z _ {i t} \gamma + \theta_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t},\tag{2}
$$

where $y _ { i t }$ is a dependent variable (i.e., the log-transformed number of rape incidents), Uber Entry is a dichotomous variable indicating whether Uber services are available in a UA i at year $t , Z _ { i t }$ is the set of control variables mentioned, and $\varepsilon _ { i t }$ represents random errors not explained by the observables. Note that the control variables in levels, such as population and median income, are log-transformed. The model also includes UA-level fixed effects (α ) and year dummies (θ ) for nationwide trends in crime. As the DID framework may be prone to serial correlations within a city (Bertrand et al. 2004), we cluster the estimates’ standard errors by UA.

Given that the entry of Uber is applied in different locations at different times, we extend the DID model to a relative time model, also known as a leads-andlags model, which is widely used in previous works to study the effects of ride-sharing (e.g., Greenwood and Wattal 2017, Burtch et al. 2018). The relative time model replaces $U b e r _ { - } E n t r y _ { i t }$ with a vector of relative time dummies that indicate the relative chronological distance between time t and the time of Uber entry into the UA i (∑ Rel Time Uber ). By explicitly measuring the effects of the treatment over time before and after the treatment is applied, the relative time model allows us to check whether the parallel trend assumption is satisfied (i.e., whether there is a significant difference between the treated and the untreated cities in the pretreatment period) (Angrist and Pischke 2008). If the relative time terms are insignificant prior to Uber entry, this indicates that the treatment and the control groups are comparable in the absence of treatment, and we can attribute the difference in outcomes between the two groups after Uber entry to the average treatment effect. Thus, our DID model is extended as follows:

$$
y _ {i t} = \alpha_ {i} + \sum R e l \_ T i m e \_ U b e r _ {i t} + Z _ {i t} \gamma + \theta_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t}.\tag{3}
$$

It is worth noting that the definition of rape, which is the main outcome of interest, was revised by the FBI in 2013 as the old definition of forcible rape caused a significant portion of rape incidents to go unreported.<sup>6</sup> Because the new definition encompasses broader cases of sexual assaults, the reported number of rape incidents has sharply increased since 2013 as depicted in Online Figure A3. If the revised definition has affected the reported number of rape incidents proportionally across all UAs, it would be absorbed by the year-level fixed effects; otherwise, it would lead to biased estimates. Thus, to address this issue, we adjust our empirical model in the following ways. First, we consider state-year, two-way fixed effects to account for state-level time-varying factors, including statewide crime reporting policies. The FBI compiles two nationwide crime statistics: the UCR Program for summary data and the National Incident-Based Reporting System (NIBRS) that compiles incident data. The state-level UCR Program compiles information from all in-state law enforcement agencies and submits it to the FBI UCR Program though not all states submit incident-level data to the FBI via the NIBRS.<sup>7</sup> Although the state UCR Programs that participate in the NIBRS adopted the revised definition of rape even before 2013, the others have gradually adopted it since 2013. Thus, we expect that state-year, two-way fixed effects can account for statewide crime reporting policies. Second, we consider UA-specific linear time trends after 2013 as the revision of the rape definition may influence the reporting behaviors of local police departments disproportionately.

According to the FBI report of Crime in the United States 2013, 58.4% out of 9,292 city agencies reported rape statistics based on the revised definition as of 2013. Thus, the UA-specific linear time trends after 2013 allow for varying trajectories in the reported number of sexual crimes across UAs and thereby capture the staggered adoption of the revised definition of rape by local police departments.<sup>8</sup>

## Matching Procedure

To further ensure the validity of our DID relative time model, we employ matching techniques to ensure that the control group is composed of untreated cities that are similar to treated cities, reducing the imbalance between the treatment and control groups and thereby mimicking a randomized experimental setup (Rosenbaum and Rubin 1983, Rishika et al. 2013). To this end, we consider both parametric and nonparametric methods: propensity score matching (PSM) and coarsened exact matching (CEM).

For PSM, we match the sample based on similarity in the cities’ propensity to receive Uber entry treatment in the postridesharing years (2010–2017), which is predicted by all socioeconomic factors (included as control variables) in the preridesharing years (2005–2009). To estimate the propensity score, we use a probit model. The samples are matched based on the nearest neighbor algorithm within a caliper of size 0.05 without replacement. Online Table A4 shows the results of the PSM. We also check whether the covariates are balanced between the treatment and the control groups before and after matching to preserve the quality of the PSM procedure. Columns (4)–(6) of Online Table A4 show that the socioeconomic factors included in our analyses are not significantly different between the two matched groups after PSM is applied, indicating that PSM ensures a statistical balance across the treatment and control groups. Online Figure A4 shows the propensity score distributions, which suggests that the common support exists between the treatment and the control groups after matching.

CEM is an approach that aims at matching the sample without imposing any assumptions about the data-generation process (Blackwell et al. 2009). This method coarsens a set of observed covariates, performs exact matching on the coarsened data, and prunes the unmatched data. Because too many coarse buckets can prevent one from matching enough of the sample, we use variables that differ significantly between the treated and the untreated cities before matching as shown in columns (1)–(3) of Online Table A4. For these variables, we define 10 coarse buckets by equally spaced cut points for each covariate and match the treated and the untreated cities belonging to the same coarse bucket. ## Spatiotemporal Analyses for Situational Contingencies

According to our theoretical framework that builds upon RAT, ride-sharing is likely to have varying effects on the reduction in sexual assault depending on the circumstances or environmental settings in which crime offenders and targets are situated. To illuminate the situational contingencies in the deterrent effect of ride-sharing, we conduct spatiotemporal analyses at the neighborhood-hour level in NYC.

## Data

We construct a police precinct-hour–level panel data set on Uber pickup transactions and crime incidents in NYC from January to June in 2015.<sup>9</sup> Online Tables A6 and A7 describe the variables in our spatiotemporal analyses and the correlations among them, respectively.

We obtain the data from two primary sources: the NYC Taxi & Limousine Commission (TLC) for Uber and taxi transactions and the NYC Police Department (NYPD) for rape incidents. NYC provides an ideal setting for our spatiotemporal analyses for the following reasons. First, Uber has been the dominant ride-sharing provider in NYC since 2011; as of 2015, Lyft provided a mere 7% of the mobile-summoned rides in NYC compared with Uber’s 90% (Bradley 2015). This aids in ruling out potential confounds from competitors. Second, NYC is one of the few cities that provide comprehensive data on the location and time of both Uber pickups and rape incidents at a granular level, which allows us to exploit spatiotemporal variations in ride-sharing transactions and crime occurrences.

Our main dependent variable is Rape Occurrence , which is equal to one if at least one rape incident occurs in police precinct i in hour t and equal to zero otherwise.<sup>10</sup> We obtain data on rape cases from the NYPD’s CompStat (Levine et al. 2017), which has publicly reported city-wide, precinct-level crime statistics for major types of felonies: rape, murder, robbery, aggravated assault, burglary, and larcenytheft. This data set provides crime records with locations and time stamps (see Online Figure A6 for the 76 police precincts in NYC). For privacy issues, the NYPD does not disclose any identifiable information on the victims and the perpetrators.

Our main independent variable is Uber Pickups<sub>it</sub>, which is the number of Uber pickup transactions within the boundary of police precinct i in hour t. FiveThirtyEight, an internet media company, obtained Uber transaction data from the TLC by submitting a Freedom of Information Act request.<sup>11</sup> This data set includes more than 14.3 million records of all Uber transactions with pickup locations and time stamps for January–June 2015. We match each pickup location to the corresponding NYPD precinct using geographic information system software. From the TLC records of taxicab transactions, we also obtain data on about 72.3 million yellow taxi pickups and 9.4 million green taxi pickups, for which each individual trip record contains precise coordinates and time stamps. The taxi pickup coordinates are matched to the corresponding police precincts as well. Our final data set includes 21 precincts in Manhattan, 12 precincts in the Bronx, 23 precincts in Brooklyn, 16 precincts in Queens, and 4 precincts in Staten Island, totaling 330,144 precincthour observations.

## Empirical Model

We estimate the relationship between the frequency of Uber transactions and the likelihood of rape occurrence using an $\mathrm { L P M } \colon ^ { 1 2 }$

$$
y _ {i t} = \alpha_ {i} + \beta \log (U b e r P i c k u p s) _ {i t} + Z _ {i t} \gamma + \theta_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t}.\tag{4}
$$

Here, $y _ { i t }$ is a binary variable indicating whether a sexual assault occurs in police precinct i and hour $t ,$ Uber $P i c k u p s _ { i t }$ is our main independent variable, $Z _ { i t }$ is a set of control variables, and $\varepsilon _ { i t }$ is an idiosyncratic random error. To correct the skewness of Uber and taxi pickups across time and regions in NYC, these variables are log-transformed after adding one to account for zero values. We also include precinct dummies and time dummies for week (27 weeks), day-of-week (Monday to Sunday), and time-of-day (24 hours). Note that we do not control for other local conditions, such as population, income level, or precinct-level policing practices, because it is reasonable to assume that these are time-invariant over the period January–June 2015 and are effectively captured by precinct fixed effects. We use robust standard errors clustered at the precinct level to account for serial correlations within each precinct.

One might be concerned that not all incidents of violent crimes, such as rapes, are reported to the police (Fisher et al. 2003). Given the precinct-hour panel structure of our data set, any reporting bias that occurs systematically by region and time can be effectively absorbed by the spatiotemporal fixed effects and control variables (see Online Appendix E for a formal exposition on the assumptions underlying our empirical models). To be conservative, we further control for the spatiotemporal triple fixed effects of precinct × time-of-day × weekends, which allows us to account for various levels of time-varying unobservable confounders.<sup>13</sup>

The model also includes several time-varying control variables to account for the general demand for transportation and the supply of potential offenders and targets across the city. First, we include the number of taxi pickups to control for the demand for transportation. Second, to account for the spatial and temporal factors that trigger criminal activities $( \mathrm { e . g . } ,$ supply of assailants), we control for the number of occurrences for other types of crimes (murders, robberies, assaults, burglaries, and larcenies) because different types of crimes are related to each other (Andresen and Malleson 2011). Third, we account for the supplies of potential offenders and targets by including two variables obtained from the city agencies. Given that police presence and activity would influence criminals’ behavior and the supply of potential offenders (Levitt 1997, Di Tella and Schargrodsky 2004), we control for the number of the NYPD’s stop-and-frisks, a policing practice of temporarily detaining, questioning, and searching civilians on the street for weapons and other contraband (Weisburd et al. 2014). We also include public events permitted by the NYC Mayor’s Office of Citywide Event Coordination and Management to account for public gatherings, which are related to supply of potential targets. Finally, we control for weather conditions in each precinct, which is known to influence crime occurrences (Cohn 1990, McLean 2007). For this variable, we obtain hourly weather data, including temperature, precipitation, and wind speed from Weather Underground based on the nearest weather station for each precinct.

## Instrumental Variable Approach

There are several sources of potential endogeneity in the relationship between Uber pickups and rape occurrences that may cause bias in our estimates. Omitted variables that affect both Uber transactions and sexual assault incidents could lead to a spurious correlation. Another source of endogeneity could be reverse causality. For instance, people who live or travel in high-crime areas may be more likely to hire Uber drivers to avoid crimes. Thus, the likelihood of rape occurrence might affect the demand for ride-sharing services, leading to biased estimates. To correct for such endogeneity problems, we employ an IV-based, twostage approach to estimate the LPM.

Specifically, we instrument for the frequency of Uber pickups with the level of traffic-related air pollution. Traffic-related air pollutants are likely to be correlated with the number of Uber pickups because they are directly influenced by the overall level of transportation demand, which simultaneously influences the endogenous variable: Uber transactions. In particular, the demands for transportation across the city have direct effects on Uber pickups by means of the dynamic pricing that Uber uses to match supply and demand. At the same time, it is reasonable to assume that transportation demands and traffic-related air pollution are not directly correlated with sexual crime occurrences, conditional on spatial-temporal fixed effects and a range of control variables that account for general mobility and transportation patterns related to crime occurrence across a city. We provide a detailed discussion on the validity of the IV with respect to the relevance and exclusion restriction in Online Appendix C.

To measure air pollution levels, we obtain hourly data on carbon monoxide, nitrogen dioxide, and fine particulate matter, all of which are major trafficrelated pollutants (Han and Naeher 2006) from the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, which mon itors hourly levels of air pollution from monitoring stations across the country (see Online Table A6 for the definitions and summary statistics of the IV). We measure the air pollution of each precinct based on data from the nearest station. In addition, pollutants suspended in the air could be the main cause of visibility degradation (Hyslop 2009). To account for environmental quality that is difficult to quantify, we also use air visibility as another IV, the data for which is gathered from Weather Underground.

## Results of Spatiotemporal Analyses The Effects of Ride-Sharing Transactions on Sexual Assault

Table 3 presents the main results of the spatiotemporal analyses from the second-stage estimates (see Online Table C1 for the results of the first-stage estimates). Through columns (1)–(5), the frequency of Uber pickups is negatively associated with the likelihood of rape occurrence. The results suggest that this deterrence effect of Uber pickups on rape occurrence is robust to a range of combinations of fixed effects that account for various levels of unobserved heterogeneity in sexual assault occurrences; for the rest of the spatiotemporal analyses, we use the LPM specification with triple fixed-effects, reported in column (5).

The estimation results imply that a 1% increase in Uber pickups leads to a 0.0049–0.0053 percentage point decrease in the likelihood of rape occurrence at the precinct-hour level. Given that the average probability of rape occurrence in our sample is 0.16% (Online Table A6), the marginal effect of a 1% increase in Uber pickups translates to the decrease in the likelihood of rape occurrence by 3.06%–3.31%. A back-of-the-envelope calculation estimates that a 1% increase in Uber pickups can lead to a reduction in rape incidents by as many as 44 to 48 cases per year in NYC.<sup>14</sup> Taken together, the results of the spatiotemporal analyses corroborate those of the DID analyses, lending further support to Hypothesis 1.

In contrast, a taxi driver with little real-time information about passengers, unlike the ride-sharing driver, is more likely to try to find a passenger at locations that are most populated, where more sexual assault incidents are likely to take place. Although we do not aim to identify the causal relationship between taxis and sexual crimes, an additional test assuming taxi pickups to be endogenous shows that its effect is not significant (see Online Appendix C for details). Taken together, although offering additional transportation options to a potential victim is not an inherent benefit exclusive to ride-sharing, IT-enabled ride-sharing platforms play a unique role in deterring sexual assaults via real-time, supply–demand matching, particularly when fewer transportation options, including taxis, are available for potential crime targets.

## The Contingent Effects by Risky Situations

To further tease out the underlying mechanisms, we explicitly focus on situations and circumstances in which ride-sharing is expected to be more effective in deterring crime occurrence as guided by RAT. In Figure 1, our theoretical framework posits that the relationship among three parties—potential assailants, target passengers, and ride-sharing—determines the likelihood of crime occurrence. Thus, we delve deeper into risker situations with higher supplies of suitable targets and potential perpetrators, in which ride-sharing could intervene. In doing so, we exploit unique data sets in NYC to zoom into the nuanced situational factors, including (i) time, (ii) alcohol consumption patterns, and (iii) an increasing probability of crime occurrence. The results are summarized in Table 4 and presented in detail in Online Appendix A.

According to RAT, a target’s exposure to potential offenders increases with the amount of time spent outside the home for leisure and social activities. In this regard, we estimate our model by splitting the full sample into subsamples of daytime (5 a.m. to 9 p.m.) and nighttime (9 p.m. to 5 a.m.). This rests on the assumptions that routine activities change throughout a day and that the risk of being suitable target would be higher at nighttime than daytime. In addition, sexual assaults that ride-sharing could prevent are likely to occur more often at night when transportation is generally scarce compared with daytime. Online Table A9 shows that the deterrent effect of ride-sharing on sexual assaults is stronger and more significant at nighttime (column (2)), whereas it is insignificant during the daytime (column (1)).

To further reveal whether the deterrent effect of ride-sharing is related to social and nighttime activities, we split our sample into high- and low-density areas of alcohol-serving places and separate Friday and Saturday nights, when most alcoholic beverages are consumed (Arfken 1988), from the rest of the week. In areas with many alcohol-serving places in NYC, the deterrent effect of ride-sharing is stronger during Friday and Saturday nights (Online Table A9, column (6)) than daytimes in any day of the week (columns (3) and (4)) and other weekday nights (Column (5)). In contrast, ride-sharing has a larger effect on weekday nights rather than weekend nights in areas with few alcohol-serving places (Online Table A9, columns (7) and (8)). Considering the significant relationship between sexual crimes and alcohol consumption, our findings substantiate the argument that ride-sharing is more effective in deterring sexual assaults in a risky situation related to social and nighttime activities on weekend nights that could increase the supply of potential targets.

In addition, we test the contingent effects by the supply of potential offenders that increases the risk of crime occurrence. First, we proxy the supply of potential offenders with whether other crimes occur in a given neighborhood at a specific time given that different types of crimes are related to each other (Andresen and Malleson 2011). Second, based on the broken windows theory that visible signs of crime may encourage further crimes and disorder (Wilson and Kelling 1989), the frequency of complaints to the city government about broken street lights is used as a proxy of the supply of potential offenders. Finally, by taking advantage of the fact that the stop-and-frisks of NYPD target likely hot spots for crimes, we use three hours prior to a stop-and-frisk as a proxy for the probability of crime occurrence. In Online Table A10, we estimate the model separately for the subsam ples with a higher and lower supply of potential offenders and demonstrate that the deterrent effect of ride-sharing on sexual assault is more salient when (i) other crimes occur (columns (1) and (2)), (ii) there are more broken street lights (columns (3)–(6)), and (iii) the police are about to conduct a stop-and-frisk (columns (7) and (8)).

In summary, our investigations of the situational contingencies reveal that the effect of ride-sharing is stronger in more crime-prone circumstances in which transportation could play a more prominent role, such as on weekend nights in areas with a higher density of alcohol-serving establishments or when there is an increase in the probability of crime occurrence in a neighborhood.

## Robustness Checks for the Spatiotemporal Analyses

To lend credence to the results of the spatiotemporal analyses, we perform a range of robustness checks, which are summarized in Table 5. In this section, we briefly discuss them, and detailed descriptions and results are available in Online Appendix C.

First, one might argue that the effect of ride-sharing may be driven by the general role of transportation that can be applied to taxis rather than the unique features of IT-enabled ride-sharing platforms that facilitate seamless matching between supply and demand. In treating taxi pickups as endogenous, we observe no significant relationship of taxi pickups with rape occurrences (Online Table C2). Second, Online Table C3 demonstrates that our findings based on the LPM with triple fixed effects are essentially same as those based on a probit model without triple fixed effects. Third, a case-control resampling test demonstrates that the effect of ride-sharing is not driven just by chance and is robust to rare event bias (Online Figure C1). Fourth, our findings are supported by a series of sensitivity tests, as reported in Online Table C4, to (i) spatial correlations, (ii) additional controls of lagged Uber and taxi pickups, (iii) dimension reduction for the triple fixed effects based on a double selection procedure, and (iv) the exclusion of influential cases (i.e., JFK and LaGuardia airports).

## Comparison with Other Types of Crimes

Although our theoretical reasoning drawn from RAT could also be applicable to other crimes, the effects of ride-sharing on sexual assault are likely to differ from those on other crimes, depending on the extent to which providing a reliable and timely transportation option reduces exposure to crimes. For instance, given that “burglars and sex offenders may have different types of targets (i.e., static versus mobile targets)” (Beauregard et al. 2010, p. 138), ride-sharing’s role in moving a suitable target to a safer place is less relevant for property crimes than for violent crimes including rapes because their targets are not easily repositioned by transportation.

Online Tables B4, C5, and C6 present the estimation results for other types of crimes—other violent crimes (aggravated assault, robbery, murder) and property crimes (burglary, larceny)—in the quasi-experimental DID analysis across U.S. cities and the spatiotemporal analyses in NYC, respectively. In both analyses, none of the other types of crimes exhibit a significant relationship with ride-sharing consistently across different identification strategies and different model specifications. These results imply that ride-sharing platforms provide an effective means to help potential targets of sexual crimes, rather than those of other types of crimes, in circumstances and situations in which reliable transportation plays a critical role in deterring crime occurrence.

## General Discussion Summary of Findings

Guided by routine activity theory from the criminology literature and coupled with the IS literature on digital platforms, this study documents the relationship between ride-sharing and sexual assaults. Using the nationwide quasi-experiment of Uber’s city-by-city rollouts, we obtain an encouraging finding that IT-enabled ride-sharing availability has the potential to prevent sexual crimes (Table 1). Moreover, we investigate situational contingencies to reveal when and where ride-sharing is most effective in deterring sexual crimes by leveraging fine-grained precincthour–level data on Uber pickups and rape occurrences in NYC. The spatiotemporal analyses provide additional causal evidence on the deterrent effect of ride-sharing on rape occurrences (Table 3). Further, as is summarized in Table 4, ride-sharing is associated with a greater reduction in rape incidents in neighborhoods with limited transportation accessibility and fewer taxi transactions (the outskirts of the city and areas with a greater percentage of non-White residents; Online Table A8) and in risker situations in which there are more suitable targets and potential offenders (around alcohol-serving places on weekend nights or in crime-prone circumstances; Online Tables A9 and A10). Our findings are corroborated by a wide range of robustness checks (Tables 2 and 5).

## Theoretical Contributions

This study makes several important contributions to the literature. First, this work contributes to an emerging research stream on the societal impacts of IT, a topic that has been called for in the IS literature (Lee 2016, Gupta 2017). We adopt a novel interdisciplinary approach by integrating the IS and criminology literatures to theorize how ride-sharing services can suppress the occurrence of sexual violence. This study is in line with recent academic efforts to integrate IS research with other disciplines to tackle critical societal challenges with IT (Chan et al. 2016, Greenwood and Agarwal 2016, Pang and Pavlou 2019, Cheng et al. 2020). To the best of our knowledge, crimes and violence, two of the most chronic societal problems, have been underexplored in the previous IS literature. Although digital technologies have profound impacts on all aspects of our lives, including crime, we still have a limited understand ing of how we can take advantage of digital technologies to address societal challenges. In this regard, this study proposes that the online platform that matches drivers and riders is an IT artifact that can play a role in addressing the societal challenge of sexual crimes, offering new theoretical insights on how IT-enabled platforms can improve social wellbeing beyond economic gains.

Second, this study provides a fresh theoretical perspective based on the IS literature that contributes to the criminology and public/urban economics literatures by examining the role of IT-enabled platforms in relation to crime prevention. By demonstrating that digital platforms can fill the gap in transport supply and thereby reduce crime occurrences resulting from lack of transportation, our study extends the body of knowledge on transportation and public safety that have been discussed extensively (Smith and Clarke 2000). From the IS perspective, this research suggests a possible IT-enabled approach to designing market-driven crime deterrence strategies that combat a major societal challenge.

Third, our empirical contribution stems from our mixed-methods approach by combining a macrolevel quasi-experiment and microlevel spatiotemporal analyses in a complementary manner, painting a more complete picture of the effects of the sharing economy and IT-enabled platforms. In the IS literature, there has been a long-standing debate on the relative importance of context and generalizability (particularism versus universalism). Davison and Martinsons (2016) argue that context should be considered more important in IS research, whereas Cheng et al. (2016, p. 262) maintain that generalizability should not be downplayed and “contextual studies, in combination with replication, can help us to cautiously make generalizable knowledge claims.” In this respect, our combined macro–micro approach that synthesizes a nationwide city-level quasi-experimental analysis with a neighborhood-level spatiotemporal analysis in NYC helps reconcile generalization and context to advance our understanding of the role of IT-enabled ride-sharing platforms in deterring sexual crimes.

## Practical Implications

This study also has meaningful managerial and policy implications. First, ride-sharing companies and city governments can leverage our findings on the contingent effects of ride-sharing by time, location, and situation to establish and better design safe-ride programs. For example, although universities have provided their own safe-ride programs (Weber 2014, Heywood and Weber 2019), many in-house programs have been replaced by partnerships with ride-sharing companies (The Chronicle of Higher Education 2019, Uber 2019). In addition, city governments may devise a policy measure to leverage ride-sharing in situations that are more prone to crimes. A case in point is a policy experiment in Washington, D.C., that converted part of the parking space around Connecticut Avenue with the highest density of nightlife establishments to ride-sharing pickup and drop-off zones on weekends (CityLab 2017). This shows how indepth analyses of ride-sharing usage patterns can be pivotal in designing urban transportation policy: “As DDOT [District Department of Transportation] began planning the pilot program, Uber and Lyft corroborated the widespread perception among club owners that ride-hailing services are the preferred mode of post-nightlife transportation. Both companies shared anonymized rider data with DDOT to help refine the location of the pilot. . .” (CityLab 2017). As the sustainability of ride-sharing business models may depend on their relationship with local communities and politics (Paik et al. 2019), ride-sharing companies can leverage our findings to collaborate with them.

Second, in a broader context beyond ride-sharing, our study has policy implications for the role that transportation can play in relation to preventing criminal activities. For effective crime deterrence, thus, it is important for local governments to expand and enhance public transportation infrastructure so that it is more accessible and reliable, particularly in vulnerable areas and at vulnerable times. Furthermore, our theoretical argument that IT-enabled ridesharing platforms can provide more reliable and timely services could also be applied to other settings of societal problems, illuminating avenues for future research. A case in point is nonemergency medical care. As 3.6 million individuals failed to receive nonemergency medical care because of transportation barriers in the United States in 2005 alone, ride-sharing platforms could provide reliable and efficient transportation services to vulnerable patients (Powers et al. 2016).

## Limitations and Future Research

This study is not without limitations. First, we do not have full information on rape incidents. We were not able to find information on, for example, how a rape occurred or the relationship between a victim and a perpetrator. Although this study has extensively in vestigated the underlying mechanisms of the relationship between ride-sharing and sexual assaults with in-depth analyses of situational contingencies, this relationship still remains a black box to a certain extent. For instance, although our empirical evidence is supportive of the ride-sharing mechanism of helping suitable targets avoid vulnerable situations, there could be another potential mechanism of ride-sharing as a capable guardian. Hollis-Peel et al. (2011) explain that guardians (including police as well as neighbors) can prevent crimes by monitoring a physical space where crimes might occur by supervising potential offenders’ behaviors or by protecting potential victims. In this regard, ride-sharing platforms’ citywide driver network can act as lookouts for passengers across a city in situations in which their personal safety is at risk, which aids in ensuring that people and communities are safer by providing more diligent eyes on the streets. More detailed data on crime incidents and ride-sharing driving and usage patterns would allow researchers to scrutinize more comprehensively how ride-sharing availability changes the behavior of potential targets and offenders.

Second, we do not intend to address and cannot draw any conclusions about the direct allegations of sexual crimes against ride-sharing drivers, but rather our findings speak to the overall relationship between ride-sharing availability and sexual crime occurrences in a given city or neighborhood. We expect our study to complement and reshape the ongoing dialogues on the sharing economy by providing an alternative perspective on ride-sharing’s benefits for passengers in crime-prone situations. Despite limited access to perpetrator profiles, future research could focus directly on the sexual crimes committed by ridesharing drivers.

Third, caution should be exercised in generalizing our findings from the spatiotemporal analyses in NYC, the largest U.S. city, to other contexts because the effect of ride-sharing might vary depending on institutions (e.g., regulations) and market conditions. In addition, the spatiotemporal analyses are also restricted to the time period of January to June in 2015 because of data availability. Although the nationwide DID estimates covering 377 U.S. cities in 2005–2017 enhance the generalizability of our findings to a certain extent, they still have limitations with regard to examining temporal effects within a year and scrutinizing situational factors in cities other than NYC. Thus, the findings from the spatiotemporal analyses in NYC may not be generalizable to other cities. Future studies could extend our spatiotemporal analyses for situational contingencies into other cities or countries in different time frames, to identify factors that distinguish a specific context and the boundary conditions in which theories are valid.

## Concluding Remarks

Given that sexual assault is one of the most chronic societal problems that inflict considerable harms and costs on individuals and broader society, this study examines the effect of IT-enabled ride-sharing platforms on sexual assaults. Drawing upon routine activity theory from the criminology literature, we propose that the ride-sharing platforms play a prominent role in reducing a potential target’s exposure to criminals by offering them reliable and timely transportation options. In relation to the research question, this study is the first to present systematic empirical evidence that a ride-sharing platform can provide a viable means to prevent sexual crimes. This study sheds new light on the potential of IT-enabled platforms to improve public safety, a topic that has been underexplored in the IS literature despite its high significance and relevance in society. We hope that our study spurs further research in the IS discipline and motivates policy discussions on the role of IT artifacts in solving societal problems and improving social well-being in broader society beyond its economic contributions.
