# Are Traditional Performance Reviews Outdated? An Empirical Analysis on Continuous, Real-Time Feedback in the Workplace

Michael Rivera,<sup>a</sup> Liangfei Qiu,<sup>b</sup> Subodha Kumar,<sup>a</sup> Tony Petrucci<sup>a</sup>

Abstract. In order to deliver real-time feedback to support employee development and rapid innovation, many companies are replacing formal, review-based performance management with systems that enable frequent and continuous employee evaluation. Real-time feedback applications enable supervisors and employees to give, seek, and receive competency-based feedback using their computers, smartphones, or other devices. In this study, we examine the role of one such real-time feedback application to understand its effects on employee performance appraisals. First, we find that relationship source (peer, subordinate, or supervisor) impacts real-time feedback: feedback tends to be more critical when it is from supervisors. What is more interesting is the effect of favoritism and retribution in real-time feedback: supervisors adopt tit-for-tat strategies, but peers do not. We also uncover that men rate women higher than men and that women rate men and women similar to how men rate men. In addition, positive real-time feedback has a stronger effect on future ratings than negative feedback. Our findings have direct implications for the design and implementation of performance management systems. Further, we highlight how companies can use information systems to create an innovative human resource operation that delivers flexibility and agility.

## 1. Introduction

Organizations today face increased business complexity and are under increased pressure to drive results in a transparent and rapid manner (Huang et al. 2015, Mani and Nandkumar 2016, Schrage et al. 2019). Employee performance reviews are one widely accepted response to this uncertainty in the business world (Coens and Jenkins 2002). Providing regular, positive feedback is a widely accepted management and coaching practice that supports employee engagement, retention, and performance (Harter and Adkins 2015, Porath 2016, Zhang 2017, Yohn, 2019). By providing employees with information on their performance and how it compares with an explicit or implicit ideal, organizations can increase intrinsic employee motivation, contribute to a positive competitive atmosphere at work, correct employee misconceptions about performance perceptions and expectations, and provide positive reinforcement for desirable behavior and thereby reduce social loafing (Jung et al. 2010).

The performance review has a long and storied history, with some type of employee appraisal dating back as far as third-century China (Vara 2015). In the United States, since at least the 1940s, an increasing number of organizations have utilized formalized performance review processes to assess individual employee competency, development, and skills (Cappelli and Tavis 2016, Buckingham and Goodall 2019). These reviews often examine the past year, half-year, or quarter. In a typical review process, a supervisor fills out a standardized form to rate a specific employee in relevant domains; sometimes, employees are asked to conduct parallel self-evaluations to compare their self-perceptions with their supervisors’ ratings (Cappelli and Conyon 2016, DeNisi et al. 2017). This formal process is variously referred to as employee reviews, performance appraisals, annual/biannual/quarterly performance reviews, performance evaluations, or employee evaluations; the specifics of the process vary from organization to organization. Numerous studies have examined appraisal rating-scale formats, ratingdata quality, rater training programs, reactions to appraisals, appraisal purposes, rating sources, cognitive processes, and demographic effects (e.g., Aggarwal and Thakur 2013, DeNisi et al. 2017).

Since at least the early 2000s, many organizations have taken this pen-and-paper process to the computer, conducting reviews by filling out active or static forms digitally (Cappelli and Tavis 2016). Differences between computer-mediated and pen-and-paper evaluations are striking: although computer-based appraisals streamline and simplify the process and save organizations many hours of paperwork (Spinks et al. 1999), some computer-based performance appraisals have been found to be significantly more negative: socialpsychological processes influence computerized peer ratings by making evaluators less likely to experience feelings of social obligation to be kind and polite (Kurtzberg et al. 2005).

As organizations experiment with optimal performance management approaches, they are simultaneously navigating a shift in workplace dynamics as millennials and generation Z (also called the iGeneration) workers enter the workforce (Twenge 2017). Millennials, born between about 1980 and 1995, prefer more frequent feedback and coaching and are more technologically savvy than previous generations (Hernandez 2015). Members of generation Z, born between 1996 and 2011, are referred to as “digital natives” because they have relied on digital communication for most, if not all, of their lives (Twenge 2017). These generations likely prefer to communicate digitally in the workplace in a manner similar to their personal life (Twenge 2017).

Traditional approaches to employee performance management may not be ideal for these newest workers. A study by TriNet and Wakefield Research including more than 1,000 full-time employees born after 1980 in the United States found that 74% of younger generations felt “in the dark” about how others believe they are performing at work, and 62% felt blindsided by performance reviews (Kaira 2015). Additionally, annual performance reviews typically take place once a year and look back on the performance of an individual over that period of time. More frequent, informal communication and feedback have been shown to be a stronger predictor of feedback satisfaction and professional development (Scott 2015). Ongoing feedback provides a more cohesive picture of real performance because performance tends to wax and wane over time (Jung and Sosik 2003). Moreover, regular feedback is needed to support employee growth and development (McCarthy and Garavan 2001). Cooper (2006) and Pampino et al. (2004) find that daily feedback better supports the effectiveness of performance interventions and contributes to better relationships between supervisors and subordinates. Finally, traditional performance reviews only provide feedback from supervisors and do not allow for peer feedback. However, peer evaluation is an important element to include in performance assessments and outcomes primarily because employees are more likely to accept peer feedback than supervisor feedback, especially when it is negative (Shore et al. 1992).

Although performance feedback is important to support employee development, some argue that the performance review system is broken because it does not always accurately appraise performance, nor does it consistently provide a compelling impetu for reviewed employees to improve behavior (Saunier and Mavis 1998, Coens and Jenkins 2002). Because of the limitations of the traditional model and the growing need to provide accurate, timely feedback to employees, some companies are moving away from the performance review model and toward a continuous performance management model (Cappell and Tavis 2016, Lawrence 2017). A continuous performance management framework facilitates a more comprehensive view of an employee’s performance, including a focus on goal setting, looking forward to future development, coaching, and the inclusion of 360-degree feedback (Larson 1984, Saunier and Mavis 1998). Real-time digital feedback helps address the shortcomings of the traditional review model and provides relevant, ongoing feedback for a younger workforce (Ewenstein et al. 2016, Silverman 2016) The integration of feedback and technology supports the immediacy of feedback (Bement 2007, Sloan et al. 2017). In particular, technological innovation has made it possible to give and receive performance feedback using a real-time feedback application (Smith et al. 2013, Yueh et al. 2016, Huang et al. 2019).

Real-time feedback applications are increasingly utilized for performance appraisals and so-called 360-degree feedback that allows for feedback from supervisors to employees, from employees to supervisors, from peers, and from the self (Goodwin 2016, Silverman 2016). For example, Bridgewater, the largest hedge fund in the world, developed a real-time feedback application used by all employees at various levels involved in investment decisions. Bridgewater’s implementation of the feedback application focused on creating a meritocracy in which the best ideas win, thereby ensuring that it offers the best investment decisions to its clients (Feloni 2017). General Electric has also recently implemented a real-time feedback application, Performance Development @ GE (PD@ GE), as part of a broader move from its well-known performance management “Session $\mathrm { C } ^ { \prime \prime }$ process to a more dynamic and agile process that includes external and internal feedback (Ewenstein et al. 2016). The complex and volatile healthcare industry, in which organizational viability is at stake while focusing on critically important issues, such as patient satisfaction and outcomes, has also begun to focus on real-time feedback as an important solution.

The healthcare revenue cycle, which costs an average hospital upward of \$20 million per year, may utilize real-time feedback to significantly reduce expenditures by focusing on feedback from admissions through the clinical stage to the billing stage (Petrucci et al. 2016).

As this trend unfolds, organizations should assess if, how, and when real-time feedback applications may fit into their communication and performance management strategies. However, there are many unanswered questions because of the recent adoption of applications as tools for real-time feedback and the limited research in this area. In this unchartered territory, there are several unknowns about feedback dynamics within the application and its effects on employee performance. Real-time feedback applications differ from traditional systems (including both paperand-pen systems and other online, nonapplication-based systems) in several important ways, and these may have implications for their implementation and effectiveness. First, real-time performance feedback applications usually reside on a mobile device, such as a phone, which provides a unique interface or medium with particular features that may influence the feedback process. Cellular phones are ubiquitous and convenient for frequent and almost ongoing use and are even thought to be habit forming. The small screen, keyboard function, and predictive text features on smartphones may influence the quality and quantity of real-time feedback produced by a user (Liu and Yu 2017).

Next, the nature of an application that provides real-time, ongoing feedback provides a channel with flexible feedback frequency, controlled by the user. Although some traditional feedback approaches may be ongoing or real time in effect (such as a sales leaderboard posted publicly in a workspace), the nature of a real-time application allows for constant updates and information. Theoretically, this constant stream of information could motivate users to constantly adjust their performance (e.g., Jung and Sosik (2003) find that increased performance feedback leads to continual behavior adjustment) or could prove to be overwhelming.

Our research questions consider whether the real-time environment of application-based digital performance feedback complicates or duplicates the findings of previous performance feedback studies in four main areas: relationships between feedback giver and recipient, gender dynamics, the effects of favoritism and anonymity, and the impact of feedback on behavior. Our first question explores the impact of source and relationship (peer, subordinate, supervisor) on feedback. Answers to this question may help feedback sources adapt feedback to ensure that it is as constructive as possible. It may also provide insights as to which sources provide the most impactful feedback and which may provide cultural and strategic insights to organizations. Although prior studies examine the impact of feedback sources when providing feedback, there are no studies, to the best of our knowledge, that address this dynamic in the context of providing realtime feedback through an application.

Our next research question considers the role of gender in the real-time feedback process. Understanding the impact of gender when providing feedback through an application could lead to important strategic recommendations to help feedback sources and receivers become aware of their potential biases and assumptions, potentially normalizing the feedback process over time. Similar to gender differences, identifying favoritism and retribution behaviors could allow the sources to recognize the biases and assumptions they may bring to the feedback process. Additionally, determining whether anonymity in applications impacts real-time feedback is an unanswered question that could provide insights to organizations about their culture and employee relations. We provide a preliminary exploration of these issues in our next research question. Finally, we seek to investigate how measurable trends of an employee’s rating could be analyzed over time.

## 2. Conceptual Model and Hypotheses Development

In this section, we develop a series of hypotheses in the following four aspects to address our research questions: (1) relationships between feedback giver and recipient, (2) gender dynamics in performance feedback, (3) the effects of favoritism and anonymity on performance feedback, and (4) the impact of performance feedback on employee’s rating trend. We also compare and contrast our work with past studies in these streams. Table A.1 in Online Appendix A further illustrates a comparison between our paper and selected related studies.

## 2.1. Feedback Source

Ilgen et al. (1979), studying traditional paper-based performance feedback, find that source can impact the perception and acceptance of feedback; feedback from peers and positive (but not negative) feedback from supervisors were considered more accurate. Feedback source may influence employee response to feedback, affecting the feedback recipient’s perception and acceptance of the feedback as well as the employee’s interest in responding to it. Many studies support the theory that source credibility influences reactions to feedback (Halperin et al. 1976, Bannister 1986, Podsakoff and Farh 1989, Steelman and Rutkowski 2004). Moreover, individual assessments have been shown to be more accurate than group assessments (Jung and Sosik 2003).

Studies that have measured the impact of supervisor versus peer feedback and its impact on employee performance are limited and contradictory. Research studying UK and Brazilian employees supports the notion that feedback from coworkers (peer feedback) and self-feedback may be more important than feedback from a supervisor (Bastos and Fletcher 1995). This is supported by earlier research showing that employees value feedback that is most under their control (Hackman and Lawler 1971, Ilgen et al. 1979). Others show that supervisor feedback supports job performance over peer and self-feedback (Becker and Klimoski 1989). Previous studies also show that in a traditional (e.g., annual or biannual) feedback cycle, negative supervisory feedback may hinder opportunities for performance improvement (Ilgen and Davis 2000, Ilies et al. 2007) because it is perceived as less accurate than positive feedback (Ilgen et al. 1979, Fedor et al. 1989). Some evidence suggests that peer feedback may have more of an impact on subsequent performance than feedback from a supervisor (Hackman and Lawler 1971, Ilgen et al. 1979, Bastos and Fletcher 1995).

No study to date has examined source biases in the context of a real-time feedback application. However, the hierarchy-flattening dynamics of the internet and social media culture, the potential for anonymity in such a process, the immediacy of real-time and ongoing feedback, and online communication norms may influence the degree to which source impacts feedback in an application setting. Essentially, realtime feedback increases the frequency of evaluations compared with traditional performance reviews. From a game-theory point of view, real-time feedback can be considered a repeated game: people rate each other multiple times in a given time frame. The real-time nature increases the intensity of people’s interactions and makes our setting more like a standard repeated game. According to the repeated-game theory, people’s decisions in the current round largely depend on the economic and social value of future interactions (Cabral et al. 2014, Bapna et al. 2017). The role of future interactions on the current decision has been widely discussed in the contexts of the folk theorem in economics (e.g., Cabral et al. 2014). Axelrod (1984, p. 126) called the prospect of future interaction the “shadow of the future.” People tend to become more cooperative and benefit the other player in repeated interactions when the economic and social value of future interactions is larger. In our context, when a supervisor evaluates subordinates, the supervisor tends to exhibit less cooperativeness and give a lower score because the economic and social value of future interactions from the subordinate is relatively low. In contrast, when a subordinate evaluates a supervisor or when an employee evaluates a peer, they tend to exhibit more cooperativeness and give a higher score because the economic and social value of future interactions is larger. Therefore, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 1. Recipients tend to receive lower scores from supervisors than from subordinates and peers.

## 2.2. Gender

Gender biases may impact the quality and quantity of feedback a source provides. We provide three reasons that men may rate women less harshly than their male counterparts. First, feedback providers may be more protective of women and therefore less likely to provide negative feedback (Carli 1990, Sagrestano 1992). Specifically, men provide women with more favorable evaluations than their male counterparts when women demonstrate greater degrees of socialcommunal behaviors or perceived levels of interpersonal warmth (Carli 1990, Eagly et al. 1992). Men may modify their feedback-giving behaviors and diverge from traditional gender norms if the perceived digression supports a stronger position of influence and is considered advantageous (Carli 2001).

Second, Bear et al. (2017) show that feedback from men to women tends to be positive but vague (e.g., “You’re doing great!”) and feedback from men to men tends to be more critical but also more specific. Moreover, research shows that male supervisors may consider women a protected minority and thus be less likely to critique them, resulting in unhelpful and vague feedback that does not contribute to workplace promotion (Correll and Simard 2016, Abel 2019). Third, Croson and Gneezy (2009) conclude that there are robust and significant behavior differences between females and males, including that females tend to be less competition oriented than males. In particular, women are more cooperative (Ortmann and Tichy 1999) and reciprocal (Croson and Buchan 1999) than men. Therefore, men may rate women less harshly than their male counterparts in order to obtain more favorable rating scores from women in the future.

Given the gender dynamics of workplace advancement, as well as the tendency of male raters to patronize female ratees with inflated ratings, we project that feedback toward women tends to be vague yet positive (note that we do not claim that the results in Hypothesis 2 are driven by the adoption of a mobile application). We hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 2. When providing real-time feedback through an application, men rate women less harshly than their male counterparts.

## 2.3. Favoritism and Anonymity

Although many feedback dynamics have been studied in a traditional workplace setting (Hashim et al. 2017), some have not been extensively researched.

Although no extant studies address these questions in the context of real-time feedback, other research has found that a perceived risk of retribution and favoritism can impact employee feedback, both causing reviewers to leave more favorable feedback than they might otherwise (Bapna et al. 2016).

Research on anonymity is less clear. One study shows that real-time feedback given under pseudonyms improves performance, potentially because it is seen as less biased (Jung et al. 2010); another study finds that anonymity leads to more thorough and accurate performance assessments because raters feel free to say things they otherwise would not (Bapna et al. 2016). By contrast, anonymous reviews may be taken less seriously by those reviewed because they lack source credibility (Ghose et al. 2009).

Although research on anonymity in performance feedback is scarce, literature in related disciplines can help provide clues to dynamics that might translate to a real-time application-based feedback environment. A qualitative study of the social network application Secret, which allows members of a social network to share messages anonymously to one another, illustrates the concept of networked anonymity, which suggests that users use contextual clues to attempt to deanonymize feedback when it is known to come from within a particular network of contacts (Sharon and John 2018). A study of anonymous online reviews shows that message affect intensity and valence affect message processing; low-affect intensity correlated with more trust toward the message when the author was anonymous than when the author was known, and high-affect intensity also correlated with higher message processing (Hong and Li 2017). Moreover, many studies illustrate the disinhibition effect (Suler 2004) that occurs when online users are anonymous, which can have results that are positive (as in relation to self-disclosure on blogs, e.g., see Hollenbaugh and Everett 2013) or negative (as in relation to sexual harassment on Twitter, e.g., see Fox et al. 2015, which is shown to have effects on offline behavior as well). These related disciplines may suggest that real-time application-based performance feedback could have unexpected and perhaps unwanted results with users attempting to deanonymize feedback, responding with more trust to lower-affect feedback but dwelling more on higher-affect feedback and/or providing feedback that otherwise would not be provided (for good or ill).

Our real-time feedback differs from traditional reviews in two important features, which make realtime feedback more like a repeated game. First, realtime feedback increases the frequency of evaluations compared with traditional performance reviews. In traditional performance reviews, typically, employees are rated once or twice a year. This long time frame makes traditional performance reviews less like a repeated game. In contrast, in real-time feedback, employees are rated multiple times a week. The real-time nature increases the intensity of employees’ interactions and makes a repeated game possible: employee i is rated by employee j now, and employee i may rate employee j back in the near future.

Second, in traditional performance reviews, typically, supervisors can rate subordinates, but subordinates cannot rate supervisors. It is not a repeated game because when a supervisor rates a subordinate, the supervisor does not need to consider that the subordinate may rate the supervisor back. In contrast, our real-time feedback environment allows a subordinate to rate a supervisor.

Because of these two features, the real-time feedback environment is more like a repeated game. Previous research has shown that fear of favoritism and retribution can impact feedback patterns in repeated games, with both dynamics generally resulting in more favorable feedback (Bapna et al. 2016). In particular, Axelrod (1984) shows that the tit-for-tat strategy is an equilibrium strategy in a repeated game: an agent using the tit-for-tat strategy first cooperates and then, subsequently, replicates an opponent’s previous action. If the opponent previously was cooperative, the agent is cooperative. If not, the agent is not. In our context, a tit-for-tat strategy means that employee i gives a higher score (“cooperative”) to a recipient who gave employee i a higher score (“cooperative”) in the previous round. Therefore, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 3. Employees adopt a tit-for-tat strategy when rating each other in a real-time application-based environment.

Anonymity in the feedback process is one way to address potential favoritism and retribution. Anonymous feedback seems to impact ratings because it is seen as less biased (Jung et al. 2010) and may lead to more accurate assessments because raters feel free to critique openly (Bapna et al. 2016). Moreover, research in related disciplines suggests the possible roles of networked anonymity (Sharon and John 2018) and the disinhibition effect (Suler 2004) in the feedback processes with both positive (Hollenbaugh and Everett 2013) and negative (Fox et al. 2015) potential effects. Essentially, anonymity transfers repeated interactions into a one-shot game, which leads to a less cooper ative outcome. Therefore, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 4. Anonymity negatively impacts real-time ratings in an application-based environment.

## 2.4. Impact of Feedback

Extensive studies examine employees’ reactions to negative, corrective, and developmental feedback.

Real-time feedback on performance is an important driver of performance improvement and professional development (McCarthy and Garavan 2001, Pampino et al. 2004, Cooper 2006, Scott 2015). In particular, regular real-time feedback has been shown to support employee growth and development in traditional work settings (Pampino et al. 2004, Cooper 2006).

In some studies, negative feedback is correlated with improved performance and outcomes (Hazucha et al. 1993, Guo et al. 2014). Moreover, research shows that even poor performers substantially increase their performance after receiving feedback about the behaviors and outcomes in need of improvement (Smither et al. 1995). However, the highly affective nature and impact of negative feedback can hinder opportunities for performance improvement (Ilgen and Davis 2000, Ilies et al. 2007). A survey of more than 100 full-time employees across different companies and industries shows that negative feedback from supervisors can generate a wide range of emotions, including anger, frustration, and disappointment (Belschak and Den Hartog 2009), as well as denial, defensiveness, and dissatisfaction (Ilgen et al. 1979, 1981; Podsakoff and Farh 1989; London 1997). Furthermore, some studies contend that negative feedback is viewed as less accurate and that it is therefore less likely to be accepted by feedback receivers than positive feedback (Ilgen et al. 1979, Fedor et al. 1989).

The relationship between positive feedback and employee behavior is not as well studied. However, Waldersee and Luthans (1994) find that positive feedback does not improve outcomes on routine tasks in a fast-food setting. Prior studies establish the importance of regular real-time feedback for performance improvement and development (McCarthy and Garavan 2001, Pampino et al. 2004, Cooper 2006, Scott 2015). Such feedback generally improves performance.

Although employee ratings are different from employee performance, because we do not have data about performance, we use future rating as a proxy for performance improvement. In our specific context, an initial low score can provide an incentive for employees to improve their rating over time. Prior literature demonstrates that social image and emotionally prompted social approval are capable of incentivizing individuals (Ariely et al. 2009, Qiu and Kumar 2017). Social image refers to an individual’s tendency to be motivated by others’ perceptions and is one of the most important motivations for social rewards (Ariely et al. 2009). Based on the image motivation theory, employees desire to be perceived as “good” (to be liked and respected by others), and one way to achieve this is to improve their scores in the evaluation process. Employees want to present themselves in a positive light and earn admiration expressed by others, which is a typical example of a social reward based on the social image. Therefore, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 5. In a real-time feedback environment, scores of recipients with initial low scores increase more significantly than those of other recipients

## 3. Industry Practices and DevelapMe Overview

The use of real-time feedback applications is on the rise within organizations. In 2015, General Electric eliminated its traditional annual performance review and launched a feedback application called D@GE (Addady 2015). This application allowed managers to provide more immediate real-time feedback to their employees, and employees can also request feedback from their manager at any time. At nearly the same time, Amazon launched its Anytime Feedback Tool, which allowed employees to provide positive or constructive feedback to their colleagues (Stone 2015). Similarly, IBM integrated a desktop feedback application called Appreciation, Coaching, Evaluation (ACE) in February 2016 as an optional tool that is being used by roughly a quarter of its workforce (Lebowitz 2016). This tool allows employees to ask for feedback from managers, subordinates, or teammates at any time and supports IBM’s goal to establish a culture of feedback across the organization.

To address this trend in business, the real-time application-based feedback industry is growing as well. Several products are being developed to meet the growing demand in the market. Oetting (2018) lists 10 tools that provide employee engagement pulse surveys, manager-to-employee feedback surveys, peer and manager feedback tools, and other tools to address these needs in the market.

Note that there is a fundamental difference between the real-time review systems (usually implemented by mobile applications) and regular-basis (e.g., weekly, quarterly, or monthly) review systems. The regular review systems (usually implemented by computers) are still in the domain of traditional performance reviews: they evaluate an employee’s job performance over the previous period by one’s supervisor. No matter what the previous period is (e.g., weekly, quarterly, monthly, or yearly), this type of review system focuses on evaluating the overall performance of an employee over a time period, which differs from event-based performance reviews supported by real-time review systems. For example, if a rater notices a particularly positive or negative performance issue in a colleague, the mobile device ensures that feedback is readily accessible to capture that feedback observation. In other words, in the environment of real-time reviews, raters can give ratings and feedback to recipients immediately after a particular action of recipients (e.g., after a recipient’s presentation, a rater can immediately give a rating and leave constructive feedback via real-time review systems). The immediate real-time reviews typically focus on a specific behavior or task of a recipient, and hence, the recipient is more likely to know how to improve it in the future. In addition, recipients are less likely to be hurt emotionally by the immediate real-time reviews (especially low ratings) because these reviews focus on behavior instead of recipients themselves. Prior studies suggest that if employees do not perceive the system to be fair (emotionally hurt by low ratings), then they are more likely to ignore and not use the feedback they receive (Waldman and Bowen 1998).

In real-time review systems, companies do not need to send a notification on a regular basis. Instead, eventbased feedback (instant feedback after a particular action, such as a presentation) is becoming increasingly popular in real-time review systems (Cappelli and Tavis 2016). As Accenture chief executive Pierre Nanterme said, “People want to know on an ongoing basis, am I doing right? Am I moving in the right direction? Do you think I’m progressing? Nobody’s going to wait for an annual cycle to get that feedback” (Lawrence 2017). By contrast, in the regular-basis review systems, the rating scores are not based on a particular behavior or task (e.g., a rating score based on the overall presentation skills in a time period rather than a rating score based on a particular presentation), which prevents recipients from obtaining immediate feedback and getting improved. A computer typically supports feedback when a rater is available to sit down to recall the event, which is the regularbasis review. It is not convenient to support eventbased performance reviews, which rate a particular behavior or task.

## 3.1. Use and Impact of Real-Time Feedback Applications

Although the prevalence of real-time feedback applications is increasing in the market, there is a gap in the literature in examining the uses of real-time feedback applications, feedback dynamics, and behaviors when using the application and the impact of real-time feedback applications to help support employee development of competencies, skills, and performance improvement. Only indirect research is found on the use of feedback applications in educational and higher-education settings (Jung et al. 2010, Hsia et al. 2016, Lefroy et al. 2017). This study seeks to address these gaps in the literature by analyzing dynamic data from a real-time feedback application.

This study uses a new real-time feedback application called DevelapMe, developed by Petrucci et al. (2016), to study several feedback dynamics in relation to the use of the application. The DevelapMe feedback ap plication was initially developed for use with student team projects in higher-education institutions (Santos 2017) but then became a commercial product used by organizations to provide competency-based realtime feedback. Given the trend of several notable organizations moving to real-time feedback via an application coupled with younger generations driving the use of applications and the desire and skill in communicating important information digitally (Modo Labs Team 2016, Newman 2017), studies on real-time feedback applications are needed to establish foundational knowledge about this burgeoning field.

## 3.2. DevelapMe Overview

The DevelapMe application (https://www.develapme .com/) enables employees to give, seek, and receive realtime competency-based feedback using their computers, smartphones, or other devices. DevelapMe contracts with organizations to set up instances of the application within their organization’s context and culture. Employee lists are loaded into the application to allow users to request or provide feedback. Organizations also work with DevelapMe to specify the competencies they would like to include as feedback options within the application. These competencies might include building trust, listening skills, and emotional intelligence. Additional items can also be included, such as goals, progress on goals, and text-based comments. Organizational users can typically provide feedback on about 20 items, and generally, two to four items make up a competency. Currently, DevelapMe is used by several small, medium-sized, and large organizations, including a number of Fortune 500 companies. The application has been profiled in media outlets such as Chief Learning Officer (Santos 2017) and Training magazines (Freifeld 2017)

Within the DevelapMe application, feedback can be given to and received from peers, direct reports, and supervisors. Note that in an instance of feedback, employee i rates employee j. Peer means that employees i and j are coworkers, supervisor means that employee i is employee j’s supervisor, and direct report means that employee i is employee j’s subordinate (in other words, employee j is employee i’s supervisor). Hereafter, we refer to direct reports as subordinates. To start the feedback process, a user taps the “give feedback” icon (see Figure 1). Users can then request feedback from anyone in their network or select an individual and provide feedback that is sent to the individual. When providing feedback, employees rate selected individuals on a one to five sliding scale. They can also enter comments for each item. We examine the issue of multicollinearity by looking at the variance inflation factors (VIFs). The VIF quantifies the severity of multicollinearity in a regression. When the independent variables are correlated, the estimated standard errors of the fitted coefficients are inflated, which can be used to check for the presence of multicollinearity (Chatterjee and Hadi 2012). In the absence of any linear relationship among independent variable j and other independent variables, VIF would be one. The deviation of the $\mathrm { V I F } _ { j }$ value from one indicates departure from orthogonality and a tendency toward collinearity. VIFs are widely used in the information systems literature to establish whether multicollinearity is an issue (e.g., Burtch et al. 2013, Singh et al. 2014, Bapna et al. 2019, Khurana et al. 2019). Most prior studies rely on informal rules of thumb applied to the VIF. For instance, in Chatterjee and Hadi (2012, p. 250), “values of variance inflation factors greater than 10 are often taken as a signal that the data have collinearity problems.” We find that the VIFs associated with each variable are less than 2.5, which is well below the conventionally accepted threshold $( \mathrm { V I F } < 1 0 )$ , indicating that multicollinearity is not an issue in our model and does not significantly impact our findings.

## 5.2. Robustness Checks

A concern in our regression Equation (1a) is that the relationship of users (e.g., supervisor, subordinate, peer) could be correlated with individual specific time trends. In other words, unobserved timevarying factors could bias our estimates. In the first check, we follow Angrist and Pischke (2008) to control for individual-specific time trends in the correlated random trend model.

In the second check, we use a Heckman-type model to examine the self-selection issues of anonymous raters. The key selection issue is that a rater may choose to be anonymous because of the individual characteristics (e.g., gender) or the relationship status with the recipient (e.g., subordinates). In the Heckman-type model, we can directly capture the selection mechanism.

5.2.1. Correlated Random Trend Model. Following Angrist and Pischke (2008), we control for time trends in the correlated random trend model:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} S c o r e _ {i j t} = & a _ {i} + g _ {i} t + \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} S u b o r d i n a t e _ {i j} + \beta_ {2} P e e r _ {i j} \\ & + \beta_ {3} A n o n _ {i j} + \beta_ {4} A v e R e c i p i e n t G o t R a t e d _ {j t} \\ & + \beta_ {5} A v e G i v e r R a t e d O t h e r s _ {i t} \\ & + \beta_ {6} A v e G i v e r G o t R a t e d _ {i t} \\ & + \beta_ {7} A v e R e c i p i e n t R a t e d O t h e r s _ {j t} \\ & + \beta_ {8} R e c i p i e n t T i m e _ {j t} + C o n t r o l s + \varepsilon_ {i j t}, \end{array}\tag{1b}
$$

where $g _ { i }$ is an individual-specific time trend for employee i. This specification allows individuals to follow different trends in a limited but potentially revealing way. It is worth noting that in the correlated random trend model, g<sub>i</sub>t can be correlated with our independent variables because, in the estimation process, g t is canceled out by first differencing Equation (1b) twice, and our estimation is unbiased. Therefore, the model with correlated random trends is likely to be more robust and convincing (Angrist and Pischke 2008). The estimation results of the correlated random trend model are presented in column (7) of Table 3, and we find that the estimated effects of interest are qualitatively the same after inclusion of these trends and the coefficients change by a very small amount.

5.2.2. Heckman-Type Model. As discussed earlier, the selection issues of being anonymous may bias our estimation. Hence, we consider a Heckman-type model (Brown and Mergoupis 2010) to directly specify the selection process of being anonymous. The Heckman correction is a method to correct for endogeneity problems by modeling the selection mechanisms. If the selection equation in the Heckman model captures the true underlying selection mechanism, the endogeneity concern is alleviated.

Our key regression model is still Equation (1a). However, we directly model whether a rater chooses to be anonymous by looking at a Probit model. In our data, the identity of anonymous raters is unknown to recipients but known to us. Therefore, we model the selection mechanism by looking at the individual characteristics of the anonymous raters and the relationship between the raters and recipients. The selection equation is as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} w _ {i j t} ^ {*} = & \gamma_ {0} + \gamma_ {1} G e n d e r _ {i} + \gamma_ {2} G e n d e r _ {j} \\ & + \gamma_ {3} A v e G i v e r R a t e d O t h e r s _ {i t} \\ & + \gamma_ {4} A v e G i v e r G o t R a t e d _ {i t} + \gamma_ {5} S u b o r d i n a t e _ {i j} \\ & + \gamma_ {6} P e e r _ {i j} + e _ {i j t}, \end{array}\tag{1c}
$$

where $\boldsymbol { w _ { i j t } ^ { * } }$ is a latent variable, Gender (Gender ) is a dummy variable indicating the gender of giver i (recipient $j ) ,$ , and $e _ { i j t }$ follows from a standard normal distribution. The indicator Anon 1 if $w _ { i j t } ^ { * } \geq 0 ;$ Anon<sub>ij </sub> 0 otherwise.

Note that in Equation (1c), we include three categories of variables: (1) relationship status (whether a giver is a supervisor, peer, or subordinate of a receiver), (2) average ratings a giver has given, and (3) gender of giver or receiver. First, relationship status matters; for example, a giver may be less likely to reveal the giver’s identity when the giver gives a lower score to the giver’s supervisor than to the giver’s subordinate. Second, the average score a giver has given in the past could be correlated with the anonymity decision. A giver’s and a receiver’s rating behavior can be thought of as a repeated game in game theory. If a giver always gives a high score, the giver may adopt a more cooperative strategy and want to reveal the giver’s identity to receivers in exchange for future favors. Humans have an inherent inclination to return favors (Harvey et al. 2010). If a giver tends to give higher scores to receivers, the giver is likely to have an incentive to reveal the giver’s name and let receivers feel indebted. Third, the prior literature on experimental game theory has found that gender is an important determinant of economic and strategic behavior (Croson and Buchan 1999, Ortmann and Tichy 1999). In particular, women are more cooperative (Ortmann and Tichy 1999) and reciprocal (Croson and Buchan 1999) than men. In our context, revealing identity is more cooperative behavior in the repeated games of a giver and receiver. Therefore, we believe that women are more likely to reveal their identity, which is confirmed by the firststage results of the Heckman model.

Taking this selection process into account, we estimate Equations (1a) and (1c) jointly, and the endogeneity issue in our regression method is less of a concern. The estimation results of the Heckman-type model are consistent with our baseline model. The details are presented in column (8) of Table 3.

5.2.3. Ceiling Effect. A potential concern is the rightcensored observations: for individuals who have high scores, they have no room to improve (the highest rating is five). We use a tobit model to directly model the ceiling effect (Cameron and Trivedi 2009). In the tobit model, the true data-generating process (the unobserved latent variable) is specified as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & y _ {i j t} = a _ {i} + \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} S u b o r d i n a t e _ {i j} + \beta_ {2} P e e r _ {i j} + \beta_ {3} A n o n _ {i j} \\ & \quad + \beta_ {4} A v e R e c i p i e n t G o t R a t e d _ {j t} \\ & \quad + \beta_ {5} A v e G i v e r R a t e d O t h e r s _ {i t} \\ & \quad + \beta_ {6} A v e G i v e r G o t R a t e d _ {i t} \\ & \quad + \beta_ {7} A v e R e c i p i e n t R a t e d O t h e r s _ {j t} \\ & \quad + \beta_ {8} R e c i p i e n t T i m e _ {j t} \\ & \quad + \beta_ {9} R e c i p i e n t T i m e _ {j t} \times (I n i t i a l R a t i n g <   4 _ {j}) \\ & \quad + \beta_ {1 0} I n i t i a l R a t i n g <   4 _ {j} + c o n t r o l s + \varepsilon_ {i j t}. \end{array}
$$

However, $y _ { i j t }$ is a latent variable that cannot be directly observed. The observed score is related to the unobserved latent variable through the following observation rule:

$$
S c o r e _ {i j t} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} y _ {i j t}, & \text {if} y _ {i j t} \leq 5, \\ 5, & \text {if} y _ {i j t} > 5. \end{array} \right.
$$

In other words, $y _ { i j t }$ can be greater than five, but when it is greater than five, we cannot observe it because the highest possible rating is five (what we can observe is the upper cutoff point of five). If the true datagenerating process is specified by our tobit model but we mistakenly think that the original regression equation is the true data-generating process, then our estimation will not be consistent (Cameron and Trivedi 2009). Intuitively, if we can directly observe y<sub>ijt</sub>, then, for example, we know that an employee’s score increases from 5 to 5.2. However, in practice, we can observe only right-censored observations: this employee’s score remains the same (from score five to score five) If we do not take this data-generating process into account, we would obtain an inconsistent estimator.

The full sample estimation results (Table 4) are consistent with our main results (the coefficient on the interaction term RecipientTime × InitialRating < 4 is positive and significant), suggesting that our results (Hypothsis 5) are not biased by the ceiling effect: scores of recipients with initial low scores increase more significantly than those of other recipients.

The explanation for these findings is related to contextual reference points. The literature on behavioral economics and marketing demonstrates that humans rarely look at things in absolute terms: they focus on the changes relative to contextual reference points and judge things in a relative way (Huber et al. 1982, Tversky and Kahneman 1991, Ariely 2008). People tend to compare things locally to available alternatives. For example, the relative attractiveness of one option compared with another option often depends on the presence or absence of a third irrelevant option (Huber et al. 1982, Tversky and Simonson 1993).

In our study, employees may also use the context information to interpret the ratings they have received from supervisors. In other words, they do not focus only on the absolute value of the ratings. They see these ratings in a context, and one of the most important contextual reference points is the rating supervisors typically give. Similar to various settings examined in the previous studies on the contextdependent decisions (Tversky and Simonson 1993, Ariely 2008), employees may tend to compare the rating scores they have received with those typically given by supervisors (benchmark). Recall that we have shown in our results in Hypothesis 1 that, typically, recipients receive lower scores from supervisors, which means that the contextual reference point is a low score from supervisors. If a recipient receives a high score from a supervisor, the context information (low score as a benchmark) makes it a very strong signal that the recipient did a great job. As mentioned earlier, the reason is that people pay closer attention to the change relative to contextual reference points than to absolute values (Tversky and Kahneman 1991). Therefore, we observe that previous positive feedback from supervisors provides a greater incentive for the recipient to increase current scores than previous negative feedback. If a supervisor gives a low rating to the recipient, it is a different case because the context information plays a different role. Because the contextual reference point is a low score from supervisors, if a recipient receives a low score from a supervisor, the recipient thinks that it is normal: the context information (low score as a benchmark) makes it a weak signal that the recipient did a bad job.

Based on this logic, we can also consistently explain another finding that the average rating that recipient j receives from subordinates up to time t has a significantly negative impact on recipient j’s score at time t (i.e., previous negative feedback from subordinates provides a greater incentive for the recipient to increase the current scores than previous positive feedback). Recall that we have shown in our results in Hypothesis 1 that, typically, recipients receive higher scores from subordinates, which means that the contextual reference point is a high score from subordinates. If a recipient receives a low score from a subordinate, the context information (high score as a benchmark) makes it a very strong signal that the recipient did a bad job. Therefore, we observe that previous negative feedback from subordinates provides a greater incentive for the recipient to increase the current score than previous positive feedback. As a summary, we provide a coherent explanation for the complex pattern of our findings. Shadish et al. (2002, p. 105) point out that “the more complex the pattern that is successfully predicted, the less likely it is that alternative explanations could generate the same pattern.” The approach of coherent pattern matching (a coherent explanation of multiple complex empirical findings) suggests that our explanation is more likely to be better than alternative explanations (Shadish et al. 2002).

The implication of these findings is profound. Traditional reviews only provide feedback from supervisors and do not allow for peer feedback. However, peer evaluation is an important element to include in performance assessments and outcomes primarily because employees are more likely to accept peer feedback than supervisor feedback, especially when it is negative (Shore et al. 1992). Our context is different from the traditional approach of supervisor rating: in traditional performance reviews, supervisors are defining what constitutes good performance and also evaluating whether it was achieved. However, our context allows peer and subordinate evaluations. In other words, prior literature on performance reviews has mainly focused on how attributes of supervisors might influence the appraisal scores (e.g., Bernardin et al. 2016, Frederiksen et al. 2020). Our research has a different focus by examining other feedback sources $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ peer and subordinate evaluations) and moderators around these feedback sources. In particular, we find that a higher score from a recipient’s supervisor is more effective in motivating the recipient to obtain a higher score in the future than a lower score from a recipient’s supervisor, which has not been reported in the previous literature.

## 6. Discussion

The advancement of real-time application-based feedback technologies has revolutionized many industries (Hao et al. 2017) and provided an unprecedented opportunity for companies to overhaul traditional performance reviews. In order to deliver real-time performance feedback to support rapid innovation, a number of companies in a broad range of industries, such as Adobe, Dell, Microsoft, IBM, Deloitte, Accenture, PwC, GAP, OppenheimerFunds, and General Electric, have begun replacing review-based performance management systems with frequent, informal check-ins between managers and employees (Cappelli and Tavis 2016). In 2014, 12% of U.S. companies had dropped annual reviews, and two-thirds of large companies in the United Kingdom are in the process of changing their systems (Cappelli and Tavis 2016). Kevin Murphy, an expert on performance appraisals, noted, “As a result, annual appraisals end up as a source of anxiety and annoyance rather than a source of useful information.”<sup>1</sup> In this context of the performance management revolution, real-time feedback systems based on applications may facilitate more frequent, meaningful conversations between managers and employees and among teams. Rather than a formal, once-a-year review, continuous dialogue and real-time feedback have become increasingly important in the current practice of corporate performance management.

## 6.1. Managerial and Industry Implications

In this study, we investigate how real-time feedback systems facilitate performance management in companies. Our study fills an important gap in the literature by providing insight into real-time feedback design. In particular, we study (1) how relationship source (peer, subordinate, or supervisor) impacts real-time feedback through an application, (2) how gender affects real-time feedback through an application, (3) how favoritism, retribution, and anonymity factor into real-time feedback, and (4) how previous ratings impact current ratings. Our research on real-time applicationbased feedback has important managerial implications for the design of new performance management systems. It also highlights how companies might create an innovative human resource operation that delivers flexibility and agility.

Regarding managerial implications, this study supports the use of an application-based performance management system in addition to or in place of a traditional review system. First, prior studies have shown that the rating measure in the traditional approach to performance management is biased (Scullen et al. 2000): 62% of the variance in the ratings could be accounted for by considering individual raters peculiarities of perception. Actual performance accounted for only 21% of the variance. In other words, the ratings mostly reflect the rating tendencies of the rater instead of the performance of the recipient. However, in this study of real-time feedback, we find that if a recipient received a higher score previously or if a rater gave a higher score previously, it does not significantly affect the rating that the recipient receives from the rater after controlling for individual fixed effects, which suggests that real-time feedback seems to be more accurate and unbiased by previous scores. Furthermore, it may be the case that instant and continuous feedback yields richer data and a clearer picture of employees’ strengths and weaknesses and provides a better sense of how employees are performing.

Second, we find that when using real-time feedback over time, the scores of recipients with initial low scores increase significantly. This suggests that realtime feedback might contribute to behavior change and performance improvement. It might be the case that effective real-time peer reviews motivate people to perform well every day of the year and not just when a quarterly or annual evaluation is approaching. Real-time feedback has the potential to deliver constructive and private criticism immediately after a specific task, which might make criticism feel less threatening and personal. Some of the specific design changes include building in data tracking capabilities to collect trending information for management and advanced analytics as well as programming push notifications or similar alerts to support engagement and remind users to leave regular feedback. Furthermore, there is an opportunity to better leverage these findings and guide application development, including feedback screen design and process workflows. Enhancing application design would make it easier for impactful feedback to be given to employees, specifically comments.

Third, our results show that positive real-time feedback has a stronger effect on future ratings than negative feedback. This result may have important implications on how to promote desired behavior using real-time feedback. When good work is noted immediately, it seems that the employee feels compelled to repeat it immediately. It might be possible that if recognition is delayed, the link might be lost, and the company might lose out on better performance and higher engagement in the meantime. Management and employees should be trained in effective feedback strategies, including providing timely and constructive feedback as well as ongoing encouragement when others perform well at a task or job function.

Fourth, our results show that in the context of realtime application-based performance reviews, men rate women higher than other men, and women rate men and women similarly to how men rate men. This could potentially represent a cultural shift and/ or new workforce biases (e.g., the current workforce is younger and has different gender norms than previous decades). Although further research should be done to understand these issues, our data suggest that same-gender ratings may hold the most validity as performance measures.

## 6.2. Limitations and Future Research Directions

This study’s findings and limitations point to several possible directions for future research. First, because of the lack of availability of data on the actual performance of employees, the impact of real-time feed back on employee performance remains unclear. Future studies should examine the relationship between real-time application-based feedback scores and actual employee performance. Second, because of the nature of our observational data, it is difficult to establish clear causal effects without relying on exogenous shocks. Future research should incorporate randomized field experiments to ensure internal validity. Third, the real time feedback studied in this paper is essentially a multidimensional rating system (Chen et al. 2018): employees can be rated based on different skill sets. In future research, we would examine the value of multidimensional rating systems within organizations with the goal of optimizing the systems to provide insight for organizations and encourage employee development. Fourth, more research should be conducted to confirm and understand the causes behind gender and other biases in real-time feedback applications; because our study is limited to one application, studies should consider whether similar gender biases are seen in other application-based performance reviews and qualitative and other data collected to understand the sources of these biases. Because our study limited its exploration of possible minority biases to gender, other studies might also look at the effects of biases related to race, sexual orientation, age, or other factors that might result in workplace discrimination. Fifth, although an aspect of our context for this study is a mobile application that enables real-time feedback, it appears that our effects are due to the real-time nature and cannot be directly attributed to the mobile setting without a further study to explore the effects of mobile versus nonmobile real-time feedback settings. Finally, future studies may include empirical comparisons between mobile real-time feedback and traditional appraisals to evaluate the effectiveness of continuous feedback and/or its potential for information overload; longitudinal studies should also consider the effects of realtime feedback on performance and promotion to understand the implications of mobile-based real-time feedback on long-term career development.
