# Leveraging Context: Re-Thinking Research Processes to Make “Contributions to Theory”

Viswanath Venkatesh<sup>a</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Business Information Technology, Pamplin College of Business, Virginia Tech, Blacksburg, Virginia 24061 Contact: vvenkatesh@vvenkatesh.us, https://orcid.org/0000-0001-8473-376X (VV)

Received: January 30, 2021 Revised: April 30, 2023; July 15, 2024; September 25, 2024; November 10, 2024 Accepted: November 16, 2024 Published Online in Articles in Advance: March 12, 2025

https://doi.org/10.1287/isre.2021.0048

Copyright: © 2025 INFORMS

Abstract. The ever-changing technology landscape has information systems (IS) researchers constantly facing new phenomena, especially in new settings, that must be understood. To address this challenge, building on calls for contextualization, this commentary complements prior works on contextualization and presents guidance within four general research steps— defining the problem, anchoring to prior research, seeking novelty, and reporting practices—in order to achieve rigorous contextualization. In doing this, the basic idea of scientific contribution in IS research is expanded from (a) the typical contribution of theory to the phenomenon (more than 80% of IS research articles from 2018 to 2021 from Information Systems Research, Journal of the Association for Information Systems, and MIS Quarterly) to (b) include the less common contribution to theory, especially reference discipline theory, thus creating the opportunity for IS research to be more impactful and IS to grow as a reference discipline. Finally, guidelines for editors and reviewers are offered to facilitate the publication of impactful contextualized research.

## 1. Introduction

The continual explosion of activities enabled and transformed by information and communications technology (ICT) is leading to new phenomena of interest to information systems (IS) researchers. Such new phenomena manifest in various ways including but not limited to a change in the existing ways of doing things, a transformation of existing industries, entire new industries and industry segments coming into play, grand challenges to study, new data sets being available, and the emergence of new settings and circumstances all over the world. “Through a glass brightly” (Barash 2018) tells us that we can learn a great deal about ourselves now and in the future by looking back at the evolution and understanding fundamental characteristics about our evolution. For instance, Burton-Jones (2014), based on a study of articles published in nine journals from 1988 (or the first year of publication of the journal, whichever came later) to 2011 that cited the seminal book by Zuboff (1988), titled “In the age of the smart machine … ,” found that theory<sup>1</sup> in the book was even more relevant at the time when the article of Burton-Jones (2014) was written than when Zuboff’s book was originally written. Further, he noted that the theory was underutilized despite its continuing relevance—and he called for studying the theory in new contexts and suggested that researchers should use concepts from the theory to make new predictions in the current world of technology.

In keeping with this, IS researchers typically use one of two common approaches to explain and understand new phenomena. First, existing theory from IS and/or a reference discipline is brought to bear, with extensions and/or modifications made to fit the IS problem, which is commonly termed midrange or contextualized theory (Grover and Lyytinen 2015). Although this has been a common practice in IS research for decades now, as the field has matured in recent times, such work fares poorly in the review process for being derivative, just an application, or inappropriate for the phenomenon, although such investigations focused on phenomena are indeed valuable (Grover and Lyytinen 2015, Leidner 2016, Dennis 2019) as empirical contributions. Colquitt and Zapata-Phelan (2007), however, rate this type of contribution to be low and term authors of such work to merely be reporters. Second, and more recently, spurred by the plethora of data sets available about emerging phenomena, researchers sometimes are data driven, which leads to observational bias, also termed the streetlight effect<sup>2</sup> (for a recent discussion in IS, see Rai (2017a)). Illus trating this problem in the context of big data research, Grover et al. (2020) noted that such an approach loses focus on theory and critically, fails to develop a cumulative tradition, and undermines IS being a reference disci pline. To overcome such problems, solutions proposed include joint special issues with journals in other disciplines and IS researchers publishing in journals in other disciplines, which, although valuable, limits the growth of IS as a reference discipline.

Researchers in IS and other disciplines have noted that an excessive reliance on theory constrains explanation of new phenomena (Hambrick 2007, Dennis 2019, Hirschheim 2019). Contextualized theory development as an alternative to the traditional positivist approach, which emphasizes generalizability across contexts (Whetten 1989, Johns 2006),<sup>3</sup> serves as a middle-of-the-road solution. Here, context is defined as situational opportunities or constraints that may alter behavior; context is further classified as omnibus and discrete that captures a wide range of groupings (e.g., country, occupations) and psychological variables (e.g., autonomy), respectively. The laments related to the lack of contextualization—and a move away from generalizability as a criterion to evaluate theory—have been more vocal in recent times in various fields including IS, given that contextualization allows for greater richness in understanding of a phenomenon and more actionable insights (Orlikowski and Iacono 2001; Rousseau and Fried 2001; Johns 2006, 2017; Hong et al. 2014; Davison and Martinsons 2016). In the traditional view, the context in which the research is conducted is typically black-boxed and/or ignored. Information about context, which would be key to help future work understand boundary conditions and/or concepts mechanisms that are germane to a particular context (Rousseau and Fried 2001; Johns 2006, 2017; Hong et al. 2014; Sarker 2016) or at least help understand the findings/theory in situ, often goes unreported in published work. Interestingly, Gupta (2018) noted that a new context alone is inadequate to be a novel contribution, suggesting a rich understanding is vital. Although scho lars promoting contextualization have been critical of generalizability as a criterion for theory evaluation, even Whetten (1989), who emphasizes generalizability, points to the idea of contingencies—that is, who, where, when— thus speaking to the role of context even in traditional theory development practices. As it relates to contextualization, a special mention of the new settings around the world is warranted because journals have been strongly biased in favor of phenomena and data in the west, with some estimates being that a vast majority of social sciences data are collected in the United States and among a convenience sample of young white people (Buchanan et al. 2021). These concerns were discussed in a set of biases in our theories and research in general; termed a western, educated, industrialized, rich, and democratic (WEIRD; Henrich et al. 2010) bias; and some of these biases have been observed to exist in various business dis ciplines including IS and management (Davison and D´ıaz Andrade 2018, Bruton et al. 2022).

Contextualized theory development as a way to make scientific contributions should focus on developing explanations for phenomena in interesting and important contexts and is key to avoid averaging effects wherein the overall results do not truly explain any specific group (Johns 2006). Nonetheless, the risk of overcontextualization must be recognized. In its extreme, contextualization leads to a customized solution (i.e., idiosyncratic, piece-meal knowledge about a phenomenon) that is so unique to the particular context that lack of generalizability is its feature, bordering on consulting practice. Thus, the problem with a context-focused position in the extreme is its inability or need to speak to a broader base of knowledge which hinders cumulative scientific knowledge being built (Jiang et al. 2022). Both approaches—one with a focus on generalizability eschewing context and another with contextualized the orizing eschewing generalizability (Johns 2017)—pose problems in terms of contributions.

Prior work has discussed how context can play a role (Johns 2006) and how it creates opportunities for knowl edge creation through breakdowns of existing knowl edge in new contexts (Alvesson and Ka¨rreman 2007). Johns discussed seven ways in which context can have an impact. He also suggests studying context with a focus on processes, events, multilevel measurement, and better reporting. Despite these calls, in 2017, Johns, whose 2006 article was the “Article of the Decade” in the Academy of Management Review, noted that the progress on contextualization was still limited (Johns 2017). In IS, Hong et al. (2014) presented guidelines for contextualization that suggested the delineation of context-specific factors or decomposition of more abstract constructs into contextual factors led to a more predictive model; additionally, they suggested that context could be a moderator, which is consistent with Whetten (1989) and Johns (2006). Grover et al. (2020) proposed a symbi otic approach that leverages both theory and data; as noted earlier, Grover and Lyytinen (2015), in related work, argued that the current contextualization script (midrange theory development) is limiting when it comes to the creation of new knowledge (see also Jiang et al. 2022). This problem is even more pronounced when the focus is on the phenomenon and eschews theory.

The answer thus lies in formulating a clear path for authors seeking to contextualize research such that they can pursue the study of interesting and important new phenomena without simply applying or replicating prior work in a new context, that is, without sufficient scientific contribution or being deemed unscientific for a contextu alized solution but rather generate sufficient scientific contribution. It is important that the unique elements of what makes and defines the context be discussed, preserved, and leveraged in theory development—and abstract knowledge be generated. Against this backdrop, the objectives of this essay are (1) refine how we leverage context, with a specific focus on making scientific contributions both to the understanding of the phenomenon and the theory, and (2) present the steps for contextualization to accomplish these dual purposes and provide guidelines for reviewers and editors.

## 2. Conceptualizing Theoretical Contribution

One widely held viewpoint regarding scientific contribution in IS centers around the question: how is theory enriched? The path is complex considering the broader issue of theory enrichment that comes with it answers to queries of what is being added to the theory—for example, new constructs (Colquitt and Zapata-Phelan 2007)—and how does this enrich our understanding, a notoriously subjective question to answer, especially given that there is almost an expectation that the answer will run counter to intuition.<sup>4</sup> This leads to the question of what specifically theoretical contribution is. For a long time, we have regarded theoretical contribution and scientific contribution to be interchangeable terms. However, distinctions must be made.

There are two types of contributions that together comprise scientific contribution: contribution to theory and contribution of theory (Whetten 2009). Contribution to theory is a fundamental advance to the core theory that is being used, be it native IS theory or reference discipline theory. The definition of native IS theory is itself somewhat ambiguous, with some suggesting that a theory that explains an IS phenomenon is a native IS theory (Straub 2012), which in turn led to Straub’s conclusion that there are plenty of native IS theories, whereas others suggest that a native IS theory is one where the core concepts and/or mechanisms in the theory should originate within the IS discipline, which is a really high bar for any discipline, let alone IS, that in turn could suggest there are no native IS theories. For example, the technology acceptance model (TAM; Venkatesh and Davis 2000) is popularly regarded as a native IS theory (Straub 2012); however, even TAM would fail the latter definition of a native IS theory because of its roots in and strong ties to social psychology and decision-making literature. Although delineating what constitutes native IS theory is beyond the scope of this commentary, given the goals of this commentary, the focus of the guidelines here will particularly focus on contribution to reference discipline theory, which is a key pathway to IS being more of a reference discipline than is currently the case.

A good way to assess a contribution to theory drawn from a reference discipline is whether a leading journal in the reference discipline will publish the new paper/ extension. In IS, there are a few good examples of contributions to theory: (1) the earlier caveat underscored, one example of a contribution to native IS theory is TAM2 (Venkatesh and Davis 2000) that extends TAM; and (2) one example of a contribution to reference discipline theory is media synchronicity theory (MST; Dennis et al. 2008) as an alternative to media richness theory from the field of communications. However, such contributions to theory are rare (more recent IS examples will be noted later), with the more typical IS paper making a contribution of theory to a phenomenon, wherein a theory is leveraged/used to understand an IS problem, which is related to the earlier midrange theory discussion. The call for a focus on understanding of phenomena (Hirschheim 2019) is consistent with this notion of contribution of theory. This may be in the form of a straight application, which, although valuable as an empirical contribution (Ågerfalk 2014), is unlikely to be an adequate contribution for leading IS journals (Leidner 2020). Such applications may be in the form of contextualization to fit the domain and/or in the form of additions to the theory to fit the domain (context-specific— e.g., IS-related—variables added to make the model applicable to an IS domain; Hong et al. 2014). Jiang et al. (2022) reviewed the use of transaction-cost economics (TCE) in IS research and found that nearly all uses of the theory were various forms of application. Such works, when accompanied by sufficient description of the context, could be useful contributions of theory. This, of course, hinges heavily on enough of a contribution to the understanding of the phenomenon. The recent editorial by Gopal et al. (2024), although delineating excellent paths for econ IS research to make theoretical contributions, does not distinguish across these two types of contributions, although their view of theoretical breadth and depth, that is, the application of one (depth) or more (breadth) theories, seems to be more in line with contributions of theory.

This commentary lays the plan for how researchers can achieve the goal of contributions of theory (typical), as well as contributions to theory (rare). The best-case scenario is when IS researchers continue to make contributions of theory as the starting point while examining opportunities for potential contributions to theory.

## 3. Review of IS Research: Assessment of Theoretical Contributions Made

Against the backdrop of these two types of contributions— contributions of and contributions to theory—as an illustration, research published in three leading IS journals over a four-year period was examined. Two coders, including the author, read and independently coded all papers published in Information Systems Research (ISR), Journal of the Association for Information Systems (JAIS), and MIS Quarterly (MISQ) from 2018 to 2021 in terms of contributions of and to theory.<sup>5</sup> Both coders read the papers in their entirety and coded the contributions. Key text related to contributions in the words of the authors, wherever available, are shown in the Online Appendix, Sections A1–A3, Tables A1-1, A2-1, and A3-1 for ISR, JAIS, and MISQ, respectively. Tables A1-2, A2-2, and A3-2 show the coding of the contributions of and to theory, and Tables A1-3, A2-3, and A3-3 show the citation

counts of the 2018 articles accumulated through the end of 2024. Table 1 shows the counts of papers in different categories of contributions by journal. Over the four-year period, there were a total of 709 peer-reviewed papers published across the three journals. In all three journals, more than 80% of the papers used theory. Although papers seldom make a distinction between the types of contributions (of versus to theory), our coding showed that the biggest category of contributions in the three journals was contributions of theory to the understanding of a phenomenon, regardless of whether theory was used—at ISR, 208 of the 235 papers that used theory and 21 (of the 269) papers that did not use theory made contributions of theory; at JAIS, 138 of the 156 papers that used theory and 2 (of the 193) papers that did not use theory made contributions of theory; and, at MISQ, 167 of the 222 papers that used theory and 7 (of the 247) papers that did not use theory made contributions of theory. Further, only 21 papers (<4%) across the three journals (4 in ISR, 5 in JAIS, and 12 in MISQ) contributed to theory<sup>6</sup>; perhaps an even more troubling observation is that only 79 papers (<11%) across the three journals contributed both to and of theory. In sum, less than 15% of the papers across al three journals made a contribution to theory. As noted earlier, the application of TCE in IS research, with hardly any contribution to the core theory, reinforces this point (Jiang et al. 2022). Finally, a qualitative review of the statements of contributions in the Online Appendix, Tables A1-1, A2-1, and A3-1, suggest that hardly any of the papers made a contribution to reference discipline theory—an especially disturbing finding because it fails to promote IS as a contributing disciple but makes IS more of an applied, dependent discipline that uses theories from other disciplines. Coding other journals (e.g., Senior Scholars’ List of Premier Journals) and time windows is necessary to further substantiate that the issue is widely prevalent; nonetheless, given that the review here covers significant ground (journals, time window), there is strong evidence for us to begin acting on the observations. Additionally, although the coding in this paper was done by two scholars (one nonauthor) and is based on a deep study of the text in the papers, others may code contributions differently, thus suggesting that conclusions I draw should be considered in a qualitative sense rather than definitively in a quantitative sense. Coding various time windows and journals could, in fact, be a useful exercise in doctoral courses as a way of understanding the types of contributions of IS papers.

When a paper makes only a contribution of theory but not a contribution to theory—although the problem focused investigation is important—an important oppor tunity, wherein the work does not speak to more abstract problems, that is, the opportunity to contribute to the archetypal problem and broader theory, is lost. Although such investigations result in vital knowledge about new phenomena, the approach has left us with fragmented knowledge on a variety of topics, with weak anchors to prior literature and weak connections to more abstract ideas underlying the phenomenon and/or theories, and lost opportunities to continue to build knowledge in the long term. To some extent, the importance of the contri bution to theory can be highlighted by comparing citation impact of papers published in 2018 in ISR, JAIS, and MISQ, which allows for citations to have accumulated over approximately 6+ years and comparing papers that made contributions to theory versus papers that made the more typical contributions of theory. These are shown in the Online Appendix, Tables A3-1, A3-2, and A3-3. The papers making a contribution to theory are a small sample but do boast a fairly large citation count. From 2018 to 2021, MISQ had the most number of papers making a contribution to theory (Table 1). Only four of the ISR papers in 2018 made a contribution to theory (including one that made both contributions to and of theory), and the most-cited papers in that year were on current topics, which stands to reason in the short to medium term. Similarly, with only four papers making a contribution to theory, JAIS shows a similar trend, with the most cited JAIS paper being the one on a blockchain research agenda. One of the four papers in ISR and JAIS making a contribution to theory was above the mean citation count. With 14 papers in 2018 making a contribution to theory, MISQ showed a much more pronounced indication of the importance of the contribution to theory in terms of citation impact, with five of the seven most-cited papers in MISQ being papers that made contributions to theory. ## 4. How Should We Proceed?: A Guide to Contextualization

In this section, I present guidance to achieve contextualization tied to four general research practices—(1) defining the problem; (2) anchoring to prior research; (3) seeking novelty; and (4) reporting practices—with an eye toward making substantial scientific contributions that include both contributions to and of theory. Table 2 and Figure 1 provide a synthesis of the ideas presented in this section. It should be noted that investigations that use or apply a theory in various ways can make a contribution of theory to the understanding of the phenomenon, and thus, most of the suggestions to make a contribution to theory can be accompanied by a contribution of theory, as noted in Table 2.

## 4.1. Step 1: Defining the Problem—Contextualize the Problem

The first step pertains to understanding the problem and the context of the problem, its importance, and how it relates to prior problems, and what its defining characteristics are.

i. Explaining the context and importance of the contextual problem: Establishing the importance of the problem is important in any paper. However, in unique contexts that are understudied, this takes on even greater significance because a thick description (Rousseau and Fried 2001) is necessary to educate the reader and the field—and it will also help readers understand how the context and the problem relate to an archetypal problem. The onus of providing such an explanation falls on the authors. As Whetten (2009) notes, all problems and by association contexts are interesting, but only those that are important are worthy of specific scientific inquiry that goes beyond curiosity. How important and widely prevalent is a particular phenomenon and associated context? The answer to this will also be affected by the extent to which the solution will matter and the context to which it will matter. Three examples of contexts are (a) intelligent manufacturing in China; (b) information technology (IT)-driven supply chain management in Denmark; and (c) IT offshoring in India. The importance of these contexts must be established, qualitatively and/or quantitatively. The authors may use an appendix or provide additional materials for review, especially if the novelty or uniqueness of the context requires significant additional explanation to help a reviewer/reader fully understand the unique characteristics of the context.

ii. Identifying and connecting to problem characteristics: What are the characteristics of the problem? Connect it to an archetypal problem (Weber 2003, Rai 2017a) and/or taxonomies. As researchers encounter new phenomena, it is important not only to think about the problem in its most concrete form, but also to conceptualize its characteristics in its most abstract form. The lowest level of abstraction belongs not only in the method section, but also in the problem definition and theory development to make contributions of theory; if a context is only pertinent in the method section, considerations of generalizability become pertinent. Importantly, context then becomes irrelevant to the theory development. Contributions to theory will need an understanding of the phenomenon in more abstract terms beyond a purely contextual specification. To achieve the goals of understanding the phenomenon at the highest level of abstraction, several interrelated questions require answers and inclusion in the paper, especially in the introduction and discussion:

a. How can the particular phenomenon being studied be represented in terms of technology, organization, people, culture, and other interesting facets/ aspects (characteristics) typically studied in prior relevant literature? A set of characteristics to represent the phenomenon is vital to represent a problem at various levels of abstraction.

b. Related to the above, what aspects of the particular phenomenon can be used to speak to knowledge that will transfer to future work and how does it relate to phenomena from the past? In other words, the really challenging question being asked here is what is generalizable?

c. What are the related phenomena studied in prior research?

Being able to answer these questions effectively is crucial. Returning to the earlier noted contexts of intelligent manufacturing in China, IT-driven supply chain management in Denmark and IT offshoring in India, a useful way to reflect on building cumulative knowledge around, say, vendor relationships will be a careful integration and/or a pairwise and overall comparisons among intelligent manufacturing, IT-driven supply chain management and IT offshoring, China versus Denmark versus India; and together, intelligent manufacturing in China versus IT-driven supply chain management in Denmark versus IT offshoring in India. There must be a deliberate effort to engage in the context. Made especially popular by Van de Ven (2007), researchers actively engaging in the context can significantly improve the richness and extent of contextualization. Given ou interest in applied problems and being motivated by practice for problems (Hirschheim 2019), researcher engagement in the context and understanding of the same is crucial to develop good theory and make significant contributions, especially contributions of theory. The have data–will travel<sup>7</sup> approach, especially with large data sets, and little understanding of the phenomenon will fall well short of delivering a contribution that is not simply a function of the large sample and/or chance (Rai 2017a). Perhaps counter to the notion of turning to archival data sets, consistent with the suggestion of Johns (2006) to collect qualitative data to aid in contextualization, is an approach that includes some upfront qualitative work that goes beyond mere engagement and understanding to somewhat more rigorous data collection (Zhang 2017) that could aid not only in a richer understanding of the phenomenon but also in subsequent theory development. The increasingly popular call for mixed-methods research in various fields provides a way to gaining an understanding related to contexts (for a discussion of the seven mixed-methods research purposes, see Venkatesh et al. (2023)). All these approaches will provide richness in the explanation of a phenomenon and help make valuable contributions of theory.

In order to make contributions to theory, however, theorizing must move to higher levels of abstraction. How a focal phenomenon can be effectively compared and related to other phenomena will drive how well knowledge can be related from one of these phenomena to the other—and more broadly, from a focal phenomenon to other phenomena, for instance, in different countries. At the broadest level, there must be a deliberate effort to think about the similarities and differences with old/known problems. In one of only four papers published (2018–2021) in ISR that made a contribution to theory, Wang et al. (2018) present a good discussion of how their work on copycat mobile app development relates to information goods in general—and importantly, how their work differs. This thus allows future research to understand their context, their contextual contribution, and creates potential ties to the broader problem. A narrow formulation loses such an opportunity—insights into a new phenomenon may lose out benefits to the broader problem, especially if it is a one-off problem (Rai 2017b). Various approaches to articulate more abstract problems are discussed in Alvesson and Ka¨rreman (2007), Alvesson and Sandberg (2011), and Johns (2006). This could also be accomplished by actively anchoring to taxonomies (more on this later).

In sum, contextualization of the problem requires a rich description of the context that will allow the reader to understand the unique characteristics of the problem and, if possible, a representation in terms of general characteristics tied to archetypal problems. This will then set up the anchor for the relevant literature (step 2) and subsequent novelty (step 3).

## 4.2. Step 2: Anchoring to Prior Research

An important step in all research is anchoring to relevant prior research that is tied to the phenomenon and relevant theories. However, in articulating how this step should be approached, there are important differences when it comes to effective contextualization, especially given the limited contributions to theory made in prior IS research. There are two ways this can be accomplished with an eye toward contribution to theory.

i. Identifying relevant prior research: Too often, contextualization efforts are quick to dismiss prior research as irrelevant. Although not all aspects of prior research (theory/theories) will be relevant, it is important to identify prior research and, in fact, also seek to delineate what aspects of prior research may not be relevant and why. This will provide important boundary conditions of the applicability of existing knowledge to the context. In sum, for the new phenomenon, what is the relevant literature base for the archetypal problem? Ask questions like the following. What aspects of prior literature are relevant [and not relevant]? What theories have been used to study the archetypal problem? How can there be a substantial addition to the prior literature (theory/theories) in the reference discipline?

ii. Engaging relevant prior research: Even in cases where prior research is identified, researchers need to engage prior research and relate it to the problem at a deeper level by casting the problem and context/phenomenon in its archetypal form, as noted in step 1 earlier. This will be expounded in greater detail in step 3 (novelty) but a key element here is how the theory (theories) can be contextualized; what additions/changes can be made to the theory; what additions/changes can be made to the theory that speak to the broader archetypal problem and/or specific characteristics of the abstract phenomenon that will not only solve the specific problem, but also enrich the theory? Jiang et al. (2022) provide some specific suggestions on borrowing theories and specifically relating back to broader, more generic ideas that can aid in making contributions to theory.

Another approach to achieve abstraction is to anchor a new phenomenon to known taxonomies/typologies. Taxonomies could be crucial in guiding cumulative knowledge creation (DeSanctis and Gallupe 1987, Yoo et al. 2010) and shaping streams of research. For instance, DeSanctis and Gallupe (1987), in motivating their seminal work on taxonomies for group decision support systems (GDSS), criticized the fragmented research on GDSS that treated systems interchangeably. They further identified three levels of systems and mapped various manual and technology-based systems to their taxonomy. They articulated, in theory, various features of technology-based systems and when they would be suitable in terms of decision situations in organizations. Interestingly, they noted that the technology did not exist for level 3 systems (the highest level), and thus they speculated on how features may unfold and how they could be examined. In addition to providing a way to reconcile prior research, they provided an effective way to move forward. Following the publication of their paper, researchers in this domain could anchor to their taxonomy and identify the level of the GDSS that they were studying and delve further into the features and conditions of decision making not only to validate theoretical mechanisms proposed by DeSanctis and Gallupe, but also to investigate new concepts and ideas to move the stream forward. Leveraging the taxonomy, similarities and differences in the drivers and consequences of patterns of use of different levels of GDSS were studied. Later, with level 3 GDSS becoming a reality, the course of investigations continued to be influenced by this taxonomy. Eventually, the features that were actually implemented in level 3 GDSS were indeed different from what was predicted in their work but that only served to extend the taxonomy fur ther. The body of work on virtual teams, which came later, was also influenced by their taxonomy. Absent such a taxonomy, rich cumulative knowledge about GDSS would not have been built as effectively as it has been. In contrast, it could be argued that a similarly effective taxonomy does not exist for enterprise systems (ES) and different researchers treat ES interchangeably and that has resulted in a somewhat fragmented and inconsistent findings related to the impacts of ES.

In sum, in situations when context is important, be it driven by different types of technologies or phenomena, taxonomies provide the anchor for the development of cumulative knowledge, specifically by contexts being represented in terms of a set of characteristics. A recent typology for cyberdeviance (Venkatraman et al. 2018) can serve as an anchor for the vast and rapidly accumulating research on different deviant behaviors online. I argue that doing so and rooting exceptional empirical works on new phenomena, such as Chan and Ghose (2014), will create a cumulative tradition wherein knowledge from say Chan and Ghose (2014) about intermediaries and harmful societal outcomes can be leveraged systematically as a basis to study the role of intermediaries in society and the role of ICTs in societal outcomes in general. Over the past decade, the popular press has reported numerous stories of cyberdeviant behaviors on various social media platforms that lead to different negative outcomes including victims committing suicide. It would be valuable to examine how our knowledge about intermediaries/tools to facilitate interactions among people are relevant across these diverse platforms and what aspects of various platforms and phenomena are unique. This will lead to both contextualized and generalizable knowledge. For this, using a taxonomy or typology, such as the one in Venkatraman et al., to organize the types of problems, technologies, characteristics of both, and outcomes will be useful.<sup>8</sup>

Overall, we must reflect on new phenomena so that we pay attention to characteristics underlying these phenomena. Further, prior theory, taxonomies, and/or typologies will help us to understand these phenomena in terms of their similarities and differences; over time, they will help us build a cumulative body of knowledge connected to related phenomena. A thick description of the context and engagement with the context throughout the research process are important to ensure that the right literature base is leveraged to make a contribution of theory. Importantly, going beyond the context will create an opportunity to build cumulative knowledge tied to archetypal problems and make contributions to theory by representing contexts in terms of a core set of characteristics.

## 4.3. Step 3: Seeking Novelty

One of the frequent complaints about theory—that is, the case against theory (Hambrick 2007, Dennis 2019, Hirschheim 2019)—is that novelty is lost because of an anchor to theory. Additionally, those criticizing the need for theory sometimes point to rich data sets available today, which are typically collected unconstrained by theoretical underpinnings and sometimes have thousands of variables that could produce insights into phenomena. Navigating these issues and creating novel contributions to theory while studying phenomena in specific contexts is critical. I suggest a few interrelated steps to achieve this.

i. Identifying new characteristics: Although existing characteristics that help define phenomena in terms of technology, people, processes, and various other attributes are important, as new phenomena emerge, new characteristics are necessary to define them and understand them. Such characteristics will not only help us understand phenomena, but also fundamentally expand our ability to understand new phenomena. For example, mobile devices introduced location as a critical characteristic to service offerings. In new contexts, outside of those typically studied (i.e., WEIRD settings), identification of new characteristics becomes critical to understand new phenomena. In one of the earliest calls for contextualized research, Rousseau and Fried (2001) noted internationalization of phenomena as a key reason for paying attention to context in research.

ii. Combining prior knowledge in novel ways: One way to introduce novelty, keeping in mind what Uzzi et al. (2013) found, that is, combination of existing knowledge, which features both conventionality and atypicality, led to the highest citation impact, is knowledge recombination that clearly requires, as noted in step 2, anchoring to prior research. Given this, it is not surprising that Nobel prize–winning works have at their heart knowledge recombination that pushes the envelope— Kahneman’s Nobel prize in 2002 was for bringing behavioral theory insights into economics, and Williamson’s Nobel prize in 2009 was for an integration of economics and organizational theory among other disciplines. The suggestions of Johns (2006) provide useful ways about what context can do to previously established relationships: reverse causal direction, change signs, and/or introduce curvilinearity. Richer ways of introducing novelty are by leveraging context while also integrating it with broader/general issues. An excellent example of this is the second most-cited paper from the 2018 MISQ list: Karahanna et al. (2018) present a needsaffordances-features perspective and make a valuable contribution to theory. They identify five psychologica needs and discuss how affordances of social media applications will fill these needs. They then connect to the broader research on human needs that can, in the future, be expanded, with the evolution of technology, as new needs get discovered. They also connect with research on technology affordances, features of technology, and social media—all topics within IS where the research will continue to grow. Together, they present several points of connection to prior reference discipline theory and potential contributions to needs theory from which future researchers could contribute to theory within IS (e.g., social media) and outside IS (e.g., human needs).

iii. Specifying and challenging assumptions (Alvesson and Sandberg 2011, see also Leidner 2016): Assumptions underlying our work (theories) are frequently overlooked. As the world is transformed by technology, widely held assumptions about life and work are continually challenged—these in turn are bound to challenge assumptions underlying our previous research (theories). It is important, as new phenomena unfold, to ask assumption challenging questions that can, in addition to generating insights about phenomena, build and extend theories that are in keeping with the changes in the world. Of course, as contexts expand outside of the typically studied WEIRD contexts, challenging assumptions will be necessary. Specifically, the key ideas are as follows.

a. Specifying assumptions: As new phenomena emerge, they can serve to test assumptions that are widely held (Alvesson and Sandberg 2011). Five broad sets of assumptions, namely in-house, root metaphor, paradigm, ideology, and field, were identified by Alvesson and Sandberg (2011).<sup>9</sup> Drawing on Alvesson and Sandberg, Schuetz and Venkatesh (2020), with a focus on artificial intelligence (AI) systems, called for assumptions in some streams of IS research to be better articulated. Often, assumptions underlying the streams of research (theories) are unspecified. As contexts of technologies change and phenomena change, specifying assumptions is a key first step to being able to test them.

b. Challenging assumptions: Challenging assumptions and formulating new assumptions about the world is an important way to go past the typical gap-spotting approach that permeates existing work including those published in our leading journals. Typical contextualization approaches suffer from this problem—that is, a variable suited to the context is added to an existing model (Jiang et al. 2022). Despite the call of Alvesson and Sandberg (2011) for such a shift in our focus, scholars in management and other disciplines, including IS, where management is a key reference discipline, have not budged significantly in their scholarly behaviors. If we are to create useful theories that anchor effectively to prior research but go beyond in meaningful ways, especially in new contexts, challenging widely held assumptions will be important. Building on the previous point of specifying assumptions, Schuetz and Venkatesh (2020) also called for testing assumptions, be they ones that have just been specified or widely held, in a variety of topic areas. Once assumptions of theories are specified, they could be tested in new contexts. For example, the widely held assumption of the linearity of models was challenged and debunked in different fields, both within IS (Venkatesh and Goyal 2010) and outside IS (Edwards 2002, Brown et al. 2008).

iv. Challenging the validity of theories in a new context (Johns 2006, 2017) as a basis for new theory development: The first step to challenge the validity of existing theories is to test them in a new context versus a context where it was originally developed and/or validated/ tested. One way this can be accomplished is a comparison using a moderator variable—see the discussion of omnibus and discrete contexts (Johns 2006). This may be feasible only if comparing different contexts can be done in the same study; alternatively, implications can be drawn about how a phenomenon plays out in different contexts—for example, a previously studied context versus the new context of interest being studied. As existing theories serve as an effective starting point for new investigations, if a theory is tested only in a new context, observing the breakdown of a theory, a related mystery formulation, and solution of the mystery (Alvesson and Ka¨rreman 2007) present excellent opportunities for the creation of contextualized knowledge that, when done effectively, can make both contributions of and to theory. Indeed, many related things can happen in the pursuit of challenging the validity of theories. Some constructs may have some dimensions that become moot (Whetten 2009). Some constructs may become completely meaningless or may have a very different conceptualization/meaning in a new context (Rousseau and Fried 2001). Compeau et al. (2022) present a discussion of how constructs may be modified to suit different contexts, and , although their particular emphasis is context as time and associated obsolescence, their discussion is relevant to societa changes or contextual differences. There may also be significant implications for base rate and range for existing constructs in new contexts (Rousseau and Fried 2001, Johns 2006).

The more unique or different the new context or phenomenon, relative to what is known, the more significant the breakdown that may occur. For example, a concept/construct that means very different things in different parts of the world is the empowerment of women such that it could be associated with growing the number of women in STEM or voting rights or being allowed to drive. Going a step further, outside of WEIRD settings, the creation of new knowledge can focus on indigenous theory by being sensitive to the local language, beliefs, and associated concepts. Such a breakdown of theories in new contexts provides the opportunity for the creation of new theories.

The solution and approach to contextualization will rely also on developing ideas, be it concepts or constructs or even mechanisms or entirely new theories that are germane to the context being studied. This is especially important the newer and more unique the context and associated phenomenon. For an example related to Ubuntu, a value system in South Africa, as a basis for digital entrepreneurship, see Abubakre et al. (2021). In some respects, Negoita et al. (2018) in their presentation of collective IS use depart from traditional views of IS use due to the evolution of the nature of the IS from primarily being standalone to enterprise and distributed applications, thus suggesting that the very nature of individual IS use theories break down in light of such collective IS use. Overall, challenging the valid ity of theories and the creation of new theories may even be accompanied by a paradigm change in topic areas or scientific disciplines (Kuhn 1962).

v. Developing native IS theories: As discussed earlier, the definition of native IS theory is itself ambiguous. However, developing native IS theories, especially if its definition is theories about IS phenomena, is important. Jiang et al. (2022) offer useful guidance on this issue, especially to think about more generic ideas and relating to IS phenomena when theories may not offer a particular fit. My recommendation ties to how such native IS theories may be developed to make contributions, or at least have strong ties, to reference discipline theory. The key to achieving this goal is to develop theories about IS phenomena such that they are rich enough and broad/general to be applicable outside the IS field by being relevant to a range of phenomena outside the IS field and/or integrate reference discipline theory about more abstract concepts and help expand the concepts (and/or constructs) and mechanisms in the reference discipline theory. Although following a traditional contextualization script, perhaps because of its broad applicability, TAM and its extensions (descendants) offer exemplars of IS theories (for a meta-analysis, see Blut et al. (2022)) that are widely used outside IS. Karahanna et al. (2018), through a strong integration with needs theories, offer a potential to contribute to the reference discipline research and theory of needs. Another interesting recent example is the integrated model of security compliance (Moody et al. 2018) that combines 11 different reference discipline theories and does so following a typical contextualization script, but given that the phenomenon of security compliance is relevant outside IS, it is possible that, like TAM, their work will have an impact on other fields, although it may not have an impact on any of the underlying reference discipline theories, which one could argue is the case with TAM as well despite its strong cross-disciplinary impact.

vi. Developing new taxonomies and typologies: Strongly connected with the need to anchor to taxonomies, identified earlier in step 2, is the need for new taxonomies. Like the definition of new characteristics and the need for new theories, although far less common, bigger contributions and future research agendas can be driven by the development of new taxonomies and typologies— and anchoring phenomena to such newly developed taxonomies or typologies. Illustrations of the more recently developed taxonomies and typologies discussed earlier (digital innovation, cyberdeviance) represent efforts in contextualization that can aid in the growth of IS as a reference discipline. As researchers study IS phenomena, an effort should also be made to develop broad taxonomies and typologies that will be used by scholars not only in IS, but also in other disciplines.

Ultimately, although contributions of theory will typically follow when theories are applied to study new phenomena, in order to make effective contributions to theory from contextualization, the new theory must extend beyond the context being studied, at least to other similar, related contexts or in some ways to the broader theory. Identifying boundary conditions of prior theory in the context of a new phenomenon could aid in making a contribution to theory, especially if the phenomenon is effectively represented at higher levels of abstraction in terms of existing and/or new characteristics. In addition, more broadly, challenging assumptions and developing new theories and/or taxonomies/typologies could give rise to contributions to theory.

In sum, I highlight that, as suggested in prior works, although contextualization is itself important to pursue, understanding phenomena based on broad, abstract characteristics that represent them in ways that consider the archetypal problems (Weber 2003, Rai 2017a) and/or tied to a taxonomy will allow us to see similarities and differences across different phenomena in terms of a collection of characteristics rather than see each new, emerging phenomenon as novel and needing a new theory or force-fitting existing theory to the new phenomenon. Such a focus on contextualized understanding of phenomena with abstraction allows for a more effective feedback loop from the phenomenon to the theory being leveraged or the creation of new theory. However, in addition to being studied by relating it to prior knowledge based on similarities with other phenomena, incorporating and considering the new phenomenon’s unique elements by adding new characteristics to define a phenomenon, developing new theories and integrating with reference discipline theories, and developing new taxonomies/typologies will create opportunities for new knowledge creation that in turn may later be relevant to other new phenomena and making contributions to theory, thus going beyond the typical contributions of theory to the understanding of the phenomenon.

## 4.4. Step 4: Reporting Practices

One key problem in the past has been the lack of good reporting about the context of investigation. Given that, in reality, all theory can only explain phenomena in context (in situ), good reporting is essential (Whetten 2009); given that findings are also only applicable in situ, regardless of how the theory may have been conceived, knowing the context of the empirical study will allow researchers to understand the potential boundary conditions of the work. Disclosure about the context in which research is conducted is thus essential to understand the considerations that may have driven the results. Johns (2006, 2017) and Whetten (2009), among others, offer insights into the importance of full disclosure about the context. Although there is some good counsel on this, one concern, which is noted both by Feynmann (1999) and Greenwald et al. (1986), is that hiding contextual information has become practically important to avoid criticisms that can often hinder publication. The move toward open access to data could help if context data are included, but sometimes this may still be constrained by legal requirements of nondisclosure agreements (NDAs) and/or an institutional review board (IRB) requirements to protect confidentiality of data. Nonetheless, aspects related to the context that should be considered for disclosure include but are not limited to the following.

i. Location: Where were the data collected? There is a strong bias in favor of North America, western Europe, and some Asian countries, resulting in others choosing to not disclose location data.

ii. Sample characteristics: What were the characteristics of the organization(s) and/or the individuals?

iii. Time of data collection: When were the data collected? This particular aspect is one that comes under scrutiny in IS journals, as if data that are old are somehow not useful or should not be published—and often this leads to hiding such information.

iv. Assumptions: Contextual assumptions are about what is being observed and conceptual assumptions are about the observer’s point of view (Whetten 2009). Going a step further, Whetten (2009, p. 39) notes that “[i]t is the responsibility of those using a particular theory to systematically compare contextual features that distinguish the proposed research setting from the setting which gave rise to the theory.”

Aside from withholding for valid reasons (e.g., NDA, IRB) or practical reasons (e.g., hindering publication), there are other problems plaguing good reporting including careless oversight. I implore academic journals and organizations to provide guidelines that mandate some level of context disclosure at the time of publication, including making such contextual information to be part of journal archives, which will aid deep analyses of the contextual boundary conditions of published papers and the theories contained therein.

## 5. Guidelines for Editors and Reviewers

Building on the framework of making a contribution (Figure 1), I offer guidelines for editors and reviewers. As with all guidelines, these are not meant to be an imperative or prescriptive but rather about how we might avoid common pitfalls and give us a way to focus on important issues related to contextualization to promote good science. Here, it refers to investigating new phenomena and generating substantial scientific contributions that include both contributions of and to theory.

Our typical research process, including the review process, is at least a significant part of the problem when it comes to effective contextualization. As discussed earlier, most papers published in ISR, JAIS, and MISQ from 2018 to 2021 make contributions of theory. To go beyond this will require a different approach that has greater openness such that, in the process of investigating new phenomena, IS researchers are encouraged to contribute to theory. I reiterate the point that research on new phenomena that eschews theory will propagate more of what we have already witnessed in that they will not produce cumulative knowledge, have limited contributions to theory, and hinder the emergence of IS as a strong reference discipline. Just as likely, research on new phenomena that use or apply theory with or without minor extensions may continue to make strong contributions of theory and even generate contextualized, midrange or native IS theories, thus meeting the requirements for publication at leading IS journals but failing to set up IS as a reference discipline. Table 3 draws on the earlier discussion of the four steps tied to defining the problem, anchoring to prior research, seeking novelty, and reporting practices—and reinforces what reviewers and editors should specifically seek. Table 3 also lays out important issues for reviewers and editors to avoid criticizing. For instance, there are two critical things that should not be criticized. First, authors should not be criticized and prevented from publishing papers because they challenge the validity of prior theories and find them to be invalid in new contexts; and second, authors should not be implicitly encour aged to hide contextual information. The current environment favors hiding disconfirming results and contextual information because these lead to rejections. There should be greater openness in the review process to theorizing and tentative conclusions—as opposed to a confirmation bias. Our forays into exciting new phenomena—as we tie to broader characteristics, seek novelty, and make contributions to theory—will be fraught with missteps. Such missteps are crucial to the creation of knowledge in the long term, as we challenge prior knowledge/theory, and this will be done more effectively if other researchers can learn from these missteps, thus suggesting products of theorizing deserve a home (Weick 1995). In this regard, paper archetypes and templates represent a key hurdle—and greater openness to how a paper with disconfirming findings is structured will be important to allow for the creation of knowledge that can speak to specific contexts and broader theories. Finally, I hope editors and reviewers call for an explicit discussion by authors of the contribu tions of and to theory that their paper makes.

## 6. Discussion

A focus on contextualization has been of interest to researchers in IS and other social sciences. This commentary complements prior work that has called for contextualization and provides guidance on how researchers can engage in effective contextualization by focusing on practices within four common areas of research practice, namely defining the problem, anchoring to prior research, seeking novelty, and reporting practices. One of the key outcomes of the suggestions proposed here is to make more contributions to theory, especially reference discipline theory. It is my hope that authors will be more explicit about potential implications their papers have for reference disciplines and reference discipline theories from which they draw. In other words, authors should explicitly state their contributions to theory or potential contributions to theory. I expect following these suggestions will also help IS to grow as a reference discipline. One point that should be underscored is that this commentary, although issuing a strong call for contributions to theory, notes that contributions of theory and empirical testing of theories are valuable; in fact, the approaches suggested here should enable making both types of contributions, as noted in Figure 1 and Table 2.

One of the undercurrents of this commentary that requires further discussion is how to achieve greater success in the study of phenomena around the world. Such challenges are being tackled in some ways, such as editorial board compositions, position statements and activities in various fields, organizations, and journals, to achieve diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI) (e.g., INFORMS, the parent institution of Information Systems Research, and MISQ have DEI statements). However, such DEI efforts are inadequate. The scathing criticism of Buchanan et al. (2021) stems from major issues that need to be systemically addressed. Certainly, the chatter at conferences and experiences of scholars from underrepresented areas are that journals are not welcoming of phenomena and contexts that are germane to such underrepresented areas, as illustrated in the editorial of Davison (2021). Papers I have coauthored based on research in a bank in India stand as a testament to how starkly different such contexts can be and how prior theories can be completely invalid, even if studying knowledge workers in the new context (Venkatesh et al. 2010, 2016). Although this commentary made some connections to indigenous theorizing insofar as new contexts may call for such theorizing, guidelines for indigenous theorizing are essential not only to help researchers conduct such work, but also to build a more inclusive discipline. In fact, various aspects of DEI, including gender and race, are likely to need attention not only in terms of research into those specific contexts, but also in terms of guidelines on the pursuit of such research because each type of context is unique and needs to careful consideration about its characteristics and uniqueness.

Is this question unique to IS or do all disciplines face it? Certainly, borrowing theories is not unique to the IS field. In fact, all disciplines turn to other disciplines for concepts, constructs, theories, or even just inspiration. However, at the heart of the issue is whether, beyond an application of the ideas, the so-drawn ideas are further extended in the borrowing discipline. Perhaps when the borrowing discipline in turn serves as a strong reference discipline, as is the case with organizational behavior or marketing, scholars in that discipline are less concerned about contributions of versus to theory. Although a systematic study of other disciplines will be necessary to understand this issue in those disciplines, the relevance of the type of contributions may be more pertinent in IS because IS as a field does not have a major impact on other fields (i.e., being a reference discipline). It is my hope that the ideas for contextualization in this commentary can help scholars in this regard and that authors will explicitly state their contributions of and to theory.

<sup>9</sup> In-house assumptions refer to a set of ideas held by a theoretical school about a specific subject matter. An example of in-house assumptions could be trait theory conceptualizing leadership as a set of attributes such as knowledge, skills, and personal traits of a leader (Yukl 2006). One can challenge in-house assumptions that leadership is defined more by the social context than by leaders traits. Root metaphor assumptions are associated with broader images of a particular subject matter (Morgan 1980, 1997). An example could be to view organizations as cultures in terms of views and beliefs held by its members. Martin (2002) challenged the conceptu alization of organization as cultures because members may differ in consensus and instead proposed differentiation to be considered as elements of culture. Paradigm assumptions refer to the ontological, epistemological, and methodological assumptions that underlie a specific literature (Kuhn 1962). An example could be professional competence as consisting of two entities: attributes of a worker and set of work activities. Sandberg (2000) challenged this assumption using an interpretive approach and found that work and person are tied together and have an inseparable relationship. Ideology assumptions include various political, moral, and gender assump tions held about the subject matter. Sievers (1986) challenged ideology assumptions underlying theories of motivation. For example, one way to challenge such an assumption is not to investigate how people are motivated to work in organizations but to ask why people need to be motivated. Field assumptions relate to the specific subject matter that are shared by several different schools of thought within a paradigm and sometimes even across paradigms and disciplines. Simon (1947) challenged the assumption that human beings are rational decision makers, arguing instead that their decisions are driven by the principle of bounded rationality.
