# Is Fitness Technology-Facilitated Social Comparison the Thief of Well-Being? The Mediating Role of Social Comparison on the Relationships Between Passion and Performance Self-Esteem

Abstract. Passionate exercisers are those who have integrated their cherished activity into their identity, meaning that their activity defines them (e.g., they consider themselves to be a “runner” or “adventure racer”). There are two types of passion, namely harmonious and obsessive, and research shows that those holding an obsessive passion are prone to negative outcomes. Specifically, the obsessively passionate are susceptible to comparative performance indicators and may rely more heavily on their cherished activity to derive self-esteem. Fitness technologies include social features that provide exercisers with information that may be used to compare their performance against others. Such information may be especially attractive to passionate exercisers and have ramifications for their self esteem when it is interpreted as evidence of success or failure. Drawing on the dualistic model of passion, social comparison theory, and the fitness technology literature, we develop and test a model that examines how passion is associated with performance selfesteem, and how exercisers’ interpretations of fitness technology-facilitated social compari son information mediate these relationships. We find that fitness technology users who have high levels of obsessive passion focus on being outperformed, and this may harm their performance self-esteem. Conversely, those who have high levels of harmonious pas sion focus on outperforming others, and this may benefit their performance self-esteem. Overall, our results indicate that fitness technology users may want to be thoughtful about the others they compare with via their fitness technologies because evidence of being outperformed may be psychologically harmful to some.

## 1. Introduction

Fitness technologies, which typically consist of wearable devices combined with fitness apps that are networked to them, are exceedingly popular tools among today’s exercisers. Nearly half of US adults ages 18 to 50 report owning a wearable device, although only around 40% report using them (Laricchia 2024, Shandhi et al. 2024). Reports state that in 2024 the global market for fitness apps was valued at \$12.1 billion and the number of downloads of health and fitness apps from the Apple and Google app stores reached a high of 3.6 billion, an increase of 6% from 2023 (Briskman 2025, Global Industry Analysts 2025). The top fitness apps include Apple Fitness, Fitbit, MyFitnessPal, and Strava, with MyFitnessPal and Strava being the top grossing (Ceci 2024, Curry 2024) (Apple Fitness and the Fitbit app are included with their devices). Strava, an app designed specifically for athletes, defined as people who take “fitness seriously,” found the leaderboard to be the “big hook” of their app, so much so that the app developed an issue with cheating despite the fact that there are no tangible rewards for being on the top of a Strava leaderboard (George 2020, p. 5). This suggests that not only are fitness technologies continuing to increase in popularity but that fitness technology-facilitated social comparisons may be key to the appeal of some apps, especially for serious exercisers.

The term serious exerciser is a loose concept but has been conceptualized in the academic literature through the notion of passion. Passion is defined as “a strong inclination toward an activity that people like, that they find important, and in which they invest time and energy”

(Vallerand et al. 2003, p. 756). When people are passionate about an activity, they regularly engage in it, pursue it over the long-term, and the activity itself is deemed important, enjoyable, and self-defining (Vallerand 2010). It is reasonable to suggest that passionate exercisers are a demographic of interest to fitness technology developers due to the likelihood that they regularly exercise over a lifetime. The dualistic model of passion (DMP) proposes that there are two types of passion, namely harmonious and obsessive, that can be held simultaneously although one will be dominant (Vallerand 2010).

Motivation theory explains that activities people do not find interesting can be internalized in different ways and the DMP extends this explanation by proposing that the same is true for activities that people like (Vallerand 2010). The two types of passion are thus distinguished by the way they are internalized. Specifically, obsessive passion results from controlled internalization, originating from intrapersonal or interpersonal pressure because contingencies such as social acceptance or selfesteem are attached to the activity’s performance (Vallerand et al. 2003). In contrast, harmonious passion results from autonomous internalization, which means that the activity has been freely accepted as important to a person with no contingencies attached to it (Vallerand et al. 2003). Research has generally found that harmonious passion results in adaptive outcomes and obsessive passion in maladaptive outcomes (Vallerand 2010). Therefore, serious exercisers whose predominant passion is obsessive may suffer in the face of failure (Vallerand 2010). This suggests that for some serious exercisers, fitness technology-facilitated social comparisons (i.e., social comparisons that can be made through the use of fitness technology features such as leaderboards and newsfeeds), especially those that suggest failure, may result in maladaptive outcomes.

One way that passionate exercisers can experience failure is by comparing themselves to others (i.e., social comparison) and finding themselves to be lacking. Social comparison is an affordance of fitness technologies, defined as the ability to evaluate one’s performance against others, that is achieved through features such as leaderboards (Rockmann and Gewald 2018, Rockmann and Maier 2019). Crucially, social comparison theory (SCT) proposes that who people compare themselves to and how those comparisons are interpreted matter (Helgeson and Mickelson 1995, Suls and Wheeler 2012). People can make lateral (others with similar performance), upward (others with better performance), or downward (others with worse performance) comparisons. The old adage is that “comparison is the thief of joy” (Summerville 2019, p. 1), and if this is the case, then comparisons made through fitness technologies to others who exercise more or perform better should make exercisers feel poorly about their own performance because it signals failure. One potential outcome of perceived failure is decreased performance self-esteem, which can be defined as the extent to which people “feel that their performance is worthy” (Heatherton and Polivy 1991, p. 907). Contingent self-esteem is thought to drive “people to obsess about the implications of negative events for selfworth, making them more vulnerable to depression and reduced self-concept clarity” (Neff 2011, p. 3). It is thus not ideal to reduce people’s performance self-esteem, and it is unlikely that this is an intended outcome of fitness technologies.

People whose passion is linked to contingencies are likely to be more vulnerable to negative outcomes in the face of failure, and fitness technologies facilitate the delivery of such failure (or success) information to those whose self-esteem may be contingent on comparative performance. Although theoretically, the tendency of obsessive passion to result in maladaptive outcomes suggests that performance self-esteem should suffer when presented with evidence of failure, these relationships have not been widely studied and have not been studied taking into consideration the insertion of fitness technologies which have novel characteristics in terms of delivery of failure (success) information. Specifically, once a user adds friends to their fitness app, or uses an app like Strava that emphasizes data such as the average completion times on routes, comparisons with others are difficult to avoid short of removing friends or discontinuing use. Additionally, the comparison information is available at any time and there are few limits on the number of others one can compare themselves to. Prior to fitness technologies, people had to explicitly ask others for their performance times, attend a fitness class, run a race, etc. to determine their status among other exercisers. We suggest that the constant availability of fitness technology-facilitated social comparison information may have adverse effects on some exercisers, and specifically we seek to answer the question:

## Does the interpretation of fitness technology-facilitated social comparison information mediate the relationship between passion and performance self-esteem?

The answer to this question is key to understanding if the provision of social comparison information, which is the dominant function of the social features of fitness technologies (e.g., leaderboards, newsfeeds), is psychologically helpful or harmful.

Efforts have been made to understand the role of social comparison in fitness technology use with mixed results. Studies have found that the tendency of users to make social comparisons may both help and hinder their continued use (Li et al. 2018, Gupta et al. 2021). Social comparisons as a motivational affordance was found to positively influence net benefits, a construct including being more physically active, having increased motivation to exercise, and finding exercise more enjoyable (Rockmann and Maier 2019). However, such studies did not always distinguish upward and downward comparisons. Hydari et al. (2023) found that less physically active users benefit more from the adoption of leaderboards regardless of how they rank. However, another study found that having more higher performing friends on one’s fitness technologies decreased physical activity (Zhou et al. 2016), possibly suggesting that user characteristics may matter in how upward or downward comparisons are interpreted. Collectively, several other studies have found mixed results regarding whether upward or downward exercise comparisons generally lead to desirable or undesirable outcomes (Burke and Rains 2019, Kim 2022, Sun and Jiang 2022, Sun et al. 2022). The literature has thus not yet determined whether fitness technology-facilitated social comparison is beneficial, or for whom. It has also not determined whether upward or downward comparisons are preferrable. We extend this literature by studying a specific user characteristic, namely passion, that may result in exercisers benefiting from, or being harmed by, fitness technology-facilitated social comparison information. Additionally, we focus on the interpretation of the fitness technology-facilitated social comparison information as evidence of success or failure, rather than just whether comparisons are made. Moreover, we examine the impact of this information on performance self-esteem, and ultimately on well-being in our additional analysis, which provides insight into whether social comparison through fitness technologies may be detrimental to some exercisers.

We make several contributions to research and practice. Specifically, we provide two key insights that may reveal potential explanations for the mixed findings regarding social comparisons made through fitness technology features such as leaderboards. First, we show that exercisers’ characteristics (e.g., passion) may determine how the fitness technology-facilitated social comparison information is interpreted. Second, we show that the way fitness technology-facilitated social comparison information is interpreted matters to the psychological outcomes of its use. Few studies have examined how user characteristics influence the use of fitness technologies or the data they curate, or how such use may influence the outcomes users experience (e.g., James et al. 2019b, Chen et al. 2020). Our findings suggest that exercisers with a dominant obsessive passion may interpret fitness technology-facilitated social information as evidence of failure, damaging their performance self-esteem and well-being, whereas exercisers with a dominant harmonious passion may be able to avoid this fate.

Additionally, fitness technology studies often emphasize physical activity outcomes (e.g., step counts) (Brickwood et al. 2019, Ringeval et al. 2020, Li et al. 2021), with fewer studies focusing on psychological outcomes of fitness technology use (Jin et al. 2022). Although physical activity indicators (e.g., step counts) are important health outcomes, psychological well-being indicators should also play a role in a holistic view of health. Our findings demonstrate that one’s passion for their exercise activity may influence how they interpret fitness technology-facilitated social comparison information, and that this in turn may influence their performance self-esteem and well-being. By demonstrating that fitness technology-facilitated social comparison may indeed be the thief of joy for some exercisers, we add nuance to the fitness technology literature, indicating that not all uses of fitness technologies may be attractive to, or beneficial for, all users (e.g., Etkin 2016, James et al. 2019b, Hydari et al. 2023, Yang et al. 2023, Babar et al. 2024). We show that although fitness technologies can increase physical activity, the psychological consequences of their use may not always be positive.

In terms of implications for practice, our findings suggest the usage guidance provided to different types of exercisers may need to be different to maximize benefits and minimize harm. Users who are prone to negatively interpreting fitness technology-facilitated social comparison information may want to forego the use of the social features, be careful about the friends they add, or even avoid fitness apps that emphasize comparisons. The importance of how fitness technology-facilitated social comparison information is interpreted suggests that developers should design interfaces that encourage users to view comparison data as evidence of success rather than failure.

## 2. Theoretical Background

Below, we introduce the DMP followed by a brief introduction to SCT. We then provide an overview of the fitness technology literature.

## 2.1. Dualistic Model of Passion (DMP)

Vallerand et al. (2003, p. 757) state that “for an activity to represent a passion for people, it has to be significant in their lives, something that they like, and something at which they spend time on a regular basis.” Distinct from motivation, which refers to internal or external forces that prompt the performance of a behavior, passion requires the activity to be both liked and integrated into the identity of a person such that they define themselves by that activity (e.g., as “a runner”) (Vallerand 2010, 2012). Harking back to early philosophical writings on passion, Vallerand (2012) describes how something one loves can be good for them, such as enjoying training for a marathon, but can also sometimes be bad for them such as when a person continues to train even when they are injured. In both cases, the person loves the activity, but in the latter case the love is accompanied by a lack of reason that can end with deleterious outcomes. Seeking to explain this duality in outcomes from a cherished activity that motivation theory was unable to explain, the DMP proposes two types of passion, namely harmonious and obsessive, that are differentiated by the way the activity is internalized (i.e., integrated into a person’s identity) (Vallerand 2012). Moreover, “whether a passion will foster positive affect and healthy persistence depends on whether [the passion] is harmonious or obsessive” (Vallerand et al. 2003, p. 757).

Motivation theory, specifically self-determination theory (SDT), describes two types of internalization processes, autonomous and controlled (Deci and Ryan 2000). Drawing from SDT, the DMP proposes that harmonious passion results from autonomous internalization, whereas obsessive passion results from controlled internalization, of the activity into a person’s identity (Vallerand 2010). Autonomous internalization refers to freely accepting an activity as important without any attached contingencies, and such a “state of mind allows the person to fully partake in the passionate activity with an openness that is conducive to positive experiences” (Vallerand et al. 2003, p. 48). Conversely, controlled internalization “originates from intra- and/or interpersonal pressure typically because certain contingencies are attached to the activity such as feelings of social acceptance or self-esteem” (Vallerand et al. 2003, p. 47). People with a dominant obsessive passion may feel an uncontrollable urge to perform their cherished activity and ultimately the activity may come to control them resulting in negative outcomes (Vallerand et al. 2003). For example, harmonious passion is positively, and obsessive passion often negatively, associated with psychological well-being (Vallerand et al. 2007, Rousseau and Vallerand 2008, Vallerand et al. 2008). The DMP postulates that when a person has a passion for an activity, both forms of passion are present, one dominating the other (Vallerand 2010).

SDT also distinguishes between autonomous versus controlled orientations, which are dispositional and independent of a particular activity but suggest some people are more likely to be oriented toward interpreting environmental cues a particular way (Deci and Ryan 1985, Vallerand et al. 2006). Specifically, “more autonomous individuals are thought to perceive performance feedback as useful information rather than as a potentially ego-threatening indicator of self-worth,” whereas more controlled individuals “chronically perceive pressures from the environment and tend to react in an egodefensive manner” (Neighbors and Knee 2003, p. 531). This suggests that exercisers who have a dominant harmonious passion, characterized by autonomous internalization of the activity, may be able to interpret fitness technology-facilitated social comparison information indicating that others are performing better as useful to them (e.g., a benchmark). Conversely, people with obsessive passion for their activity rely on it to derive their sense of self-worth, which makes them more sensitive to performance cues (Mageau et al. 2011) and likely to experience negative emotions (e.g., shame) following activity performance (Vallerand et al. 2003). Specifically, people reporting high obsessive passion tend to experience fluctuations in their state self-esteem that covary with their activity performance (Mageau et al. 2011). Research on the DMP therefore suggests that people whose dominant passion is obsessive will be more likely than those whose dominant passion is harmonious to interpret fitness technology-facilitated social comparison information showing that they performed worse than others as evidence of failure and experience maladaptive outcomes such as decreased performance self-esteem and well-being.

## 2.2. Social Comparison Theory (SCT)

In the paper that introduced SCT, Festinger (1954) noted that, for a person to feel a need to compare against others, the activity being compared must be important to them. He discussed how a person may measure how long it takes them to run a given distance but that it is difficult to judge that performance unless there is an avail able benchmark (e.g., was the time faster or slower than others?). With respect to abilities, Festinger (1954) described a unidirectional drive upward that compels people into competition, and while he acknowledged that practice may result from the drive to improve, he also noted the challenges inherent to such improvement. The social features of fitness technologies provide constant access to benchmarks that allow people to compare. Many social features (e.g., leaderboards, newsfeeds, competitions) also encourage competition. It is reasonable to suggest that the inclusion of such features reflects a desire by fitness technology makers to encourage people to improve their abilities through continued activity engagement (i.e., practice) by stimulating comparison and competition.

The foundations of SCT established the innate need for people to compare their abilities to others (Festinger 1954), and subsequent research often sought to determine which others people compared themselves to and for what reasons (Helgeson and Mickelson 1995, Suls and Wheeler 2012). If someone wants to benchmark their performance, there are three referent groups: those performing similarly (lateral), those performing better (upward), and those performing worse (downward). The direction of comparison was quickly attached to the motives for comparison, specifically, the original think ing was that people would compare with similar others as a means of self-evaluation (i.e., to benchmark) (Suls and Wheeler 2012). However, with regard to abilities, Festinger (1954) suggested that a unidirectional drive upward may spur competition to best others, leading researchers to argue that people may make upward comparisons as a means of self-improvement (Helgeson and Mickelson 1995). Research then suggested self enhancement as a motive for downward comparisons; that is, that people can make themselves feel better by comparing to a worse off other (Wills 1981). Over time, it became clear that the reasons people compared with any of the three referent groups were more complex than the three original motives, and researchers searched for more nuanced explanations for whom people compared with and why (Buunk et al. 1990, Helgeson and Mickelson 1995, Suls et al. 2002, Suls and Wheeler 2012).

Essential to our study, Buunk et al. (1990, p.1242) found that “people may construe both upward and downward comparisons as either positive or negative.” These findings led to the identification-contrast model (ICM) that suggests people have a “a strong desire to develop positive self-esteem by assigning themselves symbolic prestige and status in their reference group,” and that the “reference group need not actually be present and may even be cognitively constructed” (Buunk and Ybema 1997, p. 369). This implies, according to the ICM, that individuals are likely to want to identify with those who are doing better rather than those doing worse, but they are likely to want to contrast themselves with those doing worse than those doing better. When people identify, they see the other person as a potential future state for themselves, versus when they contrast, they see the other person as a competitor and focus on their relative success or failure with respect to that person (Carmona et al. 2008). Specifically, when people contrast themselves with better others (upward) they see themselves as the losing party, a failure, whereas when people contrast themselves with worse others (downward) they seem themselves as the winning party, a success. Conversely, when people identify with better others, they see those achievements (e.g., success) as a possible future for themselves, versus when they identify with worse others (downward) they see failure as a future state for themselves.

Notably, Buunk and Ybema (1997, p. 371) state that “when information about the scores of others is obtained, most subjects may have a better opportunity to contrast themselves with these others, whereas vivid information about or contact with others in a similar situation may more often evoke identification with them.” Fitness technologies provide scores, often for people for whom other contextual information is not available. That is, an exerciser knows how many steps the other person took or their time on a particular route (i.e., scores), but they do not typically know if they are training for a marathon, are injured or sick, are having a good or bad day, etc. Even if they know the person offline, they may not have access to immediate contextual information. Therefore, we focus on the contrast dimensions of the ICM in our main model.<sup>1</sup>

## 2.3. Overview of the Fitness Technology Literature

Research on fitness technologies has focused on factors that influence three primary outcomes: physical activity, psychological well-being, and intentions to continue using fitness technologies (see Online Appendix A for a summary table of key illustrative studies). The first and largest stream of research on fitness technologies is con cerned with determining if the use of fitness technolo gies can increase physical activity, usually step counts (Jin et al. 2022). The long-upheld association being that if physical activity is increased, physical health will improve (Blair et al. 1992, Bravata et al. 2007). Meta analyses have confirmed that fitness technology use can indeed increase step counts and moderate and vigorous physical activity (Brickwood et al. 2019, Ringeval et al. 2020). Early studies in this research stream often pro vided participants with a pedometer or wearable to see if its presence would increase their physical activity (e.g., numbers of steps or minutes exercised) (e.g., van der Weegen et al. 2015, Etkin 2016). Notably, Etkin (2016) provided participants with pedometers and found that while their use increased the number of steps taken, the participants enjoyment of walking decreased, as did their subjective well-being, which was attributed to making a previously enjoyable activity seem like work by quantifying it. These findings suggest that although fitness technologies can increase physical activity, the psychological consequences of their use may not always be positive. To understand the impact o fitness technologies on overall health, it is thus necessary to conduct research on both physical and psychological outcomes of their use.

As research on fitness technologies matured, studies in this first stream began to examine how different uses of fitness technologies may affect physical activity outcomes (e.g., Adjerid et al. 2022), with several focusing attention on using fitness technologies to socialize exercise (e.g., Babar et al. 2024). Fitness technologies features such as leaderboards and newsfeeds combined with the use of features that, for example, allow users to send messages or form teams, can encourage competition but also social support, collaboration, and cooperation. Studies have found that competition through fitness technologies can increase step counts, but more mixed results have been found for other types of socialization such as cooperation and support (e.g., Chen and Pu 2014, Chen et al. 2020, Zhang et al. 2023). Combinations of competition and social accountability or cooperation have been found to produce the most desirable outcomes (Chen et al. 2014, Zhang et al. 2023). Moreover, studies have found that the use of social features do not produce uniform physical activity outcomes for all types of users. For example, one study found that social features may improve running outcomes for stronger exercisers only (Babar et al. 2024). Another study found that competition increased step counts for extroverted, motivated people but no social interventions increased step counts for less motivated individuals (Chen et al. 2020). These results suggest that the way the social features of fitness technologies are used, and the characteristics of the exercisers using them, can affect physical activity outcomes.

A second stream of research on fitness technologies concerns factors associated with adoption and continued use. Regardless of the physical or psychological benefits of their use, if people do not use fitness technologies or they abandon them, those benefits may not be realized or sustained. Such studies have examined how factors such as those from traditional technology adoption models; for example, the unified theory of acceptance and use of technology (UTAUT2), influence adoption or continuance intentions (e.g., Yuan et al. 2015, Wei et al. 2021, Yang and Koenigstorfer 2021, Bianchi et al. 2023). Some researchers have relied on expectation confirmation theory to determine if the confirmation of users expectations about fitness technologies results in continued intention to use (Li et al. 2018, Gupta et al. 2021). Other studies have shown that the reasons for adopting fitness technologies, and whether fitness technologies satisfy or frustrate people’s basic psychological needs, may influence continuance intentions (Rockmann 2019, James et al. 2021).

A third and less studied stream of fitness technology research is concerned with psychological well-being outcomes (Jin et al. 2022). As previously mentioned, Etkin (2016) demonstrated that fitness technology use could decrease enjoyment and subjective well-being. However, research has also shown that fitness technology use can have a positive effect on psychological wellbeing (Stiglbauer et al. 2019). Hu et al. (2023) showed that the use of social features may increase both life satisfaction and emotional exhaustion through social engagement. Studies have also shown that use of different sets of features can be differentially associated with physical and psychological well-being (James et al. 2019a, 2019b; Suh and Li 2022). Research thus suggests that fitness technologies may have both positive and negative psychological well-being outcomes, depending on how, and by whom, they are used.

Researchers have begun to explore the influence of social comparison on all three outcomes of fitness technology use. With respect to physical activity outcomes, having a higher number of lower performing friends on one’s leaderboard was shown to have a positive influence on physical activity, whereas a higher number of higher performing friends had a negative influence on it (Zhou et al. 2016), suggesting upward social comparisons through a leaderboard may discourage physical activity. Another study showed that leaderboard adoption had a positive effect on physical activity but that whereas highly physically active users took less steps than before after adopting leaderboards, users who were not highly active prior to leaderboard adoption became more physically active even when they were not ranked first (Hydari et al. 2023). Yang et al. (2023) found that higher competition rankings were more helpful fo moderate and inactive exercisers then active users, suggesting it may be important for such users to win. These studies demonstrate that the competition dynamics on fitness technologies may be complex and depend on user characteristics, because although outperforming others may be beneficial for some, it may not be strictly necessary for all users.

It is also challenging to draw conclusions from social comparison studies focused on fitness technology continuance intentions (Li et al. 2018, Gupta et al. 2021, Teng and Bao 2022). Li et al. (2018) found that the tendency to make upward comparisons negatively moderated the positive relationship between confirmation and continuance intentions. Teng and Bao (2022) found that upward social comparisons had a positive association with continuance intentions. Finally, Gupta et al. (2021) found the tendency to make social comparisons (i.e., they did not distinguish between upward and downward) to be positively associated with continuance intentions through user satisfaction. These studies show that upward social comparisons may both help and hamper users’ continuance intentions.

With respect to psychological outcomes, Kim (2022) studied the influence of upward and downward comparisons on what he termed physical activity self-efficacy, which is the user’s confidence that they can improve their health and fitness. His study showed that upward comparisons may be important to increasing physical activity self-efficacy, whereas downward comparisons were found to decrease it. Social comparisons, although not both upward and downward, have been positively associated with attitudes toward exercise (Burke and Rains 2019, Sun and Jiang 2022), suggesting that they can drive proexercise attitudes. However, studies focused on outcomes related to how satisfied people are with their body (e.g., appearance, weight) have not yielded consistent results (Burke and Rains 2019, Sun et al. 2022). Sun et al. (2022) found that both upward and downward comparisons of physical activity were positively associated with body satisfaction. However, Burke and Rains (2019) found that exposure to other’s exercise information is positively associated with weight concerns, which is an aspect of body satisfaction, suggesting that social comparisons may not always be psychologically healthy.

Taken together, these studies illustrate that the outcomes associated with fitness technology-facilitated social comparisons may not be uniformly positive. In the current study, we extend the examination of fitness technology-facilitated social comparisons in two notable ways that we hypothesize below. First, we examine whether a user characteristic, passion, influences the interpretation of the fitness technology-facilitated social comparison. Second, we examine if the interpretation of the upward or downward fitness technology-facilitated comparison information as a signal of failure or success, respectively, matters to the psychological outcome obtained.

## 3. Hypothesis Development

## 3.1. The Relationships Between Passion and Performance Self-Esteem

Performance self-esteem<sup>2</sup> involves one’s evaluation of the worthiness of their performance in a domain of interest such as academics or work (Heatherton and Polivy 1991, Hauke and Abele 2020, Kuykendall et al. 2020, Fairlamb 2022, Brito et al. 2023). Passionate people love and value their activity, and thus, invest time and energy in its performance (Vallerand 2010). It is reasonable to suggest that people who invest time and energy into an activity they love are unlikely to think they are bad at it. Therefore, in the absence of information that signals failure, both harmonious and obsessive passion are likely to be positively associated with performance self-esteem.

Researchers have shown that harmonious and obsessive passion are both positively associated with higher levels of performance through a pathway in which mastery goals (i.e., a desire to develop competence) influence deliberate practice of the activity which in turn improves performance (Vallerand et al. 2007, 2008; Bonneville-Roussy et al. 2011). However, unlike the harmoniously passionate, the obsessively passionate are more susceptible to performance-approach and avoidance goals (i.e., desires to do better than, or avoid doing worse than, others) that can undermine performance (Vallerand et al. 2007, 2008; Bonneville-Roussy et al. 2011). Relatedly, research has shown that those whose dominant passion is harmonious have self-esteem that is less sensitive to fluctuations based on performance information than those with high levels of obsessive passion (Mageau et al. 2011). However, research has shown that both harmoniously and obsessively passionate exercisers report high levels of activity-based self-esteem (means � 5.29 and 5.43 on a 7-point scale, respectively, with no significant difference found between the two) (Stenseng and Dalskau 2010). Although their self-esteem may not be as contingent on success or failure as the obsessively passionate, the harmoniously passionate still work to achieve high levels of performance and report healthy activity-based self-esteem. Accordingly, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 1a. Harmonious passion will be positively associated with performance self-esteem.

We argued above that both harmonious and obsessive passion may be positively associated with performance self-esteem because exercisers expressing either dominant passion love their activity, and without evidence to the contrary (i.e., failure information), are unlikely to think they are bad at it. However, unlike harmonious passion, obsessive passion is thought to be associated with maladaptive outcomes. Indeed, it has been shown to be positively associated with outcomes such as shame (Vallerand et al. 2003) and negative affect in sport (Val lerand et al. 2006). However, the link between obsessive passion and maladaptive outcomes has not always been consistently positive, with some studies finding no significant relationship between obsessive passion and outcomes such as satisfaction with sport (Vallerand et al. 2006) and subjective well-being (Vallerand et al. 2008). Moreover, obsessive passion has been found to be positively associated with adaptive outcomes such as positive affect in sport (Vallerand et al. 2006) and deliberate practice (Vallerand et al. 2008). Therefore, although the obsessively passionate may be prone to maladaptive outcomes, research has not always found that linkage to hold true.

This may be partly because the obsessively passionate love their activity but are sensitive to success and failure information (Lafrenie\`re et al. 2012), and thus positive outcomes may result in the absence of failure information. The DMP proposes that, unlike those with harmonious passion, those with obsessive passion may rely on their cherished activity for contingencies such as social acceptance and self-esteem (Vallerand et al. 2003, Valler and 2010). Indeed, Mageau et al. (2011) found that the obsessively passionate experience fluctuations in their self-esteem corresponding to their performance. This suggests that those with higher levels of obsessive passion may be more likely than those with higher levels of harmonious passion to realize self-esteem impacts from their cherished activity. However, the obsessively passionate do desire to master their activity (Vallerand et al. 2007, 2008), and in the absence of failure information, it is reasonable to suggest that they believe that they perform the activity well. In fact, a study by Stenseng and Dalskau (2010) found that obsessively passionate exercisers had a higher level of activity-related self-esteem than harmoniously passionate exercisers although the difference was not statistically significant. This leads us to the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 1b. Obsessive passion will be positively associated with performance self-esteem.

## 3.2. The Mediating Role of Fitness Technology-Facilitated Social Comparison Information

In the previous hypotheses, we argued that both harmonious and obsessive passion would be positively associated with performance self-esteem in the absence of failure information. However, exercisers who are using the social features of fitness technologies receive constant failure and success information through features such as leaderboards, competition and challenge notifications, and newsfeeds. Short of discontinuing their use of fitness technologies or removing friends from them, such failure and success information is hard for users to avoid. Therefore, how passionate users interpret the comparison information delivered to them by their fitness apps is crucial to the psychological outcomes they experience. The ICM proposes that when presented with metrics only, people are more likely to contrast, so we focus on the contrast dimensions of the ICM. The ICM further proposes that when people contrast upward, they interpret better performing others as a signal of their own failure, versus when people contrast downward, they interpret better performing others as a signal of their own success. We argue that exercisers dominant passions for their activity will influence their tendency to contrast upward or downward, and through their interpretations of the fitness technologyfacilitated social comparison information, their passions will influence their performance self-esteem.

In discussing passion and responses to success and failure, Vallerand (2010, p. 163) states that “following success, all is well, and thus similar levels of well-being should be experienced by harmoniously and obsessively passionate individuals.” However, he argues that there will be a difference between the two types of passion in response to failure, stating that “because so much is riding on doing well for obsessively passionate individuals (e.g., maintaining their identity and their sense of selfesteem), failure may have a more devastating psychological impact on their well-being” (Vallerand 2010, p. 163). Indeed, research has shown that obsessively passionate people experience greater life satisfaction after success and less life satisfaction after failure (Lafrenie\`re et al. 2012). There is also research suggesting that obsessively passionate people rely on their cherished activity to derive self-esteem, and thus, their self-esteem fluctuates based on their performance (Mageau et al. 2011). Notably, Stenseng and Dalskau (2010) found that, for the obsessively passionate, there was a positive association between comparative performance evaluations and activity-related self-esteem (i.e., the better they compared, the higher their activity-related self-esteem). We build on this prior literature to propose a mediated model in which passion influences performance selfesteem through the interpretation of fitness technologyfacilitated social comparison information signaling success (downward contrast) or failure (upward contrast).

3.2.1. The Relationships Between Harmonious and Obsessive Passion and Fitness Technology-Facilitated Social Comparison Information. Embedded within the motivation literature, achievement goals (i.e., mastery and performance), explain the “purpose of or reason for competence-relevant activity” (Rawsthorne and Elliot 1999, p. 326) and have been shown in multiple studies to influence deliberate practice and performance of an activity (Vallerand et al. 2007, 2008; Bonneville-Roussy et al. 2011). Mastery goals “focus on the development of competence and task mastery” (Rawsthorne and Elliot 1999, p. 326). In most studies, harmonious passion has been found to be positively associated with mastery goals and not significantly associated with the performance goals that indicate a desire to benchmark one’s performance against others (Vallerand et al. 2007, 2008; Bonneville-Roussy et al. 2011), which suggests that the harmoniously passionate may be able to avoid being influenced by social comparisons altogether and instead focus solely on achieving competence at their activity.

However, it is difficult for exercisers to avoid fitness technology-facilitated social comparison information if they are using a social fitness app and have added friends to it. Researchers have found that the harmoniously passionate are not driven by fear, but rather, when presented with failure information, their activity performance does not change (Be´langer et al. 2013). Moreover, harmonious passion results from autonomous internali zation, and researchers have argued that individuals oriented toward autonomy have more adaptive responses to failure information, a stable self-concept, and are less defensive in stressful social situations (Neighbors and Knee 2003). In fact, harmonious passion is positively associated with greater self-compassion (Schellenberg et al. 2016), which is an adaptive response to failure. These findings suggest that the harmoniously passionate may not be affected by failure information and thus may be less likely to focus on it when their fitness technology presents it. We argue that such exercisers will not be prone to interpreting upward fitness technologyfacilitated social comparison information (i.e., being outperformed) as evidence of failure (upward contrast). This leads to the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 2a. Harmonious passion will be negatively associated with the use of fitness technology-facilitated social comparison information for upward contrast.

Harmonious passion has not been found to be associated with performance goals, including performanceapproach goals that focus on achieving success relative to others (Vallerand et al. 2007, 2008; Bonneville-Roussy et al. 2011). Nor have passion researchers found harmo niously passionate users to be influenced by success information (Be´langer et al. 2013). However, as previously mentioned, exercisers will find it challenging to avoid fitness technology-facilitated social comparison information if they are using the social features of fitness technologies. Passion researchers have suggested that exercisers high on harmonious passion may view competition as a way to gain experience that is valuable to them as they attempt to master their activity (Verner-Filion et al. 2014). Therefore, fitness technologyfacilitated social comparison information may be useful to harmoniously passionate exercisers if it results from competition that they feel helps them achieve mastery of their activity.

However, researchers have long suggested that competition may have a dark side; that it may improve performance because it “provides competence feedback and is therefore informational,” but is extrinsic, and therefore controlling, which suggests that it can undermine people’s desire to engage in the activity (Deci et al. 1981, p. 80). However, intrinsically motivated exercisers, those who are motivated to engage in the activity by the enjoyment that engagement provides them, report using the social features of fitness technologies (James et al. 2019b).

Researchers who have examined the effect of leaderboards and competition rankings on physical activity have found that the use of such features may not be universally beneficial (Hydari et al. 2023, Yang et al. 2023). Although leaderboards have been found to influence physical activity, their effects vary depending on how physically active the user was before adopting the leaderboard, how they perform relative to others, and how many others they outperform (Zhou et al. 2016, Hydari et al. 2023, Yang et al. 2023). Yang et al. (2023) found that active users benefited from outperforming others, in terms of stimulating more physical activity, just not as much as the less active. Highly physically active exercisers, which passionate people are likely to be, were found to benefit (in terms of increased physical activity) from leaderboards only when they ranked highly (Hydari et al. 2023). Zhou et al. (2016) argued that exercisers would be more likely to contrast with their lower performing friends and found that the higher the proportion of lower performing friends one had on their fitness app, the more physical activities they would perform. Although they did not differentiate between the type of passion the exercisers held, these results suggest that serious exercisers who hold a passion that is associated with adaptive outcomes may be likely to focus on their success relative to others (downward contrast) when presented with fitness technology-facilitated social comparison information. This may be because it provides them with information that helps them gauge their mastery of the activity. This leads to the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 2b. Harmonious passion will be positively associated with the use of fitness technology-facilitated social comparison information for downward contrast.

Performance goals “focus on demonstration of competence relative to others” (Rawsthorne and Elliot 1999, p. 326). Therefore, performance goals compel comparisons with other people to know if one has achieved the goal. There are two types of performance goals; those that focus on achieving success relative to others (i.e., performance-approach goals) and those that focus on avoiding failure relative to others (i.e., performanceavoidance goals) (Rawsthorne and Elliot 1999). Obsessive passion has been shown to be positively associated with both types (Vallerand et al. 2007, 2008; Bonneville-Roussy et al. 2011), which suggests that the obsessively passionate are likely to interpret fitness technologyfacilitated social comparison information as evidence of success and failure.

Researchers have suggested that “obsessively passionate athletes might perceive competition as a selfthreatening situation, in which they might make mistakes, perform badly, or even fail” (Verner-Filion et al. 2014, p. 530). Obsessive passion has been positively linked to threat appraisals in sport, in which people focus on negative events (Lavoie et al. 2021). For the obsessively passionate, avoiding failure and beating others is their focus, specifically, researchers have linked obsessive passion to a fear of failure when exposed to failure information (Be´langer et al. 2013). Being outperformed by others on a leaderboard, for example, can be interpreted as failure. Taken together, studies suggest that obsessively passionate exercisers will be prone to interpreting information that they are performing worse than others as evidence of failure, leading to the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 3a. Obsessive passion will be positively associated with the use of fitness technology-facilitated social comparison information for upward contrast.

The obsessively passionate have been found to experience increased life satisfaction following success (Lafrenie\`re et al. 2012). However, being exposed to success information was not found to have a significant effect on the performance of the obsessively passionate (Be´langer et al. 2013), although this does not mean that they do not pay attention to such information. Passion researchers have argued that the obsessively passionate engage in their cherished activity in a reactive and self-defensive way (Be´langer et al. 2013), which could indicate that such exercisers may be sensitive to evidence of both success and failure, especially when they have chosen to use fitness technologies in ways that provide easy access to such information. In fact, there is research that suggests the obsessively passionate focus on performing better than others. Specifically, performance-approach goals emphasize beating others at an activity and obsessive passion is positively associated with these goals in multiple studies (Vallerand et al. 2007, 2008; Bonneville-Roussy et al. 2011). These findings suggest that obsessively passionate exercisers will be alert to information that indicates they are succeeding.

The distinction between obsessive passion and extrinsic motivation is that, while extrinsic motivation means that performance of an activity is externally controlled (Ryan and Deci 2000), it does not require people to love the activity as passion does (Vallerand et al. 2010). Some forms of extrinsic motivation have been found to be positively associated with the use of the social features of fitness technologies (James et al. 2019b). Obsessive passion results from controlled internalization of the activity, and Deci et al. (1981, p. 80) state that “trying to win can be quite controlling and is often pursued at all costs.” This suggests that the obsessively passionate may be receptive to information indicating that they are winning. The fitness technology literature has provided some insight into the use of leaderboards, and while the most active users may not always benefit as much from their use, they can benefit, and their physical activity increases when users have a higher proportion of lower performing friends and rank highly on the leaderboard (Zhou et al. 2016, Hydari et al. 2023, Yang et al. 2023). Taken together, studies suggest that obsessively passionate exercisers will be receptive to information that suggests they are succeeding at their cherished activity, leading to the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 3b. Obsessive passion will be positively associated with the use of fitness technology-facilitated social comparison information for downward contrast.

3.2.2. The Relationships Between Fitness Technology-Facilitated Social Comparison Information and Performance Self-Esteem. Early research on social comparisons revealed that exposing a person to another who had socially desirable characteristics decreased selfesteem (Morse and Gergen 1970). Specifically, they found that when confronted with another job applicant who was better dressed, better prepared, and more selfconfident, people’s self-esteem decreased. Similarly, Wheeler and Miyake (1992) found that upward comparisons decrease subjective well-being. More recently, social media research has shown that upward comparisons are associated with decreases in self-esteem and well-being (e.g., depression, satisfaction with life) (Vogel et al. 2014, Liu et al. 2017, Wang et al. 2017, Alfasi 2019, Schmuck et al. 2019). This is further evidence that interpreting fitness technology-facilitated social comparison information as evidence of failure may be detrimental to performance self-esteem. In fact, providing people with failure information has been demonstrated to decrease performance self-esteem (Heatherton and Polivy 1991). Stenseng and Dalskau (2010) also showed that people who reported performing worse than others also reported lower activity-related self-esteem. The adage that “comparison is the thief of joy” suggests this relationship; that is, people may experience adverse outcomes from evidence of failure.

Researchers have not used the ICM in a fitness technology context, but we do have some understanding of the influence of upward contrast in other contexts. In a social media context, upward contrast was shown to be positively associated with stress (Yue et al. 2022) and negatively related to psychological well-being (Batenburg and Das 2015), which suggests upward contrast may harm psychological well-being. Notably, Carmona et al. (2008) found upward contrast to be negatively related to students’ self-efficacy belief in academic success, which is a concept similar to performance selfesteem, and thus suggests engagement with fitness technology-facilitated social comparison information for upward contrast is likely to be negatively related to performance self-esteem. Similarly, a study of online gaming found that upward contrast is negatively associated with online gaming self-efficacy (Esteves et al. 2021). Taken together, research suggests that interpretation of fitness technology-facilitated social comparison information as evidence of failure may be associated with decreases in users’ performance self-esteem. This leads us to the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 4a. The use of fitness technology-facilitated social comparison information for upward contrast will be negatively associated with performance self-esteem.

SCT researchers have long suggested that people may be able to “achieve self-enhancement by derogating another person” (Wills 1981), which suggests that, by comparing oneself to worse performing others, a person can improve how one feels about oneself. Indeed, early research found that when people were exposed to another job applicant who was disheveled, dirty, and unprepared, they experienced increased self-esteem (Morse and Gergen 1970). Similarly, downward social comparison has been found to increase subjective well being (Wheeler and Miyake 1992). Downward comparisons are thought to “bolster self-evaluation and expecta tions of future success in the comparison domain” (Aspinwall and Taylor 1993, p. 718). Notably, Neighbors and Knee (2003) found that outperforming another was associated with increased state self-esteem. Other studies have also found that people experience increased state self-esteem after winning (Mageau et al. 2011) and following success (Lafrenie\`re et al. 2012). Similar results were found in a study that showed that exercisers who reported outperforming others also reported higher activity-related self-esteem (Stenseng and Dalskau 2010). Such findings suggest that when people compare with worse performing others it may bolster their selfesteem.

Studies in the fitness technology context have not examined the ICM but research in other contexts suggest downward contrast can be psychologically beneficial. In online games, Esteves et al. (2021) found downward contrast to be positively related to online gaming selfefficacy. Similarly, in online health support communities, Batenburg and Das (2015) found downward contrast to be positively related to psychological well-being. Taken together, research suggests that interpreting downward fitness technology-facilitated social comparison information as evidence of success may be associated with increases in users’ performance self-esteem, leading us to the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 4b. The use of fitness technology-facilitated social comparison information for downward contrast will be positively associated with performance self-esteem.

## 4. Method

To test our hypotheses, we collected survey data in three phases during February to April 2023. In the first phase, potential participants were screened to determine that (1) they regularly used a fitness wearable and app, (2) they frequently looked at other’s exercise data (e.g., leaderboard, routes, step counts), and (3) they were 18 years of age or older. We also collected demographic information and the frequency of use of common fitness technology social features in the screener. Out of the 2,000 who completed the screener, Prolific IDs were used to invite the 869 participants who met the screening requirements to take part in the second phase. In the second phase, items to measure participants’ passions and their interpretations of fitness technology-facilitated social comparison information were administered. Beginning a week after the second phase, the 661 participants who successfully completed the second phase were invited through their Prolific IDs to take part in a third phase. During this, items to measure participants’ performance self-esteem and well-being were administered. A total of 603 participants successfully completed all three phases. Ethical approval to conduct the study was provided by the lead author’s home institution.

We used existing scales to develop our survey. The scales to measure harmonious and obsessive passion (Vallerand et al. 2003, Parastatidou et al. 2012), subjective vitality (Ryan and Frederick 1997, Bostic et al. 2000), satisfaction with life (Diener et al. 1985), and selfcompassion (Raes et al. 2011) were used without modification. The usefulness and ease of use scales from Venkatesh et al. (2011) and Venkatesh and Davis (2000) were slightly modified to reflect the fitness technology use context. The social comparison downward and upward identification and contrast (Van der Zee et al. 2000) and performance self-esteem (Heatherton and Polivy 1991) scales were also adapted for our context. After adapting the items, the survey was given to an expert panel of researchers with survey development expertise and their feedback was gathered and implemented to further improve the wording of the items (Churchill 1979, Mac-Kenzie et al. 2011). The survey was pilot tested, and results of the factor analysis revealed the items loaded on the expected factors. The full survey, along with the means and standard deviations for all items, is provided in Online Appendix B.

The survey was administered using the Qualtrics survey platform and participants were recruited from Prolific. Participants were restricted to United States (US) residents or United Kingdom (UK) nationals whose first language is English. Participants were paid to take each phase of the survey (Buhrmester et al. 2011, Mason and Suri 2012, Steelman et al. 2014, Lowry et al. 2016). The items were presented in random order to each participant (Podsakoff et al. 2003) and anonymity was assured through the use of Prolific IDs. To help ensure that parti cipants were cognitively engaged in taking the survey, attention trap items were included, which asked participants to select a specific answer for a question (Oppenheimer et al. 2009). Participants were ejected from the survey if two attention trap items were incorrectly answered. All responses including an incorrectly answered attention trap item were removed from the data used for analysis. The data were combined using the respondents’ Prolific IDs that were collected in each phase.

Table 3 also shows that the fitness technology-facilitated social comparison constructs and performance self-esteem mediate the rela tionships between passion and subjective vitality.

The DMP postulates that when a person has a passion for an activity, both forms of passion are present, with one dominating the other (Vallerand 2010). To test the robustness of our results, we ran a model with the data from only the respondents (n � 277) who reported divergent passions, to see if the pattern in the results still held. The results confirm that harmonious passion is associated with adaptive use of fitness technology-facilitated social comparison information, which positively influences performance self-esteem, whereas obsessive passion is associated with maladaptive use which negatively influences it.

Adding these variables to the main model provides insight into how fitness technology-facilitated social information influences well-being. The results resemble our main model results, especially for subjective vitality. Passion is directly and positively related to subjective vitality, and harmonious passion is positively related to upward contrast and negatively related to downward contrast (vice versa for obsessive passion), and downward contrast is positively related to subjective vitality. Notably, performance self-esteem is positively related to subjective vitality and the mediation results show that the paths between passion and subjective vitality are mediated by upward and downward contrast, as well as performance self-esteem. These results illustrate a pathway through which fitness technology-facilitated social comparison benefits or harms the passionate by influencing their performance self-esteem, which in turn influences their well-being. The story is similar for all models: fitness technology-facilitated social comparisons may indeed be the thief of joy for the obsessively passionate, whereas the harmoniously passionate may be able to avoid such a fate.

Finally, we ran models that (1) substituted upward and downward identification for upward and downward contrast and (2) included all four mediators (upward contrast, downward contrast, upward identification, and downward identification) along with eudaimonic well-being. Recall that when people contrast upward, they see others’ success as evidence of their own failure, versus when they contrast downward, they see others’ failure as evidence of their own success. Conversely, when people identify upward, they believe that success is a future state they can obtain, whereas when they identify downward, they believe failure is a potential future state they may reach. Following similar logic to our hypotheses and the results of our main model, harmonious passion should be positively associated with upward, and negatively associated with downward, identification, and vice versa for obsessive passion.

Indeed, our results show that the harmoniously passionate can interpret fitness technology-facilitated social comparison information in an adaptive way; that is, to see better others as a state they can obtain and worse others as evidence of their success. Conversely, the obsessively passionate are prone to interpreting fitness technology-facilitated social comparison information in maladaptive ways; that is, to see better performing others as evidence of their own failure and other’s poor performance as a sign that they will also falter. These additional results show that both upward and downward comparisons can be beneficial but that psychological outcomes depend on who is doing the comparing (i.e., the harmoniously or obsessively passionate). The negativity with which the obsessively passionate interpret the social comparison information may lead to psychological harm (e.g., reduced self-esteem and wellbeing).

## 7. Discussion

The objective of this study was to examine the relationships between harmonious and obsessive passion and performance self-esteem and how these relationships are mediated by exercisers’ interpretations of fitness technology-facilitated social comparison information. We find support for most of our hypotheses.

## 7.1. Contributions to Theory and Research

Our study makes several contributions to theory and research. Early research on fitness technologies illustrated that they could increase people’s physical activity (e.g., step counts) while simultaneously taking the joy out of the activity and decreasing psychological wellbeing (Etkin 2016). There are increasingly varied ways that fitness technologies and the data they curate can be used to support users’ fitness regimes, and to ensure that these technologies holistically support users’ health and well-being, it is necessary to understand the drivers of both physical and psychological outcomes from speci fic uses of fitness technology. The psychological outcomes of fitness technology use have been studied less than the physical outcomes (Jin et al. 2022), as have the boundary conditions that may help explain why psychological outcomes from fitness technology use vary. Our study contributes to the stream of research examining psychological outcomes of fitness technology use by studying how the interpretation of fitness technologyfacilitated social comparison information is associated with both performance self-esteem and psychological well-being. We show that although fitness technologies can increase physical activity (e.g., Ringeval et al. 2020), the psychological consequences of their use may not always be positive.

Fitness technology studies have started to examine how different user characteristics may drive particular fitness technology uses, as well as examine outcomes of use among different populations of users such as older individuals or more or less active users (e.g., James et al. 2019b, Rockmann and Gewald 2019, Suh and Li 2022, Yang et al. 2023). We find that passion may be a user characteristic that determines how fitness technology facilitated social comparison information is interpreted. Specifically, we find that obsessively passionate exercisers may be prone to interpreting this information negatively, which may result in undesirable psychological outcomes, but that harmoniously passionate exercisers may be able to avoid this fate. Therefore, we also contribute to the conversation about how user characteristics may determine how fitness technologies are used and the outcomes resulting from that use.

As the fitness technology literature has matured, studies have ceased to focus solely on the use or nonuse of fitness technologies and have begun to examine the use of specific features, feature sets, or data (e.g., Adjerid et al. 2022, Suh and Li 2022, Zhang et al. 2023, Babar et al. 2024). We contribute to this stream of more nuanced examinations of fitness technology use by focusing our study on the interpretation of other exercisers’ data that fitness technologies curate and provide to exercisers who add friends to their fitness technologies or use social fitness apps. By adopting the ICM (Buunk and Ybema 1997), our study provides a robust understanding of the relationships between the interpretation of fitness-technology-facilitated social comparison information and psychological outcomes. Specifically, we show that some users may interpret other exercisers outperforming them as evidence of failure and these negative interpretations may harm performance selfesteem and psychological well-being. Many technologies have social aspects to them, for example, online gaming, online health communities, and social media, and the results of this study may inform studies of such technologies.

As new technologies enter the marketplace that provide users new means of communicating and exchanging information such as social media, online gaming, and fitness technologies, a prominent stream of research on the unexpected, negative outcomes of using these technologies has arisen. This stream is often referred to as research on the dark side of technology use (Tarafdar et al. 2013). Such research examines negative outcomes from technology use such as addiction and envy (e.g., Krasnova et al. 2015, James et al. 2017). Moreover, fitness technology researchers have found that not all users are similarly drawn to or benefit equally from the use of different fitness technology features and have advocated against a one-size-fits-all approach to their use (James et al. 2019b, Hydari et al. 2023). Our study contributes to this literature by showing that some users may potentially experience detrimental impacts to their performance selfesteem, which is critical to their psychological well-being, from the use of fitness technology-facilitated social comparison information for upward contrast. However, we also show that social comparison through fitness technologies can potentially be beneficial to some users.

Offering one explanation for the divergent conclusions in prior fitness technology research exploring social comparison, our study suggests that whether users experience positive or negative psychological outcomes from their use of fitness technology-facilitated social comparison information may depend on their dominant passion for their exercise activity. Our results highlight the importance of considering individual characteristics in the etiology of positive and negative outcomes from technology use.

## 7.2. Contributions to Society and Practice

This study has implications for both exercisers and developers of fitness technologies. For exercisers, this study may help them discern between the social features of fitness technologies such as leaderboards, newsfeeds, messaging capabilities, and competitions to find the ones that suit them best. Our results suggest that how users interpret fitness technology-facilitated social comparison information may impact psychological outcomes, for example, seeing one is outperformed may be interpreted by obsessively passionate exercisers as proof of failure and may damage confidence. Therefore, such exercisers may want to avoid the use of social features that emphasize comparisons in favor of social features such as messaging that encourage socialization without the exchange of statistics, and in doing so may want to emphasize sending encouragement rather than performance data. Additionally, fitness technology makers may want to design apps in a way that either automatically adapts, or asks the exerciser to adapt, the features and the way they are presented, in order to best support obsessively passionate exercisers.

This study also suggests that exercisers should carefully consider who they add as friends to their fitness technologies. Exercisers usually add friends who are convenient (e.g., friends and family) with little regard to how active those people are. Some of our participants reported purposefully adding people they considered to be peers to their fitness technologies (see additional anal ysis in Online Appendix I). Interestingly our participants mentioned two distinct categories of “peers”: (1) others sharing similar life or demographic characteristics (e.g., other busy moms, other people of a similar age), and (2) others who similarly valued specific exercise activities (e.g., other “gym rats” or other runners) or who they thought were similar in ability. Such an approach of carefully considering who one wishes to benchmark or compete against may be an approach that could lead obsessively passionate exercisers to not be outperformed as frequently or to an extent that seems unachievable, thereby potentially reducing negative psychological outcomes.

Fitness technology developers may want to automatically suggest new peers with characteristics that reduce negative psychological outcomes. Specifically, fitness technology developers could consider providing curated options of different types of others to which users could compare (e.g., “gym rats” versus “beginners”). They could also rethink some of the social features of fitness technologies (e.g., leaderboards, newsfeeds) to emphasize social comparison information as either proof of success or failure or even tailor the display of the information based on characteristics of the user such as passion. Future research may want to investigate such approaches.

## 7.3. Limitations and Directions for Future Research

Our study is limited by the advancement and diffusion of technology. Although fitness technologies have been available for more than a decade, it has taken time for them to diffuse and for the features they offer to advance from simple quantification to facilitating social interaction. Not all fitness technology users yet make use of the social features, and therefore, our results may only be of interest to those users who do. However, as new features of fitness technologies continue to be developed and adopted, or old features improved, our results show that there is a need to consider whether all users will equally benefit from the new or improved offerings and be able to provide usage guidance, perhaps before new features can be adopted by those for whom their use may be counterproductive.

We are also restricted by the cross-sectional study design, which provides correlational evidence and cannot provide insight into causality. However, what our study does provide is initial evidence of how users’ interpretations of fitness technology-facilitated social comparison information may lead to different outcomes for different types of users, specifically those with different types of passion for exercise. Our results show that the patterns of fitness technology-facilitated social information use differ depending on the type of passion held by the exerciser. Future research could build on these findings, and those from archival data research (e.g., Hydari et al. 2023), to design experiments that could examine how the use of different types of social features lead to fluctuations in state self-esteem over time. Moreover, design science researchers could explore the development of social features that emphasize or deemphasize social comparison information or encourage either contrast or identification depending on if the referents are performing better or worse than the focal user. Socializing people’s exercise through the use of fitness technologies has been a popular area of study, and researchers have suggested that the social features of fitness technologies may be the most attractive to the most different types of users (James et al. 2019b).

Research into how users can obtain the most benefit from the social features of fitness technologies is certain to continue and our study contributes to this flourishing stream. Researchers can build on our results to tease out nuances regarding how people benefit from their fitness technology use, as well as explore the dark side consequences of inserting technology into a cherished activity.

## 8. Conclusion

We proposed and tested a model of how passion for one’s exercise activity is associated with how users interpret fitness technology-facilitated social comparison information and how such interpretations are associated with their performance self-esteem and psychological wellbeing. Our results indicate that exercisers whose dominant passion is harmonious are prone to interpreting fitness technology-facilitated social comparison information as evidence of success, which may be beneficial fo their performance self-esteem. Conversely, exercisers whose dominant passion is obsessive are prone to interpreting such information as evidence of failure, which may be psychological harmful. Although research has shown that fitness technologies can increase physical activity, our results show that the psychological outcomes from their use may not always be uniformly positive.
