# How Information Technology Overcomes Deficiencies for Innovation in Small and Medium-Sized Enterprises: Closed Innovation vs. Open Innovation

Abstract. Innovation is vital for the growth of small and medium-sized enterprise (SMEs). However, SMEs face deficiencies that hinder their innovation output. This study examines how information technology (IT) helps SMEs address two salient deficiencies: technological deficiency (deficiency in internal technical knowledge and skills) and govern ment support deficiency (deficiency in favorable government policies and incentives). Although IT can be used in a closed manner (within the firm’s boundary) or open manner (beyond the firm’s boundary) to enable innovation, closed and open approaches to IT-enabled innovation pose advantages and challenges for SMEs, resulting in a tension regarding which approach better addresses these two deficiencies. In this study, we investigate how IT Use for Closed Innovation Activities (ITC) and IT Use for Open Innovation Activities (ITO) help SMEs overcome these two deficiencies to achieve innovation output. Drawing on the theoretical concepts of internal and external absorptive capacity metaroutines, we hypothesize that ITC and ITO reduce the harmful effects of technological deficiency and government support deficiency on innovation output. Further, we hypothesize that ITC is more effective than ITO in reducing the harmful effect of technological deficiency on innovation output. In contrast, we hypothesize that ITO is more effective than ITC in reducing the harmful effect of government support deficiency on innovation output. Our empirical analysis of a unique multiyear archival data set of 319 manufacturing SMEs in Mexico broadly supports our hypotheses. We conduct a further exploratory analysis that shed light on several underlying mechanisms included in our theorization. Our main contributions lie in addressing the tension between ITC and ITO and highlighting that neither approach to IT-enabled innovation outperforms the other in combating the technological deficiency and government support deficiency of SMEs; rather, SMEs achieve greater innovation output by appropriately orienting their IT-enabled innovation efforts in a closed or open manner to address specific deficiencies.

## 1. Introduction

Small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) constitute roughly 90% of organizations globally (World Bank 2020). SMEs differ from large organizations because of their defining characteristics of limited resources (Terziovski 2010), low levels of perceived legitimacy (Thornhill and

Amit 2003), and scant opportunities to recruit specialized workers (Van de Vrande et al. 2009). These characteristics manifest in innovation-related deficiencies for SMEs relative to large firms (Freeman et al. 1983, Ebben and Johnson 2005). Because innovation-fueled growth is imperative for SMEs to adapt and succeed in an ever-changing environment (Agarwal and Audretsch 2001), overcoming resource inadequacies and other limitations to produce innovation outcomes is a key challenge for SMEs (Wales et al. 2013).

Although information technology (IT) is an important means to address innovation challenges (Kohli and Melville 2019, Karhade and Dong 2021a, Andrade-Rojas et al. 2024), prior IT and innovation research has been generally conducted in the context of large firms (Bardhan et al. 2013; Ravichandran et al. 2017; Saldanha et al. 2017, 2020b). Large firms, however, can more easily achieve innovation outcomes because of an abundance of resources (Andrade-Rojas et al. 2021). In contrast, SMEs do not have abundant resources and face deficiencies both within the firm and because of constraints posed by the external environment. Given that deficiencies obstructing innovation are particularly salient for SMEs, investigating how IT helps to address innovation deficiencies in the SME context is an important contribution to the literature. Because firms can innovate within or beyond their boundaries (Almirall and Masanell 2010), this investigation incorporates both closed and open approaches to IT-enabled innovation. Hence, we study how IT Use for Closed Innovation Activities (ITC) and IT Use for Open Innovation Activities (ITO) play distinct roles in helping SMEs overcome innovation-related deficiencies.

ITC and ITO relate to two approaches to pursuing innovation: a closed approach and an open approach (Almirall and Masanell 2010; Felin and Zenger 2014; Karhade and Dong 2021a, b). In closed innovation, firms “generate their own ideas and then develop them, build them, market them, distribute them, service them, finance them, and support them on their own” (Chesbrough 2003, p. 20). In open innovation, firms innovate by partnering with external stakeholders such as customers, suppliers, or competitors (Dong et al. 2017). Closed innovation is thus internally focused, whereas open innovation is externally focused.

We define ITC as the extent to which a firm uses internal information systems to promote innovation within the firm. For example, microblogs enhance innovation by helping staff access and share internal information (Mu¨ ller and Stocker 2011). ITC addresses the internal challenges of complexity and inefficiency in generating and developing ideas (Ravichandran et al. 2017)—issues especially pertinent for SMEs. At the same time, ITC entails highly dynamic adjustment costs that are detrimental to innovation outcomes (Karhade and Dong 2021a). ITC also requires a firm to shoulder the full costs associated with making IT investments and managing IT-enabled innovation activities (Karhade and Dong 2021a), allocations that can be burdensome for SMEs because of their resource constraints; with ITO, on the other hand, these costs can be shared between a firm and its collaborators (Chesbrough 2003).

We define ITO as the extent to which a firm uses IT to collaborate for innovation with external constituents such as market leaders, suppliers, competitors, and clients. For instance, firms use interorganizational IT systems to develop new products in collaboration with suppliers or based on client inputs (Song and Di Benedetto 2008). Although ITO helps a firm combine diverse resources from external stakeholders with the firm’s resources (Trantopoulos et al. 2017), ITO increases coordination costs because it requires cooperation with other organizations (Greenstein 1992). ITO may come with other downsides (Whelan et al. 2014, Karhade and Dong 2021b), such as the risk that collaborators will not engage with IT (Majchrzak and Malhotra 2013). These issues are especially salient for SMEs because of their lack of resources and managerial expertise (Ebben and Johnson 2005). We conceptualize ITC and ITO as distinct concepts (i.e., not as two ends of a continuum) because SMEs can pursue either, neither, or both ITC and ITO simultaneously.<sup>1</sup> ITC and ITO represent inputs into business activities that generate innovation, whereas innovation outputs (e.g., product, process, market innovations) are outcomes of such activities.<sup>2</sup>

The aforementioned advantages and challenges of ITC and ITO highlight a need to understand (a) how ITC and ITO help SMEs overcome innovation deficiencies and (b) how to resolve the tension between ITC and ITO regarding which approach to IT-enabled inno vation better addresses the specific innovation deficiencies faced by an SME. We focus on two innovation deficiencies that are especially salient and practically important for SMEs. First, SMEs are technologically deficient compared with large firms (Wales et al. 2013). We focus on technological deficiency because technology facilitates business expansion and caters to evolving customer needs. As such, technology influences the survival of SMEs, especially given the rapid technolog ical changes and short product life cycles of the modern era (Segarra and Callejo´n 2002). Practitioner surveys (e.g., a survey by Boston Consulting Group of 4,000 SMEs in Germany, China, Brazil, India, and the United States) show that technology plays a major role in the success of SMEs (Michael et al. 2013). Technological deficiency refers to the extent to which a firm lacks technological abilities such as technological knowledge, technological skills of staff, or advanced technologies (Afuah 2002, Zhou and Wu 2010). Technology here refers to internal technical resources, such as technical personnel (e.g., skilled designers, engineers) or techni cal knowledge (e.g., product, engineering designs) (Afuah 2002, Zhou and Wu 2010) that are used to inno vate in products, processes, and services—and thus does not refer to information technology. Technologica deficiency—which also encompasses resistance to tech nological change, lack of technological training, inabil ity to identify new technological opportunities, lack of technological information, and lack of technologica knowledge—is a key barrier for SMEs to generate innovation outputs (Madrid-Guijarro et al. 2009, Zhou and Wu 2010, Xie and Suh 2014, Prasanna et al. 2019).<sup>3</sup> Technological deficiency often arises for SMEs because of their small size, lack of resources, lack of perceived legitimacy (Thornhill and Amit 2003), and scant opportunities to recruit specialized workers (Van de Vrande et al. 2009).

Second, a deficiency in government support for innovation is a critical barrier for SMEs (Hadjimanolis 1999, Madrid-Guijarro et al. 2009). Governments in many nations do not provide sufficient financial or policy support for innovation, thus hindering innovation in SMEs (Castilla y Leo´n Econo´mica 2012).<sup>4</sup> Many SMEs rely on government support for innovation because government programs (e.g., subsidies) enable SMEs to change their innovation-related processes and practices to facilitate the conversion of innovation inputs to outcomes (Buisseret et al. 1995, Rojas et al. 2018). Government support deficiency refers to the extent to which firms do not receive assistance from the government and its administrative units, including “favorable policies, incentives, and programs” (Shu et al. 2016, p. 472). We focus on government support deficiency for three reasons. First, adverse government policies provide inadequate legal, regulatory, and financial frameworks, which discourage SMEs from pursuing innovation. Because of their small size, SMEs usually have a limited ability to influence the regulatory environment in their favor; hence, they suffer disproportionate hurdles when regulations hinder innovation. Second, SMEs are particularly vulnerable to government support deficiency because of their scarce funds and dependence on few clients (Doh and Kim 2014). Third, because of limited resources, SMEs often lack extensive legal teams, resulting in a weak understanding of legal frameworks and, thus, a limited ability to use legal protections in their favor. Whereas legal support from the government helps facilitate innovation in SMEs, its absence can be detrimental. Technological deficiency and government support deficiency are thus distinct and salient challenges SMEs face in the pursuit of innovation.

We pose the following research question: How do ITC and ITO help SMEs overcome their technological deficiency and government support deficiency to achieve innovation output? To develop our hypotheses, we draw on the concepts of metaroutines, which are microfoundations of absorptive capacity, and practiced routines, which express or execute metaroutines in organizations (Lewin et al. 2011). Because IT is a practiced routine that helps a firm execute metaroutines by digitizing rules, procedures, norms, and processes in the firm (Melville et al. 2004, Hsieh et al. 2011, Polites and Karahanna 2013), we propose that ITC and ITO are practiced routines that digitally execute internal and external absorptive capacity metaroutines, respectively.<sup>5</sup> We apply this theoretical lens to hypothesize that ITC and ITO reduce the harmful effects of technological deficiency and government support deficiency on the innovation output of SMEs. Further, we hypothesize that whereas ITC is more effective than ITO in reducing the harmful effect of technological deficiency on SMEs’ innovation output, ITO is more effective than ITC in reducing the harmful effect of government support deficiency on the innovation output of SMEs.

We find broad support for our hypotheses using multiyear archival data from 319 manufacturing SMEs in Mexico. We conduct an exploratory analysis to test several underlying mechanisms included in our theori zation. Our research design and data collection were conducted in partnership with the Government of Mexico, representing a form of “engaged scholarship” (Van de Ven 2007, p. 9; Rai 2019)—a key strength of our study. Our contributions are threefold. First, we address the tension regarding ITC and ITO by showing how they differ in helping SMEs mitigate the harmfu effects of technological and government support deficiencies. Our results imply that neither approach to IT-enabled innovation outperforms the other in addressing both innovation deficiencies. Rather, SMEs achieve more innovation output by orienting their IT-enabled innovation effort in a closed or open manner to address a specific deficiency. We respond to calls in the literature to study tensions in IT-enabled innovation: in particular, “how internal organizational and environmental conditions cause alternative mechanisms [to IT-enabled innovation] to be more or less binding” and how some “mechanisms are more effective than others and under what conditions” (Kohli and Melville 2019, p. 214). Second, we show that IT helps SMEs overcome a deficiency in government support, thus extending the IT innovation literature to an institutional context related to government support for innovation. Third, we complement the IT and innovation research conducted in large firms (Ravichandran et al. 2017, Saldanha et al. 2020b, Choi et al. 2021, Guo et al. 2023) by studying how IT attenuates the negative effects of innovation deficiencies prominent in SMEs.

## 2. Theoretical Development 2.1. Metaroutines of Absorptive Capacity

An organizational routine is a “repetitive, recognizable pattern of interdependent actions, involving multiple actors” (Feldman and Pentland 2003, p. 96); consists of rules, heuristics, norms, and ways of doing things; and is operationalized in organizational activities and processes (Cyert and March 1963, Nelson and Winter 1982). Metaroutines are higher-level routines associated with a bundle of specific standard operating procedures or practiced routines that express or execute these metaroutines (Lewin et al. 2011). Metaroutines are abstract microfoundations of absorptive capacity executed or expressed in organizations in the form of observable practiced routines (Volberda et al. 2010, Lewin et al. 2011, Parmigiani and Howard-Grenville 2011). Examples of practiced routines include organizational rules, IT systems, procedures, norms, and processes. (Lewin et al. 2011).

## 2.2. Internal and External Absorptive Capacity Metaroutines and IT as a Practiced Routine

Absorptive capacity is defined as the ability of a firm to recognize, assimilate, and apply new information to commercial ends (Cohen and Levinthal 1990). Absorptive capacity can be decomposed into two components— internal and external (Lewin and Massini 2004, Lewin et al. 2011). Internal absorptive capacity encompasses the processes and capabilities underlying internal information exploitation and assimilation, including interna practices related to the improvement and sharing of ideas (Lewin et al. 2011). External absorptive capacity refers to the “management of exploration for new knowledge in the external environment” and represents a firm’s ability to identify external knowledge and assimilate it into the firm (Zahra and George 2002; Lewin et al. 2011, p. 83). Because the external environment is a crucial source of knowledge (Chesbrough 2003), external absorptive capacity is essential to acquire and assimilate knowledge beyond a firm’s boundaries (Chalmers and Balan-Vnuk 2013). Both types of absorptive capacity complement other organizational factors to improve a firm’s ability to utilize knowledge (Roberts et al. 2012).<sup>6</sup>

Our theorization follows Lewin et al. (2011), who identify a configuration of metaroutines that underlie internal and external absorptive capacities. For internal absorptive capacity, we focus on the following four metaroutines: (1) facilitating variation (i.e., processes that facilitate exploration of new ideas); (2) sharing knowledge and superior practices across the organization (i.e., transmission and sharing knowledge in the organization); (3) reflecting, updating, and replicating (i.e., reflecting on, updating, and replicating organizational practices); and (4) managing internal selection regimes (i.e., implementing processes “to select various projects and activities to invest in and to determine how to allocate resources among them” (Lewin et al. 2011, pp. 87–88)).<sup>7</sup> Lewin et al. (2011) also conceptualize the metaroutine managing adaptive tension, which lies at the interface between internal and external absorptive capacities. We do not select this metaroutine for our theorizing because our focus is on internal absorptive capacity.

For external absorptive capacity, we focus on two metaroutines: (1) identifying and recognizing the value of externally generated knowledge (i.e., practices to monitor the environment for valuable knowledge) and (2) learning from and with partners, suppliers, customers, competitors, and consultants (i.e., interactions with external stakeholders to learn from them or jointly generate new knowledge (Lewin et al. 2011, p. 90)). Lewin et al. (2011) also conceptualize the metaroutine transferring knowledge back to the organization, which lies at the interface between internal and external absorptive capacity routines. We do not select this metaroutine for our theorizing because our focus is on external absorptive capacity.

IT is a practiced routine because IT helps firms execute metaroutines by digitizing rules, procedures, norms, and processes (Melville et al. 2004, Hsieh et al. 2011, Polites and Karahanna 2013). Hence, we propose that ITC is a practiced routine that executes internal absorptive capacity metaroutines. For instance, ITC, such as a computer-aided design system, facilitates the exploration of new ideas, thereby executing the metaroutine facilitating variation. Meanwhile, ITO is a practiced routine that executes external absorptive capacity metaroutines (i.e., ITC and ITO are digital expressions of internal and external absorptive capacity metaroutines, respectively). For example, ITO, such as crowdsourcing platforms, enables firms to source ideas from customers, thus executing the metaroutine learning from and with various external constituents. The conceptualization by Lewin et al. (2011) of metaroutines and practiced routines is hence an appropriate theoretical foundation to develop our hypotheses.

## 2.3. ITC and ITO

Innovation occurs in two ways—within or beyond the firm’s boundary (Almirall and Masanell 2010, Felin and Zenger 2014). Firms can take an internal approach to innovation by processing, integrating, and leveraging internal knowledge and resources (Felin and Zenger 2014); firms can also take an external approach to innovation by acquiring and processing resources from external partners to build innovations (Chesbrough 2003). Learning from partner firms and tapping knowledge residing in the external environment are critically important for innovation (Powell et al. 1996, Ahuja 2000, Bao et al. 2012). SMEs can leverage both internal and externa approaches to innovation. Employees of SMEs, where organizational structures are less complex, have more flexibility to innovate than employees at large firms (Terziovski 2010). In addition, SMEs benefit from engaging with and leveraging external partnership networks that help them overcome the challenges associated with resource constraints (Human and Provan 1997).

Building on the concept of closed innovation, we define ITC as the extent to which a firm’s internal information systems are used to promote innovation inside the firm. Firms with a high level of ITC deploy IT to a greater extent to transfer and integrate internal knowledge and resources. For example, IDEO, a global design company, uses an IT system internally to help its employees share their designs for new product development across departments within the firm (Andrade Rojas and Kathuria 2014). Here, ITC is a practiced routine that executes internal absorptive capacity metaroutines because ITC facilitates product development and process design within the firm (Chatterjee et al. 2015, Go´mez et al. 2017, Ravichandran et al. 2017, Tarafdar and Tanriverdi 2018, Saldanha et al. 2020b).

Specifically, we posit four ways in which ITC is a practiced routine that executes the internal absorptive capacity metaroutines highlighted by Lewin et al. (2011). First, ITC enables internal cross-functional integration and teamwork, which facilitate the recombination of knowledge for new ideas to emerge (Nambisan 2003), thus supporting the internal absorptive capacity metaroutine facilitating variation. For instance, ITCenabled integration of diverse functional information and internal knowledge sources increases the potential of finding novel linkages in existing knowledge (Sethi et al. 2003, Saldanha et al. 2020b). In addition, ITC promotes collaboration among cross-functional and design teams (Teo et al. 2007, Kohli and Melville 2009) to support the emergence of new and diverse ideas, thus improving product quality and reducing product cycle time and development cost (Banker et al. 2006). Second, ITC supports the internal absorptive capacity metaroutine sharing knowledge and practices in the firm (Lewin et al. 2011) by helping firms capture, store, retrieve, and disseminate internal knowledge (Kleis et al. 2012, Xue et al. 2012, Benitez et al. 2018), aiding internal task organization and data processing (Yoo et al. 2010) and enhancing internal coordination efficiency and communication in the innovation process (Wang et al. 2013). For example, ITC, in the form of internal enterprise social networks, encourages internal cross-functional social interactions and knowledge sharing (Saldanha et al. 2020b), thereby serving as a digital expression of sharing knowledge and practices in the firm. Third, ITC improves internal information processing and coordination capabilities, which help firms evaluate and select ideas and reflect upon and improve practices. Thus, ITC supports the internal absorptive capacity metaroutine reflecting and updating (Lewin et al. 2011). Finally, ITC helps firms monitor the progress of projects and efficiently allocate resources to projects by providing real-time feedback and tracking of projects and resources within the firm (Mithas et al. 2011, Yeow and Goh 2015). Thus, ITC supports the internal absorptive capacity metaroutine managing internal selection regimes (Lewin et al. 2011). Recall that we did not select the metaroutine managing adaptive tension because it lies at the intersection of internal and external absorptive capacity routines. Further, this metaroutine involves comparison with the external environment and entails selecting a comparison group, obtaining external information, and weighting that external information (Massini et al. 2005, Lewin et al. 2011). ITC does not facilitate this metaroutine because, by definition, ITC lies within the organization and therefore does not help the firm obtain external information and compare its goals with the external environment.

Building on the concept of open innovation (Ches brough 2003), we define ITO as the extent to which a firm uses IT to collaborate for innovation with its exter nal constituents, such as market leaders, suppliers, com petitors, and clients. ITO is a practiced routine that executes external absorptive capacity metaroutines. Fo example, ITO enhances innovation via crowdsourcing and open innovation platforms (e.g., the “mystarbucksidea” platform developed by Starbucks) that help firms source new product ideas from customers (Majchrzak and Malhotra 2013). ITO also aids open innovation by enabling collaborative practices with external stake holders (Sawhney et al. 2005, Tambe et al. 2012, Salda nha et al. 2017), facilitating interfirm partnerships (Ettlie and Pavlou 2006), and enhancing the search for externa knowledge (Dong and Netten 2017, Go´mez et al. 2017). ITO promotes access to external knowledge through the firm’s coordination with its upstream or down stream partners (Yang et al. 2009, Trantopoulos et al. 2017, Karhade and Dong 2021b). Knowledge created via IT-based information exchange with suppliers facilitates innovation in the supply chain. Thus, ITO facilitates the following external absorptive capacity metaroutines: (a) learning from and with various external constituents and (b) identifying and recognizing the value of externally generated knowledge (Lewin et al. 2011). Recall that we did not select the metaroutine transferring knowledge back to the organization because it lies at the intersection of internal and external absorptive capacity routines. Further, because this metaroutine involves “linking external knowledge with firm’s in-house capabilities,” it refers to the internal distribution of externally acquired knowledge (Lewin et al. 2011, p. 90). Because ITO focuses on collaboration with externa constituents only, ITO does not facilitate the interna distribution of knowledge; rather, the internal distribution of knowledge is facilitated by ITC. Hence, ITO does not execute the metaroutine transferring knowledge back to the organization. ## 2.4. Technological Deficiency and Government Support Deficiency for Innovation in SMEs

Technological capability—consisting of technological knowledge, skills, and advanced technologies—plays an important role in facilitating firm innovation (Hsieh and Tsai 2007), especially in SMEs. The presence of skilled technological personnel and advanced technological knowhow helps an SME utilize different resources in the pursuit of innovation (Anderson and Tushman 1990). Skilled technological personnel can identify and leverage market technological trends to engage in product innovation (Zhou and Wu 2010), which helps a firm adapt to changing environments (Teece et al. 1997). Better utilization of advanced technologies in research and development (R&D) enhances the efficiency and effectiveness of new product development and process design (Arnold and Thuriaux 1997).

Government support for innovation results in benefits for firms. Government support can be in the form of tax incentives, grants, state-sponsored labs, or direct investment by means of public venture capital, and it can influence a firm’s innovation outputs in the form of products and services (Jugend et al. 2018). Thus, government support helps firms enhance their internal abilities (other than technological capabilities) by additional means, termed additionalities (Buisseret et al. 1995, Rojas et al. 2018). Thereby, government support positively influences innovation output for firms in general (Jugend et al. 2018) and SMEs in particular (Guo et al. 2016). Through financial incentives, policies, and information, government support provides SMEs access to knowledge and helps them assimilate this knowledge into their innovation process (Vanhaverbeke et al. 2018). Given the importance of technological capability and government support for innovation, we expect that technological deficiency and government support deficiency hurt SMEs’ innovation output. Next, we develop hypotheses by drawing on the concept of metaroutines: microfoundations of absorptive capacity that are expressed (i.e., executed) in organizations in the form of observable practiced routines (e.g., ITC and ITO, as noted earlier) (Lewin et al. 2011). ## 2.5. Overcoming Technological Deficiency: The Role of ITC and ITO

Technological deficiency refers to the extent to which a firm lacks technological abilities, such as the staff’s technological capabilities, technological information, and advanced technologies (Afuah 2002, Zhou and Wu 2010).<sup>9</sup> Innovation output depends on a firm’s technological capability, which encompasses the processes, people, and tools that help a firm quickly and effectively generate new ideas and solutions for the market. SMEs, in particular, face technological deficiency because they generally possess limited resources and have low perceived legitimacy (Thornhill and Amit 2003), constraining their ability to acquire, manage, and retain complex technological facts, quality employees, and advanced technologies through markets and strategic networks (Baum et al. 2000). Without an ability to identify technological opportunities and without technologically trained staff, it is difficult for SMEs to develop market, product, and process innovations (Zhou and Wu 2010).

We argue that ITC mitigates the negative effect of technological deficiency on the innovation output of SMEs for two reasons. First, technological deficiency results in resistance to technological changes for innovation. For example, SME staff may resist changes in manufacturing technology, thereby hampering product innovation. SMEs can use ITC to expose employees to new ways of developing products, familiarize staff with new technologies and processes through knowledge sharing, and thereby reduce staff resistance to technological changes. This helps SMEs overcome the detrimental effect of technological deficiency on innovation output (Joshi et al. 2010). As SMEs are small and resource constrained, technologically deficient SMEs lack information-sharing resources and capabilities (Voss and Voss 2013). Because ITC (e.g., internal IT-enabled social networks) facilitates knowledge sharing, ITC digitally executes the internal absorptive capacity metaroutine sharing knowledge and superior practices (Saldanha et al. 2020b, Karhade and Dong 2021b), which enhances the understanding of new technologies across the firm. ITC facilitates learning through problemistic search processes (Anand et al. 2020, Karhade and Dong 2021b) and knowledge codification, enabling SMEs to digitally execute the internal absorptive capacity metaroutine reflecting, updating, and replicating (Cyert and March 1963, Lewin et al. 2011). In addition, ITC helps firms manage and allocate resources to different projects (Mithas et al, 2011, Yeow and Goh 2015), thereby facili tating the internal absorptive capacity metaroutine managing internal selection regimes. For example, ITC, such as enterprise social network technologies and multimedia online toolkits, enhances the prioritization, selection, and expediting of ideas with the greatest potential for commercialization, thus allowing firms to focus their resources on introducing new products and services to market (Joshi et al. 2010).

Second, technological deficiency creates difficulties in identifying innovation opportunities. ITC helps SMEs overcome a lack of technological knowhow by bringing together functional units and amalgamating diverse technical knowledge. SMEs have more adaptable and flexible organizational structures because of their small size (Terziovski 2010). Because of these characteristics of SMEs, ITC facilitates the amalgamation of technological knowledge, thereby digitally expressing the metaroutine facilitating variation (Lewin et al. 2011). This helps SMEs explore new ideas, thus improving the identification of innovation opportunities and overcoming the deleterious effects of technological deficiency (Ravichandran et al. 2017). For example, ManufCo (anonymized for confidentiality), a manufacturing SME in Mexico, implemented an IT-enabled platform that facilitated variation through digital combination and recombination of knowledge inside the firm, which eventually led to cross-business innovations. SMEs with a high level of ITC can deploy IT systems (e.g., collaboration systems such as Microsoft Teams) that help overcome the harmful effects of technological deficiency by supporting idea development and knowledge recombination within the firm (Bardhan et al. 2013, Saldanha et al. 2020b). As another example, internal microblogging systems facilitate knowledge recombination and knowledge sharing, leading to new product development.

In sum, ITC digitally executes internal absorptive capacity metaroutines (Lewin et al. 2011), thereby helping SMEs overcome technological deficiency and increase their innovation output. Hence, we hypothesize the following: 10

Hypothesis H1A. ITC attenuates the negative relationship between technological deficiency and the innovation output of SMEs.

We theorize that ITO mitigates the negative effect of technological deficiency on the innovation output of SMEs for three reasons. First, ITO helps SMEs access the knowledge residing in their external constituents and imbibe external insights into creating innovations, thereby helping to overcome their deficiencies in technological knowhow and technological skills. Such deficiencies are particularly salient because of the small size and limited resources of SMEs. For example, ITO helps firms collaborate with local suppliers to gain insights into market needs to create products for less affluent customers (Khuntia et al. 2021). That is, ITO enables SMEs to tap into technological knowhow outside the firm to develop innovative products at low price points (Furr and Dyer 2015). ITO thus enhances an SME’s ability to access external information (Cohen and Levinthal 1990, Foss et al. 2011, Saldanha et al. 2017) and thereby digitally execute the external absorp tive capacity metaroutine learning from and with partners, suppliers, customers, competitors, and consultants (Lewin et al. 2011). Automotive manufacturing SMEs, for example, can use supply chain management systems to collaborate with their suppliers to design new parts.

Second, SMEs possess low perceived legitimacy, making it difficult to project themselves as attractive employers for technological staff (Wales et al. 2013). SMEs struggle to hire and retain technologically capable staff, which adversely affects their innovation output; however, knowledge obtained via IT-based collaboration with external constituents (ITO) can mitigate this deficiency. For example, SMEs can access knowledge regarding new production methods or codevelop processes with their suppliers’ technologically capable staff (Khuntia et al. 2021) to overcome their own deficiency of technologically capable staff. ITO thus digitally executes the external absorptive capacity metaroutine learning from and with partners, suppliers, customers, competitors, and consultants (Lewin et al. 2011) by enabling access to technological knowhow and expertise beyond an SME’s boundaries.

Third, ITO facilitates searching and monitoring the external environment to help contextualize external information (Dong and Netten 2017). ITO provides opportunities for SMEs to absorb and interpret information from the external environment. Thus, ITO is a digital expression of the external absorptive capacity metaroutine identification and recognition of value of externally generated knowledge (Lewin et al. 2011). By executing this metaroutine, ITO helps SMEs overcome their technological deficiency related to the identification of opportunities. For example, IT-enabled interactions with suppliers, customers, and partners provide SMEs with insights into the technologies and actions of their partners. As another example, by facilitating monitoring and contextual interpretation of knowledge obtained from its partners, ITO can help a manufacturing SME innovate its manufacturing process by utilizing a partner’s knowledge as a way to overcome the SME’s technological deficiency. Hence, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis H1B. ITO attenuates the negative relationship between technological deficiency and the innovation output of SMEs.

Our next hypothesis concerns the relative strength of the attenuating effects of ITC and ITO. As discussed earlier, ITC digitally executes internal absorptive capacity metaroutines (Lewin et al. 2011) because it supports knowledge sharing across functional units and facilitates understanding of new technologies across the firm, thus improving the identification of innovation opportunities (Saldanha et al. 2020b, Karhade and Dong 2021b). In contrast, ITO digitally executes external absorptive capacity metaroutines because it facilitates access to external knowledge and learning from external partners via information sharing across firms.

SMEs with technological deficiency are resource constrained in terms of the advanced technologies, knowhow, and skills that are necessary to fully benefit from the external knowledge accessed through ITO (Dooley et al. 2016, Vanhaverbeke et al. 2018). Because ITO is a digital expression of external absorptive capacity metaroutines and focuses on developing innovations in partnership with external constituents, the knowledge gained from ITO is less beneficial in overcoming technological deficiency, as SMEs may find it challenging to internalize and integrate the knowledge gained externally into the firm. For example, because of its technological deficiency, an SME that uses ITO to learn from a supplier how to use a specific material for a new product may not be able to replicate the process on its own and use the material for a different product. In contrast, ITC is more beneficial in overcoming technological deficiency because ITC facilitates learning and transfer of knowledge within the firm, which helps the SME apply this knowledge across different products and overcome its lack of knowhow and skills. ITC enables the internalization of knowledge in SMEs by facilitating the integration and implementation of information on practices and aids in the storage, management, and retrieval of knowledge. ITC enables the SME to share cross-functional knowledge across business units, which helps solve business challenges unique to the SME. For instance, to innovate in the manufacturing process of a product, ITC helps SME staff gain detailed knowledge about the process and learn the SME’s norms and practices. Further, as discussed earlier, ITC helps manage internal selection regimes, thereby facilitating resource allocation, which is particularly important in overcoming technological deficiency in SMEs that are usually resource constrained. More specifically, because SMEs are constrained in their resources (or technologically deficient), selecting the right projects and efficiently allocating resources among them to convert ideas into innovation are crucial processes. Thus, compared with ITO, which is focused on collaboration with external partners, ITC more directly addresses technological deficiency by helping SMEs internalize knowledge critical for innovation. Hence, we posit the following:

Hypothesis H1C. ITC has a stronger attenuating effect than ITO on the negative relationship between technological deficiency and the innovation output of SMEs.

## 2.6. Overcoming Government Support Deficiency: The Role of ITC and ITO

There are two mechanisms by which government support promotes innovation in SMEs. The first mechanism is input additionality, which refers to how government support through programs such as subsidies, funding, or firm-government research alliances enhances an SME’s innovation efforts by increasing the resource inputs avail able to the SME (Buisseret et al. 1995, Rojas et al. 2018). For example, by providing subsidies or tax relief, or via legislative actions, governments can ensure that SMEs have more resources to engage in innovation. The second mechanism is behavioral additionality, which refers to the effects of government support and policy interventions in facilitating the adaptation or change of a “firm’s innovation processes, routines, activities, or relevant corporate business/technology strategies, thereby facilitating the conversion of innovation inputs into outcomes” (Rojas et al. 2018, p. 1113). For example, by providing financial and training support, governments can facilitate changes to an SME’s innovation processes. The concepts of input additionality and behavioral additionality imply that government support provides a way for a resourceconstrained SME to enhance its internal abilities through external means beyond those that are available to the SME internally.

We theorize that ITC mitigates the negative effect of government support deficiency on the innovation output of SMEs for two reasons. First, government support deficiency implies a deficiency in input additionality. ITC enables SMEs to mitigate this deficiency by facilitating deep and rich collaborative communications across functional units (Saldanha et al. 2020b). SMEs generally lack such structured means of communication and collaboration to facilitate the transfer of information across the firm (Terziovski 2010). By facilitating knowledge sharing, ITC digitally executes the internal absorptive capacity metaroutine sharing knowledge and superior practices across the organization. Access to detailed knowledge helps functional units uncover input and output substitutions, enabling SMEs to benefit from resource pooling. In a multiproduct SME, for example, a product team using ITC may uncover the substitutability of an input in its product (e.g., an electric component) with the input of a product from another product team, enabling the SME to benefit from economies of scale. Such resource pooling benefits of ITC enable SMEs to overcome the deficiency in input additionality arising from government support deficiency.

Second, ITC facilitates changes in managerial and organizational practices, thereby helping SMEs overcome the deficit of behavioral additionality arising from government support deficiency. ITC reduces the structural and cognitive barriers that impede knowledge sharing, thus enhancing the efficiency and effectiveness of knowledge exchange within the SME (Nahapiet and Ghoshal 1998). Thus, ITC enriches and updates the SME’s knowledge base and digitally executes the internal absorptive capacity metaroutine reflecting, updating, and replicating (Lewin et al. 2011). This is particularly important for SMEs because they have a limited knowledge base because of their small size and limited resources. Further, ITC enables the decomposition of complex innovation processes into subtasks and their coordination by organizational actors in a manner that facilitates interconnections to meet situational demands (Weick and Roberts 1993). These interconnections among various organizational actors facilitate the emergence of new ideas; in this way, ITC is a digital expression of the internal absorptive capacity metaroutine facilitating variation (Lewin et al. 2011). Through these mechanisms, ITC enables SMEs to innovate ways to organize the making, assembly, and delivery of products (Zahra and George 2002). Thus, ITC supplements behavioral additionality and enables SMEs to overcome the void left by government support deficiency. Hence, we posit the following:

Hypothesis H2A. ITC attenuates the negative relationship between government support deficiency and the innovation output of SMEs.

We theorize that ITO mitigates the negative effect of government support deficiency on innovation output for two reasons. First, as discussed earlier, because ITO is a digital expression of the external absorptive capacity metaroutine learning from and with external constituent such as partners, suppliers, and customers (Lewin et al. 2011), ITO expands an SME’s knowledge pool (Ahuja et al. 2008). For example, in India, the SME partners of SBI Life Insurance leverage an IT system to share and receive information from one another, thus enabling the units receiving this information to imbibe customer insights into new products (Khuntia et al. 2019). ITO develops digital linkages between SMEs and their external constituents, thereby forming an information network that facilitates knowledge spillovers from external constituents to the focal SME (Tafti et al. 2013). This helps the SME overcome its lack of the broad and deep knowledge base required for complex and multifaceted scientific discovery (Yang et al. 2014). Thus, by digitally executing the external absorptive capacity metaroutine learning from and with external constituents, ITO expands the SME’s knowledge pool and supplements input additionality. Hence, ITO helps SMEs overcome the void left by government support deficiency with regard to input additionality.

Second, ITO fuels the flow of knowledge and interactions between an SME and its external constituents. This helps the SME adapt its processes based on the best practices of its external constituents, which it can do faster and at lower switching costs than large firms (Qian and Li 2003). For instance, analytics-based customer feedback systems facilitate real-time detailed feedback on products that can spark changes to production processes. ITO enables IT-based collaboration linkages between firms, which in turn enables those firms to access external knowledge and best practices (Sambamurthy et al. 2003). ITO is thus a digital expression of the external absorptive capacity metaroutine identifying and recognizing the value of externally generated knowledge (Lewin et al. 2011). By facilitating the adaptation of processes, ITO helps SMEs overcome the void left by government support deficiency with regard to behavioral additionality.

In sum, because ITO is a digital expression of external absorptive capacity metaroutines related to (a) learning from and with external constituents such as part ners, suppliers, and customers and (b) identifying and recognizing the value of externally generated knowledge, ITO helps SMEs find alternative means to transcend the limitations they encounter when it comes to innovation. These alternative means thus help SMEs overcome the void left by government support deficiency with regard to input additionality and behavioral additionality. These arguments resonate with prior research suggesting that traditional (not IT-based) partnering with external constituents is an effective way for SMEs to acquire new capabilities and overcome deficiencies in government support (Pervan et al. 2015). Hence, we posit the following:<sup>11</sup>

Hypothesis H2B. ITO attenuates the negative relationship between government support deficiency and the innovation output of SMEs.

Our final hypothesis concerns the relative strengths of the attenuating effects of ITC and ITO. As noted earlier, through input additionality and behavioral additionality, government support provides a way for an SME to enhance its internal abilities by external means. Because government support deficiency is a deficiency in external support to the SME, such deficiency is more aptly overcome by external mechanisms. ITO facilitates collaboration between an SME and its partners, which enables access to knowledge bases residing in external organizations. ITO is a digital expression of the external absorptive capacity metaroutine learning from and with external constituents such as partners, suppliers, and customers because it expands the SME’s knowledge pool and exposes the SME to best practices (Lewin et al. 2011). These mechanisms supplement input additionality and behavioral additionality, thus overcoming the void left by government support deficiency (Buisseret et al. 1995, Rojas et al. 2018). ITO helps the SME acquire information about which innovation opportunities to seize, and to search and access diversified knowledge sources for pursuing innovation (Dong and Netten 2017). Because of the external nature of government support deficiency, these ITO mechanisms are particularly important in overcoming its harmful impact.

In contrast, ITC focuses on knowledge and information integration within the SME’s boundaries, which facilitates resource pooling and process efficiency and hence provides some input additionality and behavioral additionality. However, ITC is not as effective as ITO in providing input additionality and behavioral additionality because, without external sources, the additionalities provided by ITC are less than those provided by ITO. Because knowledge obtained through ITC is constrained within the SME and does not transcend the SME’s boundaries, ITC is not an expression of the external absorptive capacity metaroutines learning from and with external constituents and identifying and recognizing the value of externally generated knowledge. In comparison, ITO leverages external interorganizational relationships, which means it is an effective digital expression of the two external absorptive capacity metaroutines listed above (Uzzi and Lancaster 2003). Accordingly, ITO is more effective than ITC in filling the void left by government support deficiency in terms of input additionality and behavioral additionality. Hence, we posit the following:

Hypothesis H2C. ITO has a stronger attenuating effect than ITC on the negative relationship between government support deficiency and the innovation output of SMEs.

## 3. Method

## 3.1. Sampling and Data Collection

To test our hypotheses, we use data from manufacturing SMEs in Mexico, an apt empirical context for several reasons. First, SMEs in Mexico face challenges with respect to innovation and technological capabilities (Riquelme 2018), and there is widespread recognition of the need to overcome deficiencies toward innovation in Mexico (Romo 2020). There is also a general paucity of and wide variation in government support for SMEs across municipalities, industries, and firm sizes (Arana 2018). Second, SMEs comprise roughly 99.8% of firms in Mexico, employ 78% of the workforce, and contribute 42% to the national income (Arana 2018). Third, with advances in Mexico’s IT infrastructure, an increasing proportion (63%) of Mexico’s population has Internet access (Asociacio´n de Internet de Me´xico 2017), and SMEs in Mexico widely use IT to improve their business and overcome operational challenges (IBP 2015). Finally, using SMEs from manufacturing sectors is appropriate because service sectors have different innovation practices (Joshi et al. 2010, Saldanha et al. 2017). This approach is similar to approaches in innovation studies in information systems (IS), management, and innovation literatures that also use firms in manufacturing industries (e.g., Patel and Pavitt 1997; He and Wong 2004; Wagner 2007; Kleis et al. 2012; Saldanha et al. 2017, 2020b).

We use unique longitudinal archival data spanning two years from 319 manufacturing SMEs in the State of Jalisco in Mexico.<sup>12</sup> The data were collected by the Government of Mexico using the same approach as an economic census. like census data in many countries, such as the United States (U.S. Census Bureau 2020), these data were collected using a survey instrument. A key strength of our study is its collaborative nature— survey design and data collection involved academics and the government, which is in the spirit of engaged scholarship espoused by prior research (Van de Ven 2007, Rai 2019). The survey was codeveloped by operationalizing constructs that utilized existing scales or by adapting scales from prior literature. Then, a government institution collected the data from SMEs’ senio managers and verified the data using a multipronged approach: it (1) obtained supporting documents from SMEs’ senior managers, (2) collected the same data from other managers, and (3) compared the managers responses with information obtained from government records. The data were collected as part of a government-funded initiative by a government-affiliated institution, which implemented this data collection exercise as part of the government’s plan to evaluate the innovation ecosystem in Jalisco. Because the data collection process was organized and conducted by the government, all data collection procedures followed existing governmental regulations.<sup>13</sup> Interviewers were recruited and trained by the government institution to conduct surveys on-site, which is an effective method for collecting information in emerging economies (Kathuria et al. 2018, Khuntia et al. 2019).<sup>14</sup> The interviewers visited each SME to conduct the survey face-to-face, identified themselves with government-supported credentials, provided the survey, explained the survey’s purpose, clarified questions, and collected the completed survey.

The Government of Mexico’s approach to data collection via surveys of senior managers is similar to procedures used by governments and international institutions (e.g., the World Bank) globally to collect data. For instance, the U.S. Census Bureau collects data via an “Annual Survey of Manufacturers,” a singlesourced self-reported survey (U.S. Census Bureau 2020) recognized as “the best current measure of current U.S. manufacturing industry outputs, inputs, and operating status” (U.S. Census Bureau 2021). U.S. Census data have been used extensively in academic research (e.g., Black and Lynch 2001, Atrostic and Nguyen 2005, Saldanha et al. 2013). This approach is also similar to InformationWeek’s approach to data collection, which is widely used in IS research (e.g., Bharadwaj et al. 1999, Mithas et al. 2012, Sabherwal et al. 2019). In addition, the Government of Mexico collected the data in accordance with the laws of the State of Jalisco. These laws authorize the State of Jalisco’s government to collect firm-level data related to organizational inputs and outputs to assess firm performance. If the SME was included in the selected random sample, the government institution contacted the SME by phone to inform them that an interviewer would be coming to their premises to conduct a survey face-to-face. Senior SME managers who participated in prior data collection exercises pointed out that after they were contacted, they arranged an internal data and document collection process. This process may have involved different departments and middle managers, such that when interviewers arrived at the SME’s location, the senior manager was fully prepared to provide accurate and valid information with supporting documents. The laws state the legal obligations of the interviewed managers, which include but are not limited to answering all questions in the survey and providing accurate answers supported by documentary evidence. Finally, the laws protect the confidentiality of respondents such that responses would not be released publicly in a manner that individually identifies the respondents or the SMEs.

In sum, this unique archival data set offers several advantages over commonly used secondary data. To ensure the collection of valid and reliable data, the data collection protocol was designed following scientifically rigorous and academically sound processes, thus ensuring the collection of valid and reliable data. The Government of Mexico selected the sample from a government population directory by following a stratified random sampling approach, and it is hence representative of the population (Carpenter and Fredrickson 2001). Further, the data were collected by a government agency, and respondents were legally bound to provide accurate and verifiable responses. As a result, measurement error, social desirability, and respondents’ cognitive biases were all reduced. Moreover, before leaving each SME location, the interviewers checked the survey response to ensure that the information was complete and that they had the required data. Hence, our measures are precise, the sample is representative, and the data are accurate, thus making the data a key strength of our study relative to other studies. Further, the IT measures cap tured how IT was used for innovation activities, which represents an improvement over prior studies that proxy IT usage with measures such as IT investment dollars (e.g., Saldanha et al. 2020a). Finally, the collaborative nature of the survey design and execution by academics and the government is in the spirit of engaged scholarship (Van de Ven 2007, Rai 2019). Online Appendix B details the survey design and data collection process conducted by the Government of Mexico.

The government institution used several practices during survey design and data collection to minimize common method bias and measurement error. First, public announcements explaining the data collection enabled senior managers to gather data in advance from different individuals and departments in the SME. When the government institution sent interviewers to collect data on-site, senior managers prepared the required information and documentary evidence. Second, when the interviewers arrived on-site, the managers were required to provide documentary evidence of their responses, as required by the laws of the State of Jalisco. Third, data for firm size, ownership, and sales were obtained from official government records. Thus, the data are accurate and from multiple sources, thereby alleviating concerns of common method bias and measurement error.

## 3.3. Variables

The measures were adapted from existing studies except when existing measures were not available. The variables technological deficiency, government support deficiency, ITC, and ITO use Likert-type scales (scale anchors are one, very low, to four, very high), whereas Innovation Output (our dependent variable) is a count-based variable. Table 1 reports the variable operationalizations and survey questions.

3.3.1. Innovation Output. Following prior literature, we use innovation output data of the SME one year subsequent to the year of the independent variables (i.e., the deficiencies, ITC, and ITO), thus incorporating a time lag from the inputs to the outputs (Wadhwa and Kotha 2006; Bharadwaj et al. 2007; Sampson 2007; Rai et al. 2015; Saldanha et al. 2017, 2021). The Innovation Output measure, based on He and Wong (2004) and

Jansen et al. (2006), captures the total number of innovation outputs by the SME in (a) products, (b) manufacturing processes, (c) markets (commercialization), and (d) administrative processes. Thus, Innovation Output is a count-based variable that measures the total number of innovation outputs in the above four categories. As such, Innovation Output captures the strength and coverage of innovation output by the SME. Because our focus is on SMEs, our measure is more appropriate than patent-based measures because patent applications are prohibitively time consuming and expensive for typical SMEs in Mexico.

3.3.2. Technological Deficiency. Consistent with measures suggested by prior research (Gatignon and Xuereb 1997, Song et al. 2005, Zhou and Wu 2010), technological deficiency is measured by five items that capture the extent to which the SME has the following: (a) lack of important technological information, (b) inability to identify new technology opportunities, (c) resistance to technology changes, (d) lack of technological capabilities in staff, and (e) lack of training to master state-of-the-art technologies.<sup>15</sup>

3.3.3. Government Support Deficiency. This measure comes from Li and Atuahene-Gima (2001) and Shu et al. (2016) and comprises four items that capture the extent to which the government has not provided the SME with (a) necessary information support, (b) support to import equipment when needed, (c) direct financial support such as tax reductions and subsidies, and (d) legal support to enter a new market. Because the State of Jalisco has 125 municipalities, there is significant variation in local government policies across the municipalities, resulting in significant variation in the government support deficiency variable in our sample.<sup>16</sup>

Although one may question whether responses to this variable are accurate as the data were collected by the government, obtaining accurate responses about government support deficiency was possible because of several reasons. First, Articles 6 and 7 of Mexico’s con stitution guarantee freedom of expression and suggest that any person has the right to think and share their ideas and opinions with anyone by any means. For instance, in 2019, more than 15,000 people gathered in Mexico City and protested the government’s economic policies (Rosales A<sup>´</sup> valos 2019). In 2022, several Mexican TV personalities made videos and used social media to directly criticize the Mexican president and the ecological impact of a government-funded project related to the development of a tourist train in the Mayan jungle (De Miguel 2022). Thus, it is not uncommon for Mexican citizens to express their dissatisfaction with the government and its policies (Freedom House 2023). Second, the government institution adopted several tactics to prevent biases in the responses related to government support deficiency: (1) respondents were assured about the anonymity of their responses, in terms of both their identity and that of the SME; (2) questions related to government support deficiency appeared and were asked later in the survey after participants had time to build rapport and feel more comfortable with the interviewers, which encouraged the respondents to be honest about government deficiencies; and (3) questions related to government support deficiency were phrased to capture actual situations, not assign blame. Similar approaches are used by international institutions such as the World Bank to reduce social desirability biases and capture answers to questions about sensitive topics related to government and its policies (Birhanu et al. 2016, World Bank Group 2022).<sup>17</sup>

3.3.4. ITC. We developed the measure of ITC based on the conceptual work of Marchand et al. (2000) and Za´rraga-Rodr´ıguez and Alvarez (2013). Our measure of ITC represents the SME’s use of internal information systems to promote innovation within the firm and is formed by three items that capture the extent to which (a) information systems staff promote activities that can help innovate within the firm, (b) the use of software applications inside the firm promotes activities that can help innovate, and (c) use of information systems inside the firm helps to build an organized process for creating innovations. In line with the literature on closed innovation, these items represent how the SME’s IT-based innovation efforts are internally focused. Examples of software applications within the firm are internal IT-based social enterprise systems (Saldanha et al. 2020b) and computer-based design systems.

3.3.5. ITO. This measure, adapted from prior research (Hagedoorn 1995, Liedtka 1996, Leiponen and Drejer 2007, Gnyawali and Park 2011), consists of four items that capture the extent to which the SME uses IT for collaboration activities in the pursuit of innovation with the following external constituents: (a) market leaders, (b) competitors, (c) clients, and (d) suppliers. This measure reflects the principle of open innovation by focusing on how the SME uses IT to develop innovations with external stakeholders (Chesbrough 2003). For example, ITO can include an interorganizational system used for innovation-related collaborations with suppliers.

Because ITC and ITO capture how IT is used, our findings are largely spatiotemporally generalizable and are not bound to specific technologies. Our measurement approach is consistent with studies that recommend examining the performance implications of IT by employing nuanced measures of how IT is used rather than by looking at the deployment of specific technologies (Devaraj and Kohli 2003, Hsieh et al. 2011, Anand et al. 2020). As discussed earlier, ITC and ITO are inputs into business activities that generate innovation. In contrast, product, process, and market innovations are outputs of business activities. This distinction between innovation input and output is well recognized in innovation research (Ahuja et al. 2008, Duran et al. 2016).<sup>18</sup>

3.3.6. Control Variables. We include several control variables that account for heterogeneity. Because R&D may influence innovation output, we measure R&D expenses by the percentage of sales dedicated to R&D by the SME (Cohen and Levinthal 1990). We control for firm size (measured as logarithm of number of employees) to account for resource availability and economies of scale. We use four dummy variables to account for differences in the primary industry in which the manufacturing SME operates: food, fashion and design, electronics, and automobiles, with plastics as the baseline. This industry classification was provided by the Mexican Bureau of Statistics as part of the Economic Census. As financial resources have been linked to innovation outcomes (Hoegl et al. 2008), we control for the firm’s annual sales (log transformed). Because foreign firms enjoy advantages in innovation output (Un 2011), we control for ownership, measured as a dummy variable to classify national and foreign firms. Finally, because SMEs located in nonmetropolitan areas have limited access to resources and talent, we use a dummy variable to control for the nonmetro politan or metropolitan location of the SME.

## 3.4. Construct Validity

We model ITC as a reflective construct. ITO, government support deficiency, and technological deficiency are modeled as formative constructs because they meet the criteria of coverage of the construct domain and lack of covariance among indicators (Diamantopoulos and Winklhofer 2001). First, each item makes unique contributions to the constructs and can be viewed as “forming” them. For example, the items for government support deficiency capture deficiency in information support, import of equipment, tax reduction, and legal support. Second, an increase in any one item does not necessarily increase others. A reduction in taxes, for instance, does not necessarily imply a reduction in legal support. Third, the items comprising each construct are distinct and not interchangeable (Petter et al. 2007).

Because our model contains reflective and formative constructs for each year, we adopted a multistep approach to assess the validity and adequacy of the multiple-item measures (Kathuria et al. 2018). First, we conducted yearly principal component analyses (PCA) with varimax rotation, which generated the expected number of factors, with high loadings (above 0.70) and low cross-loadings (below 0.30). Second, to assess the reflective construct, ITC, we conducted yearly CFA. The variables exhibited sufficient construct reliability with Cronbach’s alpha above 0.85, which exceeds the minimum recommended threshold of 0.80. Composite reliability of the construct was above the 0.70 benchmark (Zhou and Wu 2010, Poppo et al. 2016), demonstrating satisfactory internal consistency reliability. We then assessed convergent validity based on outer loadings and average variance extracted (AVE). Item loadings were significant $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ and exceeded the recommended 0.70 threshold (Hair et al. 2021). The AVE was higher than the 0.50 benchmark (Zhou and Wu 2010, Poppo et al. 2016). Third, we assessed discriminant validity via the heterotrait-monotrait ratio, which was below the conservative threshold of 0.85 for conceptual distinctness (Kline 2015). Fourth, we assessed the remaining formative constructs using different criteria. We assessed convergent and discriminant validity by examining the weight, sign, and magnitude of the items. Item weightings were significant $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ signs were consistent with theory, and magnitudes of weights were greater than 0.10. The average weight of items for each construct was less than ${ \sqrt { 1 / N } } ,$ where N is the number of items specified. Though a couple of items did not meet the thresholds recommended in prior research, they were retained to preserve content validity and ensure that the entire domain of the construct was measured (Kathuria et al. 2018, Hair et al. 2021). Fifth, because variance inflation factors at the item level were less than 3.3, multicollinearity is not a concern. These assessments indicated that the formative measures demonstrate satisfactory convergent and discriminant validity. Overall, the results showed that our measures possess satisfactory validity and reliability. ## 4.2. Endogeneity and Robustness Tests

We conducted several robustness tests, a summary of which is shown in Table 4. We followed four different approaches to address endogeneity concerns. First, to address sample selection bias concerns, we conducted the Heckman (1979) two-step econometric procedure that was developed for management research by Shaver (1998). Following the approach used in several studies in the IS literature (e.g., Bharadwaj et al. 2007, Chen and Bharadwaj 2009, Zhao and Wry 2016, Go´mez et al. 2017, Saldanha et al. 2017, Khuntia et al. 2019), we split our sample into two groups based on the sum of the ITC and ITO variables. We created a binary variable, Total IT

Use for Open and Closed Innovation Activities (TOCI), which was coded as one for SMEs for which the sum was above the sample mean and zero for SMEs with the sum equal to or below the mean. Following Shaver (1998), we estimated a probit model of TOCI (as a dependent variable) on variables expected to influence the extent to which SMEs pursue ITC and ITO. In the first stage, we include industry mean $I T C ,$ industry mean ITO, firm size, and four industry dummy variables as covariates, where industry mean ITC and industry mean ITO serve as exclusion restrictions that aid in model identification (Greene 2003). We used the mean industry-level ITC and mean industry-level ITO (excluding the focal firm) as instruments for SME-level ITC and ITO, respectively. Mean industry-level ITC and ITO are good instruments for SME-level ITC and ITO because firms are likely to be influenced by industry norms (i.e., IT use by other firms in the industry is likely to drive IT use in the focal firm) (Barla 2007, Salge et al. 2015, Dong and Netten 2017). Also, industry-level ITC and ITO are unlikely to influence the innovation of the focal SME directly except via their influence on the SME’s ITC or ITO. Hence, industry-level ITC and ITO are good instruments because they are correlated with the potentially endogenous variables (ITC, ITO) and uncorrelated with the error term in the outcome (innovation) equation. Our use of industry-level ITC and ITO as instruments for SME-level ITC and ITO follows a tra dition in IS research that uses the industry average of IT-related variables (e.g., IT intensity) to instrument for firm-level IT-related variables $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , Kleis et al. 2012; Xue et al. 2012; Dong and Netten 2017; Saldanha et al. 2020a, 2022). As prior research (Xue et al. 2012, p. 518) notes, “industry-level variables are considered as exogenous to firms and influence firms’ level of investments in its IT asset portfolio.” More generally, this approach is similar to studies that instrument firm-level variables with industry-level variables (Barla 2007). We instrumented for the interaction terms by creating the interaction of the deficiencies with the industry mean (excluding focal firm) of ITC and ITO, respectively.

We accounted for endogeneity by calculating Inverse Mill’s Ratio using estimates from the first stage.<sup>21</sup> In the second stage, we used a Poisson regression model to estimate innovation output as a function of its determi nants, and we included Inverse Mill’s Ratio as an addi tional control variable. In all our estimations, Inverse Mill’s Ratio is statistically nonsignificant $( p > 0 . 1 0 ) $ , suggesting a lack of bias due to endogeneity (Heckman 1979, Shaver 1998, Greene 2003). As Table 3 shows, results in the second step (Model 4) are qualitatively similar to the main results (Model 2). We used Hausman (1978) tests to assess whether there are systematic differences in the coefficients of the main model (Model 2) and the two-step model (Model 4). The nulls of these tests were not rejected $( p = 1 . 0 )$ ), indicating that the results in the second step of Heckman’s procedure are similar to the main results, and hence endogeneity is not a problem (Heckman 1979, Shaver 1998, Greene 2003). Sargan test (chi-square � 5.18, p � 0.15) and Basmann test (chi-square � 5.02, p � 0.17) indicate nonrejection of the joint nul hypothesis, implying that our models are not overidentified (Baum 2006).

Second, we used the propensity score matching method to address any additional sample selection bias concerns due to the firms’ self-selection into ITC and ITO. This approach accounts for concerns related to systematic differences among firms regarding ITC and ITO due to unobservable characteristics (Green and Stuart 2014). Thus, TOCI was the dependent variable of our logit model, and the covariates (R&D expenses, firm size, sales, geographical location, national company, and industry) were the predictors. Propensity scores were calculated for each firm: (a) SMEs for which the value of TOCI was above the sample mean and (b) SMEs with a TOCI value equal to or below the mean. SMEs in the treatment group (TOCI � 1) were matched with corresponding SMEs in the control group (TOCI � 0) based on the nearest neighbor matching algorithm. Fifth, because we focus on interaction effects, endogeneity is less of a concern. Specifically, it is unlikely that a factor is present at the confluence of ITO and government support deficiency (or at the confluence of ITC and technological deficiency) but not when ITO or government support deficiency is individually present. Finally, although the data collection process addressed concerns of measurement error (as discussed in Section 3.1), we conducted robustness tests to assess how measurement error would impact our results. We used the regression calibration method, which introduces measurement error into the models by using replicate error-prone proxies (Hardin et al. 2003, Lockwood and McCaffrey 2020). This method creates variables by introducing noise (i.e., measurement error) into the measurements and using these error-prone proxies to estimate the models. This method assumes that some of the variance in the error-prone variables (ITC, ITO, technological deficiency, government support deficiency, and their relevant interactions) is due to measurement error variance. We conducted three robustness analyses using three different levels of measurement error variance: 5%, 10%, and 15%. These levels of measurement error variance were chosen based on guidance from prior research that suggests the use of Cronbach alpha values (i.e., 1 � Cronbach alpha) if the measurements are scaled items (Hardin et al. 2003, Lockwood and McCaffrey 2020). These levels of measurement error variance are consistent with the interrater reliability between responses from senior managers and middle managers (greater than 0.85) in our study. Results, shown in Table 6, are substantively unchanged when using each of the three levels of measurement error variance (5%, 10%, and 15%). The consistency of results across all the robustness tests discussed above suggests that our results are robust.

## 5. Discussion 5.1. Findings

Our study has three main findings for SMEs. First, ITC attenuates the negative relationship between technologi cal deficiency and innovation output, as well as the relationship between government support deficiency and innovation output. In contrast, ITO attenuates the negative relationship between government support deficiency and innovation output but does not attenuate the relationship between technological deficiency and innovation output. Second, ITC has a stronger attenuating effect than ITO on the negative relationship between technological deficiency and innovation output. Third, ITO has a stronger attenuating effect than ITC on the negative relationship between government support deficiency and innovation output. Although not hypothesized, the negative and significant unconditional effects of technological deficiency and government support deficiency on innovation output are consistent with our expectations and lend validity to our theoretical arguments, empirical setup, and data. Our findings are robust to endogeneity and other tests (see Table 4). Our exploratory analysis sheds light on several underlying mechanisms included in our theorization and suggests that ITC and ITO facilitate internal and external absorptive capacity metaroutines, respectively.

## 5.2. Theoretical Contributions

Our study makes three distinct theoretical contributions. First, as discussed earlier, the advantages and challenges of ITC and ITO create a tension regarding which approach to IT-enabled innovation better addresses the deficiencies faced by SMEs. We address this tension by showing that ITC and ITO differ in the extent to which they help SMEs attenuate the negative effects of technological deficiency and government support deficiency on innovation output. We thus contribute to IT and innovation literature that highlights the need to study tensions in IT-enabled innovation, in particular, “how internal organizational and environmental conditions cause alternative mechanisms [to IT-enabled innovation] to be more or less binding” and how some “mechanisms are more effective than others and under what conditions” (Kohli and Melville 2019, p. 214). We also contribute to the literature on open and closed approaches to innovation (Hippel 2005, Almirall and Masanell 2010, Felin and Zenger 2014). Although this literature studies the phenomenon of innovation, it does not offer prescriptive guidance regarding the type of IT-enabled innovation approaches to follow to overcome deficiencies. Our study suggests that neither approach to IT-enabled innovation outperforms the other in addressing both technological deficiency and government support deficiency. Our results underscore theoretical differences in the effects of using IT for closed versus open innovation activities. Specifically, because ITC is a practiced routine that executes internal absorptive capacity metaroutines, ITC is more effective in facilitating learning within the organization, cross-functional knowledge sharing across business units, and allocating resources across projects. Thus, ITC is more effective than ITO in overcoming technological deficiency. Further, because ITO is a practiced routine that executes external absorptive capacity metaroutines, ITO is more effective than ITC in addressing input additionality and behavioral additionality, thereby overcoming government support deficiency.

Our theorizing suggests that ITC is a practiced routine that executes the internal absorptive capacity metaroutines (1) facilitating variation; (2) sharing knowledge and superior practices across the organization; (3) reflecting, updating, and replicating; and (4) managing internal selection regimes. Our theorizing also suggests that ITO is a practiced routine that executes the external absorptive capacity metaroutines (1) identifying and recognizing the value of externally generated knowledge and (2) learning from and with partners, suppliers, customers, competitors, and consultants. Thus, we theoretically unravel distinct augmenting mechanisms by which IT impacts innovation outcomes. Although our theorizing points to specific metaroutines underlying internal and external absorptive capacities that are digitally executed by ITC and ITO, respectively, a potential theoretical extension for future research is to explore other configurations of metaroutines that may be executed by other types of IT. To our best knowledge, our study is the first to integrate the concept of absorptive capacity metaroutines to address the tension that exists in leveraging IT-enabled open and closed innovation approaches to overcome deficiencies in SMEs (Kohli and Melville 2019). Our findings suggest that distinct IT-enabled innovation approaches suppress the deleterious effects of different deficiencies on innovation outcomes. Our study underscores that depending on the innovation deficiencies an SME faces, specific mechanisms can be leveraged to overcome them using distinct IT-enabled approaches to innovation.

Second, this study is among the first to highlight that IT helps firms overcome deficiencies in government support, thus extending IT and innovation literature to the institutional context related to government support for innovation. Most of the prior literature on IT and innovation has focused on how IT complements or addresses factors internal to the firm (Joshi et al. 2010, Kleis et al. 2012, Ravichandran et al. 2017, Saldanha et al. 2020b, Guo et al. 2023) or factors related to the firm’s immediate business context such as external R&D investments, customers, or suppliers (Go´ mez et al. 2017, Trantopoulos et al. 2017). Scant research has considered how IT overcomes deficiencies in the institutional environment, such as government support deficiency. Our findings cast a new light on prior research. Depending on the context, the impacts of IT on innovation found in prior research may or may not encompass the effects of IT in overcoming government support deficiency. For example, the United States is arguably an institutional context where government support for innovation is abundant, and large firms have sufficient resources and ability to leverage this support. Given this context, findings from prior studies of IT’s impacts on innovation (Kleis et al. 2012; Ravichandran et al. 2017; Saldanha et al. 2017, 2020b) may not reflect the effect of IT in overcoming government support deficiency. More generally, our study is among the first to examine how IT attenuates the negative effects of deficiencies on innovation output. Most prior literature has studied how IT directly affects innovation or complements other resources to influence innovation outcomes (Kleis et al. 2012, Saldanha et al. 2017, Guo et al. 2023).

Third, our study of SMEs extends and complements IT and innovation research conducted in the context of large firms (e.g., Ravichandran et al. 2017; Saldanha et al. 2017, 2020b; Choi et al. 2021). Few studies have examined issues of IT and innovation in the context of SMEs exclusively. Our study extends this literature in this new direction. Related literature in other fields has demonstrated that SMEs face scarcities of resources and expertise arising from issues such as liability of smallness (Freeman et al. 1983, Terziovski 2010). Because innovation is a resource-intensive and path-dependent activity subject to time compression diseconomies, SMEs face increased challenges to achieving innovation outcomes. Despite these chal lenges, SMEs pursue innovation because it is crucial for their business success (Qian and Li 2003, Wales et al. 2013). Our results contribute to management scholarship that seeks to understand how SMEs succeed in achieving innovation outcomes despite the challenges of technological deficiency and government support deficiency. These challenges are more salient in SMEs because of their specific characteristics, such as limited resources (Terziovski 2010), low perceived legitimacy (Thornhill and Amit 2003), scant opportu nities to recruit specialized workers (Van de Vrande et al. 2009), limited ability to influence the regulatory environment, and lack of a good understanding of legal frameworks. In sum, our study complements prior research and shines a light on a fertile area for future work.

## 5.3. Managerial Contributions

Our study has two key managerial contributions. First, a key message for managers is that SMEs can achieve greater innovation output by orienting their IT-enabled innovation efforts in an open or closed manner to address the specific deficiencies the SMEs face. Our findings suggest that SME managers need to evaluate the source of innovation deficiencies and focus their IT-enabled innovation efforts accordingly. SME managers can address technological deficiency by focusing their IT efforts on promoting innovation within the firm, using IT to support closed innovation. In contrast, SMEs that face government support deficiency should give preference to IT Use for Open Innovation Activities to collaborate with external constituents such as customers and suppliers. Thus, SMEs that use IT for innovation activities in a manner aligned with the type of deficiency they face can achieve greater innovation output. Because technological deficiency and government support deficiency are significant challenges that SMEs face because of their small size, limited resources, and limited legitimacy, our study uncovers how IT use for open and closed innovation activities helps overcome these deficiencies.

Second, as open innovation has garnered significant attention recently because of the emergence of digita platforms (e.g., crowdsourcing), managers may be overly eager to use open innovation. Our results suggest that both open and closed forms of IT-enabled innovation have value. We exhort SME managers not to disregard either form of IT-enabled innovation but rather to tailor their IT-enabled innovation approach to suit their organizational context based on the specific deficiencies that their firm faces. Managers should evaluate their firm’s technological deficiency and government support deficiency and orient their IT-enabled innovation approach accordingly. Such steps can include fostering an organizational culture emphasizing open or closed approaches to IT-enabled innovation.

## 5.4. Strengths and Limitations

We highlight three key strengths of this study. First, we address recent calls for inclusivity in IS research to study domains that have thus far received less attention from IS researchers (Rai 2016, 2019; Bailey et al. 2020; Karhade and Kathuria 2020). Our examination of SMEs in Mexico is a study of underrepresented populations and geographies. Because SMEs employ a majority of the global workforce, our findings are particularly important. Our unique empirical setup afforded us an opportunity to study how SMEs in an emerging economy use IT to navigate challenges they face regarding innovation. Hence, our research is inclusive and impactful. Second, the unique archival data set used in our analysis combines the virtues of primary and secondary data sources. The instrument design followed scientifically rigorous and academically sound processes, and the IT measures captured how IT was used for innovation activities. The data were collected by a government agency from a representative sample of senior SME managers who were legally bound to provide accurate responses supported by documentary evidence. Finally, data accuracy was corroborated by validation with randomly selected alternate respondents and secondary data. Hence, our measures are precise, the sample is representative, and the data are accurate and bias free—ou study’s key strengths relative to other studies.

Third, the approach of designing and executing the survey in collaboration with the Government of Mexico is in the spirit of engaged scholarship. Engaged scholarship is “a participative form of research for obtaining the different perspectives of key stakeholders (researchers, users, clients, sponsors, and practitioners) in study ing complex problems” (Van de Ven 2007, p. 9). This research approach advances scientific knowledge and practice and represents a “shift in how scholars define their relationships with the communities in which they are located,” including “practitioners in relevant professional domains [policy makers]” (Van de Ven 2007, p. 6). Our approach of engaging with practitioners in the research process is a key strength of this study because it helps us jointly produce knowledge to advance scholarly research and provide insights for practitioners (Rai 2019). Online Appendix B showcases how we accomplished this model of engaged scholarship involving academia and government. The procedure detailed in Online Appendix B can serve as an exemplar for future research, benefiting both theory and practice.

Despite these strengths, we acknowledge the limita tions of our study. First, our sample consists of SMEs from a region in Mexico. Though this enhances internal reliability and avoids issues arising from cross-country heterogeneity and uneven economic development, it limits generalizability. Our approach aligns with prior studies that sample firms from a specific region(s) in a single country (e.g., Zhou and Wu 2010, Poppo et al. 2016, Khuntia et al. 2021). Extending our results to other countries is an area for future research. Second, although our rich data set spans a two-year period, which enables temporal ordering and addresses causal ity concerns similar to prior studies (Bharadwaj et al. 2007, Saldanha et al. 2017), panel data that span a longer period can provide further insights. At the same time, limited longitudinal data on IT-enabled innovation in SMEs exist, especially in emerging markets. Consistent with prior studies, we performed many robustness tests to address potential endogeneity issues (e.g., twostep models, GMM models, propensity score matching models, swapping of independent and dependent variables) (Bharadwaj et al. 2007; Chen and Bharadwaj 2009; Zhao and Wry 2016; Go´mez et al. 2017; Saldanha et al. 2017, 2020a; Khuntia et al. 2019; Cheng et al. 2021; Mithas et al. 2022). Even so, panel data on SMEs over a long period, if and when available, can offer intriguing possibilities.

## 5.5. Future Research and Conclusion

Our study paves avenues for future research. First, we focused on two innovation deficiencies that are particularly salient to SMEs (technological deficiency and government support deficiency). Studying other deficiencies specific to SMEs or large firms can reveal further insights into IT’s impacts on innovation. For example, large firms may suffer from core rigidities that hamper innovation. Future research can consider the role of ITC or ITO in overcoming this deficiency, and how the roles of ITC and ITO carry over to large firms more generally. Second, future work can study other conceptualizations of IT-enabled innovation in SMEs. In this research, we studied two approaches to IT-enabled innovation: IT Use for Closed Innovation Activities and IT Use for Open Innovation Activities. Although this conceptualization is in line with prior innovation research (Almirall and Masanell 2010) and fills an important gap in the IT innovation literature, studying other conceptualizations can provide further insights. Third, future work can study how IT used for different types of open innovation (e.g., inbound and outbound) may impact innovation outcomes.

Many decades ago, Weick (1974, p. 487) urged organizational scholars “to avoid big business organizations and to examine, instead, everyday events, places and questions, micro-organizations, and absurd organizations. In these sites, organizationally relevant phenomena are more visible and available for hypothesis generation than in complex organizations.” Nonetheless, few IS studies address the SME context. Given the centrality of IT in organizations, it is crucial that we systemically investigate such absurd organizations (Weick 1974) that, despite their small size, thrive through innovation. Our study contributes to expanding the scholarly research on IT in the SME context by showing the salient role of ITC and ITO in overcoming the deficiencies that SMEs face. Our findings suggest that ITC is more effective in overcoming the technological deficiency of SMEs, arising from their small size, limited resources, lack of perceived legitimacy (Thornhill and Amit 2003), and scant opportunities to recruit specialized workers (Van de Vrande et al. 2009). In contrast, ITO is more effective at overcoming government support deficiency, which is particularly important for SMEs because of their small size, limited resources, insufficient funds, and dependence on few clients (Doh and Kim 2014). Size and resource limitations can further hinder SMEs’ ability to influence the regulatory environment and use legal protections in their favor. Likewise, although we used absorptive capacity metaroutines as a theoretical lens, there is a need for research to further explore the role of absorptive capacity metaroutines in facilitating innovation in SMEs, particularly in emerging economies. Because of the specific characteristics of SMEs, such as their small size, limited resources (Terziovski 2010), and low levels of perceived legitimacy (Thornhill and Amit 2003), such studies can advance the literature on IT and innovation to complement prior studies conducted in large firms. Indeed, our study is a step in the direction espoused by Weick (1974).
