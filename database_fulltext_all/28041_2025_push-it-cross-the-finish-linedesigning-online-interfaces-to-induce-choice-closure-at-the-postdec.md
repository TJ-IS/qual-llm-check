# Push It Cross the Finish Line—Designing Online Interfaces to Induce Choice Closure at the Postdecision Prepurchase Stage

Younghwa Lee,<sup>a</sup> Andrew N. K. Chen,<sup>b</sup> Weiquan Wang<sup>c,</sup>

Abstract. As e-commerce and mobile commerce become prevalent, a notable issue indicating a lack of choice closure is that consumers do not complete their purchases after choosing a product (e.g., shopping cart abandonment). Choice closure refers to the psychological process by which decision makers come to perceive a decision to be complete and settled. To foster choice closure, online interface designs hold promising merits in terms of flexible implementations and practical implications. Drawing upon cognitive dissonance theory and self-justification theory, we examine how interface designs can tackle consumers’ negative cognitions to promote choice closure at the postdecision prepurchase stage, wherea the extant literature focuses mainly on enhancing consumers’ positive cognitions leading to their decision satisfaction. Particularly, we investigate the effects of online interface designs on reducing cognitive dissonance (i.e., a negative cognition as an antecedent of perceived choice closure) and examine the relationships between this construct, perceived choice closure, and decision satisfaction. We conducted a series of controlled experiments in a simulated e-commerce site by designing and presenting interface cues as direct and social decision reinforcements at a postdecision prepurchase stage. Our results attest to (1) the effectiveness of the proposed reinforcement cues for inducing choice closure via reduced cognitive dissonance and (2) the significant role of choice closure for consumers satisfaction with their decisions in online settings where product consumption/testing is often unfeasible. Theoretical and practical contributions are elaborated.

## 1. Introduction

In today’s e-commerce and mobile commerce environment, consumers go through the choice-purchaseconsumption process of products and services by viewing and contending with a variety of online cues and information. Consumers as well as merchants face specific challenges at distinct stages during this online shopping journey (see Appendix A). There have been rich research and practices to help consumers choose products and services at the predecision prepurchase phase (e.g., Wang and Benbasat 2013, Shen et al. 2020). Extant research and practices have also identified approaches to gratify consumers at the postpurchase phase (e.g., Cyr 2008, Arens and Hamilton 2017, Pizzutti et al. 2022). However, we observe that an important, yet often overlooked, area of consumers’ online shopping journey is the postdecision prepurchase (PDPP) stage. This stage is marked by a notable number of shopping failures (e.g., shopping cart abandonments) and a substantial loss in sales revenue, as consumers choose a product but ultimately do not complete the purchase (Kukar-Kinney and Close 2010, Luo et al. 2019, BigCommerce 2024, Statista 2024).

Consumers often ponder forgone alternatives after making product choices (e.g., Gu et al. 2013, 2018). Particularly, when shopping online, consumers usually compare their picked product with other alternatives, even after they make a choice, given that myriad alternatives are available just a few clicks away (e.g., Broniarczyk and Griffin 2014). This comparison often leads to online consumers’ conflicting (negative) cognitions regarding the pros and cons of their choice vis-a\`-vis other alternatives. This unsettled thought (i.e., cognitive dissonance) diminishes their certainty and satisfaction with their decisions, which can subsequently trigger incomplete purchases. Statistics show that the average online shopping cart abandonment rate in the recent decade is about 70% (Baymard Institute 2024, Statista 2024). Now, the question arises: can merchants take measures to alleviate this cognitive dissonance and induce a sense of “choice closure” (i.e., a perception of a completed and finalized decision) to “push consumers to cross the finish line” (i.e., purchase the chosen product)?

Gu and her colleagues introduced and validated the concept of choice closure through a series of experiments in physical retailing contexts (Gu et al. 2013, 2018). By applying a metaphorical perspective, they demonstrated that a physical act analogically associated with the concept of closure (e.g., placing a lid over a chocolate assortment after choosing one option) triggers choice closure. Although the extant metaphorical perspective of choice closure can be easily applied to explain the effects of physical acts in offline settings (where consumers are led to “unconsciously” close their choice without cognitive processing of any choiceor product-related information), interface designs in e-commerce can hold distinct promising merits in terms of flexible implementations and practical implications. Human-computer interaction (HCI) researchers have found significant effects of web interfaces on various online consumer perceptions and behaviors, including reduced dissonance (e.g., Chefitz et al. 2018, Chen et al. 2023), affective and cognitive appraisals (Lee and Kozar 2009), satisfaction (Zviran et al. 2006), purchases (Sia et al. 2009), and loyalty (Cyr 2008). In this study, we contend that a thoughtfully designed online interface can be an effective means to assert consumers of their focal product choice by reducing cognitive dissonance and enhancing perceived choice closure. However, processing interface cues (e.g., information to affirm and reinforce consumers’ product decisions) is beyond a simple metaphorical association to trigger choice closure and warrants scholarly investigation.

In addition, in the extant literature, the closurerelated perceptions of the participants in the experiments were captured after they consumed/tested their chosen products in offline settings (e.g., Gu et al. 2013). In such settings, a perception of choice closure can be easily cultivated, given consumers’ low need to examine the forgone alternatives because they already have consumed the chosen product and have a strong desire to stick with their “committed” decision. However, product consumption (or testing), especially for physical products, is infeasible during the decision-making process of online purchases. Therefore, whether and how a perception of choice closure can be facilitated in the absence of product consumption in e-commerce settings remain unknown.

The primary objective of this study is to uncover the substantive effects of online interface designs on reducing cognitive dissonance and fostering a sense of choice closure at the PDPP stage, which, in turn, increases decision satisfaction. The theory of cognitive dissonance (Festinger 1957) suggests that people who experience cognitive dissonance (i.e., a negative cognition) in their decision making are motivated to reduce the dissonance. According to self-justification theory (e.g., Holland et al. 2002), people tend to seek supporting justifications to reinforce their decisions and reduce their cognitive dissonance. Drawing on these theories, we investigate whether providing certain justification cues can help reduce consumers’ cognitive dissonance and then induce a sense of choice closure, resulting in increased satisfaction with their decision.

Our conceptual model is presented in Figure 1. We consider dealing with cognitive dissonance to induce choice closure as a means of tackling negative cognitions to boost decision satisfaction. A well-established dual-path approach to studying consumer attitude/ satisfaction suggests that focusing on the negatively valenced factors (in addition to positively valenced factors) can further enhance consumer attitude/ satisfaction (e.g., Mittal et al. 1998). Nevertheless, the extant literature has focused mainly on the factors for increasing positive cognitions (e.g., product/deal attractiveness) (e.g., Kim et al. 2023). Our research complements the literature by tackling the negative cognitions in consumers’ online shopping journeys. Particularly, we focus on designing online interfaces with various decision-reinforcing cues as a form of justification to reduce consumers’ cognitive dissonance. We study interface designs that can help consumers justify their decision by presenting an explicit, promoting cue toward their focal choice (i.e., a form of direct decision reinforcement) or a social cue to confirm their focal choice (i.e., a form of social decision reinforcement). We conjecture that well-designed interfaces with such cues (e.g., Cyr et al. 2018, Fei et al. 2021) at the PDPP stage can address consumers’ negative cognitions and produce desired outcomes (i.e., perceived choice closure, which leads to consumers’ satisfaction with their decision), despite the importance but lack of theory-based investigation of these effects. Our focus on interfaces with decision-reinforcing cues is in line with the emerging practice that e-vendors/platforms offer interventions to reinforce consumers’ decisions at the PDPP stage (e.g., Amazon.com has started to offer coupons to consumers at the PDPP stage).

We contribute to research in several ways. First, we examine and attest to the role of choice closure at the PDPP stage and particularly extend it from physical retailing contexts to online settings. Second, we advance the understanding of choice closure in online settings by going beyond the extant metaphorical perspective and offering a fitting theoretical perspective of cognitive dissonance. Third, we also advance the cognitive dissonance literature by explaining how tackling negative cognitions can lead to satisfaction via choice closure. By examining a dual-path model, we confirm the significant effect of reduced cognitive dissonance on choice closure leading to decision satisfaction, even with the presence of a second path—a commonly studied path that includes variables related to positive cognitions (e.g., product attractiveness and deal attractiveness). Fourth, we advance the HCI literature by theorizing the effects of online interface designs with decisionreinforcement cues on choice closure perception. We demonstrate that effective HCI interface cues can successfully induce choice closure in the absence of product consumption (or testing) at the PDPP stage.

In the research methodology section, we first detail three controlled experiments, along with a pretest, and then report data analyses, results, and discussions. Then, we elaborate on our theoretical and practical implications and future research directions.

## 2. Research and Theoretical Background 2.1. Psychological Closure and Choice Closure

Closure is defined as “something settled or resolved” and “a comforting or satisfying sense of finality” in dictionaries. Beike et al. (2007) state that people feel a sense of psychological closure when an experience is complete and becomes a part of the past. Namkoong (2014, p. 3) defines psychological closure as “a mental segmentation process which isolates a given event out of the subjective portfolio of ongoing experiences.” When people close the door to conflicting thoughts of the past and move on with their chosen decisions, psychological closure emerges.

Stemming from this line of thought, choice closure, referring to “the psychological process by which decision-makers come to perceive a decision to be complete and settled” (Gu et al. 2013, p. 268), has recently received increasing attention. Yet, extant studies have examined this concept only in physical retail environments. These studies have found that a sense of choice closure improves consumers’ satisfaction with choosing a variety of retail products (e.g., sets of chocolates and biscuits by Gu et al. 2013 and tea bags by Mogilner et al. 2012). By applying a metaphorical perspective, Gu et al. (2013) found that physi cal acts of closure (e.g., placing a lid over a choice set) after choosing one alternative out of a large choice set (e.g., chocolate assortment) and consuming the chosen product give a signal to consumers that no more comparison is needed, inducing perceived choice closure and thus leading to satisfaction.

## 2.2. Cognitive Dissonance Theory and Self-Justification Theory

Cognitive dissonance theory states that people experi ence an uncomfortable, negative state (i.e., dissonance) when they hold two or more contradictory cognitions (Festinger 1957). People are intrinsically motivated to reduce dissonance by changing the original cognitions, adding or subtracting cognitions (e.g., new attitudes or beliefs), or adjusting the importance of the cognitions (Hinojosa et al. 2017). In decision-making contexts, cognitive dissonance theory suggests that individuals often experience conflicts when deciding among alternatives. Once an individual decides on a choice, disso nance can arise if the person still ponders foregone alternatives (Festinger 1957, Insko and Schopler 1972). That is, dissonance occurs when people feel that they may not make the ideal choice and their desire to have the “right” choice conflicts with the realization that they did not do so. For example, after finishing a test drive of many cars and eventually picking one, a potential buyer may start thinking about alternatives that he tried and feel anxious and uncertain about whether his choice is the right one. In this uncomfortable situation, people need something to alleviate this negative cognition (i.e., cognitive dissonance) (Dickinson and Oxoby 2011).

Self-justification theory has been applied to account for the alleviation of cognitive dissonance. This theory is grounded in Festinger’s (1957) theory of cognitive dissonance and suggests that people tend to escalate their commitment to a course of action by self-justifying their prior behavior (Staw and Fox 1977). Cognitive dissonance is commonly referred to as the engine of selfjustification. When people make a choice that incurs cognitive dissonance, they tend to seek external cues that are readily available to help justify their choice (i.e., external justification).<sup>1</sup> Theory of cognitive dissonance suggests that one of the strategies to reduce cognitive dissonance is to add new cognitions (e.g., reinforcement information). Such cognitions serve as external justification for consumers’ decisions and are expected to reduce their cognitive dissonance.

## 3. Research Model and Hypothesis Development

Theory of cognitive dissonance and self-justification theory suggest that individuals can reduce dissonance with mechanisms such as getting external cues to help reinforce the focal decision. The classic study related to decision dissonance by Brehm (1956) suggests that dissonance will be reduced if the selected choice can be reinforced. Along with these arguments, we focus on external justification as a plausible means to reduce negative cognitions (i.e., dissonance) experienced at the PDPP stage in an e-commerce setting. External justification relies on external cues and reasoning to support one’s decisions (Aronson et al. 2019). In the context of online decision making, designs of direct and social decision reinforcements can be utilized as external justifications to provide assenting information to confirm the decision (e.g., Montgomery and Barnes 1993, Aronson and Aronson 2018). We term “direct reinforcement,” such as rewarding coupons, affirmative feedback, and congratulatory messages, as a form of external justification provided to confirm a customer’s decision directly. As another form of external justification, “social reinforcement,” such as positive reviews of the chosen products, peers’ similar choices, and positive opinions of experts toward the products, can also be provided to confirm the focal decision.

In the extant e-commerce practices, product- or choicerelated information cues are usually available at the predecision stage (e.g., on a product list or product details page) to promote products or facilitate consumers’ choice making. We, instead, investigate a novel design approach to providing similar interventions (cues) at the PDPP stage to foster a perception of choice closure based on self-justification theory. For instance, an e-commerce store can offer discount coupons for a chosen product, as a direct reinforcement of his decision, after a customer makes a choice. Offering coupons for a particular product at a predecision stage can promote the product, but without knowing the customer’s choice at that stage, doing so is unlikely to serve the purpose of choice clo sure. Another potential design intervention is consumer endorsements as a social reinforcement. Product ratings and reviews are often available at the predecision stage. An e-commerce store can consider offering additional consumer endorsements specific to a product after the product is chosen by a customer (i.e., at the PDPP stage). We postulate that processing these reinforcement cues can reduce consumers’ cognitive dissonance and stimu late them to feel a sense of choice closure to improve satisfaction with their decision. We intend to validate the benefits of our proposed approach to tackling negative cognitions by offering reinforcing cues as external justifi cations at the PDPP stage from a novel theoretical per spective of dissonance reduction and choice closure. At the same time, we attempt to demonstrate that provi sions of these cues at the postdecision stage, as compared with those at the predecision stage, will lead to greater reduced cognitive dissonance and increased choice closure perception.

## 3.1. Effects of Decision Reinforcements

We first examine the effect of a direct reinforcement. Online merchants can consider various direct reinforcement strategies to affirm an individual’s decision and reduce his cognitive dissonance (Brockner 1992, Keil 1995), such as supplying additional value-added information about their products (e.g., coupons), complimenting the consumers’ wisdom, and offering strong guarantees or warranties (Yap and Gaur 2014). In line with the self-justification theory, past studies found that people tend to look for supportive information to reinforce their decisions as a form of external justification (e.g., Cotton and Hieser 1980, Deng et al. 2024). When people hold contradictory cognitions (e.g., noting the relative pros and cons of their chosen product when comparing it with other alternatives), they are uncertain whether their focal choice is the right one. Receiving external justification can reduce cognitive dissonance by strengthening their original cognition of the chosen product. With this justification, consumers tend not to ponder other alternatives once a decision is made. It is well-established that if individuals have enough external justification for their decisions or actions, cognitive dissonance will be minimal (Festinger and Carlsmith 1959, Aronson and Aronson 2018). Montgomery and Barnes (1993, pp. 206–207) argue that “dissonant consumers need reassurance (e.g., support) that a wise purchase decision has been made.” Aronson and Aronson (2018) also suggest that dissonance can be alleviated with situational external justification, such as praise and rewards. Therefore, in an online environment, giving a direct reinforcement to affirm and support a person’s decision can be a powerful external justification and an effective way to alleviate his dissonance about the decision. Thus, we hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 1. At the PDPP stage, displaying an interface with a direct reinforcement will lead to greater reduced cognitive dissonance than without it.

Humans are social in nature. Potential social confirmations, such as online reviews, expert opinions, and social media discussions about the quality and user experience with the chosen product, can signify that the customer makes the right choice (e.g., Broniarczyk and Griffin 2014, Deng et al. 2022, BigCommerce 2024). People’s feelings, attitudes, and behaviors can influence each other. This phenomenon is generally referred to as social influence (e.g., Kuan et al. 2014). For example, theories related to technology acceptance such as the Technology Acceptance Model, the Theory of Reasoned Action, and the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology all emphasize social influences on individuals’ behaviors, with respect to adopting or using information technologies. Another example is e-WoM (for electronic word-of-mouth), which is referred to as “any positive and negative statement made by potential, actual or former customers about a product or company, which is made available to a multitude of people and institutions via the Internet” (Hennig-Thurau et al. 2004, p. 39).

In our research context, social reinforcement can serve as a useful external justification for consumers to reaffirm their decision and diminish postdecision cognitive dissonance. Particularly, when people are uncertain whether their focal choice is the right one, they are motivated to internalize the provided external information (Deng et al. 2022, Qiu et al. 2023), which can serve as a powerful justification for their decision. Then, consumers who received confirmative cues from reliable sources or friends/peers will be less likely to think about other alternatives, thereby minimizing their cognitive dissonance. The literature associated with selfjustification theory also suggests that individuals can utilize external social cues to deal with cognitive conflicts by justifying their focal decision (Keil et al. 2000). Thus, we hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 2. At the PDPP stage, displaying an interface with a social reinforcement will lead to greater reduced cognitive dissonance than without it.

In Hypothesis 1 and Hypothesis 2, we consider direct and social reinforcements as forms of external justification provided via interface cues to affirm consumers’ decisions and help them not think about other unselected products. As Jiang and Benbasat (2004) pointed out, certain stimulation cues (e.g., more salient cues) may play a disproportionately more important role than other cues when we present multiple cues to consumers together. Thus, we do not expect that the provision of both external justifications will necessarily lead to reduced cognitive dissonance to the extent of the sum of the individual effects of the direct and social reinforcements. We postulate that a direct reinforcement toward a choice (e.g., discount coupon and verbal praise) can “directly” resonate and reassure consumers of their choice, whereas they may need to put consider able effort into elaborating on and internalizing a social reinforcement message related to the chosen product. In addition, direct reinforcements are more functional (or financial) in nature, which can benefit consumers directly more than the social reinforcements (i.e., informational or emotional). Thus, we contend that a direct reinforcement dominates the effects on reducing custo mers’ cognitive dissonance and can substitute for a social reinforcement. That is, customers will not perceive any substantively additional effects of a socia reinforcement in the presence of a direct reinforcement. Therefore, we conjecture that a substitution effect exists between the two types of reinforcement and hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 3. At the PDPP stage, a substitution effect exists between direct and social reinforcement in terms of reduced cognitive dissonance. Displaying an interface with a social reinforcement will lead to greater reduced cognitive dissonance only when a direct reinforcement is not provided.

## 3.2. Effect of Reduced Cognitive Dissonance on Choice Closure

Dissonance reduction is needed for psychological closure (Beike et al. 2009). As the psychological closure literature suggests, minimizing cognitive dissonance can lead to a sense of closure (e.g., Choi et al. 2008). Powers and Jack (2013) state that dissonance can arise immediately after a decision is made, as the forgone alternatives might seem better than individuals had thought initially to them. Webster and Kruglanski (1997) state that lack of closure is associated with decisional uncer tainty, which often accompanies cognitive dissonance. Under this situation with negative cognitions, individuals are unlikely to be firmly certain with their choice and still ponder whether they would face a high risk of not making the best choice. By contrast, when cognitive dissonance is reduced, individuals become more certain with their decisions, and comparing the focal choice with other alternatives is no longer meaningful. As such, they will perceive their decision to be complete and settled and form a sense of choice closure.

Based on these arguments, we contend that after having reached a choice decision, receiving forms of decision reinforcement can reduce cognitive dissonance and then lead to perceived choice closure. Therefore, we conjecture that people who experience less cognitive dissonance will have a higher sense of closure related to their decision and hypothesize:

Hypothesis 4. At the PDPP stage, a customer’s reduced cognitive dissonance will lead to a greater perceived choice closure.

## 3.3. Provision Timing of Reinforcement Cues

In Hypotheses 1–4, we hypothesize the effects of direct and social decision reinforcements provided at the PDPP stage (i.e., the provision of these cues versus their absence) on cognitive dissonance and choice closure. In practice, the same or similar forms of these cues (e.g., coupons and social endorsements) are widely presented at the predecision stage (e.g., on the product detail page) of the prepurchase phase. We further examine whether providing these cues at different decision stages (i.e., the postdecision stage vis-a\`-vis the predecision stage) will make a difference in reducing cognitive dissonance and choice closure.

At the predecision stage, these informational cues usually aim to promote products or attract users to a targeted decision (i.e., choosing a specific product). However, consumers are often overloaded with a large amount of descriptive product information and information cues (Wang and Benbasat 2009, Jabr and Rahman 2022). They may not carefully consider each information cue available when making their product decision. The effects of an additional information cue at the predecision stage on their postdecision cognitive dissonance and choice closure will be limited. Moreover, most products have pros and cons. Thus, after making a product decision, consumers cannot easily ignore other numerous alternatives in an online shopping setting. That is, even with an additional information cue at the predecision stage, consumers’ cognitive dissonance at the postdecision stage tends to remain high.

By contrast, at the postdecision stage, consumers are inclined to be more receptive to information cues that can confirm their focal decision (i.e., a confirmation bias) (Hart et al. 2009). As such, a positive information cue about a product can easily function to confirm consumers’ decisions as “reinforcements,” helping them not think about other foregone products. As such, the provision of reinforcement cues at the postdecision stage, as compared with those at the predecision stage, will lead to greater reduced cognitive dissonance and higher perceived choice closure. Thus, we hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 5. At the prepurchase phase, the provision of reinforcements at the postdecision stage, as compared with those at the predecision stage, will lead to greater reduced cognitive dissonance (Hypothesis 5(a)) and greater perceived choice closure (Hypothesis 5(b)).

## 3.4. Effect of Choice Closure on Decision Satisfaction

Small and Venkatesh (1995) suggest that variations of (e.g., less) information processing do not directly explain decision satisfaction but through the mediation of a few factors, including a state of closure. Choice closure in online settings indicates that consumers no longer worry about missing any good alternatives but make a firm online decision; thus, choice closure is associated with outcomes of less anxiety, less stress, and more comfort. Previous studies support a positive relationship between choice closure and satisfaction in various contexts. For example, Namkoong (2014) demonstrates that providing consumers with a sense of ending or resolution can avoid potential negative experiences and lead to a sense of satisfaction with their decision. Small and Venkatesh (1995, p. 545) state that “satisfaction with the decision at the time the decision is made is the affective outcome of having attained the desired cognitive end state of closure.” Thus, we contend that a perceived choice closure in online e-commerce settings will induce consumers’ satisfaction with their decisions and hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 6. At the PDPP stage, a customer’s sense of choice closure will lead to greater satisfaction with hi decision.

In sum, we develop hypotheses for the effects of decision reinforcements on tackling consumers’ negative cognition (i.e., cognitive dissonance) at the PDPP stage in an e-commerce setting, which leads to perceived choice closure of consumers for higher satisfaction with their decision. Our focus on tackling the negative cognition in our research setting responds to the call of the extant literature for studying the effects of negatively valenced factors (in addition to positively valenced factors) on consumer attitude/satisfaction (e.g., Cenfetelli and Schwarz 2011). Given the wellestablished dual-path approach to studying consumer attitude/satisfaction, we depict our full research model in Figure 2, which includes both positively and nega tively valenced factors. We consider the tackling of cognitive dissonance and its consequence as Path 1 (i.e., Reduced Negative Cognition). We include two posi tively valenced factors that were studied in the extant literature (e.g., Maditinos and Theodoridis 2010, Kim et al. 2023) as Path 2 (i.e., Increased Positive Cognition). We will empirically test this dual-path model and uniquely examine the role of choice closure in enhancing consumers’ decision satisfaction through reducing cognitive dissonance (Path 1).

## 4. Research Methodology, Results, and Discussion

We adopted a four-step approach with laboratory experiments to test our research hypotheses and validate our theoretical arguments. We first conducted a pretest to examine whether a choice closure effect is present in an online choice context (i.e., Step 1). After confirming this effect in the pretest, we then conducted a controlled experiment (Study 1, our main study—i.e., Step 2) to examine the effect of reinforcement designs (i.e., direct and social reinforcement cues) on cognitive dissonance, which leads to perceived choice closure and decision satisfaction. Next, in Step 3, we designed Study 2 to investigate the impact of (1) two alternative cues of a direct reinforcement (instant coupon versus verbal praise) to check whether our findings can be generalized to different forms of direct reinforcement; and (2) the timing of their reinforcement provision (predecision versus postdecision) (i.e., Hypothesis 5). Then, in Step 4, we designed Study 3 with alternative social reinforcement cues (normative versus informational) to ascertain the generalizability of our findings across different forms of social reinforcements. Table 1 summarizes the designs of each study.

In the series of controlled experiments, we created and used a simulated e-commerce site called “Red Brick. com” for purchasing a health tracker. This experimental site mimicked major commercial websites that sell health trackers (see interface screenshots in Appendix B). We chose health trackers because (1) it is expected that experimental participants were highly aware of and generally interested in this type of products; (2) many varieties of similar products are available online; and (3) these products have similar prices, designs, features, and ratings, so people often experience a reasonable amount of choice difficulties.

All experimental conditions included a home page, a product list page, detailed product description pages, and a shopping cart page. The experiment procedure was designed to work as follows. Recruited participants were guided to the home page of the simulated experimental e-commerce site, which showed multiple product categories, including a health tracker category. They could move to the health tracker page by clicking on the health tracker from a menu bar, slideshow, or hyperlink. On the health tracker page, a total of 24 health trackers were listed (4 health trackers in each row) with a product image, a name, a price, a star rating, and a hyperlink to see the details of the product. Participants could navigate the page and click on the hyperlink to see the product details. A product-detail page contained three images of a product (taken from different angles), a price, a star rating, detailed product specifications, and an “Add to Cart” button. After examining the health trackers, participants could choose a health tracker and click on the “Add to Cart” button to go to the shopping cart page. The shopping cart page contained a summary of the chosen health tracker, as well as detailed price information, including corresponding tax and shipping fees. Individuals could see the interface design (reinforcement cues) displayed on this shopping cart page in the treatment conditions. For example, the shopping cart page is updated to reflect a “coupon” for the direct reinforcement condition in Study 1.<sup>2</sup> After confirming the product and price information from the page, participants clicked on either the “Proceed to Checkout” or “Save for Later” button, which led participants to an online survey page.<sup>3</sup> The detailed experimental procedure is described in Appendix C.

## 4.1. Pretest

Before the main Study 1, we conducted a pretest to examine whether a choice closure effect revealed in previous studies can be confirmed in an online context. That is, we examined the effect of the presence of an online “closure interface” on perceived choice closure. A controlled experiment with three conditions (no closure interface, an auto-vanished closure interface after viewing, and a user-closed closure interface after viewing) was conducted in the simulated site described above. These conditions were similar to a previous study conducted in an offline setting (i.e., Gu et al. 2013). The online closure interface presented five alternative health trackers underneath the chosen product on the shopping cart page. The closing of this interface meant the removal of these alternatives. This design was to mimic the offline scenario of “cutting” off access to other product alternatives (e.g., chocolate) by placing a lid over the assortment. In the auto-vanished condition, the closure interface automatically disappeared after eight seconds on the cart page. In the userclosed condition, a participant needed to click the “Confirm Your Choice” button on the page to make the closure interface disappear. A total of 92 students recruited from a U.S. Midwestern University (no closure: 32, auto-closure: 30, user-closure: 30) participated in the experiment.

We performed an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) test with perceived choice closure as a dependent vari able (items are reported in Appendix D) and past purchase, gender, and product knowledge as covariates. The results confirmed significant differences (F � 5.244, p < 0.01) in perceived choice closure among the three groups: no closure (Mean � 3.021, SD � 0.833), selfclosure (Mean � 3.677, SD � 0.915) and user-closure (Mean � 3.844, SD � 1.315). We conducted a post hoc analysis and found that significant differences existed between no closure and user-closure (Mean Difference � �0.657, Tukey test $( p = 0 . 0 3 9 < 0 . 0 5 )$ , Scheffe $( p = 0 . 0 4 9 < 0 . 0 5 ) ) ;$ and between no-closure and auto closure ((Mean Difference � �0.824, Tukey test (p � 0.007 < 0.01), Scheffe $( p = 0 . 0 1 < 0 . 0 5 ) )$ ) conditions, whereas no significant differences existed between user-closure and auto-closure condition (Mean Difference � �0.167, Tukey test $( p = 0 . 8 0 8 > 0 . 0 5 )$ , Scheffe $( p = 0 . 8 2 4 > 0 . 0 5 ) )$ ). This nonsignificant difference between user- and autoclosure on choice closure differs from that of an offline setting (Gu et al. 2013). This suggests that the effects of closure interface designs in online settings can be differ ent from the mere metaphorical perspective of choice closure in offline settings and need to be further explored. This difference motivated us to focus on substantive reinforcement designs to induce choice closure. Further, a regression analysis shows that perceived choice closure significantly affected online consumer satisfaction $( \beta = 0 . 5 5 8 , \ p < 0 . 0 0 1 , \ R ^ { 2 } = 0 . 3 3 9 )$ , indicating that choice closure successfully induced satisfaction. Overall, our results confirm the effect of a closure interface on perceived choice closure and the role of perceived choice closure in our context.

## 4.2. Main Study: Study 1

4.2.1. Research Designs. Study 1 examines the effects of decision reinforcements provided at the PDPP stage. We conducted a controlled experiment with a two (direct reinforcement: Yes or No) by two (social reinforcement: Yes or No) between-subjects design. We included four different interface designs on the shopping cart page in the simulated site. For a direct reinforcement, a discount coupon was presented on the page. BigCommerce (2024) suggests that one of the reasons causing cart abandonment is no discounts or promotion codes to use. After customers make a choice, displaying a coupon for an additional price discount of the chosen product can allow customers to directly reinforce their focal decision. Thus, we consider a discount coupon as an appropriate direct decision reinforcement cue.

For social reinforcement, BigCommerce (2024) suggests that adding social proof signals can be a plausible way to address the shopping cart abandonment issue. We presented two attestation statistics (accuracy and reliability) of prior buyers based on the in-depth content analysis of customer feedback of health trackers on Amazon.com and BestBuy.com. Accuracy and reliability were the top two quality measures that were most frequently mentioned by past health tracker buyers. To enhance the readability of accuracy and reliability information, we presented them using infographics. We also added an information source of the accuracy and reliability data (i.e., “Monthly Consumer Reports at the Red Brick”) to ensure the credibility of the information (see example interfaces in Appendix B).

We conducted a separate focus-group study to (1) determine the appropriate discount level of coupons as a direct reinforcement and (2) assess the content of our social reinforcement.<sup>4</sup> For the direct reinforcement, we presented 5%, 8%, 10%, 15%, 20%, and 25% discount coupons and asked them to choose the discount percentage that significantly affects their purchase decisions. For the social reinforcement, we assessed whether the content associated with the accuracy and reliability of health trackers (i.e., “99.3% accuracy and 99.5% reliability were attested by prior buyers of this health tracker”) was sufficient to induce social influence. Based on the feedback from the participants, we chose a 15% discount rate and revised the wording of the social reinforcement cue in the subsequent experiments.

For the main experiment (Study 1), we recruited business school students at a large Midwestern U.S. university. Of the 143 participants recruited, 10 students did not participate in the experiment, and 3 did not complete the survey, leaving a total of 130 usable responses. This sample size (as well as those of other experiments reported in this paper) ensured sufficient statistical power with a medium effect size (Cohen’s d � 0.5). We found that 77% of the participants had at least onetime purchase experience of a health tracker, and 67 (51.5%) were females. They perceived that the choice set of health trackers was large enough (mean � 5.59, SD � 1.21) and the choice decision was sufficiently complex $( \mathrm { m e a n } = 5 . 5 7 , \ \mathrm { S D } = 0 . 9 3 ) . ^ { 5 }$ The participation was voluntary, and they earned extra class participation points as an incentive.

All participants received detailed instructions for participating in the study. They were informed that the goal of this study was to examine how individuals make a choice decision when a large set of products is available in an online store. Participants were instructed to visit the simulated site, examine the health trackers provided, choose one they might want to buy, and complete a brief online survey. In the treatment condition, a reinforcement cue was displayed on the shopping cart page, whereas the control condition had no cue presented. Participants were randomly assigned to one of four experimental conditions (see Table 2). The total amount of time required to participate in the experiment was no more than 30 minutes.

Cognitive dissonance, perceived choice closure, and decision satisfaction were dependent variables of Study 1. Although we did not hypothesize the effects of decision reinforcements on intentions to purchase, we measured participants’ intentions to purchase, as our research tended to address the shopping cart abandonment issue. Based on the literature review, we identified five cognitive dissonance items (Koller and Salzberger 2012), three perceived choice closure items (Beike and Wirth-Beaumont 2005), four satisfaction items (Luo et al. 2012), and three purchase intention items (Fang et al. 2014) (see the list of instrument items in Appendix D). Thirteen experts, including five faculty members, four graduate students, and four customers who had previously purchased health trackers from online stores, were recruited to ensure the content validity of the measurement instruments. The wording and format of the instruments were modified based on their reviews.

4.2.5. Dual-Path Model Analysis. Study 1 tested the effects of decision reinforcements on cognitive dissonance, which was, in turn, posited to influence perceived choice closure and decision satisfaction. We theorized these effects as Path 1 leading to decision satisfaction. To empirically substantiate the role of this path (i.e., Path 1: “Decision Reinforcement” → “Cognitive Dissonance” → “Choice Closure” → “Satisfaction”) in addition to the widely recognized path (i.e., Path 2) leading to satisfaction, we conducted a dual-path model analysis. In particular, we performed a mediation test by including two variables in Path 2 (i.e., product attractiveness (PA) and deal attractiveness (DA)). We adopted items from past studies (Dodds et al. 1991, van Herpen et al. 2009) for measuring PA (e.g., “the health tracker I chose has excellent quality/is reliable/has good design”) and DA (e.g., your chosen health tracker is considered to be a good buy/a very good value/its price is very economical).

Figure 5 presents the full results of our dual-path model analysis. As expected, choice closure, PA, and DA all exerted significant effects on decision satisfaction. Additionally, we examined the potential impacts from PA and DA on the relationship between cognitive dissonance and choice closure. Our results reveal a significant effect of cognitive dissonance on choice closure, even when PA and DA were present. Interestingly, only a significant path was observed between DA and choice closure, whereas no significant relationship was found between PA and choice closure. Further, we examined the indirect effect of Decision Reinforcement → Cognitive Dissonance → Choice Closure → Satisfaction when PA and DA were included. We found that the confidence intervals did not include zero (0.03 to 0.09 for DR; 0.02 to 0.11 for SR), reconfirming the mediating role of negative cognition path (i.e., cognitive dissonance and choice closure), even when we included PA and DA (i.e., Path 2).<sup>8</sup> This result corroborates the significant role of choice closure in inducing consumers’ satisfaction with decisions after considering other influential factors.

## 4.3. Study 2: Alternative Designs of Direct Reinforcement Cue Across Predecision and Postdecision Stages

We designed Study 2 to test the effects of provision timing of a decision reinforcement (i.e., at the postdecision stage vis-a\`-vis at the predecision stage). In addition to a discount coupon used in the main study, Study 2 tested verbal praise as another design of direct reinforcement, which is often applied in various contexts (e.g., Schreiner 2024). We conducted a controlled experiment with a 2-by-2 between-subject design: designs of a direct reinforcement cue (instant coupon versus verbal praise) and timing to display the reinforcement cue (predecision versus postdecision stage), along with a control group (no cue). Like Study 1, we investigated the relationships between a direct reinforcement cue, cognitive dissonance, choice closure, and satisfaction in Study 2.

Same as Study 1, we presented the following message for the “coupon” cue: “a 15% off coupon for immediate redemption.” For the “verbal praise” cue, we presented the message “It is a great product. You made the best choice!” Regarding the manipulation of the timing for presenting the direct reinforcement, we displayed one of the cues on a product details page for the predecision condition and on the shopping cart page for the postdecision condition.

We recruited 183 participants one month after Study 1 from the same institution using the same recruiting method and compensation.<sup>9</sup> After removing the no-show and unusable survey responses, 166 participants were retained in Study 2. ## 4.5. Discussion

Results from our experiments reveal several key findings. First, the presence of a direct decision reinforcement cue (e.g., a discount coupon) and a social reinforcement cue (i.e., a social endorsement) at the PDPP stage can induce online consumers’ perceived choice closure through reduced postdecision cognitive dissonance. This induced perception of choice closure, in turn, leads to consumers’ satisfaction with their decisions. With the test of the dual-path model, we further attest to the role of reducing negative cognitions (i.e., Path 1) in inducing decision satisfaction of consumers at the PDPP stage, even with the presence of Path 2 (i.e., increased positive cognitions—product/ deal attractiveness). Second, a substitution effect exists between the direct and social reinforcements. The direct reinforcement can substitute the social reinforcement; that is, the latter reduces cognitive dissonance when the former is absent but does not in the presence of the former. Third, we further confirm that showing an interface with a reinforcement cue can reduce cognitive dissonance significantly because of not only the provision of a reinforcement cue, but also the timing $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ at the postdecision stage instead of the predecision stage). Finally, we demonstrate the robustness of findings by testing different designs of direct and social reinforcement cues.

## 5. Contributions, Limitations, and Future Research

## 5.1. Theoretical Contributions

This study contributes to information systems (IS) research in several ways. First, we extend the concept and explore the effect of choice closure from physical retailing contexts to online settings, specifically at the consumers’ PDPP stage. Choice closure is a nascent research area. The extant literature attests to the con cept and role of choice closure in physical retailing contexts after a customer consumes/tests the focal chosen product. Whether and how choice closure plays a role in online settings in the absence of product consumption remains unexplored. Our experimental results do show that perceived choice closure online can lead to consumers’ satisfaction with their decisions. In addition, in online shopping settings, numerous alternatives exist, and consumers often keep thinking of other alternatives, even after a decision, but before a purchase (i.e., at the PDPP stage). That is, cognitive dissonance arises from thinking of other forgone alternatives even after a choice is made, thus leading to discomfort and choice uncertainty at the PDPP stage. Thus, a strong need exists to deal with negative cognitions at this stage before product purchase/consumption. Kruglanski and Freund (1983) suggested that when there is a need for closure and a desire to preserve past knowledge (e.g., a choice decision), individuals tend to “freeze” upon prior judgments. We investigated these important issues by focusing on influential factors “right at the moment” in an online shopping journey (e.g., postdecision, but before purchase/consumption). We advance the choice closure literature by revealing the significant role of perceived choice closure at the PDPP stage in the absence of product consumption in online settings.

Second, we advance the understanding of choice closure from the theoretical perspective of cognitive dissonance. Consumers often face high cognitive dissonance in online decisions due to the myriad product alternatives readily and conveniently available in online environments. The extant metaphorical perspective explains the “unconscious” closure of a consumer choice in offline contexts (Gu et al. 2013, 2018), which may be insufficient to tackle such dissonance. The results from our pretest indeed suggest that mechanisms based on the metaphorical perspective used in an offline setting to induce choice closure may not have the same effect in an online setting. In online settings, many potential interface designs can be implemented to deal with consumers’ cognitive dissonance during their online shopping, and the effects of such interface designs can be beyond the metaphorical perspective. Referred to as our theoretical base, we introduce cognitive dissonance as a crucial factor influencing consumers’ perceptions of choice closure and establish its significant contribution to explaining the variance in choice closure in online settings.

Third, we also advance the cognitive dissonance literature by explaining how dissonance reduction leads to satisfaction. The extant literature focuses mainly on positively valenced factors (e.g., product/deal attractiveness) leading to satisfaction (e.g., Kim et al. 2023). Drawing up the theory of cognitive dissonance, we extend the extant literature by examining how decision satisfaction in the context of online shopping can be improved through reducing dissonance (i.e., a negatively valenced factor) at the PDPP stage. Specifically, by testing a dual-path model, we confirm the significant effect of reduced cognitive dissonance on choice closure leading to decision satisfaction, even with the presence of positively valenced factors that are commonly considered as antecedents of satisfaction.

Fourth, we advance the HCI literature by investigating the effects of interface designs on choice closure perception through reduced postdecision cognitive dissonance. Going beyond what is applicable and studied in offline settings (e.g., a closure action leading to choice closure “unconsciously”), we examine the effects of interface designs that demand consumer cognitive processing in online settings. We design online interfaces with different forms of reinforcement (i.e., external justification based on the link between theories of self-justification and cognitive dissonance). The theorized effects of these designs depart from the extant literature that focuses primarily on the beneficial effects of substantive measures themselves (e.g., the perceived attractiveness of the product or the focal deal) on decision satisfaction. We also contribute to the HCI literature by revealing the nuances and contingencies of the effects of different designs of decision reinforcement. In particular, a substitution effect of the two types of reinforcements is revealed; that is, the effect of the social reinforcement holds only when the direct reinforcement is absent.

Moreover, extant research focuses mainly on e-commerce interface designs that are provided at the predecision stage to facilitate consumers’ choice making. We demonstrated clear advantages of providing decision reinforcements at the postdecision stage visa-vis \` at the predecision stage, including a distal, substantive outcome (i.e., decision satisfaction) beyond choice closure and cognitive dissonance reduction.

Our research endeavor opens a new research area for IS scholars to study the beneficial effects of interface designs, particularly at the PDPP stage.

In summary, our research examines relationships between interface designs, cognitive dissonances, choice closure, and satisfaction at the online PDPP stage. With a fitting theoretical perspective of cognitive dissonance, we advance the extant choice closure literature that applies primarily a metaphorical perspective. We theorize the effects of HCI interface designs on choice closure and examine the mediating variables between HCI interface designs and outcomes (i.e., decision satisfaction) at the PDPP stage. We reveal and validate why and how reduced dissonance can lead to consumers’ satisfaction with their decision before their actual purchase and consumption of the chosen product.

## 5.2. Practical Implications

This study provides e-commerce practitioners with valuable insights into enhancing online store designs at the PDPP stage. First, given the findings of the signifi cant, positive effects of interfaces with reinforcement cues on perceived choice closure and satisfaction, online merchants can design and display appropriate decision reinforcement cues after consumers put a product into a shopping cart, especially when an online store has a large number of product alternatives for consumers. Designing interface cues for the PDPP stage offers a cost-effective and swift approach to combating the cart abandonment problem by enhancing customer choice closure, contrasting with other e-marketing strategies that may demand substantial financial investment and effort. Particularly, extant e-commerce practices usually present interface cues to facilitate consumers’ choice making at the predecision stage, but do not realize the benefits of offering cues at the PDPP stage. Practitioners can decide when to provide various cues according to their needs (e.g., to facilitate consumers’ decision mak ing or to improve their postdecision satisfaction and purchase intention). For instance, when an online shop per experiences decisional difficulty (e.g., taking considerable time to compare alternatives before her choice when a product seems to be superior to alternatives, yet more expensive), it might indicate that she still feels dis satisfied with her decision, although she chose a product eventually. Then, the store can offer a verbal praise or a coupon (if applicable) for the consumer at the PDPP stage to foster a choice closure perception and enhance satisfaction with her decision to push the com pletion of the purchase. Offering coupons at a predecision stage can hardly be feasible or produce such effects before the consumer choice is known. We recently found that Amazon has introduced a discount coupon occasionally on the shopping cart page—a practice tha focuses on the postdecision stage and that other e-commerce practitioners can adopt as well.

Second, although our research suggests that both direct and social reinforcements can effectively reduce cognitive dissonance and cultivate a sense of choice closure of customers, e-commerce managers can consider employing either direct or social reinforcement strategies, if applicable, but not both simultaneously. Although further validation is warranted in the context of the different products and services of online stores, we found that direct reinforcement is a more efficient solution for cognitive dissonance, at least in the context of this study, given the substitution effect of direct reinforcement over social reinforcement. That is, online merchants can implement a closure interface with only one type of reinforcement, preferably a direct reinforcement for choice closure.

Third, we tested and found that alternative designs of the direct reinforcement and social reinforcement cues all can reduce cognitive dissonance and induce perceived choice closure. Based on our findings, at the PDPP stage, online merchants can offer an instant discount coupon, a verbal praise, and an informational or normative social reinforcement to consumers to increase their purchase intention (and combat the cart abandonment issue), depending on the availability of these designs and cues. That is, based on online stores unique situations and considerations (e.g., feasibility of an extra discount and availability of positive social endorsements for a product), they can implement different decision reinforcements accordingly.

## 5.3. Limitations and Future Research

This study has several limitations. First, our research has a limitation in the generalizability of the findings due to our laboratory experimental settings. We focused on a particular type of electronic product—namely, a health tracker. Future research may extend our research to different products (e.g., high versus low price; hedonic versus utilitarian). Also, our experiments involved college students as the participants and did not involve financial consequences. Thus, future research can involve nonstudent participants in a real-world e-business setting.

Second, this study only examined two particular designs of direct and social reinforcement cues, respectively. Although these cues are prevalent and can be readily applied in online settings, there’s room to explore alternative reinforcement cues as external justifications in these contexts. For example, highlighting key features of the chosen product and its major advantages over other alternatives after a consumer puts a product into a shopping cart can serve as another direct reinforcement cue. Other forms of reinforcements (including those for internal justification) also deserve future investigation. In addition, some designs that have been applied in practice (e.g., recommending other products similar to or related to the customer’s focal choice at the PDPP stage) may hurt choice closure. Future research can investigate the conflicting effects (positive and negative) of these designs on choice closure.

Third, we study the effects of interface cues presented at the PDPP stage only. Although we offered all product review information at the predecision stage in our experiments, social reinforcements were only offered at the PDPP stage, but could be offered at the predecision stage as well at the same time. Whether a repetitive provision of a social reinforcement at the PDPP stage (in addition to the predecision stage) can augment choice closure is unknown, meriting further investigation. In addition, choice closure can be an issue at other stages of an online shopping journey. For instance, consumers may ponder other alternatives after their purchase, but before they receive or consume the product (e.g., issues related to purchase regret). We call for future research to study choice closure and relevant inducing mechanisms at other stages of the online shopping journey.

Fourth, although this study attested to the significant role of choice closure in inducing consumers’ satisfaction with their decision before product consumption, our manipulation of the interface cues did not generate exclusive effects on choice closure. Instead, we examined a dual-path model to account for other potential effects of our manipulations. We call for future research to explore potential “cleaner” interface designs that can lead primarily to effects on choice closure. Moreover, consumers may even have a reactance response when they realize that they are “encouraged” to close their choices (especially when consumers are under a suspicious condition). We call for future research to examine the scenarios where decision reinforcements lead to consumers’ reactance response, but not choice closure.

Finally, this study only considered gender, product knowledge, and past purchases for individual differences when examining the effects of a closure interface. Future research could further investigate other individual differences (e.g., age, socio-economic status, and need for closure) to examine whether different interface designs might be less or more effective for diverse user groups.

## 6. Conclusion

E-commerce consumers frequently navigate through vast amounts of information and cues during their decision-making process, due to the plethora of prod uct and service options available online. Pondering foregone alternatives at the PDPP stage does not add value to consumers, but can lead to discomfort and dissonance toward the choice. In practice, online cart abandonment is still a prevalent, but detrimental, issue for e-commerce. We propose and test a theory-driven approach for designing effective interfaces with reinforcement cues (i.e., external justifications) to foster the choice closure perception of consumers, including designs of direct reinforcement and social reinforcement at the PDPP stage. Our research not only provides a theoretical understanding of the effect of a reinforcement cue on online consumers’ perceived choice closure, but also offers novel design implications for online merchants. By stimulating the choice closure perception of online consumers via the reduction of cognitive dissonance to increase satisfaction with their decisions and intention to purchase at the postdecision stage, online merchants can provide consumers with a better experience of online shopping, while pushing them to cross the finish line (i.e., complete the intended purchase).

## Appendix A. Online Shopping Journey of Consumers

Prepurchase phase:

1. Predecision stage:

Consumers browse through different alternatives and similar products. The process provides information to reach a decision to pick the “right choice.” Mechanisms and cues can be designed to promote different or specific products.

2. Postdecision stage:

After consumers make a product decision, they put the “choice” into the shopping cart before they decide to proceed to checkout or save it for later. Cognitive dissonance (e.g., when consumers are still comparing the focal choice with other potential alternatives) can occur at this stage. Mechanisms and cues can be designed to support consumers’ purchase intention of the “choice” and induce consumers’ satisfaction with the “decision.” That is, mechanisms and cues can be designed to reduce dissonance and induce “choice closure.”

Note: For our study, although we assert that most online shoppers derive a decision or make a choice on items of interest so that they put product alternatives in the online shopping cart with an intention to complete the shopping process of those products (i.e., making a purchase), we acknowledge that there are other potential reasons where consumers put items into the online shopping cart “temporarily,” such as obtaining more information on certain products before their final decision or using the cart as shopping research to buy via another channel (Kukar-Kinney and Close 2010). Nevertheless, we distinguished product decision from purchase decision and considered putting items into a cart as a product decision to highlight the need to facilitate consumers’ conversion from postdeci sion prepurchase to purchase, which has received insufficient attention in academic research.

<sup>1</sup> In addition to external self-justification (hereafter abbreviated as “external justification”), internal self-justification is another strategy studied in the literature to confirm a decision. Internal self-justification refers to an individual’s self-formed perception to justify her decisions (Holland et al. 2002). Based on the insufficient justification argument, when people experience cognitive dissonance in their decisions, but cannot find external justification for the decisions, they tend to justify internally to reduce the dissonance (Aronson and Carlsmith 1963, Aronson and Aronson 2018). In this study, we focus on the effects of external justification on cognitive dissonance because online interface designs and cues provide abundant forms of external justifications.
