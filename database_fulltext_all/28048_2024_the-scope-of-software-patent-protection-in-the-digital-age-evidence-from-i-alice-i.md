# The Scope of Software Patent Protection in the Digital Age: Evidence from Alice

Yu-Kai Lin,<sup>a,</sup>\* Arun Rai<sup>a</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Robinson College of Business, Georgia State University, Atlanta, Georgia 30303

Abstract. Software is the foundation of the digital economy. The scope of software patent protection and the social and private value of software patenting are hence central to recent policy debates. The landmark 2014 U.S. Supreme Court ruling in Alice Corp v. CLS Bank had a profound impact on software patenting, drastically narrowing the scope of patent protection on software innovation. We theorize and provide empirical evidence that a narrower scope of software patent protection is associated with a greater degree of opensource activities and improved sales in software firms. We also evaluate how individual firm’s ex ante software patent stock may moderate the proposed relationships. These findings offer insights into the roles of patent scope for software innovation in the digital economy and have important implications for the software patent and digital innovation literatures. They also have compelling implications for patent policies on software and software-related inventions, such as artificial intelligence, blockchains, cloud computing, and other emerging digital innovations.

## 1. Introduction

The pervasive digital connectivity and data, rapid growth of mobile and cloud computing, and advances in digital platforms and infrastructures over the past two decades have led firms in almost all industries to digitalize their processes, strategies, and business models (Bharadwa et al. 2013, Constantinides et al. 2018). Labels, such as the fourth industrial revolution and the digital age, have been coined to describe the dramatic changes underlying the digital economy. These changes have also engendered novel conceptualizations of the organizing logic for software innovation, such as layered modular architecture (Yoo et al. 2010), incomplete by design (Garud et al. 2008), generativity (Yoo et al. 2012), and distributed innovation agency (Nambisan et al. 2017). A theme across these conceptualizations is a key role of open and large-scale collaborations involving the exchange, deliberation, and combination of knowledge from a dynamic and often unexpected set of actors (Levine and Prietula 2014, Malhotra et al. 2021). This new organizing logic, however, is in significant tension with the conventional patent-based innovation strategies and practices, which focus on identifying actors from the firm or a small set of partner firms who develop the innovation and actively limiting the diffusion and reuse of knowledge based on predefined plans and assumptions on how to create and appropriate value.

Against this background, we ask the following questions. To what extent should software innovations be protected by the patent system in the digital age? How might software firms—which innovate primarily in the software space—benefit or be adversely affected by a narrower scope of software patent protection? These questions are important because the scope of software patent protection can have direct implications for firms business performance and innovation strategies (Chien 2012, Graham and Vishnubhakat 2013). Indeed, prior research has suggested that software patenting is associated with sales and other firm performance measures (Lerner and Zhu 2007, Branstetter et al. 2019). Meanwhile, given that firms’ innovation activities are shaped by patent law, narrowing the scope of software patent protection will naturally decrease the propensity of patenting software innovations (Bessen and Hunt 2007,

Hall and Harhoff 2012). As such, we conjecture that firms will be likely to employ more inclusive, collaborative, and nonproprietary innovation strategies, such as open-source projects. Specifically, we investigate the impact of a narrower scope of software patent protection on open-source activity and sales in software firms because the former represents a distinct orthogonal innovation strategy compared with software patenting and the latter is a plausible concern when commercial firms engage in open-source activities and contribute their knowledge for “free.” As with most public policies, the impacts of narrowing the scope of software protection will be stronger for some firms and weaker for others, especially because firms had different degrees of reliance on software patents prior to the policy change. Therefore, we also investigate the heterogeneity of the impacts of a narrower scope of software patent protection arising from the firm’s ex ante software patenting.

The unique nature of software innovation has prompted debates about the usefulness of patent protection. Modern software innovation is typically characterized by rapid, emergent, cumulative, and interdependent development, with frequent recombination of third-party modules and customization of existing software for new application contexts. Researchers have found that formal patent protection can adversely impact the development of cumulative and sequential innovations (Galasso and Schankerman 2015), which explains why even commercial firms that value their intellectual property (IP) rights are engaging in open-innovation and open-source projects. As innovations in the digital age exhibit more fluid boundaries and distributed agency (Nambisan et al. 2017), patenting software innovation could discourage contributions from open communities, hinder the supply of third-party complements, and obstruct the formation of network externality (Lerner and Tirole 2005, Parker et al. 2017).

Meanwhile, there are systemic legislative and administrative issues with software patents in the U.S. patent system that create significant frictions in the innovation ecosystem. Studies on patent thickets, patent trolling, and patent infringement litigations have revealed that the patents at issue are often software related (Allison et al. 2010, Graham and Vishnubhakat 2013). This is in large part because of the relatively abstract and vague claims pervasive in the extant software patents, making them more likely subjects of litigation (Bessen and Meurer 2008).<sup>1</sup> As such, Bessen and Meurer (2008) suggested that an effective reform for the U.S. patent system would require subject matter tests to limit the range of software innovations that can be patented.

Despite the collaborative and combinatorial nature of modern software innovations and the issues with software patents, the extant software patent literature overwhelmingly supported firms’ efforts to build and expand their patent profiles for software innovations. However, according to Benkler (2017, p. 236), “the early focus on strong IP may be a holdover from an era that more generally saw IP as a core strategic imperative and had not yet incorporated the benefits of open commons or shared resources in reciprocal, collaborative dynamics.” Indeed, as documented extensively in the information systems (IS) literature, the landscape of software innovations has transformed radically in the past 15 years (Yoo et al. 2010, 2012; Nambisan et al. 2017). To this point, Jim Whitehurst, former president of IBM (currently the biggest patent holder in the United States), argues that “[w]ithout a doubt, the most exciting technological innovations occurring today are taking shape in open-source communities.” More recently, Chen et al. (2022) also suggest that “open-source software (OSS) has become the status quo for many commercial firms to achieve technological innovation.” As such, the roles of software patents and the effects of narrowing the scope of software patent protection on firms have become less apparent in the digital age.

The purpose of this study is to investigate how the scope of software patent protection affects software firms in the digital age. Leveraging the landmark 2014 Supreme Court ruling in Alice Corp v. CLS Bank (henceforth, Alice) as a natural experiment, we find that narrowing the scope of software patent protection is associated with an increase in open-source activities and sales in software firms. Our results also suggest that the effect on open-source activities from narrowing the scope of software patent protection is moderated by the firm’s ex ante software patent stock.

This article contributes to the research and policy on software patenting. We provide a useful empirical complement for the extant literature on software patents, which has drawn heavily from historical records of software patenting before 2006. From a policy perspective, patent offices around the world are reviewing or revising their IP policies in response to the emergence of artificial intelligence (AI), blockchain, internet of things (IoT), and other software-based innovations. Our findings contribute to this ongoing critical dialogue and offer compelling implications for the design of patent and innovation policies in these emerging areas in the digital age.

This “machine-or-transformation test” later became a key investigatory tool for software patentability analyses in courts and at the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO). As a result, the U.S. patent system gradually established the requirements for patenting software inventions.

Several cases in the 1990s on which the Federal Circuit ruled eroded the eligibility requirement for software inventions to be tied to a particular machine or yield any physical transformation. In In re Alappat (1994) and In re Lowry (1994), the court ruled that loading a conventional computing device with a novel algorithm or data structure makes the computer “a new machine” and thus, patent eligible. This case involved a patent for a data processing system used in managing mutual funds (U.S. Patent No. 5,193,056). The court not only sided in favor of the validity of the patent in suit but also, explicitly recognized the patentability of “business method” patents, most of which were implemented through software. The court opined that any numerical calculations that yielded a useful, concrete, and tangible result—even one expressed in numbers—would be eligible for patenting. As a result, State Street drastically expanded the scope of software patent protection, opening a floodgate of software and business method patent applications (Hall 2003).

The growth of software patenting accelerated at an unprecedented rate in the late 2000s and early 2010s (Figure 1). Amid this surge of software patents, the Supreme Court made three decisions related to the scope of patentable subject matter: Bilski (2010), Mayo (2012), and Alice (2014). In Bilski, the court held that steps instructing investors on how to hedge the risk of price changes resulting from fluctuations in market demand were not patent eligible because these were abstract ideas. In Mayo, the court ruled that methods for calibrating the right drug dosage of thiopurines for each individual patient based on their metabolism were not patent eligible because these methods merely followed the laws of nature. In Alice, the court decided that abstract ideas do not become patent eligible simply through implementation on a generic computer. ## 2.2. The Alice Ruling

On June 19, 2014, the Supreme Court announced its decision in Alice. This landmark legal case involved four patents owned by Alice Corporation. The claimed inventions in these patents concerned a computer-implemented method designed to facilitate an agreed-upon financial exchange between two parties. To mitigate settlement risk (i.e., the risk that one party will not uphold their contractual obligation in a transaction), the method outlines how to use a computer system as a third-party intermediary in real time on a global scale. The court unanimously ruled that the claimed inventions were not patentable under the patent law. Justice Thomas delivered the opinion of the court, stating that “the claims at issue amount to ‘nothing significantly more’ than an instruction to apply the abstract idea of intermediated settlement using some unspecified, generic computer.” In her concurring opinion, Justice Sotomayor further argued that “any claim that merely describes a method of doing business does not qualify as a ‘process’ under §101 (of the patent law).”

Section 101 of the U.S. patent law outlines four statutory categories of patentable subject matter: process, machine, manufacture, or composition of matter. It further explicitly prohibits patents of abstract ideas, laws of nature, and natural phenomena. The primary holding of the Alice decision is that reciting the use of a generic computer in a claim to implement a known and abstract idea does not confer patent eligibility upon the claimed invention. In other words, simply stating an abstract idea and adding the words “do it with a computer” are not a sufficient inventive concept to transform the abstract idea into patentable subject matter. This is consistent with Mayo, in which the court opined that when a patent is directed to a law of nature, it should also contain an “inventive concept” to ensure that the patented invention contains significantly more content than the natural law itself.

The consensus among patent scholars and practitioners has been that the Alice decision imposed a much narrower boundary of patent eligibility on software innovations, hence narrowing the scope of software patent protection in the United States. In view of the Alice decision, lower courts have received appeals to challenge existing software patents granted before Alice and have since invalidated at least hundreds of them. Gugliuzza and Lemley (2018) found that the Federal Circuit rejected 92.3% of patents challenged under the Alice precedent in the three years since Alice. In the meantime, the USPTO responded to the Alice ruling and issued “interim eligibility guidance” (IEG) to the examining corps in December 2014. The IEG set out a formal eligibility test to be used during examination in accordance with Alice. Chien and Wu (2018) analyzed office actions made during patent examination and found that the rejection rate among software and business method patent applications grew from 25% to over 75% after Alice.

In sum, the impact of Alice on software patenting is devastating; some analysts even considered it “deadly” (Tran 2016, p. 355). Although the U.S. patent system still allows software patents, it now has a much narrower scope of software patent protection. On the one hand, the narrower scope has led to a high rejection rate of software patent applications during the examination process. On the other hand, for software patents issued before Alice, there is a substantial chance that they would become unenforceable and declared invalid when brought to court because they may now fall outside the permissible scope.

## 2.3. Empirical Literature on Software Patents

Despite the significant presence of software patents in the U.S. patent system, assessing the causal effects of software patenting and its scope in the patent system has been difficult because of two empirical challenges. First, estimating the effects of software patents on firms’ outcomes requires researchers to explicitly ad dress the problem of endogeneity. As Lerner and Zhu (2007) pointed out, “there is no single event that unambiguously established the patentability of software” (Lerner and Zhu 2007, p. 512), and “more patenting activities could be the cause as well as the reaction of better performance” (Lerner and Zhu 2007, p. 523). The absence of a strong exogenous shock in firms’ soft ware patenting behavior made it a thorny task to obtain a reliable estimate for the effects of software patenting. Second, identifying software patents was typically a necessary first step in studying their effects, but it is notoriously difficult to define and identify software patents (Bessen and Hunt 2007, Hall and MacGarvie 2010). This is because software patents are not confined to any specific set of patent classes, and software elements in a patent document could be described without the usual keywords, such as “software” or “computer programs.”

As summarized in Table 2, there exist several empirical studies on software patenting. Most of these studies identify software patents through a combination of software-related keywords and patent classes. The extant evidence collectively suggests that a firm’s size, capital, and research and development (R&D) intensity are key determinants of software patenting. As for the consequences of software patenting, prior research consistently showed that obtaining more software patents was associated with better firm-level outcomes, such as firm value (Noel and Schankerman 2013, Branstetter et al. 2019), sales (Lerner and Zhu 2007, Ceccagnoli et al. 2012), survival rate (Wagner and Cockburn 2010), and new venture fundraising and exits (Cockburn and MacGarvie 2009, Ceccagnoli et al. 2012). However, it is noteworthy that all the extant evidence, even among the most recent studies, was derived from data before 2006.

The landscape of software innovation has changed significantly since 2006 in ways that motivate reconceptualizing and reevaluating the roles of software patents and particularly, their scope in the digital age. For example, the popularization of cloud computing has enabled new opportunities for software as a service, but the first public cloud, Amazon Web Service, was not available until late 2006. By the same token, GitHub was launched in 2008 and quickly became the leading digital ecosystem of open collaboration, encouraging commercial firms to engage in open-source software development at an unprecedented scale (Lin and Maruping 2022). Finally, although the notions of big data, deep learning, and IoT have existed in academia for a long time, their use cases in driving business innovations began to gain ground in the 2010s. Taken together, these recent developments have shifted the paradigm of software innovations toward one that values emergence and generativity (Yoo et al. 2012), data network effects (Gregory et al. 2021), and platform ecosystems (Constantinides et al. 2018). This new paradigm challenges our current understanding about the benefits of strong and expansive software patent protection, which tends to limit the interoperability and network externality of software innovations. After juxtaposing the extant empirical evidence of software patenting from before 2006 and the changing landscape of software innovations thereafter, we recognize a need for explicit exploration of the scope of software patent protection on firms in the digital age. From a theoretical perspective, this would help resolve ambiguity regarding the roles of scope of software patents when software innovations (and their development and management) have become increasingly distributed, open, and interconnected. From a pragmatic standpoint, such evaluation can be the basis for guidance on how managers, whose firms differ in the level of patent stock, should organize their software innovations. To this end, we now turn to develop our theory and hypotheses before proceeding to describe our method and results.

## 3. Theory and Hypotheses

This section develops our theory regarding the effects on software firms from reducing the scope of software patent protection in the digital age. We focus on opensource activities as an outcome, as they represent an orthogonal perspective to patent protection in organizing software innovation. We select sales as another outcome as it represents a key aspect of a firm’s business model that is likely to be associated with software innovation and has been a key outcome examined in past empirical IS research on patent value. We theorize how a narrower scope of software patent protection may promote open-source activities and sales in software firms. We then explicate how firms’ ex ante software patent stock may moderate the proposed relationships.

## 3.1. Scope of Software Patent Protection and Open-Source Activities

Although patenting is a common innovation strategy, it is not the only way to create and capture value from software innovations. We predict that a narrower scope of software patent protection would expand open-source activities in software firms owing to two reinforcing “push” and “pull” forces.

On the one hand, a narrower scope of software patent protection will push software firms away from software patenting and motivate them to explore other means to appropriate their software innovations. Indeed, researchers have argued that the “cost-effectiveness” of software patents contributed to the surge of software patenting (Bessen and Hunt 2007). When it was easy to obtain patents, firms were incentivized to utilize them rather than pursue more costly and uncertain alternative strategies to appropriate their software innovations (Hall and Harhoff 2012). In the mid-1990s, the computerized implementation of an abstract idea was sufficient to demonstrate “inventive concept” and patent eligibility; in the late 1990s, a numerical calculation that produced a useful, concrete, and tangible result was adequate to demonstrate inventive concept and patent eligibility, leading to the explosive growth in business method patents. Narrowing the scope of software patent protection, which deems broad tests as insufficient to demonstrate inventive concept and patent eligibility, alters the incentives for firms to pursue patents and consequently, their “patent production function.” It makes software patent rights more costly to obtain and less effective to assert because firms need to make sig nificantly more investments to legally establish that their software innovations involve satisfactory inventive concepts. As a result, firms likely would shift their attention from exclusive proprietary rights to more open and inclusive strategies for their software innovations. This creates an incentive for software firms to engage more in open source.

On the other hand, a narrower scope of software patent protection will increase the expected rewards from open-source activities, pulling firms into more opensource engagement. The value of a software innovation is determined not only by the innovation itself but also by the supply of third-party complements and the mod ifications and improvements originating from external actors (Yoo et al. 2010, 2012). Indeed, the primary value of a firm engaging in open-source activities derives from attracting complementary contributions from a largescale community and thereby, promoting generativity (Yoo et al. 2012). Prior research has suggested that software projects built on or related to patented inventions receive significantly lower user interest and voluntary developer contributions in the open-source community (Bar-Gill and Parchomovsky 2003, Wen et al. 2013). When a firm decreases its reliance on software patent ing and shifts its innovation strategy toward opensource activities, it is likely to generate more interest from voluntary contributors, hence promoting generativity. Moreover, because of the increased costs and risks to demonstrate patent eligibility and assert patents accompanying a reduced patent scope, firms are likely to see greater value from participating in open-source activities.

Hypothesis 1. Narrowing the scope of software patent protection is associated with an increase in open-source activities in software firms.

## 3.2. Scope of Software Patent Protection and Sales in Software Firms

Innovation scholars and practitioners have long recognized that patents are a double-edged sword. Although patents create incentives for the development, disclosure, and diffusion of inventive knowledge, the shortterm monopoly achieved through IP protection by patents erects frictions and raises costs in the market for technology (Hall 2007, Cockburn and MacGarvie 2009). We argue that a narrower scope of software patent protection may improve sales in software firms, doing so through two main mechanisms.

First, as we suggested earlier, narrowing the scope of software patent protection can help promote generativity because firms would likely employ more inclusive, collaborative, and nonproprietary innovation strategies. For users and consumers, combinatorial and cumulative software innovations developed with a large-scale community can be more valuable than their stand-alone counterparts developed with a go-it-alone strategy. Studies have shown that patents can inhibit the combination of new ideas and impede cumulative innovations (Galasso and Schankerman 2015, Benkler 2017). This is because patent protection inevitably incurs additional transaction costs and barriers for knowledge reuse, stymying the diffusion of cutting-edge technologies to both complementors and users (Lerner and Schankerman 2010). With a broader scope of patent protection, these costs and barriers, and the resulting diffusion, of proprietary software are likely to be hurt to a greater extent. By reducing software patenting, transaction costs, and barriers for complementors, a narrower scope of software patent protection should make software innovations more valuable and marketable for consumers in the digital age.

Second, narrowing the scope of software patent protection can lower patent litigation risks in software firms. Software patents are more likely to be litigated, with most patents owned by patent assertion entities (also referred to as “patent trolls”) being software patents (Bessen and Meurer 2008, Allison et al. 2010). As such, Bessen and Meurer (2008) point out that the litigation burden is growing rapidly for software patents, particularly with broad and abstract claims. All other things equal, consumers in the market would be less willing to transact with a firm involved in patent litigations because of the concerns that the litigations may adversely affect the seller or its products (Raghu et al.

2008, Mezzanotti 2021). Furthermore, the plaintiff of a patent litigation could seek an injunction relief from the court to remove the defendant’s products from the market if an infringement is found, which should directly reduce the defendant’s new product sales and market share. Narrowing the scope of software patent protection will reduce such patent litigation risks for software firms because (a) more software patent applications that are ambiguous would be screened out during the patent examination process and (b) software patents issued earlier with questionable validity could be more easily challenged in courts, making these ambiguous software patents unenforceable. As a result, there should be fewer patent litigations in software markets and lower patent litigation risks in software firms, thereby increasing market confidence and sales in software firms.

Hypothesis 2. Narrowing the scope of software patent protection is associated with an increase of sales in software firms.

## 3.3. Moderating Role of Ex Ante Software Patent Stock

Although it is generally understood that firms comingle the development of proprietary and open-source software to generate synergies in costs and incentivize adoption by consumers, it is important to recognize that the extent of comingling by firms is likely to be influenced by economic considerations (Lerner and Schankerman 2010). Firms clearly have varying degrees of focus on software patenting and correspondingly, patent stock before a patent system changes from a more permissive to a more restrictive scope of software patent protection. Differences in patent stock will likely alter firms’ risks and value from patenting following a reduction in the scope of software patent protection. Accordingly, we expect the firm’s ex ante software patent stock to moderate the effects from narrowing the scope of software patent protection that we proposed earlier.

For software firms with a higher ex ante software patent stock, we expect a greater increase in open source activities when the scope of software patent protection is reduced. Because these firms emphasized software patenting as their appropriability and compet itive strategy (Ceccagnoli 2009), the efficacy of these strategies is reduced. They are no longer able to achieve IP protection for broad claims, and the hurdles to patenting are likely to be greater in patentability assessment with narrower scope claims. Consequently, these firms would be more motivated to adjust their strategy for software innovation by expanding open-source activities to overcome the reduced efficacy of their emphasis on software patenting. Moreover, as user interest and voluntary developer contributions from the open-source community are likely to be lower in firms with a greater emphasis on patenting (Bar-Gill and Parchomovsky 2003, Wen et al. 2013), increased open-source activities by these firms can be useful to catalyze user interest and complementor contributions. Based on this, we suggest that firms with a higher patent stock have stronger motivations from a risk-reduction and value-creation perspective to increase their open-source activities following a narrowing in the scope of software patent reduction.

Hypothesis 3(a). Narrowing the scope of software patent protection is associated with a greater increase in open-source activities in software firms with a higher ex ante software patent stock compared with those with a lower software patent stock.

As suggested in our theorization for Hypothesis 2, narrowing the scope of patent protection should promote generativity and reduce litigation risks, thereby increasing sales. In terms of generativity, firms vested with a higher ex ante patent stock are likely to be better positioned to leverage the expanded generativity prospects arising from patent scope reduction because of two reasons. First, as the scope of software patent protection becomes narrower, it should lower the transaction costs and make it easier for these firms to access complement innovations from the market (Arora and Gambardella 2010), leading to a greater potential to recombine own with outside innovations. Second, because firms with a higher ex ante software patent stock are likely to have a greater increase in open-source activities (per Hypothesis 3(a)), these firms would also realize a greater access to complements and network externalities for their open-source offerings (Yoo et al. 2010, 2012; Nambisan et al. 2017). As for litigation risks, firms with a higher ex ante software patent stock are likely to have lower patent litigation risks because their proprietary IP assets should better guard against patent litigations (Mykytyn et al. 2002). Collectively, we expect firms with higher ex ante software patent stocks to accrue a greater increase in sales from narrowing the scope of software patent protection.

Hypothesis 3(b). Narrowing the scope of software patent protection is associated with a greater increase in sales in software firms with a higher ex ante software patent stock compared with those with a lower software patent stock.

## 4. Research Method

## 4.1. Research Design and Empirical Strategies

We leverage the Alice ruling as a natural experiment and employed a difference-in-differences (DiD) research design to examine the impact of narrowing the scope of software patent protection. Consistent with earlier work (Branstetter et al. 2019, Chung et al. 2019), our sample comprises U.S. public firms. The unit of analysis in our study is firm-quarter observations (as opposed to the common firm-year observations in prior software patent studies) because Alice was decided on June 19th, 2014. Therefore, we could more precisely capture the beforeafter “treatment” variation using quarterly observations compared with yearly observations. That is, in our study, the post-Alice period begins in the third quarter of 2014 and continues onward (July 2014 onward).

In our research design, we distinguish three groups of firms (software (SW) firms, other IT (IT-OTHER) firms, and non-IT (NON-IT) firms) following prior work and using Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) codes to categorize firms into the three groups (Li et al. 2010, Kim et al. 2022).<sup>2</sup> Although firms in all three groups could be patentees of software innovations, we expect their propensity of software patenting and thus, the impact of Alice to differ across groups. In theory, SW firms should see the greatest impact because their business models were precisely about developing software innovations. As such, it is fitting to consider SW firms as subjects taking the strongest dose of treatment in the Alice experiment and IT-OTHER and NON-IT firms as subjects taking a weaker dose of the Alice treatment. In Figure 2, we validate this theoretical prediction and indeed find a sharp decrease of patent grants to SW firms but not IT-OTHER and NON-IT firms after Alice.

## 4.2. Data and Variables

We collect and integrate data from Compustat, GitHub, and USPTO to construct a panel data set from 2010 to 2018, covering roughly four years of observations before and after Alice.<sup>3</sup> To estimate the effects of Alice, we limit our sample to public firms that had at least one observation before Alice and exclude observations with missing values in any of our variables. In all, our data include 75,885 firm-quarter observations from 3,062 public firms. We construct a set of variables for each observation. A summary of our variables appears in Table 3 (see Table B1 in the online appendix for the correlation matrix).

4.2.2. Key Independent Variables. As in the standard DiD design, our key independent variables are two binary variables: SW firm and post Alice. The value of SW firm is one if the focal firm is a software firm, and the value of post Alice is one if the observation comes from the third quarter of 2014 or later.

4.2.3. Moderator Variable. Software patent stock represents the cumulative knowledge assets and technology capability related to software innovation within a firm. To measure software patent stock, we first identify software patents granted to a firm based on the keywords and patent classes as in Kim et al. (2022). When calculating patent stock, prior studies typically applied a 15% depreciation rate for year-to-year stock depreciation (Hall and MacGarvie 2010, Chung et al. 2019). Given that the time unit in our analysis is a quarter, we use a smaller depreciation rate because the software patent stock is depreciated quarterly instead of yearly. Accordingly, we set the quarterly patent stock depreciation rate to 5%. Although we use software patent stock to investigate the potential moderating effects, we also include this variable as a control when estimating the main effects of Alice motivated by prior work on software patenting (Hall and MacGarvie 2010, Chung et al. 2019, Kim et al. 2022).

4.2.4. Control Variables. The literature has suggested a wide variety of factors that could affect innovation behavior and performance, which we included as controls in our model. First, we include nonsoftware patent stock (with the same 5% quarterly stock depreciation rate as software patent stock) to represent a firm’s cumulative knowledge and capability in nonsoftware fields (Kim et al. 2022). Second, we include firm age and size (measured by the total amount of assets) as controls. They have been frequently included in prior research when estimating the production and consequences of software patenting (e.g., Lerner and Zhu 2007, Hall and MacGarvie 2010, Ceccagnoli et al. 2012). Third, we control for R&D intensity, which is R&D expenses divided by total assets (Branstetter et al. 2019), and market share measured by the percentage of a firm’s sales at the four-digit SIC industry level. Finally, we follow Xue et al. (2012) and Chung et al. (2019) to operationalize and incorporate three industry-level factors at the SIC4 level, including industry Tobin’s q (using the median q), industry concentration (through the Herfindahl index), and industry dynamism (based on the volatility of sales).

## 4.3. Model Specification

Our baseline empirical model has the following specification:

$$
\ln (y _ {i t} + 1) = \beta (S W f i r m _ {i} \times P o s t A l i c e _ {t}) + \gamma X _ {i t} + \lambda_ {i} + \pi_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t},
$$

where $y _ { i t }$ is a firm-level outcome (open source or sales) and $X _ { i t }$ is a set of time-varying, log-transformed control variables discussed earlier. The firm fixed effects (FEs) $\lambda _ { i }$ account for any time-invariant, firm-specific unob servables, whereas the time FEs $\pi _ { t }$ address seasonality and any quarter-specific unobserved shocks. To determine the choice between FEs or random effects (REs), we find the Hausman test to be significant, suggesting that FEs were appropriate. The coefficient of interest is $\beta ,$ representing the elasticity of $y _ { i t }$ with respect to effects of the Alice ruling in software firms.

An important aspect of our DiD design and model specification is that we consider nonsoftware firms (IT-OTHER and NON-IT) as the control group, but some of these firms may still be software intensive. For example, Amazon, Meta, and other platform companies also heavily rely on software in their businesses and patented many software innovations, but they are in the control group in our research design. As a result, we likely will underestimate the effects of Alice. This is because in the potential outcome framework of causal inference, a causal treatment effect is conceptually the outcome difference between the treated group and the control group. If some firms in the control group were also affected by Alice (but to lesser degree compared with SW firms as we showed in Figure 2), the mean outcome of the control group will reflect some degree of the Alice effect. As such, when we take the difference in the outcomes between the treated group and the control group, the difference will be smaller (being attenuated) than what we would have if we were able to perfectly identify software firms.

Having presented the results of our estimation and their robustness, we now turn to examine the potential heterogenous treatment effects of Alice. ## 5.3. Analysis of Underlying Mechanisms

We conduct additional analyses to obtain further insights on the link between the Alice ruling and software patenting and provide suggestive evidence for the underlying mechanisms. We begin by probing how the Alice ruling led software firms to expand their open-source activities. We theorized that narrowing the scope of software patent protection would decrease the propensity of software patenting, and as a result, software firms would consider open source as an alternative means for appropriating their software innovations. Accordingly, we investigate if Alice resulted in fewer patent applications and patent grants on software innovations. The results of this analysis are presented in columns (1)–(6) of Table 6. We find that software firms indeed applied and obtained significantly fewer software patents after Alice, but there were no significant reductions in their nonsoftware patent applications and grants. Interestingly, we detected a significant increase in nonsoftware patent applications (but not nonsoftware patent grants) in software firms after Alice, suggesting that software firms had futile attempts to circumvent the impact of Alice through nonsoftware patenting. To complement these results, we further examine the scope of individual patent grants measured by the average number of claims in all the patents granted to a firm in a specific quarter (Merges and Nelson 1990). Column (7) of Table 6 shows that the scope in individual patents assigned to software firms decreased significantly in the post-Alice era. In all, the Alice ruling not only reduced the production of software patents but also, reduced the scope of individual patents granted to software firms. These results are consistent with our theoreti cal reasoning that as the patent system narrows the scope of software patent protection, the pursuit and ownership of IP protection through patents will decline in software firms, incentivizing them to use alternative means, particularly open source, to appropriate value from their software innovations.

We next examine how Alice affected sales in software firms. We first analyze whether and how the Alice ruling affected patent litigations. In our theoretical development, we suggested reduced litigation risks as a mechanism through which a narrower scope of software patent protection may increase sales. Accordingly, we integrate our data with the Patent Litigation Docket Reports Data Set from USPTO, which compiles litigation cases filed in and before 2016. We find that most firms (94.6%) in our sample did not have any litigation during 2010 and 2016. Therefore, we model the quarterly number of software patent litigations within each firm using a zero-inflated negative binomial (ZINB) model. Because ZINB models do not permit FEs, we instead include firm and time REs in our model. The results of this analysis are presented in column (8) of Table 6. The negative and significant coefficient of SW firm × Post Alice confirms that software firms indeed had lower numbers of patent litigations after the Alice ruling.

Finally, we explore the potential benefits of generativity to sales as software firms increase their opensource engagement after the Alice ruling. Specifically, we examine the mediating role of open-source activities on sales to evaluate this mechanism through causal mediation analysis (Imai et al. 2010). Causal mediation analysis is a recent methodological framework designed for the analysis of causal mechanisms (see Online Appendix C for an overview), and it has been used in recent IS research to investigate the underlying mechanisms for an observed effect (Lin et al. 2022, Park et al. 2023). In our setting, the intuition is that the total Alice effect on sales may be decomposed into two effects: direct and indirect. If open source (and the generativity afforded by it) is indeed a mechanism channeling the influence of Alice on sales, the total effect of Alice on sales should have a significant proportion of indirect effect that is mediated by open-source activities. The results show that a significant proportion of the total Alice effect on sales is channeled through software firms’ open-source activities (column (1) in Table 7). This supports our theorizing regarding generativity afforded by employing open-source development as a mechanism linking a narrower scope of software patent protection with increased sales. By splitting the software firms (columns (2) and (3) in Table 7), we further find that the mediation effect of Alice on sales through open source is significant only for software firms with a high level of ex ante software patent stock. This is consistent with our reasoning for Hypothesis 3(b) on the mechanism through which these firms will realize greater sales from open-source activities relative to those with low ex ante software patent stock. As we did not detect a difference in the total effect of Alice on sales between firms with high and low levels of ex ante patent stock (Table 5), we concluded that Hypothesis 3(b) is not supported. Competing mechanisms to open source might be in play that favor firms with low ex ante software patent stock, which warrants future research.

## 6. Discussion and Conclusion

Software is driving important developments in business and technology in the digital economy. As firms embrace digitalization, there is a significant need— now more so than ever—to understand how to effectively create and appropriate software innovation and to reexamine the assumptions and organizing logics. Indeed, there are salient theoretical tensions between the logic of organizing for emerging digital innovations, which stresses openness, recombination, interconnectivity, and generativity (Nambisan et al. 2017), and the conventional logic of proprietary IP protection for value creation and appropriation. Against this backdrop, we examined some of the economic consequences from narrowing the scope of software patent protection as a result of the landmark 2014 Alice ruling by the U.S. Supreme Court. We found that it increased open-source activities and sales in software firms.

This study provides three main contributions. First, we add to the IS and innovation literatures by surfacing the understudied link between software patents and open source, which are based on radically different assumptions of how to create and appropriate value from innovations. Echoing this tension, Mann (2005, p. 1010) argued that “the biggest question about the effectiveness of software patents involves the interrelation between commercial software development and open-source development.” However, “very little systematic analysis has examined the implications of patents for open source” (Lerner and Tirole 2005, p. 114). Our results suggested that a narrower scope of software patent protection could incentivize software firms to expand their open-source activities. This firm-level finding complements the Wen et al. (2013) individual-level finding that IP rights enforcement has a negative impact on user interest and developer activity in open-source projects. By and large, software innovations are cumulative and sequential innovations characterized by reuse and recombination of components and rewarded with the network effects of interoperability and standardization. The open-source paradigm naturally thrives in developing such software innovations. Our results imply that a broad scope of software patent protection is a strong disincentive for commercial firms to engage in open-source development. As Hall and Harhoff (2012, p. 548) observed, “introducing or strengthening a patent system (lengthening the patent term, broadening subject matter coverage or available scope, improving enforcement) unambiguously results in an increase in patenting and also in the use of patents as tools of firm strategy.” Our findings corroborate and extend this view by showing that a narrowing of patent scope not only reduced the extent of patenting (arising from a decline in patents applied and granted) and therefore, the use of patents as an IP strategy, but it also catalyzed the use of novel mechanisms for innovation, such as open source. We further show that software firms with a higher ex ante software patent stock would expand their open-source activities to a greater degree in response to the Alice ruling. Future IS research considering how to create and capture value from digital innovations can build on these insights to understand the interdependencies between IP protection for value appropriation and open innovation for generativity and their implications for policy design and corporate governance.

Second, we contribute to the literature of software patents by theorizing and evaluating the relationship between the scope of software patent protection and sales in software firms in the digital age. Earlier re search on software patents found a positive association between sales and strong IP rights in the software industry by examining data from the 1990s and early 2000s (Lerner and Zhu 2007, Ceccagnoli et al. 2012). Our findings extend and complement these earlier studies by considering more recent observations to represent the digital age, focusing on the scope of software patent protection (rather than software patent stock within a firm), and showing improved sales in software firms as the scope of software patent protection becomes narrower. In addition, we analyze and provide suggestive evidence that increased generativity afforded by a shift to open-source development and reduced cost and risk of patent litigations are potential mechanisms driving the improved sales per formance. Overall, our findings indicate a significant shift in the roles of software patent protection in appropriating value from software innovation in the past 15 years driven by the changing nature and landscape of software innovations.

Finally, our study contributes to the growing literature on patent policy design and evaluation by showing that narrowing the scope of software patent protection yields the positive private and social value intended by the patent system. Our findings provide insights on how the innovation paradox (i.e., to encourage the develop ment of cutting-edge innovations without sacrificing the social gains that can be accrued from their widespread diffusion (Lerner and Schankerman 2010)) can be incentivized by a less-is-more software patent protection policy. In other words, a narrower scope of software patent protection can lead to a win-win situation for software firms and society because the firms can still create and capture value from their innovations without the need to rely on formal IP protection to exclude other members in society from using these innovations. This less-ismore policy insight is likely applicable to a broader set of software-related innovations, such as AI, IoT, blockchain, and other emerging digital innovations, which are embedded in products and services in a wide range of industries. This is because these types of digital innovations often involve software and share technologica and economic characteristics with software innovations. With IT business value as an enduring topic in IS scholarship, IS researchers can play a key role in developing an important bridge linking the value of software innovations with policy design. As patent offices around the world are developing IP guidelines for emerging digital technologies, our findings offer empirical evidence from a closely related technological field of software about policy design and illuminate potential economic and social benefits from a narrower scope of patent protection on emerging, software-related inventions in the digital economy.

Our work has some limitations. First, the Alice ruling also affects nonsoftware firms in our primary analysis and low-software intensity firms in our robustness tests, albeit to a lesser degree. As such, our estimates reflect the differential effects between high and low dosages of treatment rather than between treated and untreated. As a result, our estimates were likely more conservative than the magnitude of the actual effects. Second, we only consider firms’ open-source activities on GitHub following the recent open-source literature (Chen et al. 2022, Lin and Maruping 2022). Although GitHub is by far the leading and most popular open-source platform, there exist similar sites, such as SourceForge, Bitbucket, and GitLab. Future work may consider firms’ open-source activities across these platforms to assess the impact of Alice more comprehensively. Third and finally, firms can engage in both patenting and open-source activities at the same time. A promising direction of future research is to explore the contingencies and configurations in firms’ choices of different IP strategies and the resulting benefits and costs.
