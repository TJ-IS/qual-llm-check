# Clocking in or Not? Optimal Design of a Novel Gamified Business Model in Online Learning

Abstract. Clocking-in cash-back (CIC), an emerging gamified business model in online learning, has recently garnered significant attention. CIC allows users to secure a full refund of the course fee through consecutive completion of specific tasks within a required time window. These tasks, known as clocking in, encompass activities such as daily assignments and sharing progress updates on social media. By employing this gamification system, the firm effectively monitors user efforts, categorizing them as winners or quitters based on clocking-in completion. Despite its growing popularity, this new business model has yet to be systematically analyzed in the literature. This paper fills this critical gap by examining how an online learning firm should set the optimal time window for its course and how the time window is affected by context-specific factors. We identify two opposing effects associ ated with extending the time window on users’ quitting time: the psychological-disutility increasing effect (negative) and the effort-cost decreasing effect (positive). Our results indicate that as quitters’ positive word-of-mouth (WOM) effects increase, there are cases in which the firm should opt for shortening the time window, primarily because of the psychologicaldisutility increasing effect outweighing the effort-cost decreasing effect. Furthermore, we find that it is not always beneficial for the firm to extend the time window when there is an increased presence of high-ability users. Additionally, we find that as the marginal content creation cost rises, instead of reducing the difficulty level of each task, the firm may find it more advantageous to raise the difficulty level by shortening the time window. Our find ings provide valuable insights that online learning firms can utilize to enhance their design of the CIC mechanism.

## 1. Introduction

Online learning has emerged as a non-traditional education model that utilizes technology to deliver educational content to geographically separated students, enabling their synchronous or asynchronous interaction with instructors (Allen and Seaman 2017). It is often described as a flexible mode of learning that grants easy access to learning materials anytime and from anywhere (Muljana and Luo 2019). The online learning industry has experienced rapid growth because of the recent development of new web-based tools. According to Research and Markets (2022), the global online learning market is projected to reach \$585.48 billion by 2027, up from \$269.87 billion in 2021, partly driven by advancements in information technologies and a growing number of users willing to pay for online courses (Shah 2019).

Facing the fast-growing online learning market, firms have increasingly ventured into exploring new gamified business models for paid online courses (Pappas 2019). However, these business models are still in their early stages of development, and there is a lack of practical and academic guidance on how to optimize their design to maximize firms’ profits. In this paper, we aim to address this important issue by analyzing one such gamified business model called clocking-in cash-back (CIC), in which users are entitled to a refund of the course fee upon completing specific tasks consecutively for a required number of days.

## 1.1. Motivation

Although online learning provides easy access to learn ing materials from anywhere and usually at any time, the users, especially the working professionals who rely on fragmented time to study, often struggle to adhere to their study plans and complete courses.<sup>1</sup> To tackle this challenge, firms in the online learning industry have actively explored new business models. One such model, known as CIC, which has been adopted by some renowned online learning firms in China (e.g., Liulishuo, QingClass, and YLYK), has recently received substantial public attention (Duanshu 2019). Under the CIC model,<sup>2</sup> users who purchase a course are entitled to a refund only if they successfully complete specific daily tasks consecutively for a required number of days. Examples of such tasks include completing daily assignments and sharing progress updates on social media. Here, we refer to the user’s endeavor to complete a specific daily task as clocking-in and the required number of days as the clocking-in time window or the time window. According to iResearch (2019), clocking-in has become a regular practice across various domains, ranging from language learning to physical exercise.

Clocking-in is essentially a gamified process that motivates users to exert effort toward a specific goal (Karen 2018). On the one hand, users incur certain costs when they clock in. These costs encompass not only the effort needed to complete each daily task, but also the psychological disutilities that arise when users work toward fulfilling the clocking-in requirement. Such psychological disutilities are commonly observed in learning environments, in which individuals often experience anxiety and a decline in willpower when their learning goals have not been achieved (Jones and Cale 1997, Miller et al. 2012). On the other hand, users are driven by both extrinsic motivation (i.e., the potential for a course fee refund) and intrinsic motivation (i.e., their valuation of the course) within the gamified process. These two types of incentives are typically present in a gamification system, aiming to keep users engaged and motivated (Fortunato and Cruz 2018). Prior studies (e.g., C¸akırog˘ lu et al. 2017) show that gamification can positively influence users’ engagement and academic achievement, thereby potentially helping online learning firms enhance their profitability.

Online learning firms can generate revenue from two distinct user groups: (i) users who complete the clocking-in requirement, termed the winners, and (ii) those who quit the clocking-in process, termed the quitters. Under the CIC model, users can share their progress updates and experiences on social media after completing each clocking-in instance. Therefore, both winners and quitters can help improve the firms’ profitability by contributing to the word-of-mouth (WOM) effects, which refers to the impact of user-generated words, reviews, or opinions about the product on future sales and consumption (Ceran et al. 2016). Marketers have increasingly leveraged WOM effects to promote products and disseminate information (Godes and

Mayzlin 2009, Kumar and Qiu 2022, Kumar et al. 2022). It is widely acknowledged that firms critically depend on user-generated WOM effects in acquiring and engaging customers (Hong et al. 2017, Mallipeddi et al. 2021). In our paper, we refer to the benefits that firms derive from user-generated WOM effects as WOM revenue. It is important to note that firms can also generate revenue from the course fees that quitters have paid up front when joining the program. In addition, the firm incurs costs associated with content creation, particularly when the course involves numerous complex assignments. Furthermore, there is a potential revenue loss resulting from quitters who may spread negative comments about the course after leaving the program.

With the CIC model at their disposal, online learning firms are now in a better position to engage users. Specifically, through this technology-enabled gamification system (i.e., the CIC model), firms can accurately monitor users’ clocking-in behavior at minimal cost and provide rewards (i.e., course fee refunds) to those who successfully complete the course as winners. Previous studies (e.g., Lee et al. 2023) show that offering rebates or other economic incentives is an effective method of motivating users to accomplish specific goals.

Although the CIC business model holds significant commercial potential, it remains unclear how firms should design the CIC mechanism to improve their profitability. In practice, the selection of clocking-in time window lengths may vary among firms offering similar courses. For example, as shown in Figure 1,<sup>3</sup> two online learning firms (i.e., YLYK and DAVINCI) offer courses with similar content (i.e., English magazine reading) and the same length (i.e., 100 days). The prices of the courses and the required daily tasks are also comparable. However, YLYK opts for an 80-day time window, whereas DAVINCI sets it at 100 days. This variation in determining clocking-in time windows highlights the lack of consistent guidelines for online learning firms. Extending the time window for a specific course leads to users perceiving a diminished utility from the potential refund at any given time before fulfilling the clocking-in requirement. Moreover, this extension may subject users to increased psychological disutilities as they work toward

On the other hand, because the objective of a specific course is to lead users through the process of learning a predetermined amount of content on a particular subject, extending the time window reduces the difficulty level of a daily task, resulting in decreased costs for users to complete the task. Consequently, this can lead to changes in the total number of clocking-in instances completed by users and the associated content creation cost, potentially impacting the firm’s profitability.

In this paper, our focus is on examining the strategies employed by online learning firms to determine the optimal length of the clocking-in time window in the CIC model. We also investigate how various contextspecific factors, such as the marginal WOM effects of quitters, impact the time window. Moreover, we aim to gain a deeper understanding of the crucial role played by the time window in the design of the CIC mechanism by analyzing its impact on the total number of clockingin instances and the revenue generated from users. Our analysis provides valuable managerial insights for online learning firms. In the next section, we delve into the specific research questions, key findings, and contributions of this paper.

## 1.2. Research Questions and Key Findings

The growing adoption of the CIC business model by online learning firms has sparked considerable interest in designing a more effective mechanism for CIC. However, the existing literature on online learning offers limited insights in this regard. The present study seeks to fill this critical gap by employing a dynamic programming modeling approach to examine how online learning firms should determine the optimal time window under the CIC mechanism.

Before determining the optimal time window, it is essential for the firm to understand the role of the time window in the design of the CIC mechanism. One important metric for measuring the effectiveness of the mechanism is the total number of clocking-in instances. Therefore, our investigation begins by addressing the following question: How does extending the time window impact the total number of clocking-in instances? Our findings suggest that increasing the time window can lead to either more or fewer clocking-in instances, depending on various context-specific factors. This result is driven by two opposing effects caused by the increase in the time window on users’ quitting time: the psychologicaldisutility increasing effect, which leads to higher disutilities and an earlier quitting time for users, and the effort-cost decreasing effect, which reduces the difficulty level of each clocking-in instance and results in a later quitting time and more completed instances for users. Our study reveals that there are cases in which the firm should set a higher (lower) clocking-in requirement, that is, a longer (shorter) time window, to generate more clocking-in instances as the effort-cost decreasing (psychological-disutility increasing) effect dominates in such cases.

The optimal decision for the firm regarding the time window is influenced by various context-specific factors, including the positive WOM effects generated by quitters. In our context, quitters play a crucial role by contributing to the firm’s WOM revenue and generat ing course fee revenue. Given that WOM revenue has become a significant source of revenue for online learning firms, an interesting question naturally arises: How should the firm adjust the time window when the positive WOM effects from quitters increase? Our findings suggest that the firm has an incentive to extend the time window as quitters’ marginal positive WOM effects increase from a relatively large value. In practice, many online learning firms offer courses that cover skills or topics frequently used or discussed by users. Such courses naturally generate substantial WOM effects as users usually have abundant words, reviews, or opinions to share. In light of our findings, firms may find it necessary to extend the time window for such courses, particularly if they plan to employ tactics such as discussion forums to further enhance the WOM effects.

Additionally, the composition of users in terms of their ability to complete clocking-in instances is an important factor that directly impacts the firm’s profitability. Here, we consider the heterogeneous nature of users’ ability to complete each clocking-in instance, dis tinguishing between high- and low-ability users with their marginal clocking-in costs exhibiting a negative correlation with their ability. Hence, a natural question arises: How should the firm adjust the time window as the proportion of high-ability users increases? Our finding suggests that, under certain circumstances, the firm is better off shortening the time window as the proportion of high-ability users increases. Thus, when determining the optimal clocking-in time windows for various courses, online learning firms must carefully consider the composition of their user base. For example, when designing programming language learning courses targeting professionals, who typically comprise a significant proportion of high-ability users, the firm may sometimes need to shorten the time window for such courses when there is an increased presence of high-ability users.

Moreover, given the firms’ current significant costs associated with creating high-quality online content (Chapman Alliance 2010), we delve into the impact of marginal content creation cost on the optimal time window. Specifically, we ask the question: How should the firm adjust the time window as the marginal content creation cost increases? Our findings reveal that, in certain cases, the optimal time window may decrease as the marginal content creation cost increases. In practice, companies that provide advanced skill training courses often face significant content creation costs. When their courses include more visually stunning and engaging elements (e.g., high-quality videos and customized photos), which often come with relatively high content creation expenses (ReviewMyLMS 2021), it may be necessary for them to extend the time window.

We also conduct analyses of how various contextspecific factors impact consumer surplus. Additionally, we examine whether the key insights from the main model can carry over to other settings by studying several model extensions that address issues such as partial refunds and network effects.

Our research makes substantial contributions from both theoretical and practical perspectives. First, this work represents one of the pioneering endeavors to explore the CIC model, providing valuable guidance for online learning firms on how to maximize their profits by improving the design of the CIC mechanism. We also contribute to the literature on price discrimination by modeling a novel way of segmenting users based on their clocking-in costs. Second, to the best of our knowledge, most of the extant research on gamification systems in online learning (e.g., Shi et al. 2014) is conducted from an empirical or experimental standpoint. Our study, on the other hand, is among the first to approach this topic using an analytical framework. In particular, we analytically model the gamified process of the CIC mechanism and determine the optimal clocking-in time window for this technology-enabled gamification system. Third, our study delves into users’ purchase behavior in the context of online learning. Prior research highlights the challenges faced by firms in generating sufficient revenue from online content sales (Myllylahti 2014) as individuals often exhibit reluctance when it comes to paying for online content. However, as people have become increasingly accustomed to purchasing online content (Newman 2017), it is both timely and crucial to study users’ purchase behavior within the emerging CIC model. Moreover, there is a scarcity of research on paid content in the field of online learning, and our study aims to fill this gap in the literature. Fourth, we undertake an in-depth investigation of the WOM effects in the context of online learning, an aspect that has not been systemically studied in the extant literature. Specifically, we explore the relationship between the optimal time window and quitters’ WOM effects.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 discusses the related literature and highlights the contribution of our work. Section 3 introduces our model framework. Section 4 analyzes how the firm should determine the optimal time window and how the time window and the consumer surplus are affected by various factors related to online learning. In Section ${ \check { 5 } } ,$ we expand our analysis to encompass more general cases, including the consideration of partial refunds within our model. We conclude the paper with discussions on research contributions and managerial implications in Section 6.

## 2. Related Literature

Our work is mainly related to four streams of literature: (i) online learning, (ii) gamification in learning, (iii) price discrimination, and (iv) paid online content. Next, we briefly review the relevant research and highlight our contributions to each stream.

## 2.1. Online Learning

Online learning has become increasingly popular because of its potential to provide more flexible access to content and instruction from anywhere and at any time (Muljana and Luo 2019). Demetriadis and Pombortsis (2007) con duct an experiment to investigate the extent to which students’ knowledge acquisition improves through the adoption of e-lectures that support flexible learning experiences. Most studies in this stream of literature focus on exploring various factors that affect users’ participation and performance. In an earlier study, Vonderwell and Zachariah (2005) identify technology characteristics, content area experience, student roles, instructional tasks, and information overload as factors affecting individuals participation in online learning. In a similar vein, Hrastinski (2009) makes an initial attempt to develop a theory that characterizes online learning as online participation. Lu et al. (2013) employ social capital theory to establish a causal relationship between social capital and student satisfaction. More recently, De Barba et al. (2016) investigate the influence of motivation and participation on students performance in a massive open online course and find that participation is the most significant predictor of performance. Our study complements this stream of literature by taking into account various factors that affect users’ participation, such as the perceived reward, which have not been systematically examined before.

Previous studies demonstrate that student percep tions in online learning are significantly influenced by three key factors: consistency in course design, interaction with course instructors, and active discussion (Swan 2002). However, one major challenge faced in online learning is the lack of interaction. Kear (2010) shows that social presence in online learning communities can be improved when communication systems are designed in cooperation with attention to communication styles within these environments. Moreover, Swan (2003) reviews the literature on the effectiveness of online learning in terms of interactivity and explores various aspects of learner interactions, including interactions with course content, instructors, and fellow classmates in online course environments. However, these studies have not examined the business mechanisms associated with paid content in the context of online learning. In this paper, we aim to fill this gap by proposing a modeling framework for a novel business mechanism called the CIC model within the online learning context.

## 2.2. Gamification in Learning

Gamification, defined as the incorporation of game design elements in nongame settings (Deterding et al. 2011), has emerged as an effective approach for enhancing students’ engagement and performance in the learning environment. Shi et al. (2014) employ flow theory and self-determination theory to boost intrinsic motivation and propose a set of contextual gamification strategies specifically designed for e-learning environments. Urh et al. (2015) show that integrating gamification into the e-learning context can have a positive impact on the learning process, resulting in higher satisfaction and greater engagement among students. Santhanam et al. (2016) focus on competition in game design and explore its adaptation to improve learning outcomes and foster learner engagement. More recently, Landers et al. (2017) adopt the goal-setting theory to explain the framework of leaderboards and find them to be nearly as effective as difficult-to-impossible goals in enhancing task performance.

Previous studies primarily investigate the incorporation of game elements in the learning context from an empirical or experimental perspective. However, to the best of our knowledge, our research represents one of the first attempts to analytically model the gamification system in online learning. In particular, we focus on the time window within this gamification system when identifying the optimal design of a gamified business model in the online learning context. Under the employment context, whereas extant literature extensively addresses the significance of establishing appropriate challenge levels for daily tasks to motivate employees within an employment context (Kumar 2013), scant attention is given to this issue in the context of learning. Thus, we aim to fill this gap by analyzing how online learning firms should optimally determine the difficulty level of daily tasks under the CIC model.

## 2.3. Price Discrimination

The CIC model is essentially a price discrimination mechanism wherein winners can receive a refund and access the course for free, whereas quitters are required to pay for the course. Price discrimination is commonly employed in various marketing strategies (e.g., loyalty reward programs, referral programs, and versioning) to promote products or services to consumers.

The CIC mechanism is closely related to the frequency reward program (FRP), a type of loyalty reward program in which consumers earn points by spending with merchants and redeeming these points for dollar-valued rewards after reaching a certain point threshold. Previous studies on FRP primarily explore the conditions under which consumers would participate in the program and when firms can benefit from its implementation. For example, Kivetz and Simonson (2002) examine how the effort level participants invest in obtaining rewards impacts their preferences for different types of rewards and their decisions to join an FRP. In another related study, Sun and Zhang (2019) study whether a monopolistic firm should set a long or short expiration term for reward redemption. Our study focuses on a new business model that is similar to yet different from the conventional frequency reward program. In conventional FRPs, consumers are required to make purchases each time, and the money they spend negatively impacts their utility (Hartmann and Viard 2008). However, in our model, once users have paid the course fee up front, they actually gain a positive reward instead of incurring expenses with each clocking-in instance, which distinguishes it from the mechanisms of conventional FRPs.

Another commonly used strategy for price discrimi nation is the use of referral programs. Several studies in the literature examine the effectiveness of referral programs, such as giving coupons and cash rewards, as well as how to enhance their design. Biyalogorsky et al. (2001) investigate when referral rewards should be offered to motivate referrals and derive the optimal combination of rewards and prices that results in the most profitable referrals. Hong et al. (2017) examine the effects of social distance and monetary incentives on the perfor mance of competing designs for online referral systems. Jung et al. (2020) conduct a large-scale randomized field experiment to demonstrate how firms can benefit from framing calls to action for referral programs. The business model we study is different from traditional referral reward programs in a few key aspects. First, the reward in this model is not dependent on another person acting on the recommendation but rather on the user’s own completion of specific tasks. Second, this model requires users to consecutively complete specific tasks to qualify for a refund, which is a unique feature not commonly seen in traditional referral reward programs. These distinctions highlight the novel aspects of the CIC model as a price discrimination mechanism in the context of online learning.

The discrimination of the user base in our work bears a resemblance to that in the versioning literature (e.g., Chellappa and Shivendu 2005). However, our work diverges significantly from traditional versioning problems in terms of how discrimination is implemented. Unlike the versioning literature, in our specific context, all users can access the same course content, and discrimination is based on their ability to complete the clocking-in instances (i.e., their marginal clocking-in costs of completing each instance), resulting in varying refund amounts for each user.

## 2.4. Paid Online Content

Online content providers, through their long-standing adoption of an advertising-based business model, have established a prevailing “reference price of zero” mindset among individuals (Punj 2015). Consequently, users are unaccustomed to paying for online content. Previous studies primarily focus on examining the antecedents of willingness to pay (WTP) for online content. Wang et al. (2013) find that perceived value and ethical self-efficacy for online piracy are positively related to music purchase intentions. More recently, Li et al. (2020) estimate a demand model for paid online apps and quantify the effect of price promotions on download volume. Our paper incorporates the concept of WTP and complements this stream of literature by further examining how the time window affects users’ joining decisions. Moreover, prior studies predominantly explore the determinants of WTP for online content from empirical perspectives, whereas our study models WTP using an analytical approach.

Considerable debate surrounds the revenue-generating potential of different business models for online content, specifically between advertising- and subscription-based approaches. Fan et al. (2007) analyze the trade-offs between pricing and advertising strategies for media companies to distribute digital media. Building upon this, Kumar and Sethi (2009) show that hybrid models, which are based on a combination of subscription fees and advertising revenues, are increasingly replacing the pure revenue models. On the other hand, Pauwels and Weiss (2008) find that transitioning from an advertising-based model to a subscription-based model negatively affects advertising revenue. Furthermore, they examine how the firm should adopt promotional strategies to attract paid subscribers.

In this paper, we study the CIC model, a novel business mechanism for paid content in online learning. The operational mechanism of the CIC model differs from that of the subscription-based business model. Specifically, in the subscription-based model, users are required to make periodic payments for accessing the content. In contrast, in the CIC model, users only need to pay the course fee once upon joining the program and can receive a full refund after consecutively clocking in over a specific time period. Despite its growing popularity, there is limited research exploring the business models of paid content in online learning. To the best of our knowledge, this research represents one of the pioneering endeavors to analytically explore the mechanism design problem concerning paid online content.

## 3. Main Model

In this section, we start with a discussion of the objective functions of the users and the firm under the CIC mecha nism, followed by an introduction to our optimization model.

## 3.1. Model Description

We consider a typical online learning firm that employs the CIC mechanism, such as QingClass, a renowned Chinese company specializing in English learning courses. In reality, as shown earlier in Figure 1, firms may differ in their decisions regarding the clocking-in time window for courses with similar content. We use T to denote the clocking-in time window and L $( 0 < T \leq L )$ to denote the course length (i.e., the total number of course segments). We consider L to be fixed and exogenously given in our model. In this program, a user is entitled to a full refund of the course fee only when the user has completed T consecutive clocking-in instances. The clocking-in time window T, which is the decision variable of the firm in our model, can directly impact both the users’ utilities and the firm’s profit.

A clocking-in instance refers to the completion of a daily task assigned within each course segment, which is typically based on the learning content covered in that segment. To illustrate, in the vocabulary improvement course depicted in the left panel of Figure 2, users are required to take a quiz after each day’s learning session. Specifically, to successfully complete the daily task, users must answer a minimum of 24 out of 30 questions correctly. Note that users have the option to retake the quiz multiple times until they meet the completion criteria. The right panel of Figure 2 showcases an illustrative example of the course fee refund certification wherein users who complete the required learning tasks are entitled to a full refund. In the following sub sections, we start with formulating the users’ problem and then investigate the firm’s profit and its optimization problem.

3.1.1. Problem of Users. As shown in Figure 3, in stage 0, the firm determines the clocking-in time window before releasing the program. We consider the following multistage problem for users: In stage 1, at time $t = 0 ,$ users decide whether to join the learning program. Their purchase decisions are based on various factors, including the time window, their prior valuations, and the course price, all of which are observable prior to making the purchase. Without loss of generality, we normalize the total number of users in the market to one and denote the mass of users joining the learning program in stage 1 (referred to as joining users) as N $( 0 \leq N \leq 1 )$ . Following prior studies $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , Chen and Guo 2014), we consider users to be homogenous in their prior valuations of the course (denoted by V). In stage $2 \AA ( 1 \leq t < T )$ , where t � 1 refers to the start of stage $\bar { 2 } ,$ each user who has joined the program needs to decide whether to clock in at time t before becoming eligible to receive a refund. In stage $3 \ ( t = T )$ , users decide whether to clock in and receive a refund if they choose to do so. In stage 4 $( T < t \leq L )$ , winners decide whether to clock in after receiving the refund. In the following, we provide a detailed exploration of users’ decisions regarding joining the learning program and their clocking-in choices across stages 1–4.

3.1.1.1. Stage 1: Joining Decision. In stage 1 $( t = 0 )$ when making the joining decision, user i takes into consideration (1) the utilities to be derived from completing future instances (denoted by $E _ { U } )$ , which are hereafter referred to as future utilities; (2) the utility from the potential refund (as viewed from time $t = 0 ,$ denoted by R(0)); (3) the psychological disutilities incurred from working toward fulfilling the clocking-in requirement (as viewed from time $t = 0$ , denoted by G(0)); and (4) the disutility associated with the price (denoted by p) that the user needs to pay to enroll in the course. The utilities to be derived from completing future instances $( E _ { U } )$ in stage 1 consists of two parts: the prior valuation of the course (V) and the effort costs of completing the course.

For user i, the effort costs of completing each clockingin instance can be calculated as $c _ { i } d ,$ , where $c _ { i }$ denotes user i’s clocking-in cost per unit difficulty level (marginal clocking-in cost for short) and $d > 0$ denotes the difficulty level of each clocking-in instance. In reality, the ability to complete clocking-in instances can vary among users. Generally, given the course content, high-ability users tend to incur lower effort costs to complete each clocking-in instance compared with low-ability ones. Thus, we consider that users exhibit heterogeneity in their marginal clocking-in costs $c _ { i } ,$ which are negatively correlated with their ability to complete instances. In the main model, we consider the existence of two distinct types of users in the market based on their marginal clocking-in costs: low-cost users (with $c _ { i } = c _ { l } )$ , who have a relatively high ability to complete instances, and highcost users (with $c _ { i } = c _ { h } )$ , who have a relatively low ability to complete instances, where $0 \leq c _ { l } \leq c _ { h } \leq 1 . { } ^ { \mathrm { \scriptsize { \tilde { 4 } } } }$ We denote the proportions of low- and high-cost users among all joining users by η and $1 - \eta ,$ , respectively. Moreover, d is determined by the per-period workload (i.e., workload per course segment). In practice, the workload of completing a course segment typically increases with the per-period valuation of course and decreases with the clocking-in time window (Hansen 2014, Johnson 2016); thus, we consider that d takes the functional form $\frac { \alpha V ( \gamma - T ) } { L }$ , where $\alpha > 0$ is a scaling factor and $\gamma > L$ is a positive constant.

Drawing on the existing literature (e.g., Xu et al. 2019), we employ the exponential discounting framework to capture the future utilities, denoting the exponential discounting parameter as θ. Recall that, if users have completed $\check { T }$ clocking-in instances consecutively, they are entitled to a full refund of the course fee (p). Thus, as viewed from time $t = 0 ,$ , the utility from the potential refund can be calculated as $R ( 0 ) \dot { = } \theta ^ { T } p ;$ moreover, the discounted effort costs of completing all future instances (i.e., the entire course) can be calculated as $\scriptstyle \sum _ { \tau = 1 } ^ { L } ( \theta ^ { \tau } c _ { i } d )$

When working toward fulfilling the clocking-in requirement, users also incur certain psychological disutilities, primarily stemming from anxiety (Jones and Cale 1997) and willpower depletion (Miller et al. 2012). 3.1.1.2. Stage 2: Clocking-in Decision Before Receiving the Refund. In stage $2 \ ( 1 \leq t < T )$ , users make their clocking-in decisions in each period before becoming eligible to receive a refund. When making these decisions, users consider the current-period utility derived from clocking-in at time t and the utilities to be derived from the future clocking-in instances. They also take into account the utility from the potential refund and the psychological disutilities associated with working toward fulfilling the clocking-in requirement. To fully capture users’ clocking-in behavior over time, we follow the literature $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ ., Hartmann and Viard 2008, Xu et al. 2019) and formulate a dynamic programming (DP) model. We first specify the exact utility a user can receive in each period from clocking in and then describe how a user’s state variables transition from one period to the next. We use $u _ { i , t }$ to represent the current-period utility of user i at time t. If user i decides to clock in, $u _ { i , t }$ includes the user’s updated valuation of completing the current course segment and the corresponding effort cost that the user would incur. Thus, it can be calculated as

$$
u _ {i, t} = v _ {t} - c _ {i} d = \frac {V _ {t}}{L} - c _ {i} d,
$$

where the first term $\left( v _ { t } \right)$ calculates the updated valuation of one clocking-in instance at time $t ,$ and the second term calculates the cost of completing one clocking-in instance.

Following previous studies $( \mathrm { e . g . } ,$ , Shapiro 1982), we consider that the users’ valuations of the course in stage 2 may change over time because of the learning effect. In particular, we use $V + k$ to represent users’ true valuation of the entire course, where k captures the deviation of their true valuation from the prior valuation of the course (V). When accounting for the learning effect, the updated valuation of the course for users after clockingin t instances can be calculated as $V _ { t } = V + f ( t ) k$ , where $f ( t )$ can be interpreted as the rate at which users gradually update their valuation of the course (similar to Huang et al. 2014). As users complete more clocking-in instances, their valuation for the course converges toward the true valuation $( V + k )$ . In particular, when a user completes the entire course $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , t = L )$ , the user’s belief about the course value equals $V + k$ . To capture this valuation updating process, we consider a specific functional form: ${ \bf \widehat { f } } ( t ) = t / \widehat { L }$

In reality, many online courses $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , English learning courses on Liulishuo, Mint Reading, and QingClass) provide only a general overview of the course content without offering a specific syllabus (as shown in Figure 4). Consequently, users face challenges in determining the value of the material they will learn before making a purchase. Even if the firm provides a specific syllabus prior to purchase, users may still need to update their valuations of the course over time because of the varia tion in content across different course segments. Specifically, when k is positive $( k > 0 )$ , users enjoy the daily learning process, leading to an increased valuation of the course compared with the prior one $( \mathrm { i . e . , } \ V + f ( t )$ $k > V )$ . Conversely, when k is negative $( k < 0 )$ , users perceive the course as less valuable than expected, resulting in a decreased valuation of the course compared with the prior one $( \mathrm { i . e . , } V + f ( t ) k < V )$ ). Note that, in our model, all users follow the same valuation updating process $( V + f ( t ) k )$ . Each user who joins the course needs to decide whether to clock in at time t based on the user’s updated valuation of the course, which represents the user’s intrinsic motivation.

As previously explained, the future utilities $E _ { U } ( v _ { t } )$ encompass the value expected to be derived from completing the remaining periods (i.e., from period t + 1 to period L) and the corresponding anticipated costs. Thus, $E _ { U } ( v _ { t } )$ can be written as

3.1.2. Firm’s Problem. From the standpoint of the firm, there are two revenue sources: (i) course fees collected from quitters (termed course fee revenue) and (ii) revenue generated from participating users who share their learning experience with others (termed WOM revenue). The firm also incurs certain revenue losses: (i) costs for content creation, which are positively correlated with the difficulty level of each clocking-in instance, and (ii) negative WOM effects generated by quitters.

Based on this discussion, the firm’s profit function can be written as

$$
\pi = p N _ {q} + \tau_ {w} S _ {w} + \tau_ {q} S _ {q} - \mu d L - \delta N _ {q},\tag{4}
$$

where $N _ { q }$ represents the number of quitters. The first term in (4) calculates the course fee revenue from quit ters. The second and third terms in (4) calculate the revenue generated from the positive WOM effects of winners and quitters, respectively. In practice, users can share their progress updates or experiences via social media after completing each clocking-in instance. Because the users need to have a positive utility when deciding to clock in at a certain time, it is reasonable to consider that the clocking-in users generate positive WOM effects for the program. Here, we use $\tau _ { w }$ and $\tau _ { q } \ ( 0 < \tau _ { q } < \tau _ { w } )$ to denote the marginal impact of positive WOM effects generated by winners and quitters on the firm’s revenue, respectively. $S _ { w }$ and $S _ { q }$ denote the total number of clocking-in instances completed by winners and quitters, respectively. Following prior studies (e.g., Samson 2010), we consider that the WOM effect is positively correlated with users’ engagement in the application (e.g., usage volume or frequency).

The fourth term in (4) represents the firm’s cost incurred for content creation, which can be significant, particularly when dealing with numerous complex assignments. Here, we use µ to denote the marginal content creation cost per unit difficulty level (marginal content creation cost for short). Moreover, in reality, some quitters may develop negative perceptions of the course because of not receiving the refund, potentially imposing an adverse impact (e.g., spreading negative comments about the course) on the firm’s profit. We use the last term in (4) to quantify such negative WOM effects generated by quitters after leaving the program, where δ $\bar { ( \delta } \ge 0 )$ denotes the impact of negative WOM effects generated by quitters. ## 4. Equilibrium Analysis

This section first examines the scenario in which the clocking-in time window is exogenously given and then investigates the scenario with an endogenous time window. In addition, we examine the impact of key contextspecific factors on the optimal time window.

## 4.1. Exogenous Time Window

We begin by computing the total number of joining users in the program by setting $U _ { i } = 0$ in stage 1. Following the literature $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , Mitchell 2021), we consider the exponential discounting parameter θ to be one for the rest of the analysis in this paper, which implies that users value future utilities as important as the current ones.<sup>9</sup> The results are summarized in the following lemma. The proofs for all the lemmas, propositions, and theorems can be found in Online Appendix B.

Lemma 1. For a given time window T, the total number of joining users (N) is given by:

a. $\begin{array} { r } { I f T \le \frac { V ( 1 - c _ { h } \alpha \gamma ) } { b - V - c _ { h } V \alpha } , } \end{array}$ , all users join the course, and $N = 1$

b. $\begin{array} { r } { I f \frac { V ( 1 - c _ { h } \alpha \gamma ) } { b - V - c _ { h } V \alpha } \leq T < \frac { V ( 1 - c _ { l } \alpha \gamma ) } { b - V - c _ { l } V \alpha } , } \end{array}$ , only high-ability users join the course, and $N = \eta$

c. $\begin{array} { r } { I f T > \frac { V ( 1 - c _ { l } \alpha \gamma ) } { b - V - c _ { l } V \alpha } , } \end{array}$ , no one joins the course, and $N = 0$

As shown in Lemma 1, three possible cases may exist regarding the number of joining users. A further comparison across these cases suggests that the total number of joining users (N) may decrease with the time window. This is because, at $t = 0$ , although the extended time window can help reduce the effort cost for users to complete the entire course by lowering the difficulty level of each instance, the negative impact of the extended time window on users’ psychological disutilities dominates, thus resulting in fewer joining users. 4.3.4. When Course Price Is Endogenized. In this subsection, we investigate a more general case in which the firm optimizes the time window (T) and the course price (p) jointly, to determine if the key insights from the main model still hold. The optimization problem resembles the one formulated in Section 3.2 except that, in addition to choosing the time window, the firm needs to jointly decide the course price to maximize its profit. By solving the first order conditions of the firm’s profit with respect to $T$ and $p ,$ we obtain the optimal time window and course price. Notably, our key insights regarding the optimal time window remain valid even when p is endogenized. Specifically, the increasing/decreasing relationships between the optimal time window and various context-specific factors $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , \tau _ { q } , \eta , \mu )$ ) still hold.<sup>15</sup>

In the aforementioned analysis, we consider the value of the exponential discounting parameter (θ) to be one, which is in line with the setup of our main model. In the following, we explore a more general scenario in which $\theta$ can be less than one. Because solving analytically fo the optimal time window and course price becomes intractable, we resort to numerical studies for further analysis. When $T$ and p are jointly determined to maximize profit, we find that most of the key insights concerning the optimal time window (T<sup>∗</sup>) derived in the main model still hold. In particular, as shown in Online Figure A.2, $T ^ { * }$ can still decrease with the marginal impact of positive WOM effects from quitters $( \tau _ { q } )$ . However, unlike Proposition 3, we find that $\rvert _ { T ^ { * } }$ increases with the marginal content creation cost (µ), suggesting that the decreasing relationship indicated in Proposition 3 may not hold when $p$ is endogenized. This finding can be explained as follows. With an extended time window, the firm can benefit from lower content creation costs because of the reduced difficulty level of each clockingin instance. In the meantime, the firm may also suffer a loss in the WOM revenue from quitters because they quit earlier due to the increased psychological disutilities. However, because p is endogenized, as $\mu$ increases, the firm can mitigate the psychological-disutility increasing effect by raising the course price, thereby establishing the dominance of the effort-cost decreasing effect. As a result, the firm benefits from extending the time window as $\mu$ increases.

## 5. Model Extensions

We have so far derived the optimal clocking-in time window and examined its sensitivity to key model parameters. In this section, we extend our analysis by exploring the following aspects: (i) examining the case in which quitters can receive a partial refund, (ii) considering the uniform distribution of users’ marginal clockingin costs, (iii) considering the network effect of users, and (iv) examining an alternative model in which the clocking-in time window equals the course length.

## 5.1. Partial Refund for Quitters

In our main model, we focus on courses in which the firm does not allow partial refunds. However, in practice, firms can employ an alternative strategy by offering a certain portion of the course fees as a refund to users who partially complete the clocking-in process. For example, as shown in Online Figure A.3, Liulishuo, an online learning firm, offers a 180-day English learning course. Users who successfully clock in for 180 consecutive days receive a full refund, whereas those who clock in for at least 150 days receive a half refund. Based on this discussion, in this section, we consider a type of course in which users can receive a partial refund even if they do not fulfill the clocking-in requirement.<sup>16</sup>

From the perspective of the firm, the introduction of a partial refund scheme alters the course fee revenue as it becomes contingent upon the number of clocking-in instances that quitters have not completed;<sup>17</sup> thus, the profit function can be written as follows:

$$
\pi = p N _ {q} \left(1 - \frac {S _ {q}}{N _ {q} T}\right) + \tau_ {w} S _ {w} + \tau_ {q} S _ {q} - \mu d L - \delta N _ {q},
$$

where the first term calculates the course fee revenue generated from quitters after accounting for the refund. The explanations of other terms remain the same as those in Equation (4). Because incorporating the partial refund makes the model intractable, we resort to numerical methods for further analysis.

The analysis shows that most of our key insights derived from the main model remain robust with the inclusion of a partial refund scheme. For example, as shown in Online Figure A.4, the optimal clocking-in time window can still decrease with the marginal impact of positive WOM effects from quitters $\begin{array} { r } { ( \mathrm { i . e . , ~ } \frac { \partial T ^ { * } } { \partial \tau _ { a } } { \stackrel { . } { < } } 0 ) } \end{array}$ However, unlike Proposition 3, we find that the optimal time window always increases with the marginal content creation cost $\begin{array} { r } { \mathrm { ( i . e . , ~ } \frac { \partial T ^ { \ast } } { \partial \mu } \geq 0 \mathrm { ) } } \end{array}$ . This occurs because, when a partial refund is offered, compared with the main model, the firm has less incentive to prolong quitters’ stay in the system because the revenue generated from their course fees, in this case, decreases with the number of completed clocking-in instances. In such a case, the marginal benefit of extending the time window to reduce content creation cost outweighs the loss result ing from higher psychological disutilities as $\mu$ increases. Hence, the firm is better off extending the time window.

## 5.2. Uniform Distribution of User’s Cost

In our main model, we consider two types of users with different marginal costs associated with completing each clocking-in instance: low- and high-cost users. In this extension, we consider the user’s marginal cost of completing each clocking-in instance $c _ { i }$ to be uniformly distributed over [0, 1]. In stage 1, from Equation (1), by setting $U _ { i } = 0 _ { , }$ , we derive the number of joining users as $\begin{array} { r } { N = \frac { - \bar { b } T + V + T V } { V \alpha \left( \gamma - T \right) } } \end{array}$ . Subsequently, we compute the respective numbers of winners and quitters. In stage 2, based on Equation (3), user i decides to clock in at time t if the user’s net benefit of doing so is nonnegative. By setting $U _ { i } ( J _ { i , t } ; v _ { t } , n _ { i , t } ) = 0 .$ , we obtain the quitting time for each user $\overline { { t } } _ { i }$ . Then, we can identify the user who is indifferent between being a winner and a quitter (denoted by c˜) by setting ${ \overline { { t } } } _ { i } = T$ . Regarding quitters, it holds that ${ \overline { { t } } } _ { i } < T ,$ which indicates the quitters are distributed over [c˜, 1]. Thus, we can further derive the number of quitters as $N _ { q } = ( 1 - \tilde { c } ) N$ . In a similar vein, we can derive the number of winners as $N _ { w } = \tilde { c } N$ . The results are summarized in the next lemma.

## 5.3. Network Effect

In the main model, our focus is on a type of course commonly observed in practice, in which little to no competition exists among participating users. However, certain online courses may involve a network effect that significantly influences users’ purchase decisions. In this extension, we consider a course type wherein users take the network effect into consideration when deciding whether to purchase the course. Here, in stage 1, the utility function of user i is formulated as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} U _ {i} = I _ {i} \{E _ {U} + R (0) - G (0) + \sigma N - p \} \\ \qquad = I _ {i} \bigg \{L \bigg (\frac {V}{L} - c _ {i} d \bigg) + p - (b - V) T + \sigma N - p \bigg \}, \end{array}
$$

where $\sigma \geq 0$ is the intensity of the network effect. Recall that, in the main model, users are of discrete types, and we consider that all users purchase the course in stage 1. In such cases, the network effect, which only impacts users’ purchase decisions, does not affect the determination of the optimal time window. Consequently, the results obtained from the model considering the network effect and discrete user types remain the same as those in the main model. To better understand the impact of network effects on the optimal time window, we consider a scenario in which the user’s cost $c _ { i }$ is uniformly distributed over [0, 1].

Following the commonly adopted assumption of rational expectations (e.g., Sheffrin 1996), we calculate the number of joining users as $\begin{array} { r } { N = \frac { b T - ( 1 + T ) V } { V \alpha ( T - \gamma ) + \sigma } } \end{array}$ . Because the uniform distribution of $c _ { i } ( \mathrm { i . e . , } c _ { i } \sim U [ 0 , 1 ] )$ ) makes the model intractable, we resort to numerical methods for further analysis. Our findings indicate that most of our key insights from the main model still hold when the network effect is considered.

Moreover, as shown in Online Figure A.5, as the intensity of the network effect (σ) increases, the firm should set a shorter time window. The underlying reason for this is as follows. When the network effect is considered, the number of joining users plays a more significant role in users’ purchase decisions in stage 1. As σ increases, it can be shown that the number of joining users also increases. Notably, in this model extension, unlike the main model, because $c _ { i }$ is uniformly distributed over [0, 1], the numbers of winners and quitters are directly affected by the time window. Consequently, as σ increases, the decline in the number of winners becomes more pronounced than the increase in the number of quitters as the time window expands. Thus, the potential revenue increase from quitters cannot offset the revenue loss from winners as the time window expands. Thus, there exists a decreasing relationship between the time window and $\sigma .$ In practice, to enhance the network effect, online learning firms can employ tactics such as rewarding users for referring new users to their courses. Additionally, as suggested in some articles (e.g., Reddy 2018), firms can boost the network effect by partnering with companies that offer complementary products. For example, a firm offering Spanish language courses can partner with a company providing introductory courses on Spanish culture. Based on our findings, the firm should consider setting a shorter time window if it attempts to enhance the network effect of the course.

## 5.4. Alternative Model in Which Time Window Equals Course Length

In reality, some courses have clocking-in time windows that equal their course lengths. Therefore, in this section, we consider an alternative setting in which the clockingin time window is the same as the course length $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ $T = L )$ ). In this model, users are required to keep clocking in until the course ends in order to receive a refund. Sim ilar to the previous discussion in Section 3.1, each joining user needs to decide whether to clock in at time t based on the user’s current-period utility $\left( u _ { i , t } \right)$ and future utilities $\left( E _ { U } ( v _ { t } ) \right)$ ). Thus, in stage 2, we have $\begin{array} { r } { \ddot { u _ { i , t } } = v _ { t } - c _ { i } \frac { \alpha V ( \gamma - T ) } { T } . } \end{array}$ $\begin{array} { r } { E _ { U } = v _ { t } ( T - t ) - c _ { i } \frac { \alpha V ( \gamma - T ) } { T } ( T - t ) } \end{array}$ . The user’s cumulative utility of clocking in at period t can be formulated similarly to Equation (3). The firm’s profit can then be written as $\pi = p N _ { q } + \tau _ { w } S _ { w } + \tau _ { q } S _ { q } - \mu d T - \delta N _ { q } ,$ where $S _ { w }$ equals $T N _ { w }$ in this extension.<sup>18</sup>

Because solving the first order condition of the profit maximization problem with respect to $T$ is not analytically tractable, we resort to numerical methods to study how the optimal time window is affected by various context-specific factors. Our findings indicate that most of our key insights from the main model still hold when the time window equals the course length. However, as shown in Online Figure $_ { \mathrm { A } . 6 , }$ for a $\mathrm { g i v e n } \tau _ { q } ,$ unlike Proposition $^ { 3 , }$ the optimal time window always increases with the marginal content creation cost $\begin{array} { r } { ( \mathrm { i . e . , } \frac { { \partial T ^ { * } } } { \partial \mu } \geq 0 ) } \end{array}$ . The underlying reason is that, when the time window is equal to the course length, extending it not only reduces users effort cost for completing each clocking-in instance and the firm’s content creation cost, but also has an additional positive impact on the total number of clocking-in instances. This is mainly because the total number of instances in which winners have clocked in $( S _ { w } )$ is positively correlated with T. In such a case, the effort-cost decreasing effect and the aforementioned additional positive effect, when combined, dominate the psychological-disutility increasing effect. Consequently, the firm benefits from extending the time window as $\mu$ increases.

## 6. Discussion and Concluding Remarks

The CIC model, an emerging gamified business model in online learning, has recently garnered widespread public attention (iResearch 2019). However, it remains unclear how online learning firms should optimally design the gamification system to maximize profits. To address this issue, we build a dynamic programming model to examine how the firm should strategically determine the clocking-in time window within the system. Our analysis incorporates various revenue streams of the online learning firm and considers several key factors that influence user engagement in the learning program. We delve into the impact of context-specific factors, such as the marginal positive WOM effects from quitters on the optimal time window and consumer surplus. Furthermore, to better understand the critical role played by the time window in the CIC mechanism, we investigate its impact on the total number of clocking-in instances and revenue generated from users.

## 6.1. Main Findings and Managerial Implications

Our study yields several noteworthy findings. First, we identify two opposing effects imposed by the time window on users’ quitting time: the psychological-disutility increasing effect and the effort-cost decreasing effect. We find that a longer time window can result in either more or fewer clocking-in instances. Second, given the significance of quitters in our context, we examine how the marginal positive WOM effects of quitters impact the optimal time window. Our analysis reveals that the optimal time window can sometimes decrease with quitters positive WOM effects. Moreover, we find that the optimal time window decreases when the proportion of high-ability users increases under certain conditions. Another interesting finding is that the optimal time window can decrease with the marginal content creation cost although, intuitively, the firm should do the opposite. Additionally, we look deep into how context-specific factors impact consumer surplus. Furthermore, we explore several model extensions, including cases involving partial refunds and network effects and find that most of our key insights regarding the clocking-in time window still hold.

Our paper contributes to the literature in several aspects. First, we provide a comprehensive examination of the optimal design of the CIC mechanism, an emerging gamified business model in online learning that has received limited attention in the extant literature. We contribute to the literature on price discrimination by studying a novel form of discrimination, specifically investigating how the firm leverages users’ ability to complete each clocking-in instance as a discrimination tool for user segmentation. Second, we advance the literature on gamification by analytically modeling the gamified learning process and identifying the optimal design of the technology-enabled gamification system. Third, we contribute to the literature on paid content by studying users’ purchase behavior in the online learning context, an area that is largely overlooked in the existing literature. Furthermore, this paper represents a pioneering endeavor to study the WOM effects in online learning. Specifically, we examine how quitters’ positive WOM effects influence the firm’s selection of the optimal time window.

Our results provide valuable managerial insights that can assist online learning firm managers in improving their decisions regarding the design of the CIC mechanism. For example, courses that cover high-usage skills or frequently discussed topics tend to exhibit more sig nificant WOM effects compared with courses focusing on low-usage skills or less frequently discussed topics. This is because the former courses typically generate a higher volume of words, reviews, or opinions (Berger and Schwartz 2011). In reality, when firms employ discussion forums to enhance WOM effects, strategic adjustment of the time window for courses with varying levels of WOM effects becomes essential. Moreover, because some online courses target inexperienced users $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ ., English learning courses for beginners), whereas others cater to experienced users (e.g., English learning courses for professionals), the proportion of high-ability users may vary across courses. Based on our findings, for courses targeting professional, as the proportion of high-ability users increases, the firm should consider implementing a relatively long clocking-in time window. Our findings also shed light on how the firm should adjust the time window when the content creation cost changes. For example, firms offering courses such as training courses for advanced skills typically face relatively high content creation costs. When these courses include more visually stunning and engaging elements (e.g., high-quality videos and customized photos), extending the time window may be necessary for the firm.

## 6.2. Limitation and Future Research

Our work is not without limitations. For example, in this paper, we consider that users must complete the required number of consecutive clocking-in instances to receive a refund, which indicates that, once a user chooses not to clock in at a particular time, the user will not return to the program. The same assumption applies to the winners as well; that is, we consider that they will continue to clock in consecutively (as long as their utilities are nonnegative) even after receiving a course fee refund. Future studies can explore an interesting alternative scenario in which a user can skip some clocking-in instances and later on return to resume the clocking-in process. As one can imagine, the user under this new setting has a higher probability of becoming a winner compared with the setting in the present study. Moreover, if we consider a more general scenario in which winners are allowed to skip some clocking-in instances after fulfilling the clocking-in requirement, it is conceivable that the revenue generated from winners’ WOM effects may be lower than in the present study. This is because the total number of clocking-in instances generated by winners, if they are allowed to skip some instances, would be smaller.

In addition, this study assumes that users’ beliefs about whether they will complete the required number of clocking-in instances are deterministic. Future works can incorporate uncertainties into users’ decision making by considering factors such as users’ perceived probability of completing the required clocking-in instances. Another limitation of the study is that we cannot determine whether the thresholds identified in the propositions are higher or lower than the parameter values typically observed in practice because of the absence of real-world data. To address this limitation, future research can conduct experiments or collect real-world data to validate the key findings of the present study. Additionally, with real-world data, future studies can examine the impact of implementing the CIC model on a firm’s success.
