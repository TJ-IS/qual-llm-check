# Mobile Payment Adoption: An Empirical Investigation of Alipay

Abstract. The rapid growth in the adoption of mobile payments has already begun to reshape bank payment practices. Utilizing a unique data set from a leading bank in Asia that records credit card transactions of its customers before and after the launch of Alipay mobile payment, the largest mobile payment platform in the world, this study aims to understand the impact of mobile payment adoption on bank customer credit card activities and the change of this impact after the mobile payment expansion. To do so, we employ the difference-in-differences method coupled with matching to estimate the effects. We find that mobile payment adoption not only increases customer credit card activities at the focal bank through both off-line and online channels, but also enhances customer loyalty to the bank by reducing churn. Specifically, the total credit card transaction amount and frequency of our focal bank increased by 9.4% and 10.7%, respectively. Moreover, we examine the change in the treatment effect after the mobile payment expansion and find an increase in adopters’ credit card activities and a reduction in their churn after the expansion. Next, we discuss the underlying mechanisms and show that mobile payment acts as a substitute for physical card payment in the off-line channel, and this supports the key underlying mechanism of the reduced transaction cost. However, a certain complementary effect exists between personal computer and mobile payments, likely driven by the coadoption of the two. Finally, we provide empirical evidence on conditions that facilitate the use of mobile payments, following the unified theory of acceptance and use of technology.

## 1. Introduction

With the widespread usage of smartphones, mobile payments are becoming increasingly popular among customers worldwide and are gradually reshaping the landscape of noncash payments. According to a recent report in Fortune,<sup>1</sup> the global market size of mobile payments has reached US\$1.97 trillion in 2021 and is projected to reach US\$11.83 trillion by 2028. Additionally, a recent report by eMarketer<sup>2</sup> indicates that mobile payment users in the United States are expected to surpass 50% of all smartphone users by 2025 with a significant portion using Apple Pay and Google Pay. In countries such as India and China, mobile payments have already become prominent alternatives to traditional bank cards. The use of mobile payments enables a seamless shopping experience by enabling customers to continue shopping without carrying additional physical cards. Consequently, customers experience greater convenience, such as ease of use and reduced transaction time (Schierz et al. 2010, Leong et al. 2013, Oliveira et al. 2016). For example, the average duration of a transaction made by mobile payment is approximately 29.45 seconds compared with 40.26 seconds for traditional physical card transactions with a signature (Polasik et al. 2012). Therefore, it is evident to see the increasingly essential role of mobile payments in the global payment system. To cope with the growing popularity of mobile payments, major banks worldwide are collaborating with technology companies to introduce mobile payments as a new payment method for their customers. Bank of America and Chase, among others, have partnered with Apple Pay to promote mobile payment services to their customers (Nykiel 2014). Despite the practical significance of mobile payment adoption, thus far, academic studies on its effects or outcomes remain limited.

Although previous research examines the effects of different mobile app adoptions on purchase-related measures (see, for example, Bellman et al. 2011, Einav et al. 2014, Kim et al. 2015, Narang and Shankar 2019, Son et al. 2020), to the best of our knowledge, none of these studies specifically focuses on mobile payment apps, which have disparate functionalities. First, most mobile apps considered in prior studies are used exclusively through the mobile channel. In contrast, mobile payment apps can be utilized through both off-line and online channels for conducting transactions. Second, the primary function of other mobile apps, such as e-commerce apps, is to provide alternative channels or environments for customer activities and transactions, which requires additional payment methods to enable transactions within the app. On the other hand, mobile payment apps are primarily designed for payments, parallel to off-line physical card payments and online PC payments. Third, there is a distinct mechanism in our context that arises from the interplay between mobile payments and traditional payment methods, which is absent in the adoption of other mobile apps. For example, if mobile payment simply replaces existing payment methods such as off-line physical card payments or online PC payments, its adoption may not significantly impact transaction activities through off-line and/or online channels. Therefore, it remains an empirical puzzle to determine the extent to which this new technology affects customer bank card activities. Therefore, it is interesting to explore how the underlying interrelationship of mobile payment adoption influences customer transactions through different channels. This aspect cannot be directly inferred from prior studies on mobile app adoption because of the unique nature of mobile payment apps. Additionally, from a bank’s perspective, it is crucial to understand the change in the treatment effect of mobile payment adoption after its expansion with other competing banks. This aspect sets our study apart from previous research on mobile app adoption.

To summarize, our paper aims to address these research gaps by investigating the following questions:

Q1a. (Overall activities) What is the impact of mobile payment adoption on bank customer credit card activities and their loyalty to the bank?

Q1b. (Off-line versus online) Do the effects of mobile payment adoption differ between off-line and online transaction channels?

Q2. (Mobile payment expansion) How do the treatment effects of mobile payment adoption change after its expansion with other competing banks?

Q3. (Mechanism) What are the underlying mechanisms that drive the observed effects of mobile payment adoption?

Q4. (Heterogeneous effects) What are the factors and conditions that influence the use of mobile payments?

The answers to these questions offer important managerial implications and actionable insights for banks and mobile payment platforms to enhance their business performance. To address our research questions, we obtain access to a unique data set from a leading bank in Asia that records customer credit card transactions associated with different payment methods (i.e., mobile, physical card, and PC payments) through online and off-line channels. The mobile payment under study here was launched by Alipay, a third-party payment platform founded by Alibaba. Our unique data set covers two critical dates of Alipay’s policy changes. First, the mobile payment service was launched in collaboration with our focal bank on December 23, 2010, marking the first bank collaboration for Alipay. This service enables customers to link their credit cards with their mobile phones through the Alipay mobile app, allowing them to make direct payments using their mobile devices without the need for physical cards. Our data set spans from September 2010 to February 2013, capturing the period before and after the launch of mobile payments. Second, starting on May 26, 2011, Alipay began expanding and forming collaborations with other banks worldwide. Our data set covers the period before and after this expansion, en abling us to examine the changes in treatment effects following the mobile payment expansion. To establish a causal relationship, we carefully address concerns regarding the potential endogeneity of mobile payment adoption arising from temporally changing individual characteristics. To do this, we leverage the granularity of our transaction data by employing both the difference-indifferences (DID) method coupled with dynamic matching and the double machine learning (DML) approach (see Chernozhukov et al. 2018, Dube et al. 2020). Our empirical findings contribute to the literature by providing the following managerial implications.

First, our findings show that mobile payment adoption has a significant impact on credit card activities for bank customers both online and off-line. This results in increased transaction amounts and frequencies for our focal bank, leading to additional revenues for banks, retailers, and technology firms. Specifically, we observe a 9.4% increase in total credit card transaction amount and a 10.7% increase in transaction frequency for our focal bank. For off-line transactions at our focal bank, there is an approximately 6.32% increase in amount and 7.66% increase in frequency, whereas for online transactions, the increases are approximately 10.0% in amount and 12.9% in frequency. Furthermore, mobile payment adoption reduces customer churn and enhances customer loyalty to the bank. An immediate takeaway for bank managers is that this new technology offers an improved shopping experience, ultimately driving increased bank revenue through enhanced transaction activities both online and off-line.

Second, we examine the effects of mobile payment expansion by leveraging the event when Alipay began expanding its collaboration with other competing banks. We find an increase in adopters’ credit card transaction amount and a reduction in their churn after the expansion. This finding indicates that the positive effect of mobile payment adoption will not diminish even in the face of increased competition from other banks. Interestingly, the entry of other banks into the mobile payment market actually facilitates more off-line credit card activities for our focal bank. Furthermore, our analysis reveals that the positive effect resulting from the expansion is mainly driven by increased off-line credit card activities, whereas online activities have decreased.

Next, we discuss the underlying mechanisms by conducting granular analyses with respect to the three payment methods. We find that mobile payments act as a substitute for physical card payments in the off-line channel, thereby supporting the key underlying mechanism of reduced transaction costs. However, in the online channel, a certain complementary effect exists between PC and mobile payments, which is likely driven by the coadoption of the two. This complementary effect may help explain the higher positive impact of mobile payment adoption on online credit card activities. Moreover, additional analyses on mobile payment expansion suggest that as mobile payment becomes compatible with more banks, it may lead to a further reduction in transaction costs with better facilitating conditions for using mobile payments, particularly off-line; hence, the entry of other banks will have a positive externality on the focal bank.

Finally, following the unified theory of acceptance and use of technology (UTAUT), we provide empirical evidence on conditions that facilitate the use of mobile payments. Our findings suggest that bank managers should focus on promoting mobile payments among the older generation and in lower tier cities. Additionally, improving supply-side conditions is essential to enhance the adoption and usage of mobile payments.

## 2. Related Literature

In this section, we review four streams of literature related to our work: (i) mobile payment, (ii) mobile app adoption, (iii) mobile device adoption, and (iv) interaction between online and off-line channels.

Previous studies on mobile payment mainly focus on investigating factors associated with customer adoption decisions (see Schierz et al. 2010, Oliveira et al. 2016 for an overview). For example, Leong et al. (2013) examine the impact of factors such as perceived financial cost, usefulness, and ease of use on the adoption decision of mobile payment users via survey analysis; moreover, Slade et al. (2015) also collect survey data to further explore trust and perceived risk associated with mobile payment adoption decisions. However, to the best of our knowledge, there is limited research on the impact of mobile payment adoption on customer bank card consumption. Therefore, our study contributes to this stream of literature from three perspectives. First, we identify the impact of mobile payment adoption on customer credit card activities of our focal bank through both off-line and online channels, which is new to the literature on mobile payments. Furthermore, we provide results regarding the change in the treatment effect after the mobile payment expansion. Third, we examine the nuanced relationships between mobile payment and traditional bank payment methods (i.e., physical card and PC payments).

The second stream of literature closely related to our work is mobile app adoption as the mobile payment platform can be viewed as a particular type of mobile app. The existing literature on mobile app adoption examines the impact of app adoption on various purchase-related measures (see Bellman et al. 2011, Einav et al. 2014, Xu et al. 2014, Kim et al. 2015, Liu et al. 2016, Jung et al. 2019, Narang and Shankar 2019, Son et al. 2020) and the drivers of mobile app adoption (Ghose and Han 2014, Han et al. 2016, Sun et al. 2019, Zheng et al. 2019). However, none of these existing papers investigates mobile payment apps that have distinct functionalities. As mentioned earlier, a mobile payment app can be used through both off-line and online channels to conduct transactions, whereas most other mobile apps considered in the literature can only be used through the mobile channel. Moreover, mobile payment apps work in a manner that is similar to an off-line physical card payment or an online PC payment; its underlying payment function differs fundamentally from other mobile apps examined in the literature. Consequently, the mechanism driven by the interrelationships between mobile payments and other traditional payment methods within different channels is unique in our setting. Because of the different functionalities and underlying mechanisms, it is difficult to make direct inferences from prior mobile app studies to a mobile payment setting. Therefore, our study contributes to this stream of literature by providing the first empirical evidence on the effects of mobile payment adoption on customer credit card activities at the focal bank through different channels and different payment methods.

Next, our work is also related to the literature on mobile device adoption. Existing studies on the impact of mobile device adoption generally reveal that mobile device adoption positively affects aggregated purchasing behavior (e.g., Lee et al. 2016). Furthermore, prior research explores the interdependencies of mobile transaction channels with other channels (e.g., Brynjolfsson et al. 2009, Ghose et al. 2012, Bang et al. 2013, Jung et al. 2014, Napoli and Obar 2014, Ghose 2018). Compared with the prior literature on mobile device adoption, the mobile payment studied in this paper is fundamentally different in the sense that it is a payment intermediary (similar to the physical card or PC payment) that can be used either off-line or online. Therefore, mobile payment is not directly comparable to off-line or online channels. In contrast, the mobile device studied in the extant literature is considered a mobile channel comparable to the off-line and online channels. Consequently, although prior literature on mobile device adoption investigates the complementary and substitution effects between channels, our study complements this stream of literature by investigating the interrelationships among payment methods (i.e., mobile, physical card, and PC payments) instead of among transaction channels, which have not yet been studied in the literature.

Finally, our work is related to the vast, growing information systems literature on the interaction of online and off-line channels. Existing papers in this stream study this interaction from different aspects, including the impact of off-line stores on online consumption (Wang and Goldfarb 2017, Kumar et al. 2019, Sun et al. 2024), the “showrooming” effect (Tang et al. 2016, Mehra et al. 2018, Soysal et al. 2019, Samuel et al. 2020), channel competition (Brynjolfsson et al. 2009), channel differences (Overby and Jap 2009, Avery et al. 2012, Gao and Su 2017), and the online–off-line customer cycle (Han et al. 2023). Although our work does not particularly examine the interaction between online and off-line channels, we study the mobile payment that can be used through both channels. Therefore, our work contributes to this stream of literature by investigating the impact of mobile payment adoption on credit card activities at the focal bank through both online and off-line channels. Moreover, we study the unique mechanism in our setting driven by the interrelationship between mobile payments and other traditional payment methods in these two channels.

## 3. Hypothesis Development

In this section, we examine the key hypotheses that explain the impact of mobile payment adoption on customer behavior. According to the transaction cost theory (Coase 1937, Williamson 1989), customers’ decision to purchase a product depends on the utility they derive from using the product minus the cost of acquiring it. Specifically, a purchase occurs when the utility earned is greater than the sum of the product price and the transaction cost. Therefore, the purchase decision is affected by the transaction cost, which can be impacted by payment methods. We posit that the adoption of mobile payment will likely reduce transaction costs by enabling a seamless shopping experience.

The Alipay mobile payment under study here uses quick response (QR) code technology<sup>3</sup> to handle payments for bills, goods, and services. Customers initiate the payment process by scanning the QR code displayed by the merchant using their mobile phones. The QR code conveys the transaction information to the application on the mobile phone, and hence, customers do not need to present a physical card to the merchant off-line or type in the card information to the merchant online. As a result, mobile payment brings great convenience to customers by enabling a seamless shopping experience without the need for a physical wallet (Pham and Ho 2015) and also provides the advantage of speed (Teo et al. 2015). In fact, the average duration of a transaction made using mobile payment is approximately 29.45 seconds, whereas it is 40.26 seconds for a traditional physical card with signature (Polasik et al. 2012). Therefore, the payment process using a QR code technology is perceived as easy to use by customers (see Yan et al. 2021), leading to a lower customer utility threshold required for making a purchase. Consequently, we expect to see an increase in credit card activities in the initial stage of mobile payment adoption as there is an increase in purchase probability because of the convenience of mobile payment.

Nevertheless, it is possible that the advantages of mobile payment outlined could result in the substitution between mobile payment and other existing payment methods both off-line and online, such as physical card and PC payments. If mobile payment only serves as an alternative payment technology that diverts from existing bank payments, the increase in credit card activities may not be significant. Therefore, we propose the following hypotheses.

Hypothesis 1A. Mobile payment adoption increases overall credit card transactions at the focal bank and its online and off-line transaction activities.

Hypothesis 1B. Mobile payment adoption does not change overall credit card transactions at the focal bank and its online and off-line transaction activities.

Next, we discuss the change in the treatment effect after the mobile payment expansion. On the one hand, as mobile payment becomes compatible with more banks, it may further reduce transaction costs, leading to a positive externality on the focal bank. Thus, we may anticipate seeing a further increase in credit card activities of our focal bank’s customers after the mobile payment expansion. On the other hand, we may also expect a decrease in credit card activities of our focal bank because of the increased competition after the mobile payment expansion. For instance, as the switching cost between banks decreases (i.e., competing banks also offer the same mobile payment service), customers might shift some of their credit card transactions to other banks o entirely move away from our focal bank. Therefore, we propose the following competing hypotheses for the effects after the mobile payment expansion.

Hypothesis 2A. Mobile payment expansion increases adopters’ overall credit card transactions at the focal bank and their online and off-line credit card activities.

Hypothesis 2B. Mobile payment expansion decreases adopters’ overall credit card transactions at the focal bank and their online and off-line credit card activities.

In Section 7, we conduct additional empirical analyses to provide further support for the proposed mechanism of reduced transaction cost.

## 4. Data

This section discusses the details of our data set and the key variables used in our estimation.

## 4.1. Data Description

Our credit card data set comprises three parts. The first part records detailed information on credit cards issued by our focal bank to its 1,541,769 customers in Jiangsu Province, including the card issuance date, card type, annual fee, credit limit, etc. The second part of our data set includes customer demographic information, such as age, gender, job position (manager or not), education (graduate, bachelor’s, high school, middle high school, and elementary school degrees), marital status (married or not), and living status (rent or own). The third part documents the transaction-level information from September 2010 to February 2013 (a total of 29 months): transaction amount, account balances, transaction date, interest rate, promotion programs, etc. This part of the data is crucial to measure customer transaction activities. More importantly, each transaction observation is associated with two labels: one indicating the payment method (i.e., mobile, physical card, and PC payments) and the other indicating the transaction channel (i.e., online and off-line channels).

Next, we discuss how we process our data set. As mentioned earlier, our credit card data set includes 1,541,769 customers in Jiangsu province. Our focal bank, particularly its credit card division, is the first bank in Asia that collaborated with Alipay to launch the mobile payment service on December 23, 2010. Alipay began growing its collaborations with other banks worldwide in May 2011, when it officially obtained the “payment license” on May 26, 2011. Therefore, we conduct our main analyses of the impact of mobile payment adoption based on the observation period between September 2010 and April 2011 to exclude the impact of Alipay users of other bank cards or our focal bank’s debit cards. This step leaves us with 1,014,318 customers involved in these eight observational months. Another reason to narrow the postadoption study period is the potential marketing campaign and promotion effects of the newly launched mobile payment. Note that, during the first four months after the launch of mobile payment, Alipay or our focal bank did not engage in any targeted campaign for its mobile payment users except for launching a new cobranded credit card, which enables cardholders to become VIP members of Taobao (the online platform of Alibaba and the previous owner of Alipay). Customers who use this cobranded credit card can enjoy discounts from merchants on Taobao.com. To exclude this targeted promotion effect, we exclude users of this new credit card, which leaves us with a total of 976,543 customers. Moreover, during our study period, Alipay was the only leading mobile payment service provider, and mobile payment transactions made through Alipay account for more than 90% of all observations. Our analysis eliminates individual mobile payment transactions made through other mobile payment apps.

Next, we exclude customers with missing or false information in their demographics, and this step leaves us with 897,708 customers. Finally, to investigate the differences in behavioral changes between treated and control customers after the launch of mobile payment and provide a complete measurement of customer behavior, following Wang et al. (2019) and Zhang et al. (2019), we exclude customers who joined after the launch of mobile payment in or after January 2011. This step leaves us with 705,208 customers. Next, we exclude customers with missing values in their important credit card information, such as credit limit and interest rate, which leaves us with 609,750 customers. Among these remaining customers, 25,710 adopted mobile payment between January 2011 and April 2011 and are used as our treatment group. The remaining 583,628 customers did not adopt mobile payment before April 2011; however, 44,464 of them adopted mobile payment in or after May 2011 and are considered our control group. Consequently, in our main analysis, we have four months of a preadoption period (i.e., September–December 2010) and four months of a postadoption period (i.e., January–April 2011), and 70,174 (i.e., 25,710 + 44,464) customers before matching by the end of April 2011. In our main analyses, we aggregate our data into a customer-month-level panel, thereby resulting in 561,392 (i.e., 70,174 × 8) observations before matching. The unit of analysis (e.g., transaction amount) is at the customer level by aggregating transactions conducted through different credit cards of one customer. One potential concern here is that, because of the short postadoption period and the limited number of adopters by that time, our results might be subject to the tempora excitement effect of the launch of new technology instead of the actual adoption effect. To eliminate this alternative explanation, we subsequently conduct falsification tests in Section 6.3.9 to further validate our main results

Finally, we note that a transaction in our data set is referred to as “Alipay mobile payment” only if the customer makes payments (either online or off-line) with a mobile phone to scan the QR code. Alipay also owns a PC payment platform launched earlier than its mobile payment app. Customers can make purchases online through this PC payment platform; however, this PC platform does not allow off-line transactions. Note that the analyses conducted in this paper only focus on the Alipay mobile payment app. In the matching process, we control this PC payment adoption status to balance our treatment and control group customers.

Appendix presents the summary statistics of key variables after matching. In the following account, we discuss the details of these key variables and begin with the dependent variables.

4.2.1. Dependent Variables. The outcome variables used for our main regression analyses include credit card transaction amount, frequency, shares, and customer churn. Specifically, to understand credit card activities, we compute the total monthly credit card transaction amount and frequency from all credit cards of our focal bank held by a customer. In addition, to study activities through online and off-line channels separately, we compute credit card transaction amount, frequency, and shares for each channel. Precisely, the share of a particular channel is measured as the credit card transaction amount (or frequency) in that channel as a percentage of the total credit card transaction amount (or frequency) for both channels combined. Moreover, to quantify the substitution and complementary effects between payment methods, we compute credit card transaction amount, frequency, and shares for each payment method. Similarly, the share of a particular payment method is measured as the credit card transaction amount (or frequency) through that payment method as a percentage of the total credit card transaction amount (or frequency) for all three payment methods combined. We remark that, if a customer holds multiple credit cards (e.g., credit cards A, B, and C) and links card A with Alipay, then the total credit card transaction would be computed by aggregating transactions from all credit cards A, B, and C, but mobile payment transaction only comes from A. Finally, we also introduce the customer churn measure. Ideally, we want to identify customer churn by a particular labeled event, such as account closure. However, we do not have such information in our data set, and it is shown in the literature that customers may leave a company by simply ceasing to make purchases instead of completely closing their accounts (Retana et al. 2016). Therefore, we use the idle period of a customer to measure customer churn. Prior studies reveal that a longer idle period may indicate a lower probability of future purchases and a higher probability of customer churn (Lewis 2004, Gu et al. 2022). In our data, the idle period for 97% of the observations falls within 180 days. Therefore, following Gu et al. (2022), we define customer churn as having an idle period longer than or equal to 180 days.

Next, we discuss the independent variables used in the matching process and DID regressions.

4.2.2. Customer Demographics. To control for customer demographics, we include age, gender (male or female), education level, marital status (married or not), living status (own or rent), and job position (manager or not). Note that these time-invariant demographic variables are only used in the propensity score matching, whereas the main analyses do not include these variables because we control for individual fixed effects. In addition, we introduce one binary variable on whether the focal customer is an Alipay PC payment adopter before the treatment in the matching process. In particular, we find that 73.94% of Alipay mobile payment adopters have used Alipay PC payment before the launch of mobile payment; hence, it is reasonable to include this variable in the matching process.

4.2.3. Card Status. We exploit detailed credit card information in our data set and control for the card type, credit limit, annual fee, interest amount, and credit card balance. In particular, the card type identifies whether this credit card is a main or attached, thereby reflecting a customer’s financial status. If a customer does not have proper financial income, the customer can only apply for a credit card as an attached card to the main card from another cardholder, and often students or older people hold an attached card. Note that, even if a customer has multiple credit cards from our focal bank, all these cards belong to either the main or attached type. The credit limit is the maximum amount of credit offered, which reflects the consumption ability of the cardholder to a certain extent. An annual fee is a fee that our focal bank automatically charges once a year to the card account for the benefits associated with the credit card. Note that the variation of credit limit and annual fee across time is rather limited during our eight-month observation period, and the card type is fixed over time. Therefore, these three time-invariant variables (i.e., card type, credit limit, and annual fee) are only used in the propensity score matching.

Next, we describe two card status variables used in both matching and DID analysis. The interest amount is the amount of interest charged in a focal month when an outstanding balance of a credit card is not fully paid off. The credit card balance is the total amount of money owed to our focal bank in a month. In our data set, a customer can hold multiple cards, and hence, we consider the values of credit limit, annual fee, interest amount, and credit card balance as the total amount from all credit cards of a focal customer. The purpose of introducing these variables is to control the customer consumption ability, which could affect adoption and consumption behaviors. Note that our data set also includes customer income; however, we decided not to use this variable because the income information is self-reported in our focal bank and many customers choose not to report it (i.e., many missing values).

4.2.4. Consumption Behavior. We control customer past consumption behaviors in both the propensity score matching and the DID regressions. Specifically, we introduce behavioral measures on preperiod monthly average credit card transaction amount and frequency in the propensity score matching. Note that we also conduct matching without controlling for these behavioral measures in our robustness check. In the main regression analysis, we use measures on the average credit card transaction amount and frequency in the past five months before the focal month to capture the dynamic behavioral changes (Xu et al. 2016).

4.2.5. Other Control Variables. In addition to the variables mentioned, we control for three other variables in our main analyses. The first is the local penetration variable, measured as the number of existing mobile payment adopters within the same zip code area in the previous month. This variable controls the potential impact of local word-of-mouth—that is, individuals may be more likely to use mobile payment if more people around them have adopted it. The second is the promotion variable, measured as the number of promotions involved in the focal month, which varies at the customer-month level. To avoid the concern that the observed results might be an outcome of marketing promotions, we control the number of promotions involved. The third is the supply-side adoption, measured as the number of unique merchants (suppliers) accepting Alipay mobile payment in our data set because the use of mobile payment is highly dependent on whether the businesses or sellers accept this payment method (i.e., facilitating conditions). This measure varies at the citymonth level. Note that we cannot observe the supplyside adoptions of the entire industry, so the variable here is a conservative measure that approximates the trend of supply-side adoptions.

## 5. Econometric Model

## 5.1. Difference-in-Differences

Recall that our focal bank collaborated with Alipay to launch its mobile payment service at the end of December 2010, and our data set records credit card transactions before and after this intervention. Therefore, we are able to identify the specific date when each adopter first utilized mobile payment for their purchases. However, it is important to note that the decision of individual adoption is endogenous, meaning that customers self-select into adopting mobile payment. To address this endogeneity concern, we employ the launch of mobile payment as the treatment variable in our main analysis. We delve into the rationale behind this choice in Section 5.2.

We now discuss the details of our DID estimation, which is an econometric method commonly used to measure the treatment effect within a given period. The DID approach involves comparing the difference in outcomes between a treatment group, which received the intervention, and a control group, which did not (Meyer 1995). In our study, the treatment group consists of customers who adopted mobile payment between January and

April 2011. On the other hand, the control group comprises customers who did not adopt mobile payment during the first four months after its launch but eventually adopted it in May 2011 or later. In our baseline model, we analyze the changes in outcomes for both adopters and nonadopters before and after the launch of mobile payment. We write our estimation equation for customer i in month t in the following manner:<sup>4</sup>

$$
\begin{array}{c} \text {Outcome} _ {i t} = \delta \times (\text {TreatGroup} _ {i} \times \text {AfterTreat} _ {t}) \\ + \mathbf {X} _ {i t} + \alpha_ {i} + \tau_ {t} + \epsilon_ {i t}, \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

where Outcome represents the credit card transaction amount, frequency, or shares for the overall activities or activities through different channels or payment methods. To facilitate interpretation, we log-transform the transaction amount and frequency outcomes to obtain elasticity measures. The coefficient δ represents the average incremental effect of mobile payment adoption on the percentage share change for share-related outcomes. The variable TreatGroup<sub>i</sub> is one if customer i belongs to the treatment group and zero otherwise. The variable $A f t e r T r e a t _ { t }$ is a dummy variable that indicates the posttreatment period with one indicating the period after the launch of mobile payment and zero otherwise. The coefficient δ serves as the DID estimator of the treatment effect. Vector $\mathbf { \boldsymbol { X } } _ { i t }$ includes the observed time-varying characteristics, such as interest amount, credit card balance, average credit card transaction amount and frequency over the past five months, local penetration, and the number of promotions involved. Additionally, the individual fixed effect $\alpha _ { i }$ captures unobserved time-invariant characteristics specific to each customer, such as innate abilities or habits. Finally, $\tau _ { t }$ is the monthly dummy that controls for the time trend, and $\epsilon _ { i t }$ is the error term that is clustered by customers to account for potential correlation over time (Moulton 1990).

The key challenge in identifying the adoption effects characterized in Equation (1) is the endogenous adoption decision. Mobile payment users decide to adopt the technology based on their individual characteristics, both observed and unobserved, which can create endogeneity. In the following sections, we employ empirical strategies to address this endogeneity issue.

## 5.2. Endogeneity and Self-Selection

In our analysis, we consider two potential interventions: the launch of mobile payment by our collaborator bank and the individual adoption of mobile payment. The launch of mobile payment represents a policy shock and is exogenous to bank customers. On the other hand, the individual adoption decision is endogenous as it is driven by customers’ self-selection. To mitigate potential self-selection bias, we choose to use the launch of mobile payment as the intervention in our main analysis. This conservative approach assumes that the impact of mobile payment starts accumulating right after its launch, independent of the self-selected adoption dates (Manchanda et al. 2015). We employ this approach to identify our main results. However, as a robustness check, we also estimate our model treating individual adoption as the intervention in Section 6.3.2, acknowledging the potential endogeneity of the adoption decision.

Whereas using the launch of mobile payment as the intervention has its advantages, one concern is whether the effects observed are because of the launch itself or the actual adoption of mobile payment. To address this concern, we adopt a DID approach that attributes the treatment effect to the act of adopting mobile payment. In our DID setting, we compare the outcomes between adopters and nonadopters rather than simply comparing the changes before and after adoption, similar to the approach used by Manchanda et al. (2015) and Narang and Shankar (2019) in similar settings.

However, even with this approach, self-selection bias can still arise. To address this concern, we aim to ensure comparability between adopters and nonadopters by balancing both observable and unobservable characteristics. We achieve balance in observable characteristics using PSM to match individuals based on observed covariates. Additionally, we consider an alternative control group consisting of future mobile payment adopters to balance unobservable characteristics. Furthermore, we employ the double machine learning method to further validate our main results. In the following sections, we discuss our approaches for balancing observables and unobservables, respectively.

5.2.1. Selection of Observables: Matching. The PSM strategy (Heckman et al. 1998, Aral et al. 2009) derives a sample of treated customers (adopters) with similar observed characteristics to a sample of control customers (nonadopters), thereby balancing the observables between adopters and nonadopters (Heckman et al. 1998, Aral et al. 2009).

To implement PSM, we begin by computing each customer’s propensity score, defined as the probability of adopting the mobile payment. The propensity score is estimated using a logistic regression based on factors that can affect the adoption decision. These factors include customer demographics (e.g., gender, age, education, marital status, living status, and job position), credit card information (e.g., card type, interest amount, credit limit, credit card balance, and annual fee), behavioral measures (e.g., preperiod monthly average credit card transaction amount and frequency), and the adoption status of Alipay PC payment prior to the treatment date. The logistic regression results can be found in Online Table 10. Based on estimated propensity scores, we construct the contro group by identifying nonadopters similar to adopters.

In this study, we use both dynamic and static matching approaches. Both approaches match adopters with nonadopters who resemble them most closely based on propensity scores. The difference between the two approaches is the following: in a dynamic matching, unique propensity scores for customers at each time period (each month in our setting) are computed, and adopters might be matched with different nonadopters over time, whereas in a static matching, the matched control customers remain the same for the entire study period. The dynamic matching procedure is likely to perform better in this setting because it accounts for time-varying customer characteristics. Therefore, we use dynamic matching for our main estimation and consider static matching as a robustness check.

Next, we use the one-to-one matching with replacement to derive the closest matched nonadopter. In particular, we consider a caliper size of 0.2 times the standard deviation of propensity scores in our main estimation. Under this procedure, all mobile payment adopters have been appropriately matched (i.e., a 100% matching rate). We then employ alternative matching algorithms to assess the robustness of our main results. We first conduc nearest-three-neighbors matching with replacement as one robustness check, in which all treatment customers have been appropriately matched (i.e., a 100% matching rate). Next, we utilize matching specifications with a com mon support requirement and a tighter caliper size (i.e., 0.05 times the standard deviation of propensity scores) to further assess the stability of results under more stringent matching conditions. Under this tighter caliper size, approximately 96.7% of mobile payment adopters are appropriately matched based on both one-to-one matching with replacement and the nearest-three-neighbors matching. Finally, following Austin and Small (2014), we bootstrap the clustered standard errors in the specifications with a caliper size of 0.2 × standard deviation to account for the variance from the estimation of propensity scores, and the matching rate is the same as our main estimation without bootstrapped standard errors.

Finally, to evaluate our matching results, we compare the propensity score distributions of the matched and unmatched samples (Haviland et al. 2007, Caliendo and Kopeinig 2008) as depicted in Online Figure 2. We find the distributions for the control and treated customers much more similar after both the one-to-one and nearestthree-neighbor matching procedures. Moreover, we run a Kolmogorov–Smirnov test to compare the distributions (Smirnov 1939) and find the p-values of the difference between the two distribution functions of the treated and matched control groups are always larger than 0.05, indicating that the difference in propensity score distributions between the two groups is insignificant. Moreover, following Haviland et al. (2007), we compare the standardized bias before and after the matching given by

the following formula:

$$
B i a s = | \mu_ {x _ {t}} - \mu_ {x _ {c}} | / \sqrt {0 . 5 \times (s _ {x _ {t}} ^ {2} + s _ {x _ {c}} ^ {2})},\tag{2}
$$

where $\mu _ { x _ { t } } \left( \mu _ { x _ { c } } \right)$ and $s _ { x _ { t } } ^ { 2 } ~ ( s _ { x _ { c } } ^ { 2 } )$ are the mean and variance of covariate X for the treated (control) customers before or after matching. We present the balance check for the standard bias and the percentage improvement of matching in Online Table 11. Online Table 11 indicates that, before matching, the covariates between the treated and control groups are unbalanced (i.e., the standard bias is relatively large), and the biases are greatly reduced across most covariates after the matching. In addition, we perform a t-test to compare the means of the treated and control groups before and after the matching as shown in the last two columns of Online Table 11. The t-test results confirm that the means of these covariates are statistically similar after matching.

5.2.2. Selection of Unobservables. To balance the unobservables, following Narang and Shankar (2019), we construct our control group with future adopters because the future adopters are closer to the current adopters (in terms of both observables and unobservables) than nonadopters. Specifically, we define the control group as customers who adopted mobile payments in or after May 2011, considering customers who adopted mobile payment in the first four months (between January and April 2011) after the launch of mobile payment as the treatment group. By comparing the differences in outcomes between the treatment and control groups, we can assess the impact of mobile payment adoption.

5.2.3. Double Machine Learning Approach. Recall that the key endogeneity here is associated with temporally changing individual characteristics. Therefore, individual fixed effects and static matching cannot alleviate this concern, and we use a dynamic matching strategy to match individuals in each period in our main analyses. We now consider an alternative method—the DML approach (Chernozhukov et al. 2018, Dube et al. 2020), which enables us to leverage the granularity of the transaction data in conjunction with machine learning methods to alleviate concerns regarding temporally changing individual characteristics associated with the endogeneity.

Specifically, following Dube et al. (2020), we implement the DML approach in three steps to estimate the treatment effect in the DID regression for each customer i in month t:

$$

$$

$$
\text { Step   2: } \text { TreatGroup } _ {i} \times \text { AfterTreat } _ {t} = m _ {0} (\mathbf {Z} _ {i t}) + \tau_ {t} + \mu_ {i t},\tag{4}
$$

$$
S t e p 3: (O u t c o m e _ {i t} - \hat {O u t c o m e} _ {i t}) = \delta \times \mu_ {i t} + \gamma_ {i t},\tag{5}
$$

where Outco $\imath e _ { i t }$ represents the key dependent variables used in our main analyses, $\tau _ { t }$ is the time dummy, $\epsilon _ { i t }$ is the error term in step $1 , \mu _ { i t }$ is the residual in step $2 , \gamma _ { i t }$ is the error term in step 3, and δ is the DID estimator of the treatment effect in step 3. Step 1 estimates the outcome using observables (vector $\mathbf { Z } _ { i t } )$ used for both matching and DID regression in our main model and time dum mies (i.e., capturing the time trend). Step 2 estimates the treatment term using the same control variables as in step 1. Functions $g _ { 0 } ( \cdot )$ and $m _ { 0 } ( \cdot )$ ) can be estimated through machine learning approaches. Specifically, in our analysis, we use multitask elastic net to estimate the $g _ { 0 } ( \cdot )$ func tion and logistic regression and multilayer perceptron classifier to estimate the $m _ { 0 } ( \cdot )$ function. Finally, step 3 uses the residual in step 1 as the dependent variable and the residual computed in step 2 as the independent variable and estimates Equation (5), where δ is the DID esti mator. The advantage of this method is to debias the estimation of δ from the endogenous treatment concern (i.e., customer self-selection decisions because of the anticipation of future purchase needs).

The intuition of this approach is that control variables such as the ones included in $\mathbf { Z } _ { i t }$ could be correlated with the selection of the treatment group. To address this potential self-selection bias, we leverage the residual of the outcome variable estimated in step 1, which is part of the outcome that cannot be explained by control variables. Similarly, the residual estimated through step 2 is also part of the treatment that cannot be explained by control variables. Thus, in step 3, by using the residual of the treatment to predict the residual of the outcome, we can eliminate the influence of control variables and get the causal effect of treatment on the outcome. Note that this double machine learning approach does not involve matching; instead, it jointly estimates the endogenous adoption deci sion and its impact on the consumption outcome.

5.2.5. Placebo Test. To further assess the validity of our DID specification, following Bertrand et al. (2004), we examine the potential spurious correlation by randomly assigning treated customers. ## 6.1. Treatment Effects of Mobile Payment Adoption

This section presents our main empirical findings on the impact of mobile payment adoption; see Table 2. The full estimation results using dynamic matching with replacement are presented in Online Table 15.

6.1.1. Credit Card Activities and Customer Churn. The results from the first two columns of Table 2 support the first part of Hypothesis 1A by demonstrating an increase in both the monthly credit card transaction amount and frequency at the focal bank after the adoption of mobile payment. Specifically, using dynamic one-to-one matching with replacement in Table 2, we find that the average individual credit card transaction amount and frequency at the focal bank increase by approximately 9.4% and 10.7%, respectively, following the adoption of mobile payment. These results remain consistent under the nearest three neighbors matching, caliper size of 0.05 × standard deviation, bootstrapped standard errors, and the double machine learning method with different machine learning algorithms as shown in Table 2. However, we note that the estimation results without matching are opposite $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ negative effects on both credit card transaction amount and frequency), indicating potential estimation bias be cause of unbalanced observables between adopters and nonadopters. To gain a deeper understanding of the economic value of these changes, we consider a monthly average credit card transaction amount of US\$1,000.94 per customer in our data set (as shown in Online Table 9). Our findings suggest an average increase of US\$94 in total consumption per month for each adopter at the focal bank, which may originate from their bank cards outside our focal bank, cash payment, or new consumption.

To examine the impact of mobile payment adoption on customer churn, we estimate a hazard model with Cox regression on churn as shown in column (3) of Table 2. Our results indicate that customer churn decreases following the adoption of mobile payment, suggesting that mobile payment adoption not only increases customer credit card activities at the focal bank in the short run but also enhances customer loyalty and stickiness to our focal bank in the long run. These findings have important implications for bank managers. One plausible explanation for these positive outcomes is the reduced transaction cost associated with mobile payment adoption. The payment process using QR code technology is perceived as easy to use by customers (see Yan et al. 2021), leading to a lower customer utility threshold required for making a purchase. Consequently, the purchase probability and customer consumption increase because of the convenience of mobile payments. Therefore, we observe increased credit card transaction activities following the adoption of mobile payment.

Finally, we perform the Rosenbaum (2002) sensitivity analysis to evaluate the robustness of our main results by assessing the magnitude of hidden bias required to alter the observed effects. Given the potential presence of hidden bias (i.e., unobservables), individuals matched based on observed covariates may have different probabilities of adoption, which could affect the effectiveness of the matching procedure. Our statistical analysis indicates that an unobserved factor needs to increase the likelihood of adoption by more than 39% to alter the current results, which further validates the robustness of our main results.

6.1.2. Online and Off-line Credit Card Activities. We then estimate the effects of mobile payment adoption on online and off-line channels separately; see columns (4)–(11) in Table 2. ## 6.2. Change in the Treatment Effect After Expansion

As previously noted, our focal bank is the first bank in Asia to collaborate with Alipay and launch its mobile payment service. Alipay subsequently expanded and established its collaborations with other banks worldwide starting from May 26, 2011. To exclude the impact of Alipay users with other bank cards, our analysis in the previous section was based on the observation period between September 2010 and April 2011. However, it is also important for bank managers to understand how the treatment effect of mobile payment adoption changed after the expansion.

To achieve this goal, we utilized the intervention on May 26, 2011, to investigate how the treatment effect of mobile payment adoption changed after the expansion. Specifically, we estimate the following regression for customer i in month t:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} O u t c o m e _ {i t} = a \times (T r e a t G r o u p _ {i} \times A f t e r T r e a t _ {t} \\ & \times A f t e r E x p a n s i o n _ {t}) + \delta \times (T r e a t G r o u p _ {i} \\ & \times A f t e r T r e a t _ {t}) + \mathbf {X} _ {i t} + \alpha_ {i} + \tau_ {t} + \epsilon_ {i t}, \end{array}\tag{7}
$$

where Outcome again represents the credit card transaction amount, frequency, or shares for different channels or payment methods as in our main estimation. The treatment group here consists of customers who adopted mobile payments between January and May 2011, whereas the control group includes customers who did not adopt mobile payments during our entire observation period. The variable AfterExpansion is a dummy variable indicating the postexpansion period with one representing the period from June to October 2011 and zero representing the period from September 2010 to May 2011. The coefficient a estimates the change in the treatment effect after the expansion. All other control variables in vector $\mathbf { \boldsymbol { X } } _ { i t }$ are the same as those in our main model. We again use dynamic one-to-one matching with replacement to construct comparable treatment and control groups. The estimation results are presented in Table 3.

From Table 3, we observe a positive change resulting from the expansion of mobile payment, specifically an increase in credit card transaction amount and a reduc tion in customer churn for our focal bank’s mobile payment users, which supports Hypothesis 2A. It is worth noting that customer churn was again estimated using Cox regression. This positive effect can be attributed to the reduced transaction costs associated with mobile payment adoption. As mobile payment becomes compatible with more banks, it further reduces transaction costs because of the improved facilitating conditions for using mobile payments. Therefore, the entry of other banks into the mobile payment market can have a positive externality on the focal bank. A key takeaway for bank managers is that the positive effect of mobile payment adoption will not diminish with increased competition from other banks. On the contrary, the entry of other banks into the mobile payment market can facilitate more credit card activities for the focal bank.

Furthermore, our analysis reveals that the positive effect resulting from the mobile payment expansion is mainly driven by increased off-line credit card activities, whereas online activities have decreased. This phenomenon can be attributed to the fact that mobile payment adoption reduces transaction costs, and its influence is more pronounced off-line than online. Customers often hold cards from multiple banks, and the expansion of mobile payment compatibility with more banks improves its facilitating conditions, particularly off-line. Consequently, we observe a positive change in the off-line channel after the expansion. However, the expansion has had a negative effect on the online channel, possibly because of increased competition. As the switching cost between banks decreases (i.e., competing banks also offer the same online mobile payment service), customers may shift some of their online credit card transactions to other competing banks. Section 7 provides a further empirical examination based on different payment methods.

## 6.3. Robustness Checks

6.3.1. Deal-Prone Customers. Although we have matched customers on various factors, an alternative explanation could be that the estimated effect is mainly driven by deal-prone customers rather than mobile payment adoption. However, it is worth noting that Alipay did not send any unique or targeted offers to adopters in the first four months after the launch of its mobile payment. To ensure that the deal-seeking behaviors or promotions did not affect the two groups differently, we reanalyzed our main results by removing customers involved in any promotion program during the first four months after the launch of mobile payment. The estimation results for the remaining customers are presented in Online Table 16 and are consistent with our main findings.

6.3.2. Alternative Intervention. As mentioned previously, our main analyses use the launch of Alipay mobile payment as the intervention. To ensure the robustness of our findings, we also perform a robustness check using an alternative intervention based on individual mobile payment adoptions. In this revised approach, we modify the regression Equation (1) by changing the $A f t e r T r e a t _ { t }$ variable into a binary variable that equals one after individual adoption and remains the same for each matched treatment and control group member. For instance, if a customer first adopted mobile payment in February 2011, the value of $\bar { A } f t e r T r e a t _ { t }$ is set to one for all subsequent months and zero otherwise. The matched nonadopters also share the same value. As a result, the time window for comparison remains consistent for each matched treatment and control pair. Online Table 17 shows consistent results.

6.3.3. Static Matching. In our main analyses, we utilize dynamic matching, assigning unique propensity scores to customers each month. However, in this analysis, we explore static matching, in which the matched control customers remain the same throughout the entire study period. We again consider different matching specifications: a caliper size of 0.2 × standard deviation of propensity scores, a caliper size of 0.05 × standard deviation, and bootstrapped standard errors. Additionally, we employ different matching algorithms: one-to-one and nearest-three-neighbors matching. The estimation results obtained from this static matching analysis as presented in Online Table 18 are consistent with the main analyses.

6.3.4. Subset of Frequent Credit Card Users. Although our focal bank is the main collaborator with Alipay mobile payment during our study period, a limitation of our data set is that it only captures credit card transactions from one bank. However, customers may use credit cards from different banks for their purchases, allocating specific cards for different activities. To address this concern, we analyze a subset of customers who are likely to predominantly use our focal bank’s credit cards for their transactions. Specifically, we narrow our focus to customers who hold more than one credit card from our foca bank and utilize more than 50% of their total credit limit every month. It is worth noting that, on average, each customer in our data set holds 1.5 credit cards from our focal bank. Furthermore, based on our discussions with the collaborating bank, the number of cards held and credit usage are key factors used by our focal bank to determine whether a customer primarily relies on their credit cards. We reestimate our main model using this subset of customers and present the results in Online Table 19. We find that our main results remain consistent.

6.3.5. Alipay Online Platform Effect. In addition to its mobile payment platform, Alipay also operates a PCbased online payment platform that was launched even before its mobile payment platform. This PC-based payment platform enables customers to make online purchases, particularly through Taobao.com, which is owned by Alibaba. In our main analysis, the PC payment method includes this Alipay online payment platform. However, a potential concern arises that the increase in online credit card transactions and the observed complementary effects on PC payment might be predominantly driven by the usage of this Alipay online payment platform rather than the adoption of mobile payment. Despite controlling for the adoption status of this Alipay PC platform in our matching process, the observed effects could potentially be attributed to the brand effect of Alipay. To address this concern, we exclude transactions made through the Alipay online platform and reanalyze our main findings. The estimation results are presented in Online Table 20, and we find that our main results remain consistent even after excluding transactions made through the Alipay online platform.

6.3.6. Seemingly Unrelated Regressions. In our main analyses, the independent variables in all of our main DID regressions are the same with the only difference being the dependent variable (outcome) on the left-hand side of Equation (1). In order to provide further validation for our model estimation, we employ a unified approach by estimating these regressions in a single model and comparing their coefficients. To achieve this goal, following Zellner (1962), we perform seemingly unrelated regressions to estimate the 10 regressions of our main analyses together; see Online Table 21. Based on the results in Online Table 21, we find that all our main results remain consistent, and the values of the coefficients are similar to when we estimate them separately, which fur ther validates the robustness of our results.

6.3.7. Matching Without Behavioral Measures. In our main analyses, we incorporated two behavioral measures, TXN\_AMT and TRAN\_FREQ, in the matching process to control for dynamic customer behaviors. However, there may be a debate regarding whether to include outcome-related measures in the matching process. To address this concern, we conducted an additional estimation by excluding the two behavioral measures from the matching process. The estimation results of this analysis are presented in Online Table 22. Based on the estimation results, we again find that our main results generally remain consistent.

6.3.8. Supplier Categorical Fixed Effects. The adoption and usage of mobile payment can be influenced by how businesses or sellers promote and advertise their payment methods. For instance, e-commerce sellers may offer discounts or incentives for using specific payment methods such as mobile payment. However, we acknowledge that our data set, being from the bank, does not include information on promotions or discounts offered by sellers, which is a limitation of our data. Nevertheless, it is important to note that sellerside promotions or discounts would not systematically bias our treatment effects because they would not be applied uniformly to all mobile payment adopters simultaneously. To further address this concern, we perform a robustness check by introducing fixed effects of suppliers who accept mobile payments. Given that we have nearly 120,000 suppliers accepting mobile payment by the end of our observation period (as depicted in Online Figure 3), including fixed effects for all these suppliers would result in a large number of dummy variables in the estimation, especially considering the inclusion of individual fixed effects. Therefore, we introduce supplier categorical fixed effects based on their industry categories (such as grocery, entertainment, travel, healthcare, service, and education). The intuition is that certain types of suppliers are more likely to prefer mobile payment, such as grocery retailers. The estimation results are presented in Online Table 23, and we find that all the results are consistent with our main estimation.

6.3.9. Falsification Analyses: Impact of Unobserved Events. To further eliminate the potential influence of unobserved events and determine if the observed effects are spurious, we conduct a falsification check by analyzing the period after the launch of mobile payment but before actual individual adoption. The rationale behind this check is that the launch of mobile payment itself, without customer adoption, should not result in significant behavioral changes except for the possibility of spillover effects caused by the news of its release. To implement this idea, we create different cohorts of adopters based on their adoption dates and examine the postperiod between the mobile payment launch date and their individual adoption date. For example, for adopters on March 15, 2011, the pre-adoption period would be from January to February 2011. Importantly, the preperiod for these adopters remains the same as in our main analyses, spanning from September 2010 until the end of December 2010. We then estimate Equation (1) for each cohort of adopters, comparing them with their matched nonadopter counterparts. The results of this falsification test are presented in Online Table 24. We find that the estimated coefficients of the treatment effect are not statistically significant in this analysis. This indicates that our DID estimator is unlikely to be affected by spurious correlation. It is worth noting that this test does not precisely replicate our main analyses because of the reduced number of postperiod observations. Nonetheless, it provides additional confidence in our estimated effects by demonstrating the absence of significant effects before actual adoption.

## 7.1. Interrelationships Among Payment Methods After Adoption

Our main findings suggest that the adoption of mobile payment increases both online and off-line credit card activities at the focal bank with a larger impact observed for the online channel. To better understand the underlying mechanism, we conduct a granular analysis of the three payment methods, namely, physical card, PC, and off-line and online mobile payments.

In Table 4, we estimate the effects on credit card transaction amount, frequency, and shares for each payment method. Results in columns (1)–(4) of panel A show that mobile payment adoption leads to a reduction in credit card activities through physical card payments. Specifi cally, the physical credit card transaction amount and frequency decreased by approximately 4.33% and 6.80%, respectively, after the adoption of mobile payment, and their shares decreased by approximately 14% and 15.2%, respectively. However, columns (1)–(4) of panel B in Table 4 reveal that credit card activities through off-line mobile payment significantly increased. Combining the results from both panels A and B, we conclude that mobile payment acts as a substitute for physical card payment in the off-line channel. This finding provides empirical evidence of the underlying mechanism of reduced transaction costs. As previously mentioned, Alipay mobile payment employs QR code technology, eliminating the need for customers to present a physical card to the merchant off-line and thereby reducing transaction costs.

Next, we discuss the relationship between PC and mobile payments. From columns (5)–(8) of panel A in Table 4, it is evident that mobile payment adoption increases the credit card transaction amount through PC payment by approximately 3.29%, whereas its frequency decreases by approximately 3.97%. Moreover, columns (5)–(8) of panel B in Table 4 demonstrate a significant increase in credit card transactions through online mobile payment. This implies that there is a certain complementary effect between PC payment and mobile payment with regard to credit card transaction amounts. To understand the underlying reason for this complementary effect, we conduct two subset analyses with and without bank customers who adopted PC payments before the launch of mobile payment, respectively; see Online Tables 25 and 26. Results in Online Table 25 suggest that, for PC payment users before mobile payment adoption, the adoption of mobile payment replaces their usage of PC payment. However, Online Table 26 shows that, for non-PC payment users before the adoption, the adoption of mobile payment complements their usage of PC payment. This finding indicates that the adoption of mobile payment also facilitates the adoption of PC payment for customers who did not use PC payment before its launch. Consequently, the complementary effect between PC and mobile payments is likely driven by the coadoption of the two. Moreover, this complementary effect may help explain why we observe a higher positive effect of mobile payment adoption on credit card activities through the online channel as opposed to the off-line channel as previously discussed in Section 6.1.2.

## 7.2. Interrelationships Among Payment Methods After Expansion

Our analysis of the impact of mobile payment expansion reveals that the entry of other banks into the mobile payment market can facilitate increased credit card activities for our focal bank. Moreover, this positive effect is primarily driven by increased off-line credit card activities, whereas online activities decreased after the expansion. To gain insights into the underlying mechanism, we conduct analyses of the three payment methods. Table 5 presents the estimation results.

We first find an increase in the credit card transaction amount, amount share, frequency, and frequency share for adopters through off-line mobile payments after the expansion with all DID estimators being positive under a 1% significance level. However, we did not find a statistically significant effect on physical card payment. This finding supports our mechanism of reduced transaction costs. Specifically, as mobile payment becomes compatible with more banks, it further reduces transaction costs, improving the facilitating conditions for using mobile payments, particularly off-line. Therefore, the entry of other banks has a positive externality on the focal bank.

Furthermore, we observe a decrease in credit card transactions through online mobile payment, whereas an increase is seen through online PC payment after the expansion. This finding suggests that the complementary effect on PC payment continues to hold even after the expansion; however, the initial growth of online mobile payment decreases after the expansion, possibly because of increased competition. It is possible that, with mobile payment becoming compatible with more banks, competing banks are also offering the same online mobile payment service. This reduces the switching cost between banks’ online channels, leading customers to shift some of their online credit card transactions to other banks. A key takeaway for bank managers is that mobile payment is likely to continue playing a crucial role in the off-line channel in the long run, whereas PC payment will remain a significant player in the online channel even after the mobile payment expansion. As a result, it is essential for bank managers to prioritize improving the facilitating conditions for off-line mobile payments and also enhancing online PC payments.

7.3.1. Effort Expectancy. Effort expectancy pertains to the ease-of-use associated with the technology (Venkatesh et al. 2012, Miltgen et al. 2013). In other words, credit card activities increase when the technology is perceived as easy to use and does not require too much effort. The Alipay mobile payment, which uses the QR code technology, is perceived as easy to use by customers (see Yan et al. 2021). To further investigate the aspect of effort expectancy, we conduct subset analyses based on age groups. Previous research demonstrates that age plays a vital role in technology acceptance and perceived ease of use (Phang et al. 2006). A prior study shows that younger customers are more likely to perceive a greater ease of use of mobile payment technology than older generations (Lie´bana-Cabanillas et al. 2015), whereas older generations are less likely to be active in using smartphones (see Silver et al. 2019). It is important to note that we included the age variable in our matching procedure to reduce the potential self-selection bias and construct balanced observables between adopters and nonadopters. In this section, we focus on understanding the heterogeneous effects based on different age groups.

Following the analysis conducted by the Pew Research Center,<sup>5</sup> we conduct subset analyses based on four different generations: Millennials (1981–1996, ages 15–30 in 2011), Gen X (1965–1980, ages 31–46 in 2011), Boomers II (1955–1964, ages 47–56 in 2011), and Boomers I (1946– 1954, ages 57–65 in 2011); see Table 6. The estimation results indicate that the effects on overall credit card transaction activities and activities through the off-line and online channels are more significant for Millennials, Gen X, and Boomers II, which are relatively younger generations. However, these effects are generally not significant for Boomers I. This finding suggests that bank managers should prioritize facilitating the use of mobile payment among the older generation. For example, Alipay has recently launched a relative card service that allows customers (especially the older generation) to pay using mobile payments through their relatives’ accounts. This function helps address the challenges faced by the older generation who do not possess bank cards.

7.3.2. Social Influence. Social influence is an important factor that affects the adoption and use of mobile payment technology. It reflects the opinions of people around a focal mobile payment user, and if other people around the user have a positive attitude toward the technology, the user is more likely to use it (Venkatesh 2003). To investigate the effect of social influence, we examine the city tiers in China as different cities have different levels of technology adoption and services. According to a recent report (see Mozur 2017), mobile payments are used by a majority of people in top-tier cities in China, indicating that these cities are likely to have a more favorable environment for adopting this technology. We use the tier system<sup>6</sup> to classify our customers into three tiers based on their residence address: tier 1 cities, which are larger and likely to have a more favorable environment for accepting new technology; tier 2 cities, which are medium-sized cities; and tier 3 cities, which are smaller and may have a lower level of social influence. We conduct subset analyses based on these three tiers as shown in Table 7. The results suggest that the effects of mobile payment adoption are generally higher for customers in tier 1 and 2 cities and much lower for customers in tier 3 cities, supporting the role of social influence in technology adoption. Hence, bank managers might want to focus on promoting mobile payments in lower tier cities and develop appropriate strategies to enhance their adoption.

7.3.3. Facilitating Conditions. Finally, we discuss facilitating conditions, which refer to the resources or facilities available to conduct mobile payment transactions. Customers are more likely to use mobile payment if there are more supporting resources available. Here, we consider one particular facilitating condition, which is the number of suppliers accepting mobile payments, denoted as NUM\_SUPPLY in our estimation. Specifically, the greater the number of existing suppliers that accept mobile payment around the focal user, the better the facilitating conditions for the user. Because NUM\_SUPPLY is a continuous variable, we introduce the interaction term between NUM\_SUPPLY and the treatment measure (denoted as NUM\_SUPPLY ∗ TreatGroup ∗ AfterTreat) into our main estimation model and present the results in Table 8. From Table 8, it is evident that better facilitating conditions intensify the positive impact of mobile payment adoption on credit card transaction activities as well as activities through off-line and online channels. This finding suggests that, to facilitate the use of mobile payments, bank managers should not only consider demandside conditions but also improve supply-side conditions.

## 8. Concluding Remarks

The emerging mobile payment technology is rapidly growing in popularity and is gradually leading to changes in bank customer behavior. Thus, it is inherently crucial for bank managers to understand the effects of mobile payment adoption on the activities of their customers through different channels. To investigate these effects, we acquire access to a unique data set from a leading bank in Asia, which records customer credit card transaction activities using different payment methods through online and off-line channels.

Our findings provide several practical implications. First, we establish that mobile payment technology improves the shopping experience, creating a seamless process that ultimately enhances credit card activities either off-line or online and potentially increases bank revenue. Moreover, mobile payment adoption results in increased customer loyalty to the focal bank, a valuable consideration for bank managers in the long run. As a result, bank managers should focus on promoting mobile payment through both online and off-line channels to reap the benefits. Second, we demonstrate that the positive impact of mobile payment adoption persists even in the face of competition from other banks. In fact, the entry of other banks into the mobile payment market can facilitate an increase in credit card activities for the focal bank. Finally, our study offers practical insights for bank managers seeking to improve the adoption and use of mobile payments. First, it is important to focus on facilitating the use of mobile payments among the older generation. Second, strategies to promote mobile payments should be targeted toward customers in lower tier cities. Third, improving the supply-side conditions, such as promoting mobile payments among suppliers, can further enhance the benefits of mobile payments. By taking these steps, bank managers can promote the adoption and use of mobile payments, which, in turn, can lead to improved credit card activities and enhanced customer loyalty.

To the best of our knowledge, our work is the first attempt to explore the impact of mobile payment adoption on bank customer credit card activities and the interrelationships among different payment methods through off-line and online channels at the focal bank. Whereas our findings provide valuable insights, there are some limitations to our work that provide potential areas for future research. First, our data set does not include information on customers’ bank cards beyond our focal bank or cash payment. Future research with a more comprehensive data set could investigate how mobile payment affects bank cards beyond the focal bank, providing insights into the exact source of increased transaction activities observed in this study. Second, although our analyses provide insights into postadoption behavioral changes, long-term mobile payment adoption might be associated with fundamental changes in customer consumption behaviors. Future studies with more extended time periods and more customer behavioral information might bring new insights into long-term behavioral changes, such as the potential drivers of abandonment of mobile payment, negative hidden consequences of mobile payment, safety concerns, and network or technical uncertainties. Finally, because our data set is from one major bank, we are only able to observe promotions offered by the bank, not those from the seller’s side. Future research could extend our work to include promotions on the seller’s side to identify how the effects of mobile payment adoption and expansion vary based on different seller promotions.

Despite these limitations, our analyses demonstrate a significant economic impact on our focal bank’s credit card consumption even at an early stage after the launch of mobile payment and after the initial expansion of mobile payment. As mobile payment continues to grow with more advanced functionalities, we expect to see an even more profound impact on customer multichannel consumption behaviors and implications on bank and technology management. We hope our empirical findings can stimulate future empirical and theoretical research in this direction.
