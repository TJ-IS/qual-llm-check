# Beyond Digital vs. IT: The Untold Story of Their Relationship from an Organizing Logic Perspective

Abstract. Digital and information technology (IT) are two concepts that scholars and practitioners use to refer to organizing around technology. Although some consider these concepts synonymous, others consider them distinct. Relatedly, both digital and IT units are increasingly found in many organizations aiming to incorporate digital technologies into their customer offerings and operational processes. This coexistence suggests a qualitative difference in the underpinning logic of organizing around digital and IT. Although prior studies have focused on exploring how digital and IT are conceptually distinct, there is an acknowledgment that they coexist as vocabulary in practice. Indeed, not only do digital and IT coexist, they also interact, giving rise to relationships that have hitherto remained untheorized. We depart from the focus on digital versus IT and take a digital cum IT perspective to uncover these relationships and their implications for organizing around technology. Building on an organizing logic perspective, we synthesize prior literature into a conceptual framework to empirically investigate interactions between digital and IT units in three ethnographic studies of digital transformation. From this, we identify and theorize three relationships between digital and IT—interdepending, interlocking, and interfacing— and further explain why these relationships emerge and how they manifest in different dynamics. The resulting theorizing informs a research agenda for understanding the relational nature of organizing around technology.

## 1. Introduction

Organizations increasingly seek avenues to incorporate digital technologies into their operational processes and customer offerings (Sebastian et al. 2017, Vial 2019). To achieve this, they no longer only rely on information technology (IT) units and chief information officers (CIOs) as the main custodians of digital technology knowledge. Instead, they increasingly establish digital units (Svahn et al. 2017, Raabe et al. 2020) or appoint chief digital officers (CDOs) (Tumbas et al. 2017, Lorenz and Buchwald 2023). This introduction of new executive roles and organizational units shifts established approaches to organizing around technology, that is, how organizational activities are arranged to benefit from technology (Yoo et al. 2012, Peppard 2018, Baskerville et al. 2020)—In this paper, unless we specify, we use “technology” to refer to digital and information technology. Although IT units have traditionally managed technology in organizations, digital units are emerging as new organizationa arrangements that also focus on leveraging technology. This coexistence signifies a qualitative difference in the way these two units organize around technology as they draw on different practices, skills, and goals (Nambisan et al. 2017, Baiyere et al. 2020, Wessel et al. 2021). Beyond these observations about organizational units, prior literature has also conceptually delineated digital from IT across several concepts, such as digital versus IT transformation, digital versus IT innovation, and digital versus IT strategy (Bharadwaj et al. 2013, Kohli and Melville 2019, Wessel et al. 2021).

However, these conceptual delineations between digital and IT, although valuable, focus on what separates them as opposed to what connects them. Such a focus risks obscuring scholarship from examining their relationship. Indeed, prior studies have shown that at the empirical level, digital and IT coexist and interact within organizations (Urbach et al. 2017, Tumbas et al. 2018, Jo¨hnk et al. 2022). For example, studies report interactions among different configurations of IT, digital, and business units (Dremel et al. 2017, Jo¨hnk et al. 2022, Haskamp et al. 2023), and between CIOs and CDOs (Horlacher and Hess 2016, Tumbas et al. 2018, Lorenz and Buchwald 2023). These studies acknowledge in their empirical accounts that digital and IT engage in short-term, context-bound interactions (Kern and Willcocks 2002, Al-Natour and Benbasat 2009). However, their inquiries rarely focus on these empirical-level interactions or on how they reflect conceptual-level relationships between digital and IT. Rather, scholarship has placed emphasis on investigating their conceptual differences. We argue that it is important to theorize relationships between digital and IT to avoid treating them as isolated silos. In other words, our study seeks to balance the search for what separates digital and IT with an understanding of what connects them.

Using interactions between digital and IT units as our empirical anchor, we outline how theorizing digital and IT relationships is important for at least three reasons. First, organizations often rely on both digita and IT units to benefit from digital transformation (DT) initiatives (Urbach et al. 2017, Ross et al. 2019, Jo¨hnk et al. 2022). In fact, existing work shows that organizations tend to separate DT initiatives (e.g., founding independent digital units) from IT efforts (Svahn et al. 2017, Tumbas et al. 2017, Chanias et al. 2019). However, practitioners increasingly acknowledge that reaping long-term DT benefits requires digital and IT units to interact: “[ … ] at least 60% of the highest-value technology projects companies pursue require collaboration and delivery [ … ] across both digital and IT teams” (Khan et al. 2017, p. 2). Implementing such relational approach, however, challenges organizations as they “struggle to seamlessly unite digital [ … ] and traditional IT work.” (Torres 2001). Thus, to harness the coexistence of digital and IT, we must understand why and how digital and IT relate. Second, existing literature has indicated that interactions between digital and IT units can lead to either collaboration or conflict, without explaining why such dynamics occur (Tumbas et al. 2018, Lorenz and Buchwald 2023). Understanding the conditions under which these interactions lead to collaboration or conflict can provide insights into how to organize efficiently around technology to reap long-term benefits. Indeed, practitioners often state that DT efforts fail due to ongoing, lasting conflicts between digital and IT units (Leung 2019). Third, theorizing relationships between digital and IT has the potential to provide new avenues for scholarly inquiry into how organizations engage concurrently with digital and IT phenomena. Hence, we pose the question: How do interactions between digital and IT units reflect relationships between digital and IT, and what dynamics characterize these relationships?

To answer this research question, we draw on ethnographic data from three organizations that were undergoing DT. We position our study within prior lit erature on interactions between digital and IT units. By synthesizing existing literature on digital and IT, we then unravel the organizing logics of digital and IT across three rationales—strategy, routine, and technol ogy. Based on this, we derive a conceptual framework that outlines how organizing logics serve as our analytical lens to theorize relationships between digital and IT (i.e., the conceptual level). We then proceed to examine how employees within the digital and IT units of the three organizations drew on specific organizing logics in their interactions.

We put forward several contributions to both theory and practice. First, we identify three relationships between digital and IT: interdepending, interfacing, and interlocking. Second, we outline propositions for explaining the antecedents of these relationships— why they emerge, the dynamics these relationships exhibit, and the conditions under which a certain dynamic is more likely to prevail. Third, we provide a relational model of digital cum IT that highlights how the interplay of relationships’ dynamics can affect organizing around technology. Lastly, we present a research agenda that advances further scholarly efforts in investigating relationships between digital and IT and thus, it mobilizes future research within the information systems (IS) discipline and beyond (Sambamurthy and Zmud 2000, Yoo et al. 2012).

## 2. Theoretical Background 2.1. Overview of Prior Literature on Digital and IT Interactions

The foundation for understanding the relationships between digital and IT is recognizing that they are distinct concepts. This distinction is evident in prior work that delineates digital and IT as separate concepts (Wessel et al. 2021, Piccoli et al. 2022, Baiyere et al. 2023). Simultaneously, this work acknowledges interactions between digital and IT units (Dremel et al. 2017, Tumbas et al. 2018), CDOs and CIOs (Tumbas et al. 2018, Lorenz and Buchwald 2023), and digital and business units (Nambisan et al. 2017, Svahn et al. 2017). Although IS scholars have posited that, in general, short-term, context-bound interactions reflect abstract and enduring relationships (Kern and Willcocks 2002, Al-Natour and Benbasat 2009), reviewed studies rarely conceptualize the relationships that these interactions reflect. Instead, they predominantly focus on investigating various DT-related topics, with interactions implied only in their empirical narratives. We reviewed these empirically implied interactions to establish a foundation for examining the abstract relationships between digital and IT (Table 1).

Based on our review, we infer that the interactions between digital and IT units or between CIOs and CDOs can be characterized by collaborating or conflicting dynamics. Collaborating dynamics outline complementarities between digital and IT in organizations (Dremel et al. 2017, Haffke et al. 2017, Lorenz and Buchwald 2023). Such collaborations can range from providing IT support for digital initiatives to active involvement of IT units in planning and designing these initiatives. For example, Dremel et al. (2017) outline how a car manufacturer’s IT unit participated in car design activities as part of a collaboration with the digital unit. Similarly, Gregory et al. (2018) describe how a bank established a digital factory to allow business-focused and IT-focused employees from different units to collaborate on digital innovation projects. These empirical examples indicate the effect that collaborating dynamics can have on an organization’s DT initiatives.

Conflicting dynamics outline clashes between digi tal and IT units or CIOs and CDOs (Tumbas et al. 2018, Chanias et al. 2019). Such clashes often stem from dif ferences between digital and IT units (Haffke et al. 2017, Leonhardt et al. 2020). For example, IT units acting as service providers may not be able to meet digital units’ requests due to legacy IT systems, competing demands from core business units, or a lack of adequate IT capabilities, such as staff trained in emerging technologies. Additionally, their overall goal of ensuring the stability of the organization’s IT systems may clash with digital units’ demands (Nwankpa and Datta 2017, Jo¨hnk et al. 2022). As a result, digital units may deploy workarounds, such as developing their own IT systems (e.g., enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems; Lorenz and Buchwald 2023) or engaging in IT outsourcing (Dremel et al. 2017). These actions may bypass the IT unit and further intensify existing conflicts (Haffke et al. 2016, Gregory et al. 2018). This can slow down organizations’ DT initiatives (Nwankpa and Datta 2017, Pola´kova´-Kersten et al. 2023).

The existing literature suggests that through these collaborative or conflicting interactions, the units can influence each other. Most studies emphasize that IT units, influenced by digital units, can commit to digital practices, such as fast and agile development (Tumbas et al. 2018, Leonhardt et al. 2020). IT units may also adopt bimodal IT practices, which focus on both exploration and exploitation (Haffke et al. 2017, Teubner and Stockhinger 2020), or acquire new digital capabilities, such as data analytics (Dremel et al. 2017). This can transform IT units’ identities from service provider to digital innovation partner (Leonhardt et al. 2020) or shift IT governance modes from functional IT governance to platform-based (digital) governance (Sambamurthy and Zmud 2000, Gregory et al. 2018). Conversely, digital units can be influenced by IT units to incorporate security and privacy standards into their digital innovation processes (Lorenz and Buchwald 2023).

Although prior studies allude to digital and IT as distinct concepts, the emphasis on their delineation can lead to at least three shortcomings. First, because these studies do not focus on interactions between digital and IT units or CIOs and CDOs in organizations, the relationships these interactions reflect remain undertheorized. Second, prior literature highlights that interactions can take on collaborative or conflicting dynamics, but studies rarely focus on these dynamics. This creates an opportunity to examine interactions between digital and IT units for other possible dynamics. Third, we have limited knowledge about why different dynamics emerge and what conditions shape them. For example, we do not know why digital and IT units sometimes interact collaboratively, although at other times, their interactions are marked by conflict. We suggest that a serious examination of the relational nature of digital and IT offers opportunities to theorize their combined effects on organizational initiatives, such as DT.

## 2.2. Using Organizing Logics to Study Relationships Between Digital and IT

Organizing logics are rationales, meaning observable patterns in organizational arrangements that are established to reflect environmental and business imperatives (Sambamurthy and Zmud 2000). These rationales capture the dominant organizational reasoning, as reflected in the assumptions and actions that underpin organizational arrangements (Baiyere et al. 2020). They are grounded in past experiences, which form the basic premises characterizing a domain. These logics can become dominant once proven effective in achieving organizational success (Vargo and Lusch 2004). However, as these rationales gradually become embedded as a defining modus operandi, they may clash with new or changing organizational contexts. Indeed, they can become unsuitable as organizational contexts evolve (Sambamurthy et al. 2003). This makes organizing logics a crucial concept for understanding how organizational arrangements change, differ, and relate to each other.

At the dawn of the recent digital wave, Sambamurthy and Zmud (2000) used organizing logics to anticipate how changes in the organizational context would alter IT units’ organizational arrangements. Their proposed logic suggested that IT would evolve into a platform-based information system as they observed IT units increasingly interacting with othe organizational units. Schwarz and Hirschheim (2003) expanded on this idea by proposing that IT units would shift from managing their internal structures to managing their relationships with other units. Later, scholars outlined a new organizing logic of digital, suggesting a shift by stating that “the tables have turned,” as digital logic increasingly seeped into organizations (Yoo et al. 2012, Yoo 2013). This initiated research into understanding digital logic as distinct from IT logic (Baiyere et al. 2020, Wessel et al. 2021). This distinction provides the foundation for studying how digital and IT units relate. This reveals two key points. First, the organizational arrangements of digital and IT units, in the context of organizing around technology, follow distinct organizing logics. Second, organizing logics serves as a relevant concept for understanding how these organizational arrangements relate.

To theorize how digital and IT units relate, we build on their organizing logics, referred to here as digital or IT logics. Next, we synthesize these logics based on prior literature. We identify three core rationales that underlie each of these logics: strategy, routine, and tech nology. These rationales, although not exhaustive, align with similar conceptual frameworks found in existing literature, which suggest strategic, operational, and technical considerations for organizing around technology (Markus and Keil 1994, Arvidsson et al. 2014, Ross et al. 2019).

2.2.1. Strategy Rationale. The strategy rationale refers to the relative positioning of digital and IT in relation to the organization’s overall strategy. It reflects the vision, decision-making authority, and leadership structure that define these logics within organizational arrangements (Henderson and Venkatraman 1999, Bharadwaj et al. 2013). Our review of prior work suggests that the strategy rationale of digital is predominantly “businesscentric,” meaning that it is deeply embedded in the core business strategy. This contrasts with the strategy rationale of IT, which is described in prior literature as “business-aligned,” indicating that IT supports but does not drive the overall business strategy.

The literature on digital logic identifies digital as a central driver of both organizational and business strategy (Bharadwaj et al. 2013, Chanias et al. 2019,

Teubner and Stockhinger 2020). This assumption is reflected in the idea that digital strategies prioritize technology-centered solutions, which create new value propositions aimed at addressing customers’ needs (Ross et al. 2017). As a result, digital has become an intrinsic component and often a major driver of the overall business strategy (Sia et al. 2016, Yeow et al. 2018, Teubner and Stockhinger 2020). Furthermore, scholars and practitioners view digital as a means of uncovering new strategic opportunities and developing innovative strategic directions (Matt et al. 2015, Zimmer 2019, Jo¨hnk et al. 2022). However, digital strategies are not merely technology strategies; rather, they are business strategies that inherently incorporate digitally enabled opportunities (Hess et al. 2016, Sebastian et al. 2017).

In contrast, most academic and practitioner discourse around IT suggests that the strategy rationale of IT is focused on alignment with the broader business and organizational strategy (Weill and Ross 2005, Bharadwaj et al. 2013, Yeow et al. 2018). Under this rationale, IS scholars and practitioners view IT as a functional necessity for achieving organizational and business objectives. This implies that IT generally follows, rather than determines, the organizational strategy. This assumption is reflected in the relative position of IT units within organizational structures. Many studies have found that IT units report to chief financial officers while the CIO heading the IT unit is often excluded from the executive management team (Armstrong and Sambamurthy 1999, Banker et al. 2011)—with some exceptions (Haffke et al. 2016, Tumbas et al. 2017). It is important to note that there has been at least one shift in the strategy rationale of IT since its early days within organizations—when IT was largely seen as a mere supporter of achieving organizational goals (Galliers and Sutherland 1991, Nolan and McFarlan 2005). In this shift, IT moved beyond its initial support role to become a key strategic consideration for organizations (Lederer and Salmela 1996, Henderson and Venkatraman 1999, Kohli and Grover 2008). Prior literature suggests that this shift may represent the evolution of IT’s businessaligned rationale into the more recent business-centric rationale, which is now associated with digital strategies. However, IT’s dominant focus remains to align rather than drive business strategy. Hence, we surmise the following.

Synthesis 1. Digital logic’s strategy rationale is businesscentric, whereas IT logic’s strategy rationale is businessaligned.

2.2.2. Routine Rationale. Routine rationale refers to the operational aspects that define the design and organizational responsibilities of digital and IT within an organization. Our synthesis indicates that digital logic’s routine rationale is predominantly enterprising, meaning that related routines are less risk-averse and are focused on speed, adaptability, and continuous learning. In contrast, IT logic’s routine rationale is predominantly focused on stabilizing, with routines designed to ensure the reliable delivery of IS that sup port critical business operations.

Prior literature acknowledges that digital logic’s routine rationale is shaped by efforts to harness the generative potential of technologies, which enables the creation of new possibilities and organizational realities (Kallinikos et al. 2013, Baskerville et al. 2020). As a result, new and often unpredictable ways of organizing emerge, which are deeply intertwined with individuals and groups within organizations (Zammuto et al. 2007). These new forms challenge existing routines by leveraging the potential of new technologies. For example, digital units often differentiate their activities from IT units by establishing organizational structures that operate independently (Leonhardt et al. 2018, Fuchs et al. 2019, Raabe et al. 2021, Jo¨hnk et al. 2022). Additionally, DT requires a comprehensive cultural shift that emphasizes experimentation, prototyping, and adopting a fail-fast approach, thus viewing failures as opportunities for learning and improvement (Hartl 2019, Zimmer and Niemimaa 2020, Eberl and Drews 2021, Zimmer et al. 2023). The emergent routine rationale of digital logic also requires employees to develop new knowledge, competencies, and skills to ensure that they have the necessary digital fluency to operate effectively (Colbert et al. 2016). Organi zations often form interdisciplinary teams with a high degree of autonomy, enabling them to quickly develop and deliver new products and services (Raabe et al. 2020, Lueg et al. 2022). These teams aim to strengthen collaborations both internally, within the organization, and externally with stakeholders, such as customers (Faraj et al. 2011, Chanias et al. 2019).

The routine rationale of IT is based on the assumption that ensuring the stability of IT systems is critical for supporting organizational processes. This rationale is reflected in the operational focus of IT units (Recker 2014, Sidorova et al. 2015). By adhering to established and tested routines, IT units serve as the operational backbone, ensuring that the organization’s essential systems remain continuously operational or that the “lights are always on” (Ross 2017). This is reflected in the IT unit’s perceived role within organizations (Fu¨ rstenau et al. 2019), which is akin to being vital as “air”: a necessity without which organizations could barely function. This arrangement positions IT units to follow well-established routines and structures focused on maintaining stability, ensuring operational excellence and the reliability of the organization’s IT infrastructure (Recker 2014, Fu¨ rstenau et al. 2019). This emphasis on stability has significant implications for IT units’ practices, as there is a constant concern that interruptions could lead to substantial negative impacts on organizational performance (Kumar 2004). With this positioning, the dominant routine rationale of IT logic is ensuring high quality and stable delivery of technology services to enable all other units to fulfill their functions (Kohli and Grover 2008, Peppard 2018). Hence, we surmise the following.

Synthesis 2. Digital logic’s routine rationale is enterprising, whereas IT logic’s routine rationale is stabilizing.

2.2.3. Technology Rationale. The technology rationale refers to the predominant view of the role of technology within digital and IT logics. Our review of prior literature indicates that digital’s technology rationale views technology predominantly as an outcome. In contrast, IT’s technology rationale views technology predominantly as an enabler. In digital logic, technology is regarded as an essential component of the final output of digital units or initiatives, such as creating digital offerings. Prior literature on IT logic’ positions technology as an enabler, meaning it is viewed as an infrastructure to be utilized as a means to an end (Markus and Benjamin 1997, Orlikowski and Iacono 2001, Markus 2004).

Digital logic’s technology rationale views technology as more than just a tool (Yoo et al. 2010, Faulkner and Runde 2019, Baskerville et al. 2020). This rationale reflects the view that technology is a central component in the creation of digital offerings. In this context, the key characteristics of digital technologies—such as reprogrammability, data homogenization, generativity, and self-referential capabilities—are leveraged to enable new and often unexpected technological innovations (Henfridsson and Bygstad 2013, Svahn et al. 2017, Ross et al. 2019). In other words, technology is not just a means to an end; it is about using technology to create new technological innovations. Thus, the technology rationale in digital logic represents a rethinking of how technology can generate value in both existing and new business offerings (Lyytinen et al. 2016; Nambisan et al. 2017, 2019).

In IT logic, prior literature describes the technology rationale as how an organization utilizes IT artifacts (e.g., software, hardware) to fulfill its business and operational mandates. This view of technology as an enabler can be traced back to the early days of IT in organizations. Most IT conceptions originate from viewing technology as a tool and from the persistent dependence on legacy IT systems (Bharadwaj 2000, Mitra 2005, Boh and Yellin 2006). This applies particularly to large incumbent organizations with a complex IT landscape. The literature on IT infrastructure emphasizes how the standards and policies governing IT in organizations are primarily focused on facilitating and supporting business requirements (Ciborra et al. 2000, Edwards et al. 2009, Tilson et al. 2010). The way various IT applications and subsystems are structured and interact to support current and future business processes illustrates how IT is relied on to meet business requirements and organizational needs (Weill et al. 2002, Kruchten et al. 2006, Weill and Vitale 2008). Hence, we surmise the following.

Synthesis 3. Digital logic’s technology rationale views technology primarily as an outcome, whereas IT logic’s technology rationale views technology primarily as an enabler.<sup>1</sup>

## 2.3. Synthesizing a Conceptual Framework

To investigate the relationship between digital and IT, we develop a conceptual framework (Figure 1) to analyze our empirical data. To uncover their relationships, we zoom in on interactions between digital and IT units within organizations at the empirical level. This connection between interactions and relationships is well established in the existing IS literature, where short-term, context-bound interactions (e.g., between actors or between actors and IT artifacts) reflect enduring relationships between those entities (Kern and Willcocks 2002, Al-Natour and Benbasat 2009).

In their interactions, digital and IT units enact their respective organizing logics; that is, they draw on their underlying assumptions (rationales) about strategy, routine, and technology, which determine how the actors (e.g., managers and employees) in these units think and act (Sambamurthy and Zmud 2000, Schwarz and Hirschheim 2003). For example, IT units’ perception of technology as an enabler informs their interactions with digital units, which approach these same interactions based on their perception of technology as an outcome (a key assumption of the technology rationale; Table 2). In this sense, the units interact across the different rationales that underpin their organizing logics. Thus, we can study the interactions between digital and IT units by drawing on the synthesized rationales that underpin their respective organizing logics (analytical level). Subsequently, the relationships we derive between digital and IT (conceptual level), based on interactions between digital and IT units at the empirical level, reflect digital and IT logics.

To theorize the relationships between digital and IT, we must first unpack the interactions between digital and IT units. To this end, we investigate why these interactions emerge and the dynamics they exhibit. Subsequently, we use these insights to abstract key characteristics of the relationships between digital and IT, such as the relationships’ antecedents (why relationships emerge), their dynamics, and the conditions that explain why these dynamics occur. Note that here, dynamics refer to the different characteristics that interactions between digital and IT units can take in specific situations—and, by extension, the relationships that these interactions reflect. This means, in one instance, interactions between digital and IT units can manifest in one dynamic, while in another, they exhibit a different dynamic. Although interactions can reflect the same relationship, in different situations, they can take different dynamics, which depends on specific conditions. Thus, relationships are characterized at any given point in time by a specific dynamic. Hence, this study focuses on conceptualizing the interactions between digital and IT units (represented by the dashed arrow) as relationships between digital and IT (represented by the dashed box) based on the rationales that underpin their organizing logic.

## 3. Research Approach

To unpack the relationships between digital and IT, we adopt a multiple case study design based on ethnographic data collected from three single case studies (Yin 2003). Although each of these cases presents a valuable empirical footing for studying interactions between digital and IT units, we combine their insights for a cross-case analysis to theorize interactions across all three cases, thus going beyond each case’s idiosyncrasies (Leonard-Barton 1990, Lee and Baskerville 2003).

## 3.1. Case Selection

When we began this research project, three of the authors had already spent more than one year in the field studying how the selected organizations, which we named Auto, Bank, and Engr (pseudonyms), were undergoing DT. Although our individual studies initially focused on the digital units of the three organizations, we observed that these units regularly interacted with the IT units regardless of how they were embedded in the organizational structure (see Appendix A, Figure A.1). These observations provided an essential foundation and impetus for further investigation of interactions between digital and IT units. Combining the three cases allowed us to study their commonalities while also considering their inherent differences. The structural embedding of the digital and IT units in these organizations (see Appendix A, Figure A.1) present a theoretical difference that ensures that our findings are not attributed to idiosyncratic case characteristics (Siggelkow 2007) but reflect a variety of contexts that strengthen our theoretical abstractions (Lee and Baskerville 2003, 2012; Yin 2003).

Auto is a European car manufacturer that produces and sells passenger and commercial vehicles. As part of launching its DT, Auto founded a central digital unit intended to transform its existing business units (e.g., HR, production, and IT). The central digital unit’s goal is to drive the DT of the company’s value offerings and internal business processes. In addition, Auto established multiple decentralized digital units within its business units. Our study of Auto focused on the transformation activities of the central and decentralized digital units, as well as their interactions with the company’s IT unit. Auto’s IT unit acts as an internal service provider to business units. It handles change requests for enterprise IS, operates the company’s IT infrastructure, and liaises with external service providers that manage parts of this infrastructure, for example, hardware and software deployment.

Bank is a leading financial institution in Northern Europe, offering an extensive portfolio of financial services. The study of Bank focuses on investigating a digital unit established within the institution’s existing organizational structure in response to changing competitive dynamics and customer demands. The digital unit’s objective is to develop a new digital payment platform and contribute to the bank’s overall DT. The digital unit, which consists mainly of business analysts, UX designers, and data analysts, established close interactions with Bank’s IT unit early on. The initial prototype of the digital payment platform was created through a close, ad hoc collaboration between the digital unit’s employees and a few members of the IT unit. The success of the payment platform subsequently led to the formation of a dedicated IT unit within the overall structure of Bank’s IT group. The main purpose of this IT unit is to support the digital unit’s activities.

Engr is a Northern European engineering company that established a digital unit as part of its new organizational structure. The unit’s objective is to develop digital services offered alongside Engr’s traditional machine products. As part of the restructuring that accompanied the establishment of the digital unit, Engr integrated the existing IT unit into the new digital unit. This structure implies that the CIO reports to the CDO, who is part of the top management team. The Engr study captured the activities of the digital unit and its interactions with IT and business units. Because the digital unit was given the dual mandate of (a) generating innovations and profit and (b) infusing digital initiatives within business units, the IT unit became pivotal in fulfilling the second mandate. Additionally, the IT unit was responsible for providing infrastructural and service support to the entire organization, including the digital unit. ## 3.2. Data Collection

Our data collection was based on fieldwork conducted by three authors, with one author present at each of the three organizations. We gained access to the three organizations through research and project contracts, which allowed us to collect data about the composition and functions of the digital and IT units as members of the organizations. Additionally, we collected data on observations and events in other units that were relevant to our research. The data collection process followed an ethnographic approach, which involved participant observations (Myers 2009b, Ingold 2014), documented via field notes (Emerson et al. 2001, Van Maanen 2011), semistructured interviews, and comprehensive archival records (e.g., internal and external news, presentations, documents, emails, and posts on enterprise social media platforms and corporate websites) (Pettigrew 1990, Linders 2008). By immersing ourselves in the field, we observed the interactions between the digital and IT units in situ (Myers 2009a, Van Maanen 2011). This approach allowed us to exam ine not only what people say but also what they do. All researchers were regularly present in the field (ranging from daily to several days a week), and they also participated remotely at different times. Thus, our access to the organizations was as insiders with the privilege to collect firsthand observations on actions and events as they took place. Table 4 outlines our methodological considerations during data collection.

Through our firsthand field experience, we drew on the strength of ethnographic fieldwork: the richness of thick descriptions. However, field immersion also meant that, at times, the researchers participated in interactions between digital and IT units (Ingold 2014). Although this immersion is central to ethnographic fieldwork (Pettigrew 1990, Myers 2009a, Van Maanen 2011), it carries the risk of losing analytical distance and impartiality, as well as research focus. Thus, it requires continuous reflection on the researchers’ role in the field and their stance on the observed phenomenon. To mitigate these risks, we continuously challenged our emerging interpretations by discussing our work as a team during data analysis (Silverman 2014). During these discussions, one author, who was not immersed in the field, assumed a reflective, observational role (Pratt et al. 2019). As an outsider to the three cases, this author offered the most unbiased analysis of the commonalities and differences across the cases. In addition to these collective reflections, each field-based author individually considered the impact of their immersion on their research (see Appendix B, Table B.1).

## 3.3. Data Analysis

We analyzed the collected data using a four-step process (Figure 2). First, we formulated thick descriptions of Auto, Bank, and Engr to establish a shared understanding of the three case organizations. These descriptions captured the authors’ field experiences, focusing on the organizations’ DT processes, their efforts to establish digital units, and the extent to which these digital units engaged with IT units. To ensure comparability between these descriptions (Pratt et al. 2019), we defined—a priori—a set of aspects that they should encompass, based on our research objective and a preliminary literature review. These aspects included general characteristics of the case organizations (e.g., number of employees, industry, and DT process) and characteristics of their digital and IT units, such as their governance structure (e.g., reporting lines and ties to business units), tasks and responsibilities, performance measures, and involvement in digital innovation. This first step resulted in thick descriptions of the digital and IT units in the three organizations, ensuring that the cases were comparable.

Second, when tracing interactions between digital and IT units, we found that the units interacted regularly, indicating ongoing relationships. Upon scrutinizing these interactions, we investigated how the digital and IT units drew on the three rationales of their respective organizing logics during their interactions. To do this, we focused on specific interactions between digital and IT units identified in our data and analyzed which organizing logics’ rationale (i.e., strategy, routine, or technology) the units enacted in these interactions. We found that each interaction predominantly reflected one of the three rationales. For example, when driven by the strategy rationale, the two units focused on securing each other’s support to accomplish their respective strategic objectives. From these empirical insights (Figure 1), we conceptualized three relationships between digital and IT at the conceptual level. Thus, the interactions between digital and IT units reflect different relationships between digital and IT (for more details, see Appendix C, Table C.1 and Table C.2).

Third, upon further examination of our data, we noticed that the same relationship could manifest in various ways during different interactions. For example, in some interactions that reflect the interdepending relationship, the units teamed up to accomplish shared objectives, whereas in others, one unit acted uncooperatively, hindering the other from achieving its objectives. We refer to the first as a synergistic dynamic and to the second as a conflicting dynamic.

Fourth, we explored why these relationships emerge by examining why and when digital and IT units interact. To this end, we investigated the antecedents of each relationship and the conditions that shaped their dynamics. We analyzed our data for antecedents by asking: why do interactions between digital and IT units occur and why do particular relationships exist? Additionally, we sought to uncover the conditions for each dynamic by asking: why and when do relationships manifest in specific dynamics? Thus, we investigated why the two units engaged in interactions reflecting interdepending, interfacing, or interlocking relationships (Appendix C, Table C.1). Subsequently, we conceptualized the conditions that explain why a relationship manifests in a specific dynamic (Appendix C, Table C.2).

To present evidence from the three case organizations, we adopt vignettes as an analytical tool (Van Maanen 2011, Jarzabkowski et al. 2014). Vignettes “are vivid portrayals of specific incidents—such as a conversation, a critical event or moment in the field, or particular practices or routines—that illuminate a theoretical concept the author wishes to convey” (Jarzabkowski et al. 2014, p. 280). Vignettes can present a composite narrative, combining several distinct observations into one vignette, or a processual narrative, following specific actors across locations and time to provide a coherent account of a phenomenon (Jarzabkowski et al. 2014). To present our vignettes, we use both composite and processual narratives. In these vignettes, and throughout this paper, we use pseudonyms for the three case organizations and depicted informants.

## 4. Findings

In this section, we outline the three relationships between digital and IT: interdepending, interlocking, and interfacing. We identify these relationships and their characteristics by investigating interactions between digital and IT units in the three case organizations (Table 5). Below, we outline these relationships and their antecedents, as well as their dynamics and the conditions that shape them.

## 4.1. Interdepending Relationship

We observe an interdepending relationship when digital and IT units rely on each other to achieve their strategic objectives. Digital units with the objective to focus on developing new offerings and value propositions rely on IT units to support these objectives by providing and maintaining the necessary IT infrastructure. Similarly, to accomplish their strategic objective of aligning IT opera tions to support business and wider organizational needs, IT units must understand the digital units’ strategic objectives to plan accordingly. Next, we present the antecedents and dynamics of this relationship.

4.1.1. Antecedent. The interdepending relationship emerges from the need for digital and IT units to interact to fulfill their strategic objectives. In the case of Engr, the IT unit required an Internet of Things (IoT) platform as a key resource to fulfill its objective of providing diverse data to meet organizational demands. However, senior managers consistently rejected requests to invest in developing such a platform. When Engr decided to establish a digital unit, this unit had the strategic objective of creating IoT-driven digi tal products. As their strategic objectives aligned—the need to develop an IoT platform was relevant for both units—the digital unit supported the IT unit’s request until it was finally granted as indicated in the following statement:

“[ … ] so, you saw that we have the IoT and digitalization already mentioned here [points to a record from 2015 on a slide], [ … ] Management team as such did not follow any of these through. [ … ] that’s why these things are stuck somewhere in limbo, like our IoT. However, the IT unit’s abandoned IoT proposal was revived after the digital unit made a case for IoT over a year later” (Engr, Interview and Observations).

To meet its objectives, the digital unit also relied on the IT unit to provide enabling technologies to support its goals:

“We [the IT unit] are trying to support the new [digital] business [ … ]. We are providing business enablers and the [technical] capabilities, [with which] we can then realize these [intended] services. With technical enablers, what I have in mind, are all the digital platforms, technologies, software applications, technical platforms on top of which you can build digital services” (Engr, Interview).

4.1.2. Dynamics. The interdepending relationship between digital and IT units can manifest in two dynamics: synergistic and conflicting. We observed that the compatibility between the units’ need to interact to fulfill their strategic objectives and the underlying strategy rationales with which they typically approached such interactions served as a condition that determined whether the interdepending relationship manifested in a synergistic or conflicting dynamic. This compatibility is exhibited when, during their interactions, both units can achieve their strategic objectives, even if their underlying strategy rationales, on which they draw during these interactions, differ (business-centric versus business-aligned; Table 2). When compatible, these interactions present a synergistic dynamic. When incompatible—for example, when the rationales that inform their interactions only support one unit’s objectives, whereas hindering the other—the units interactions take on a conflicting dynamic. Thus, interactions between digital and IT units exhibit different dynamics, depending on whether, during these interactions, they find compatibility or incompatibility between the need to fulfill their strategic objectives and the underlying strategy rationales that they draw on. This condition shapes why the two dynamics manifest differently in specific interactions. Vignettes 1–4 illustrate these dynamics and the compatibility or incompatibility conditions that shape them (for further evidence, see the Online Appendix, Table 2).

4.1.2.1. Synergistic Dynamic. Despite their different strategic objectives (as captured in their strategy rationales), digital and IT units can exhibit a synergistic dynamic when the compatibility condition is met. This dynamic is evident at Engr, where the digital and IT units unite to promote the use of IoT technology.

An IoT initiative by the CIO that had been ignored for years was revived with the support of the new CDO, gaining management’s backing. As a result, a synergy was born, and IoT was elevated to a strategic objective jointly pursued by both units: “For example, now we [the IT unit] are making that gateway for our IoT solutions, which is implemented by the [digital] team. [ … . In] one meeting with the vice presidents [with the IT team and the CDO present], it was decided that the [IoT] gateway must be done right now because a unit had a business case and some event was coming where they wanted to show and [tell] the customers [about it]” (Engr, Interview).

Next, we draw on Vignette 1 from Auto and Vignette 2 from Bank to further illustrate the synergistic dynamic and the condition that shapes it.

## Vignette 1. Synergistic Dynamic at Auto

Auto’s DT journey involved transforming from a car manufacturer into a mobility service provider. To facili tate this transformation, Auto established several digital units, one of which focused on the business of vans. The unit developed digital solutions to extend the service offerings around the company’s vans portfolio. One of these solutions checked a craftsman’s van inventory of tools and spare parts against the upcoming customer appointments. If spare parts or required tools were missing, the solution informed the craftsmen about these missing parts and the necessity of returning to the warehouse. “This service is really something. We intended to flip the whole thing. Instead of craftsmen driving to the warehouse in the morning, the warehouse would deliver the missing [parts] during the night, so craftsmen could drive straight to the customer,” Mark, who headed the digital unit at Vans, explained. This solution required the integration of a new IT module that the digital unit had created into the existing vehicle’s IT architecture. “This module includes various sensors, like GPS to locate the vehicle, and communication devices to send and receive information on the next service routes and a van’s inventory of spare parts. [It] connects the vans to a fleet; it makes them a node in a web of vans, spare parts, tools and customer appointments,” Mark described. However, the vehicle’s IT architecture—the set of embedded systems in the vans—was not the responsibility of the digital unit. As the IT unit oversaw its operations, digital and IT units had to collaborate. Former IT employees who now worked within the digital unit contacted their peers in the IT unit to request the module’s integration. They set up a meeting to discuss the specifics, and afterward, the IT unit integrated the module, recognizing that thi would support Auto’s digital business needs. Mark explained, “[Fortunately], we had hired people who knew the IT guys. Knowing whom to talk to makes these things a lot easier, and eventually, they [IT unit] supported us in integrating the module smoothly into the existing infrastructure.

Vignette 1 illustrates the synergistic dynamic between the digital and IT units as part of their interdepending relationship. The digital unit required the IT unit’s support to integrate the module they developed into the existing IT infrastructure. Integrating the module fell within the IT unit’s strategic objective of aligning with the organization’s business needs, while allowing the digital unit to accomplish its own objective of developing digital offerings. Thus, Vignette 1 highlights how the units’ need to interact to accomplish their objectives is compatible with their respective strategy rationales. In this case, the two units’ objectives can be achieved simultaneously, without infringing on one another.

## Vignette 2. Synergistic Dynamic at Bank

One of Bank’s digital units aimed to develop a customer-centric, easy-to-use, and reliable digital payment platform in the form of a mobile application. To support the digital unit, a special IT unit was created as part of Bank’s IT Group, consisting of several developers. The IT unit operated in a separate location from the digital unit and followed the strategic objectives, processes, and routines established within Bank’s IT group. These included business alignment as a key strategic objective, task prioritiza tion, reporting structure, budget allocation, etc. The purpose of the IT unit was to “deliver project deliverables on schedule with agreed scope and quality” and to “ensure cost effective and short time to marke mobile application development.” Although most employees in the digital unit lacked software development expertise, some had enough technical knowledge from their prior experience at Bank to enter into a dialogue with the IT unit on how best to develop IT systems to enable the design and delivery of the digital payment platform. Similarly, select employees at the IT unit were also interested in proposing suggestions for developing front-end digital services for the digital payment platform, which centered around different technologies that the IT unit investigated, such as QR codes, Near Field Communication, and Bluetooth. Such dialogue initiatives occurred both formally and informally. Senior IT managers regularly reported to the digital unit’s senior managers to ensure synergy between the strategic objectives of both units. Additionally, select employees from the IT unit presented some of their ongoing projects to the digital unit during ad hoc events. These events led to informal conversations between IT and digital uni employees, allowing them to coordinate their tasks with the strategic objectives of each unit.

Vignette 2 illustrates the synergistic dynamic between the digital and IT units at Bank, where the IT unit aligned with the digital unit’s business objective to support the delivery of digital offerings quickly (i.e., short time-to-market). Through formal and informal interactions, the IT unit delivered high-quality offerings in a cost-effective manner. Thus, although the digital unit pursued a business-centric strategy rationale and the IT unit followed a business-alignment strategy rationale, each unit’s objectives were achieved without compromising the other unit’s objectives.

4.1.2.2. Conflicting Dynamic. Interactions between digital and IT units can exhibit a conflicting dynamic when there is incompatibility between their needs to interact to achieve specific strategic objectives and their organizing logics’ strategy rationales (businesscentric versus business-aligned). We observed that IT units could not always support digital units in certain situations, particularly when achieving the digital units’ objectives is not aligned with the IT units’ objectives. For instance, although IT units’ objective of being business-aligned implied supporting the digital unit, it also involved operating the IT infrastructure reliably and supporting business units. Depending on the context of a specific interaction, IT units can prioritize infrastructure stability over supporting digital units innovative offerings if the latter compromises the IT infrastructure. Therefore, the incompatibility between the needs of the units to achieve certain strategic objectives and their underlying strategy rationales can lead to a conflicting dynamic.

At Auto, for example, the IT unit had to balance supporting the digital unit’s demands while meeting its own objectives, all within a strict IT budget. This resulted in the digital unit’s objectives being incompatible with the IT unit’s strategy rationale in certain interactions:

“Outside the IT unit, everyone seems to think we have unlimited resources, so we could simply provide all the IT equipment and infrastructure demanded. If we deny these demands, we become the nay-sayers— the ones who slow things down or stump ideas and requests that would move the digital strategy forward— but in reality, our hands are tied. While our IT management urged us to accommodate these demands for digital, they also cut our IT budget [because] cost-efficiency is key” (Auto, Interview)

Vignette 3 from Engr and Vignette 4 from Bank further illustrate the conflicting dynamic of the interdepending relationship.

## Vignette 3. Conflicting Dynamic at Engr

With the strategic objective of developing new digital offerings in mind, Oliver, a manager in the digital unit at Engr, requested changes to the ERP system to enable receiving data in specific formats. However, due to the IT unit’s strategic objective of ensuring IT infrastructure stability, it had an established procedure for making changes to the ERP system, which could delay implementation by months. Specifically, the IT unit had to contact the ERP vendor, and only after several preplanned steps were met could the vendor implement the requested changes. These changes were then carefully tested before being deployed and integrated as an approved functional module into the ERP system by dedicated super users, predefined by the IT unit. Although this slow, risk-averse process frustrated the digital unit, the IT unit was unwilling to jeopardize its established process to accommodate requests that were deemed incompatible with its strategic objectives. For example, Susan, an employee in the digital unit, described the challenge of getting the right data to pursue her unit’s goals: “Because, like I said, [with our] ERP, we are [in a] situation where it has been [changed in a] certain way. When it has been changed, it [was not] based on business [needs … ]. Now, the same data are updated in many ways, and that is not good because you cannot [do a] search. If I need to find all the customers, who [have] a certain kind of product … or combination of things [in order] to offer them something, I cannot use the data.” Anna, the head of the IT unit in Engr, commented on the improbability of changing their ERP at the pace the digital unit required: “[ … ] at the moment, there’s no chance in [expletive] hell that we could change our ERP vendor fast and agile! It would not happen.”

Vignette 3 illustrates the conflicting dynamic stemming from the incompatibility between the need for the digital and IT units to achieve their strategic objectives and their underlying strategy rationales. Although the digital unit required rapid integration of its digital solution into Engr’s IT infrastructure (i.e., ERP system), the IT unit argued that it could not meet the requested timeline due to the risk of compromising the ERP system’s stability, which was essential for achieving its strategic objective. Thus, in this vignette, the IT unit prioritized the stability of the ERP system, which was critical to Engr’s business operations (business-aligned), over supporting the digital unit’s objective of quickly delivering digital offerings (business-centric). Additionally, when comparing Vignette 1 and Vignette 3, we observed that in Vignette 1, the compatibility between the need to achieve the strategic objectives of both units and their respective strategy rationales allowed for a synergistic dynamic with which the IT unit willingly supported the integration of a new technological artifact. In contrast, in Vignette 3, the IT unit considered the digital unit’s pursuits incompatible as they required rapidly modifying an existing technological artifact that was central to the company’s entire business operations (i.e., the ERP system). This change posed a risk to the stability of the IT infrastructure, which in turn threatened the IT unit’s ability to support the business—its key strategic objective, thus resulting in a conflicting dynamic. Vignette 3 demonstrates that this dynamic stems from the incompatibility between the need of the two units to interact to achieve their strategic objectives and the underlying strategy rationales that they draw from.

## Vignette 4. Conflicting Dynamic at Bank

At Bank, while the dedicated IT unit was enthusiastic about the strategic initiatives proposed by the digital unit and eager to assist, the digital unit’s employees often felt that the IT unit could not provide adequate support. To develop new digital offerings, which was the digital unit’s main strategic objective, employees required advanced IT capabilities, including state-ofthe-art APIs to connect to third parties, advanced data analytics, machine learning tools, and cloud computing—capabilities the IT unit did not possess. When employees asked the IT unit for help in developing novel digital offerings using advanced technologies, the IT unit often responded that such projects were not feasible or would take too much time. Frustrated, the digital unit’s employees claimed these were excuses, asserting that the IT unit lacked the necessary capabilities in-house and was unwilling to hire people to fill the skill gap. During a digital unit’s department meeting, one of the long-term employees, Jane, openly expressed frustration that the IT unit rejected certain projects due to a lack of skills and tools and because they were not agile enough to match the fast development cycles needed for digital offerings. Meanwhile, the IT unit indicated that requests from the digital unit to develop in-house capabilities for specific digital technologies should not be prioritized, as their focus was on maintaining the stable operation of the underlying IT infrastructure, where their employees’ main expertise lay. Additionally, hiring specialists with expertise in advanced digital technologies solely to support the development of digital offerings would reduce the budget for hiring specialists needed to maintain the stability of the IT infrastructure.

Vignette 4 captures a conflicting dynamic in the interactions between the digital and IT units at Bank. When the digital unit required specific IT capabilities to support the development of digital offerings, which constituted its main strategic objective, the IT unit prioritized enhancing its existing capabilities to ensure the stability of the organization’s underlying IT infrastructure, which was essential for supporting broader organizational needs. The IT unit believed that diverting resources toward the digital unit’s requests for advanced IT capa bilities, rather than strengthening its current capabilities, might jeopardize their capacity to maintain the stability of the IT infrastructure and conflict with its strategic objective. The IT unit further believed that its existing capabilities could sufficiently support the innovation demands of the digital unit in line with its own strategic objective of maintaining business alignment. However, these capabilities fell short of meeting the digital unit’s needs, which required expertise in advanced digital technologies to develop innovative offerings. Vignette 4 thus demonstrates how interactions between the digital and IT units can take a conflicting dynamic due to the incompatibility between the units’ need to achieve their strategic objectives and their underlying strategy rationales.

## 4.2. Interlocking Relationship

We identify an interlocking relationship when digital and IT units link their routines, which can result in those routines mutually influencing each other. Typically, this interlocking occurs as the two units explore possibilities to benefit from collaborating across routines. Such interactions may be initiated to encourage employees from both units to come together, learn about each other’s routines, and potentially integrate some of these routines into their own. Alternatively, these interactions may be driven by the necessary linkages in the organization’s operational processes. Interactions that involve linking routines can be formalized, such as creating a joint hub where employees from both units interact regularly (e.g., Engr structure), or ad hoc, such as organizing joint ideation events, workshops, or even unplanned exchanges (e.g., Vignette 5 from Bank). In what follows, we present this relationship’s antecedents and dynamics.

4.2.1. Antecedents. The interlocking relationship emerges from the need to link the routines of the digital and IT units to carry out specific activities. Although the digital unit’s enterprising routines may appear to clash with the IT unit’s stabilizing routines, we identified numerous interactions in which the digital units sought to link their routines with those of the IT unit’s, valuing the stability these routines provided for their operations. Conversely, the IT units also valued the enterprising nature of the digital units’ routines and occasionally adjusted their own routines to incorporate enterprising elements. Therefore, the routines of both units do not exist in isolation; instead, they mutually influence each other. Thus, the antecedent for the interlocking relationship reflects an effort to link the units routines, despite differences in their organizing logics.

For example, at Bank, the digital and IT units organized joint workshops to discuss and codevelop innovative ideas, focusing on their scope and feasibility in relation to the existing IT infrastructure maintained by the IT unit (Bank, Observations). These workshops also allowed IT employees to present their innovative ideas for functionalities and further refine them with input from the digital unit, thereby adopting enterprising routines, such as rapid prototyping. Similarly, at Auto, the IT unit assigned employees to work alongside digital units. The idea was that by establishing shared organizational arrangements, employees could interact directly rather than relying on default IT support services:

“The Digital House presents a joint effort between the digital folks in marketing and the IT unit. The goal was to bring them together under one roof so they can draw on each other’s expertise and ways of doing things when developing digital solutions, or simply to be in the same boat from the start rather than coming to the party late” (Auto, Interview).

Similarly, at Engr, the digital and IT units exhibited an interlocking relationship when the IT unit outsourced its primary stability-focused routines. This allowed the IT unit to concentrate on collaborating with the digital unit to create solutions for client problems. This change was reflected in the unit’s renaming, highlighting its focus on solution development:

“We [the IT unit] partner with [digital] business to develop business capabilities, we deliver solutions [ … ]. I like to think that there is no IT anymore—the normal ICT department. [ … ] And that’s why [we have] the new name, the ICSS—the Information and Communication Services and Solutions” (Engr, Interview).

Thus, the interlocking relationship emerges because both units need to link their routines in pursuit of common benefits. For more evidence of the interlocking relationship’s antecedent across the three cases, see the Online Appendix, Table 3.

4.2.2. Dynamics. Depending on the compatibility between the need for digital and IT units to link their routines and their underlying routine rationales, the interlocking relationship can manifest in either con verging or diverging dynamics. Our data show that, although the routine rationales of the two units are different (enterprising versus stabilizing; Table 2), in certain situations, they can be compatible with the units needs to link their routines in pursuit of operational activities. When such compatibility exists, the interac tions between the digital and IT units exhibit a converging dynamic. In situations where the units’ needs to link their routines are incompatible with their routine rationales, their interactions take a diverging dynamic. Vignettes 5–9 illustrate the two dynamics of the interlocking relationship and the conditions that shape them. For more empirical evidence, see the Online Appendix, Table 4.

4.2.2.1. Converging Dynamic. When the digital and IT units’ need to link their routines is compatible with their underlying routine rationale, we observe a converging dynamic. This means that over time, each unit incorporates routines—or elements of routines—from the other. For example, the digital unit may realize that its digital offerings require ongoing operational maintenance and strategic development, such as updating the user interface, launching additional functionalities, and preventing IT outages. This necessitates further engagement with the IT unit, during which the digital unit adopts routines that promote stability, a hallmark of the IT unit. Similarly, the IT unit’s routines can evolve to incorporate elements of the digital unit’s routines as a result of these interactions. For example, they may adopt business discovery processes and an agile mindset to develop and deliver IT systems or functionalities more quickly. However, even as their routines converge over time, the digital and IT units retain the core assumptions of their routine rationales. For example, although the digital units considered how to ensure the continuous operation of their offerings (stabilizing routines), they continued experimenting to discover novel ideas (enterprising routines). Similarly, the IT units accommodated enterprising routines while still focusing on operational excellence through stabilizing routines.

At Auto, for example, the digital unit developed digital services to provide employees with an improved workplace experience. However, after launching several HR digital initiatives, the digital unit realized that its ability to quickly release new services (enterprising routines) was constrained by legacy IT systems. They needed to work with the IT unit to set up a structured planning process for redesigning the overall IT landscape. Consequently, they shifted their focus from pushing small-scale digital initiatives to planning the redesign of the IT landscape (stabilizing routines). For this, they collaborated with the IT unit, which governed the existing IT landscape, to plan large-scale technology renewals of Auto’s IT infrastructure:

“At the start, we [the digital unit] thought, ‘cool, let’s develop some new apps, etc.’ We focused on trying new things, organizing workshops, and setting up projects. When we realized we didn’t need apps but [rather] a plan and roadmap for how we can change our IT landscape, we contacted IT. We brought in their planning skills to draft the future IT landscape at [Auto]” (Auto, Interview and Observation).

We illustrate the converging dynamic in Vignette 5 from Bank and Vignette 6 from Engr.

## Vignette 5. Converging Dynamic at Bank

Following the successful launch of its digital payment platform, the digital unit at Bank continued to rapidly develop novel digital offerings. In just a few months, the unit released various new functionalities, includ ing in-app payments, online payments, loyalty offerings, and an expense-sharing app. As the platform grew in users, the digital unit shifted its focus from constantly developing and adding new functionalities to ensuring that the newly released features operated smoothly. One of the unit’s employees, Adam, who developed several of the most successful functionali ties on the digital payment platform, often stated in meetings that the digital unit “launch[es] innovative offerings and runs [away from them, without having the time to track their performance and ensure that they operated as intended].” Thus, overseeing the performance of newly released functionalities was crucial to ensuring their long-term operational excellence. This issue was also raised during a quarterly workshop in which employees from both the IT and digital units discussed how to work more productively. After hearing the digital unit’s frustrations, the IT unit employees shared their reflections, drawing on their experience with maintaining and operating IT systems. As a result of these exchanges, the digital unit appointed a dedicated product owner for each of the launched innovative offerings, responsible for ensuring stable day-to-day operations and developing strategies for future improvement. This role was inspired by the IT application owners in the IT unit, whose responsibility was to maintain the stability of an IT application and plan its future development. The digital unit also adopted various routines associ ated with IT change and release management, particularly in planning and prioritizing the release of upcoming functionalities. Additionally, because most of the innovative functionalities were launched hastily to meet market demands, they often suffered from built-in IT inefficiencies. To address this, the product owners collaborated with the IT unit employees to identify the source of the inefficiencies and potential solutions. In an informal conversation, Mike, a prod uct owner in the digital unit, shared that he considered the employees from the IT unit, who worked in a different location and with whom he communicated online on a weekly basis, as “his colleagues on par with his colleagues from the digital unit.”

Vignette 5 illustrates that the digital unit at Bank adopted stabilizing routines, such as appointing product owners for launched functionalities and creating a robust functionality release map, which were predominantly associated with the IT unit and aimed at ensuring operational excellence. Although the digital unit relied on enterprising routines to deliver innovative digital offerings, its employees realized that, after launch, these offerings needed to operate efficiently. After familiarizing with the IT unit’s stabilizing routines during a dedicated workshop aimed at linking the two units’ routines, the digital unit realized that some of these stabilizing routines were compatible with its goal of ensuring the efficient operation of released innovative offerings. The digital unit continued exploring new digital functionalities through enterprising routines while ensuring the stable operation of released offerings by incorporating stabilizing routines. Thus, Vignette 5 demonstrates the converg ing dynamic between the two units, as one unit incorporates elements of the other’s routines due to the compatibility between the units’ need to link their routines to achieve operational goals and their respective routine rationales.

## Vignette 6. Converging Dynamic at Engr

Anna, the head of the IT unit at Engr, commented on how the IT unit was shifting its focus from simply being in a support role to creating solutions alongside the digital unit. She shared, “I had hired Mr. Smith to start working on IoT the previous summer $[ \dots ] \ S \mathrm { o } ,$ this was the first step toward the situation that we could start developing something of our own instead of just supporting [ … ] [which] was the situation when I came into Engr. Vignette 6 illustrates how interactions between the digital and IT units can manifest in a converging dynamic as one unit incorporates elements of the other’s routines. As the IT unit became embedded in the digital unit, it needed to rethink the relevance of its stabilizing routines to effectively support the digital unit’s goal of becoming a proactive partner. As a result, the IT unit shifted its focus from solely stabilizing routines (e.g., service desks) to adopting enterprising routines (e.g., developing innovative offerings). In this case, linkages between the digital and IT units’ routines enabled them to work together to provide solutions to Engr’s clients. This represented a shift for the IT unit toward incorporating elements of the digital unit’s routines. Thus, the interactions between the units manifested in a converging dynamic, driven by the compatibility between their need to link routines to achieve certain activities and their respective routine rationales.

4.2.2.2. Diverging Dynamic. When the need to link the digital and IT units’ routines to achieve specific tasks is incompatible with their routine rationales, their interactions result in a diverging dynamic. Instead of mutually influencing each other, the units routines develop in separate silos, with each unit focusing on either enterprising or stabilizing routines. For example, we observed that when the digital and IT units began linking their routines, they were initially eager to explore each other’s routines to identify mutual benefits. However, in certain interactions, the units found the linkages between their routines incompatible with their rationales. Linking the units’ routines can be counterproductive if it undermines their existing routines. For example, IT units incorporating enterprising routines may disrupt their stabilizing routines. Thus, the interlocking relationship between the digital and IT units exhibits a diverging dynamic. For example, at Engr, the head of the unit overseeing both digital and IT operations described the attempt to get both teams to adopt agile methods and the realization that followed:

“Agility [in the IT unit], at the moment, is on a level of a concrete pillar … [laughs.] Okay, that’s something we are working [on] at the moment. In a sense, in some places we are extremely agile, [like the digital unit]. But it does not go all through the organization, [like in the IT unit]. Because of the fear, you know, fear of [expletive]-up and things like that” (Engr, Interview).

Vignette 7 from Bank and Vignette 8 from Auto further illustrate the diverging dynamic.

## Vignette 7. Diverging Dynamic at Bank

At Bank, initially, select teams from the digital unit (the Concepts team) and the IT unit (the Front Runner team) decided to work closely together to “find ways to fail fast through the use of prototyping.” As a result, they met every two months to identify common projects. Several ideas were initially discussed, and early-stage mock-ups were created. Both teams valued the collaboration and actively contributed during the workshops. However, although many ideas were discussed, almost none were launched, leading to a stalemate in the col laboration. On one side, the IT team proposed ideas based on existing technologies, such as QR codes, that were unlikely to disrupt the IT infrastructure and focused on incremental improvements (e.g., launching gift cards, allowing users to store loyalty cards with merchants). Although these ideas were appreciated, the Concepts team found them insufficiently innovative due to their limited scope and reliance on simple technologies. On the other hand, the Concepts team struggled to evaluate the business potential of these innovations and their fit with the digital unit’s strategic objectives. This struggle stemmed from the team’s difficulty in designing a prioritization process to deter mine which ideas to launch first (stabilizing routine). Although a prioritization process was desired, the digital unit feared that formalizing it would limit their ability to innovate and react quickly to changing consumer demands and a competitive environment. After several workshops where ideas were discussed but not executed, the joint meetings became increasingly infrequent until they stopped altogether.

Vignette 7 demonstrates how the IT and digital units at Bank initially attempted to collaborate through joint workshops aimed at rapidly codeveloping innovative ideas. Initially, the units’ routines appeared to mutually influence each other as the IT team embraced rapid proto typing and a “fail fast” mindset, which conflicted with the IT unit’s stabilizing routines but were central for the digital unit’s enterprising routines. However, the units eventually realized that their routines were not aligned with what each of the other unit sought to achieve, and thus there was no benefit in linking them. As a result, they maintained their separate routines. The IT unit maintained its emphasis on stability (e.g., limited use of unfamiliar technologies) because fully adopting enterprising routines required a focus on continuously evolving offerings that conflicted with their stabilizing routines. Meanwhile, the digital unit accumulated numerous innovation ideas (enterprising routine) without a clear prioritization process (stabilizing routine). Introducing such a process was considered potentially impeding to the digital unit’s ability to innovate quickly, thereby undermining its enterprising routines. As a result, the units reverted to their typical routines, leading to a diverging dynamic.

## Vignette 8. Diverging Dynamic at Auto

Auto’s digital unit focused on identifying and imple menting new collaboration tools and organizing workshops and seminars to engage Auto employees with new ways of working. “[We] focused on the technical aspects and the human aspects. We were convinced that when we really want to break up the silos in our company, we have to provide our colleagues with the right tools and skills,” stated Kim, who worked at the digital unit. “The IT unit also worked on this,” she continued, “they had a team that focused on operating and maintaining the available collaboration tools.” This highlighted the difference between the digital unit and the IT unit. While the digital unit iden tified and tested new collaboration tools, the IT unit focused on their technical implementation and operation. Complementing each other, both units joined forces. The digital unit continued identifying new col laboration tools, while the IT unit handled their techni cal integration and operation. Over time, this increased number of available tools. “A ‘collaboration tools jungle’ emerged,” said Kim. “People kept asking us over and over again whether Auto had a tool they could use, let’s say, for project management. We figured we had to do something. We then created the Collaboration Tools Compass to provide employees with an overview of available tools per use case.” Employees adopted the Compass quickly. For example, they used it to ask for help with applications or to suggest addi tional tools. This created different reactions within the digital and IT units. While the digital unit was eager to explore these requests, the IT unit pushed back, insist ing that each tool must comply with IT security poli cies, which required thorough vetting. Additionally, licensing and budget considerations had to be clarified. As a result, they added a blacklist of tools to the Com pass. The two units’ routines regarding the use of the Compass began to diverge. While the IT unit initially supported the digital unit’s push for new collaboration tools, they later slowed the integration of new tools. Eventually, the digital unit handed the Compass over to the IT unit, which took over its maintenance.

Vignette 8 illustrates how the digital and IT units at Auto initially attempted to link their routines to expand the organization’s work infrastructure. The digital unit identified new collaboration tools, reflecting its enterprising routines. However, they needed to collaborate with the IT unit to integrate these tools into the IT infrastructure. Thus, the two units worked together to link their routines. The digital unit’s enterprising routines, focused on identifying new collaboration tools, linked with the IT unit’s stabilizing routines, focused on seamlessly integrating these tools into the existing infrastructure. However, the two units’ routines began diverging as the IT unit deemed some of the digital unit’s enterprising routines (e.g., ongoing addition of numerous collaboration tools) counterproductive for its own purposes (e.g., new tools had to undergo strict vetting to comply with IT policies, such as IT security, licensing, budgeting). As a result, their interaction manifested in a diverging dynamic due to the incompatibility between the units’ need to link their routines to achieve a specific purpose and their underlying routine rationales. Eventually, the digital unit handed the col laboration tool over to the IT unit.

## 4.3. Interfacing Relationship

We identify an interfacing relationship when, during their interactions, digital and IT units seek to engage with the same technology. The use of technology is central to the activities of both units. In these interactions, digital and IT units probe the same technology for possibilities to unlock value in accordance with their organizing logics’ technology rationale (outcome versus enabler; Table 2). In what follows, we present this relationship’s antecedents and dynamics.

4.3.1. Antecedent. The interfacing relationship emerges because of the need for digital and IT units to engage with the same technology. For example, the digital unit at Auto developed artificial intelligence (AI)- based solutions, which were at the heart of its digital customer services (e.g., ride-sharing services or intelligent routing of delivery vans). Likewise, the IT unit acknowledged that:

“[We use] artificial intelligence to increase transparency about dependencies of production factors. Furthermore, it enables us to plan even more precisely, which again increases the availability of our produc tion assets” (Auto, Archival Records).

Similarly, at Engr, the IT unit spearheaded the adoption of an IoT platform in the organization primarily to allow them to meet the digital unit and business units disparate data requests that their ERP system could not meet, which became clear after a series of repeated interactions with their ERP vendor (Engr, Observation). Engr’s digital unit also tinkered with IoT, as captured by this quote:

“[We also work] with IoT because many kinds of things can be created. It’s about connecting those devices and systems [sold to clients] and getting data out of those. And now we have selected new technol ogies for those remote connections, and those future connections will be true online connections, meaning [ … ] that we have online access to the data our systems are collecting. And it creates also new possibilities for teleservice and lots of possibilities for new services” (Engr, Interview and Observations).

At Bank, the migration to cloud computing, which was undertaken by the IT unit, benefited both the IT unit by potentially reducing IT crashes of the digital payment platform (Bank, Press release) and the digital unit by allowing for opening the platform to thirdparty developers who can easily create novel digital offerings, as one employee noted:

“Last year, we [the digital unit] were not even thinking of opening to third parties, now we are open about it. The change is because the technical platform has matured by moving toward the cloud-based environment” (Bank, Observations).

For additional examples of the antecedent of the interfacing relationship across the three cases, see the Online Appendix, Table 5.

4.3.2. Dynamics. The interfacing relationship can manifest in intersecting or parallel dynamics, depending on the compatibility between the digital and IT units’ need to engage with the same technology and their underlying technology rationales. An intersecting dynamic occurs when the units engage with the same technology artifact but use it differently, based on their technology rationales. As a result, the shared technology artifact serves as both an enabler (IT logic’s technology rationale) and an outcome (digital logic’s technology rationale). In contrast, we observe a parallel dynamic when digital and IT units engage with the same technology but opt to implement it using different technology artifacts. This parallel dynamic occurs when the two units find it counterproductive to share the same technology artifact. Thus, their need to engage with the same technology artifact is incompatible with their underlying technology rationales. Vignettes 9–12 illustrate the interfacing relationship’s dynamics and their conditions. For more empirical evidence, see the Online Appendix, Table 6.

4.3.2.1. Intersecting Dynamic. When digital and IT units use the same, shared technology artifact, albeit sometimes for different purposes, an intersecting dynamic occurs. Although their perceptions of technology differ due to their technology rationales (i.e., outcome versus enabler), their interactions can involve engagement with shared technology artifacts. Examples include IoT platforms, machine learning (ML) algorithms, and HR platforms, which are adapted to fit each unit’s needs. For example, at Bank, both the IT and digital units decided to build APIs on a shared digital payment platform. The IT unit used internal APIs to ensure segmentation and connectivity between various IT systems that were part of the payment platform. Meanwhile, the digital unit focused on external APIs that were added to the platform to enable cocreation activities with third parties (Observations). Vignette 9 from Engr and Vignette 10 from Auto further illustrate the intersecting dynamic.

## Vignette 9. Intersecting Dynamic at Engr

At Engr, Anna (the CIO), who leads the IT unit, and Andy (the CDO), who leads the digital unit, collaborated to establish a joint IoT platform in response to the digital unit’s need for diverse data beyond what the existing systems, such as ERP, could provide. However, the two units engaged with the same IoT platform for entirely different purposes. For the IT unit, it served as a data aggregator, gathering information from multiple systems to deliver more targeted operational data to various units within the organization. As one IT employee explained, “[With business] unit XYZ, we [i.e., IT] saw the possibility to do [the] same thing in IoT. The old solutions were not able to do the IoT things because it was [an] on-demand connection. The XYZ unit came to us [IT] and said, ‘ … we need the remote connection’.” In contrast, the digital unit used the IoT platform as a source for new heterogeneous data from IoT devices, which enabled the creation of new digital solutions and provided insights for developing new business models and value propositions for customers. For the digital unit, the IoT platform represented business opportunities. As one digital team member noted: “[We had an IoT] business case and some event was coming, and [we] wanted to show customers that you can now get it [i.e., as a product/service]! We have IoT services and everything.”

Vignette 9 illustrates how the digital and IT units at Engr intersect in their engagement with a shared technology artifact—an IoT platform—while leveraging it for different purposes. The digital unit, viewing technology as an outcome, utilized the IoT platform to collect and analyze data to create new digital offerings and services. In contrast, the IT unit, seeing technology primarily as an enabler for its activities, used the same IoT platform to collect and analyze data aimed at ensuring the operational excellence of the organization’s IT infrastructure. Consequently, each unit could utilize the same shared technology artifact for its own purposes without infringing on the other unit’s use. As a result, the need for both units to engage with the same technology proved compatible with their respective technology rationales.

## Vignette 10. Intersecting Dynamic at Auto

The IT unit managed an internal video platform. When a new accessibility regulation required the platform to provide captions for people with hearing impairments, the IT unit considered shutting the plat form down due to a lack of resources to produce captions. Simultaneously, the digital unit needed training data to develop an internal translation application using deep learning (DL). Since the IT employees who created the platform wanted to keep it running, they decided to collaborate with the digital unit. While the IT unit continued to operate the video platform, the digital unit used the audio files from the videos as training data for their DL algorithm. The IT unit then applied the DL algorithm to generate captions for both existing and newly uploaded videos.

Vignette 10 illustrates how the IT and digital units at Auto engaged with the same, shared technology artifact—the DL algorithm underlying the translation application—but for different purposes. Although the IT unit perceived the DL algorithm as an enabler for operating the internal video platform in compliance with new regulation, the digital unit viewed the DL algorithm—central to the development of its internal translation application—as an outcome. Thus, the two units’ need to engage with the shared technology artifact was compatible with their underlying technology rationales. As a result, the interactions between the digital and IT units manifested in an intersecting dynamic.

4.3.2.2. Parallel Dynamic. When digital and IT units utilize the same technology but implement separate technology artifacts, we observe a parallel dynamic. A parallel dynamic between digital and IT units arises from the incompatibility between their need to engage with the same technology and their organizing logics’ technology rationales. For example, although digital units use AI (e.g., ML or DL) to create digital business offerings (outcome), IT units use AI to support process optimization (enabler). When the two units interact by engaging with the same technology (e.g., AI) but realize their technology rationales—how they intend to use the technology—are incompatible with each other’s desired purpose or approach, they choose to implement separate artifacts of the same technology. Alternatively, they may opt to employ different technologies that best suit their needs. For example, in Engr, although both units adopted programming languages, their choices differed substantially. Although the digital unit adopts Java and HTML5, among other tools, as part of its programming toolkit to create customer-facing products, the IT unit fulfills its programming needs with different languages, such as JavaScript and HTML. Vignette 11 from Bank and Vignette 12 from Auto further illustrate this parallel dynamic.

## Vignette 11. Parallel Dynamic at Bank

As part of its efforts to portray itself as a modern financial institution, Bank explored how it could use data analytics and machine learning (ML) algorithms in various business areas. To this end, Bank established a dedicated data analytics unit, which utilized the latest advancements in data analytics and artificial intelligence (AI) to prevent fraudulent behavior among both its existing and new customers. By doing so, the data analytics unit aimed to reduce account fraud and optimize Bank’s core business. The data analytics unit cooperated closely with the IT unit, which collected and provided the required account and transaction data stored in separate databases. One of Bank’s digital units similarly explored data analytics and AI. Operating a digital payment platform, the digital unit generated a large amount of transaction data. To gain better customer insights in its bid to develop novel digital offerings, the unit hired a dedicated data scientist, Jim, whose job was to drive the unit’s efforts in data analytics. The data scientist first had to locate all relevant data stored across multiple databases, extract it into a data lake, and convert it into an appropriate format. Only then could Jim use various data analytics tools to generate customer insights. Despite several knowledge-sharing meetings between the data scientist at the digital unit, members of the data analytics unit, and the IT unit— focused on sharing best practices for navigating Bank’s complex IT infrastructure to create data lakes and deploy the necessary tools—the data analytics efforts of the digital unit still developed in isolation. Broader cooperation between the three units was not established. This was mainly because the units realized that they relied on different data and needed different tools. Instead, the data scientist at the digital unit used his prior experience to build the necessary data lake and proposed dedicated analytical tools relevant to the digital unit’s needs.

Vignette 11 illustrates that although Bank’s digital and IT units engaged with the same technology (data analytics and AI), they chose to deploy separate technology artifacts. As a result, their interactions manifested in a parallel dynamic. Bank’s digital unit employed data analytics and ML tools and hired a data scientist to leverage insights from its digital payment platform to develop new offerings (outcome). Conversely, Bank’s digital analytics unit along with the IT unit, used ML tools to support the business units’ efforts to prevent fraud (enabler). Thus, both units utilized the same technology—data analytics and ML—but not the same artifact (i.e., the same data ana lytics tools, ML tools, or even the same data lake). Instead, each unit deployed its own data analytics platforms. The data scientist from the digital unit could not repurpose an existing data analytics tool, which had been used to detect fraudulent transactions (enabler), to meet the digital unit’s goal of using insights to develop new digital offerings (outcome). As a result, the previously developed data analytics tool could not accommodate the digital unit’s needs.

Therefore, the data scientist built a separate data analytics tool that was not compatible with the other units’ needs for detecting fraudulent transactions. Thus, a parallel dynamic occurred due to the incompatibility between the units’ need to engage with the same technology and their technology rationales.

## Vignette 12. Parallel Dynamic at Auto

During Auto’s DT journey, the head of its IT unit, Kate, announced a new IT strategy, declaring that Auto’s business strategy would serve as the compass for the IT unit and its operations. Moreover, the IT strategy explicitly outlined key resources—such as data platforms, APIs, AI, and open-source technologies—for the IT unit to achieve its goals. To this end, the IT unit developed APIs to enhance data integration and transfer, particularly within Auto’s research and develop ment unit. The goal was to create a data platform that integrates and provides access to Auto’s vehicle data (e.g., usage analytics, maintenance data) for internal developers working on the next car models. Similarly, the digital unit aimed to provide vehicle data via a platform and APIs to external developers. Although these external developers would not use the data to build new cars, they would use it to develop datadriven services for Auto’s customers. When discussing these ideas, the digital and IT units decided to establish separate data platforms. The IT unit planned to enable the integration of existing systems via APIs into a unified platform, while the digital unit aimed to build a separate platform that restricted access to certain data. Thus, while both units engaged with the same technology (i.e., APIs to create data platforms), the IT unit supported the existing business, while the digital unit strove to create new business opportunities.

In Vignette 12, the IT and digital units used the same technology but relied on different technology artifacts (data platforms), which they applied according to their technology rationales. Although the IT unit perceived offering a data platform as an enabler that could help achieve better data integration and data transfer among Auto’s units, the digital unit viewed offering a data platform as an integral part of its efforts to stimulate the creation of third-party digital offerings (outcome). Moreover, the two units did not share these data platforms, as they were developed as separate, independent artifacts. In contrast to Vignettes 9 and 10, Vignette 12 illustrates that Auto’s digital unit used a digital platform and APIs to provide access to third parties, whereas the IT unit leveraged APIs to connect IT systems into a data platform for internal data exchanges. Because the resulting data platforms served different purposes and required different governance (internal or external access), the two units developed and leveraged them separately. Thus, this interaction between the digital and IT units manifested in a parallel dynamic, as their need to engage with the same technology was incompatible with their respective technology rationales.

## 5. Discussion and Implications

This study makes four key contributions to the discourse on digital and IT. First, by drawing on organizing logics, we identify three relationships between digital and IT—interdepending, interlocking, and interfacing—as reflected in digital and IT units’ interactions. Second, we outline propositions that capture antecedents, dynamics, and conditions that characterize these relationships. We offer these as a conceptual vocabulary that future research can leverage to investigate further the relationships between digital and IT. Third, we abstract these individual relationships into a model of digital cum IT, which highlights how the interplay of multiple concurrent relationships and their resulting dynamics affects organizing around technology within contemporary organizations. We posit that this relational model helps us shift our focus from digital versus IT to digital cum IT. This shift is crucial for understanding how interactions between digital and IT influence the likelihood of successful DT initiatives. Fourth, we propose a research agenda aimed at advancing scholarly efforts to investigate the relationships between digital and IT and to promote further research on relational organizing. Together, these four contributions have the potential to reshape our assumptions about organizing around technology within the IS discipline and beyond. Next, we elaborate on these contributions and outline the practical implications of our study.

## 5.1. Unpacking the Relationships That Characterize the Coexistence of Digital and IT

The identified relationships between digital and IT— interdepending, interlocking, and interfacing—along with their antecedents, dynamics, and conditions that shape them (Table 6), provide a vocabulary for understanding why and how digital and IT relate. These relationships are reflected in various context-specific interactions between digital and IT units, which draw on their respective organizing logics. In previous research, interactions between digital and IT units have rarely been the primary focus; instead, they are often embedded in empirical accounts. Although scholars recognize the coexistence of digital and IT units (Sambamurthy and Zmud 2000, Svahn et al. 2017, Pep pard 2018), there is little theorizing of their interactions, with a few notable exceptions (Tumbas et al. 2018, Lorenz and Buchwald 2023). Moreover, although scholars have suggested that the interactions between units can result in either collaboration or conflict, their explanations as to why such dynamics occur tend to focus on factors such as personal relationships or top management attention (Svahn et al. 2017, Lorenz and Buchwald 2023). Building on these insights, we probed deeper to uncover how relationships between digital and IT are reflected in specific interactions, why these relationships exist (i.e., antecedents), how they manifest in specific dynamics, and what conditions shape these dynamics.

The three outlined relationships between digital and IT are reflected in the interactions between digital and IT units that we observed empirically (Figure 1). To abstract relationships from these interactions, we used the concept of organizing logics for digital and IT, which we synthesized from prior literature (Table 2). Accordingly, the identified relationships reflect the dominant rationale behind the units’ interactions. Grounding these relationships in the organizing logics’ rationales offers a basis for theorizing their antecedents, dynamics, and conditions. Although antecedents explain why the units interact based on any of the three rationales, conditions—the compatibility between the units’ need to interact and the underlying rationales of their organizing logics— determine in which dynamic the relationships manifest. We depict the grounding of these relationships and their characteristics in the organizing logics rationales in Figure 3.

Antecedents are the need of the digital and IT units to interact, leading to the emergence of relationships. Empirically, digital and IT units need to interact to achieve their strategic objectives (Vignettes 1–4), link their routines (Vignettes 5–8), or engage with technology (Vignettes 9–12). Thus, the antecedents of these relationships reflect the underlying rationales of their organizing logics. Grounding the antecedents in these rationales provides a basis that the three relationships exist at the empirical level of the units’ interactions and at the conceptual level of digital and IT (Figure 1).

Conditions explain why a relationship manifests in specific dynamics. Each of the three relationships can manifest in at least two dynamics depending on the compatibility between the need to interact (i.e., antecedents) and the rationales underpinning each relationship. Interactions occur either when one unit has a need to interact with the other or when both units have simultaneous needs to interact. For example, if the IT unit needs to interact with the digital unit to link their routines to achieve a certain purpose (i.e., an interlocking relationship), the relationship’s dynamic (converging or diverging) depends on whether the IT unit’s need is compatible with the digital unit’s routine rationale when they interact. If compatible, a converg ing dynamic manifests (Vignettes 5 and 6); if incompati ble, a diverging dynamic manifests (Vignettes 7 and 8).

## 5.2. Digital Cum IT: Effect of Relationships Interplay on Organizing

This study focuses on identifying and characterizing relationships between digital and IT. This required us to closely examine specific interactions between digital and IT units, which reflect these relationships. Thus, we present our vignettes to capture each relationship by focusing on its antecedents, dynamics, and condi tions. When comparing the vignettes within each case, we observed that interactions between digital and IT units within a single organization reflect all three relationships and exhibit different dynamics. This suggests that multiple relationships and their dynamics can coexist within organizational contexts. Moreover, we observed that the interplay between these dynamics shapes how organizing efforts around technology are directed toward achieving broader organizational goals.

We noted, for example, that incompatible dynamics (e.g., conflicting, parallel) can interplay in a counterintuitive way, sometimes accelerating DT initiatives. For instance, in some situations at Bank, both units’ interactions as part of an interdepending relationship could be characterized by a conflicting dynamic as the digital unit perceived that the IT unit lacked advanced IT skills (see Vignette 4). This threatened to slow down DT initiatives, highlighting one dynamic’s effect on organizing. However, this effect was counterbalanced by another interaction between the units, characterized by a parallel dynamic as part of an intersecting relationship. In this case, the digital unit appointed its own data analyst to spearhead DT initiatives, reducing its reliance on the IT unit (Vignette 11), thus demonstrating the effects of the interplay of multiple dynamics on organizing. This suggests that valuable insights can be gained by shifting focus from singular relationships and dynamics to understanding their interplay. To this end, we present a model (Figure 4) that captures the presence of multiple, simultaneous relationships, as reflected in the numerous concurrent interactions between digital and IT units. Although our study focuses on the relationships and their characteristics (solid lines in Figure 4), we suggest that the interplay of relationships’ dynamics has the potential to influence organizing (dotted lines in Figure 4). The dotted lines indicate areas beyond the scope of this study, which present opportunities for further scholarly investigation.

The model reflects our findings that the relationships between digital and IT are driven by the need for both units to interact across different rationales. These relationships manifest in specific dynamics, depending on the (in)compatibility between the need to interact and the underlying rationales in each interaction. Because multiple relationships can exist simultaneously, several dynamics may occur concurrently within an organization. For example, across our vignettes, we observed that the digital and IT units engaged in multiple, simultaneous interactions reflecting all three relationships, with both compatible (i.e., synergistic, converging, intersecting) and incompatible (i.e., conflicting, diverging, parallel) dynamics. Additionally, we observed that concurrent interactions between units reflecting the same relationship (e.g., an interlocking relationship) can exhibit different dynamics (e.g., converging or diverging). For instance, the digital and IT units interacted frequently across their routines, reflecting an interlocking relationship. While some of these interactions manifested as converging dynamics, others manifested as diverging dynamics (see vignettes). This suggests that interactions between digital and IT units can reflect the same relationship but exhibit different dynamics.

Although existing literature has acknowledged the presence of different relationship dynamics (e.g., conflicting and collaborating; Dremel et al. 2017, Gregory et al. 2018, Chanias et al. 2019), our study goes further by emphasizing the plurality of relationship dynamics. Given the concurrent presence of various dynamics across multiple relationships, we suggest that the interplay of these dynamics can influence how an organization organizes around technology—for example, business operations, DT activities, and digital innovation initiatives, among others (dotted arrow pointing to effect on organizing; Figure 4). Concurrently, the interplay can also reshape the dynamics in a continuous feedback loop (dotted arrow pointing back to the dynamics; Figure 4). This suggests that dynamics are not static but can evolve with input from the emerging interplay of manifold dynamics over time. Relatedly, the idea of interplay suggests that organizing around technology is not dependent on a single relationship (i.e., how digital and IT units interact in a specific instance) but on the interplay of the ensuing dynamics manifested by multiple coexisting relationships.

The relational model of digital cum IT (Figure 4) shifts our understanding of organizing around technology from isolated silos (i.e., solely within IT or digi tal units) to a relational approach (Bailey et al. 2022). This approach emphasizes the importance of understanding the relationships and interactions between different units. Although our study focuses on the relationships between digital and IT, as reflected in the interactions between digital and IT units, we also noted that scholars have reported interactions between digital and IT units and business units (e.g., human resources, marketing, product development; Dremel et al. 2017, Svahn et al. 2017, Jo¨hnk et al. 2022). Furthermore, we observed that the organizing logics characterizing digital and IT units are increasingly being reported in other functional units (Adner et al. 2019, Ritala et al. 2021, Gierlich-Joas and Zimmer 2023). This body of work suggests that the relational organizing is crucial not only for understanding digital cum IT but also for organizing around technology more broadly.

The shift toward relational organizing follows the observation of Sambamurthy and Zmud (2000) that the changing role of IT at the dawn of digitalization requires relational organizing around technology. They proposed that IT units should assemble, deliver, and disassemble IT activities through interactions with other intra- and interorganizational units (Sambamurthy and Zmud 2000). This view aligns with our observations and the new organizing logic of digital, which emphasizes organizing around technology through networks of organizational units (Yoo et al. 2010, Bharadwaj et al. 2013, Nambisan et al. 2017). Furthermore, studies building on this work show that organizational units such as product development (Lyytinen et al. 2016, Svahn et al. 2017), marketing (Dremel et al. 2017), and human resources (Giermindl et al. 2022, Gierlich-Joas and Zimmer 2023) interact with IT or digital units when innovating with digital technology. The proposed relational model challenges the accumulated knowledge that treats digital and IT as siloed loci of organizing around technology. Instead, we propose that studying their relationships allows us to harness the growing dispersion of digital technology-related knowledge across organizational units (Peppard 2018, Tumbas et al. 2018, Ritala et al. 2021). Thus, these relationships highlight the critical need for increased engagement between organizational units as they leverage digital technologies.

## 5.3. Digital Cum IT: Research Agenda and Limitations

Our focus on digital cum IT and the proposed vocabu lary for understanding their relationships provide a foundation for future research. To this end, we propose a research agenda that outlines potential research areas and highlights exemplary research questions (Table 7). We encourage further scholarly work on the nature of the relationship between digital and IT. The relationships we identify, along with their characteristics (antecedents, dynamics, and conditions), lay the groundwork for further study of interactions between digital, IT, and business units. This offers ample opportunities to uncover new relationships between digital and IT. Moreover, future research can explore how these relationships evolve over time, how their dynamics change, and how their interplay shapes organizing around technology and organizational outcomes. By investigating this interplay, IS scholars can examine how a relational view of digital and IT influences organizing around technology.

An unanswered challenge for scholars and managers is recognizing the presence of both compatible and incompatible dynamics as typical features of these relationships. The greater challenge lies in navigating these inherent differences and harnessing them to achieve organizational goals. Our study opens this area of scholarly inquiry, as indicated by the dotted lines in Figure 4. We call for future research to build on this foundation and explore how the interplay of these dynamics influences the organizing of business operation, digital innovation and DT initiatives. Additionally, future research opportunities arise from leveraging the concept of organizing logic to study the relationships and delineations between digital and IT across different phenomena. These opportunities could include (a) identifying new rationales of digital and IT logics, (b) exploring the plurality of organizing logics (i.e., what other logics exist beyond digital and IT), (c) examining how digital and IT logics disperse across other functional units, and (d) understanding how organizing logics change and evolve over time.

Besides the opportunities for future research, our study is not without limitations. First, we draw on organizing logics that we synthesized from prior literature to analyze the interactions between digital and IT units and abstract relationships between digital and IT. Although organizing logics provided a compelling perspective on organizing around technology within the discourse among IS scholars, we recognize that other theoretical foundations—such as ambidexterity (O’Reilly and Tushman 2013, Gregory et al. 2015) and organizing vision (Swanson and Ramiller 1997, Wang 2001)—could offer valuable insights into digital cum IT. For example, ambidexterity theory may explain why digital and IT coexist in contemporary organizations, particularly regarding the mechanisms that sustain this coexistence. Organizing vision theory (Swanson and Ramiller 1997) may offer a different lens for understanding how organizational actors interpret, legitimize, and mobilize the digital label as an organizing vision in relation to IT during cross-unit interactions.

In summary, we believe our study has only begun to scratch the surface, and there are significant opportunities for future research to deepen our understanding and stimulate indigenous IS theorizing that holds value beyond our discipline (Grover and Lyytinen 2023).

## 5.4. Practical Implications

Our theorizing of the relationships between digital and IT and their characteristics has several practical implications. We present these practical implications as three considerations: (1) about the interactions’ context that determines whether compatible or incompatible dynamics engender desired effects on organizing; (2) about whether and how dynamics can be managed; and (3) about marshalling organizing logics for virtuous cross-unit relationships.

Our findings suggest that neither compatible nor incompatible dynamics are inherently good or bad. Rather, the context of the interactions determines whether compatible or incompatible dynamics are favorable. Counterintuitively, compatible dynamics can lead to undesired effects on organizing. For exam ple, intersecting dynamics can create technological path dependencies (or technology debt), making organizations’ IT legacy infrastructure more cumbersome, which may impede future technological developments or threaten stable IT operations (Magnusson and Bygstad 2014, Fu¨ rstenau et al. 2019). In such contexts, parallel dynamics, although they may suggest siloed technology development (Fu¨ rstenau et al. 2019), might be more desirable. For example, at Bank, the digital unit decided to hire its own data analysts to work on parallel data analytics tools, which accelerated the unit’s digital initiatives (Vignette 11). Similarly, interactions characterized by conflicting dynamics can be both favorable and unfavorable for organizations, depending on the context of the interactions. For example, at Engr, the IT unit refused to change its approval process for implementing ERP changes, resulting in conflicting dynamics in its interactions with the digital unit (Vignette 3). This ensured the resilience of IT operations, which had a desirable effect on organizing and at the same time galvanized action toward implementing the IoT platform. In contrast, at Bank, the interactions between units, reflecting an interdependent relationship, resulted in conflicting dynamics because the IT unit did not see the need to hire specialists with more advanced IT skills (Vignette 4). This resistance, which slowed the digital unit’s initiatives, was not strongly rooted in a need to ensure operationa excellence, but rather in the IT unit’s reluctance to change its skill set and embrace DT. Hence, managers and other organizational actors must consider the context-specific aspects that underlie these dynamics when managing cross-unit relationships. Based on our data, we caution managers against seeking to establish compatible dynamics at all costs without due consider ation of the particular context.

Practitioners can manage interactions to guide relationships toward specific dynamics. Although we did not find evidence that actors in the field intentionally steered interactions toward specific dynamics, we observed that certain managerial decisions were likely to influence these dynamics. For example, we note that synergistic dynamics within interdependent relationships are more likely to emerge due to interpersonal relationships between employees of different units (Vignette 1). This recommendation also echoes previous studies that highlight the importance of personal relationships between CDOs and CIOs for subsequent cross-unit relationships (Tumbas et al. 2018, Lorenz and Buchwald 2023). Similarly, conflicting dynamics may be less likely to emerge by hiring employees from both inside and outside the organization, bringing diverse perspectives that can balance viewpoints (see Vignette 1 and Vignette 2). Moreover, we observed that joint forums or task forces where employees from both units collaborate to discuss ideas can foster mutual understanding and encourage converging dynamics. However, we caution that these factors cannot solely determine the emergence of a dynamic. At all three organizations (Auto, Bank, and Engr), the units established joint forums to foster converging dynamics; however, in certain interactions at Auto and Bank, a diverging dynamic emerged due to the incompatibility between the units’ need to interact and the underlying rationales (Vignette 7 and Vignette 8). Thus, our study highlights the importance of understanding why units interact and which organizing logic underpins their interactions. Practitioners can use this understanding to actively intervene in and manage interactions and their dynamics, depending on the context. For example, if a conflicting dynamic arises due to the IT unit ensuring the resilience of IT operations (Vignette 3) or because the IT unit lacks personnel to embrace DT (Vignette 4), practitioners can determine how to respond to such dynamics. In the first case, digital leaders can adjust their expectations, whereas in the second case, IT leaders can increase their budgets to hire personnel with the required capabilities. Thus, although the management of dynamics was not the focus of our study, these abstracted insights from our empirical observations can assist practitioners to better understand how to respond to and manage the dynamics manifesting from the relationships.

Understanding organizing as relational emphasizes the importance of cross-unit relationships (Bailey et al. 2022). Our empirical insights indicate that such cross-unit relationships benefit from organizational arrangements that nurture interactions among actors from different organizational units. These arrangements include, for example, the digital work infrastructure available for collaboration (Kane 2015, Dery et al. 2017, Zimmer and Niemimaa 2019), policies on key performance indicators (Hartl and Hess 2017), and organizations’ leadership culture (Eberl and Drews 2021, Zimmer et al. 2023, Gierlich-Joas et al. 2024). To establish organizational arrangements that nurture cross-unit relationships, managers and organizational actors first need to ask how their organizations’ current arrangements impede or support such relationships. Based on this reflection, they can then plan and design organizational changes that alter these arrangements such that they facilitate and support cross-unit relationships. This can involve establishing cross-unit collaboration that provides rationales for handling plurality of organizing logics across various organizational units (e.g., digital, IT, HR, marketing, product development, etc.). Rather than seeking assimilation of distinct logics, practitioners should consider designing for organizing around technology that encourages and facilitates cross-unit relationships despite plurality of logics.

## 6. Conclusion

Our research seeks to uncover relationships between digi tal and IT, as well as their characteristics. By doing so, we aim to promote new thinking on organizing around technology—one that moves beyond delineating digital versus IT toward relational organizing of digital cum IT. To this end, we identify three relationships between digita and IT—interdepending, interlocking, and interfacing— and describe their antecedents, dynamics, and the conditions that shape these dynamics. Based on these findings, which we capture in three propositions, we offer vocabulary and a research agenda to inform future scholarship on digital cum IT. We argue that by focusing on what connects digital and IT rather than what sepa rates them, IS scholars can offer impactful theoretical and practical insights by facilitating the relational integration of digital and IT across various concepts. This is crucial because DT efforts in organizations are widespread and not confined to a single unit; rather, the success of these efforts depends on cross-unit collaboration.

The IT unit’s head thus reported to the head of the digital business unit. In terms of personnel, the IT unit hired mostly internally for IT service man agement since they focused on managing outsourced IT services. The hired employees were diverse in age and gen der and had educational backgrounds, as well as work experience in software development, computer science, and IT service management.
