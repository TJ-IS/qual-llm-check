# Digital Contact Tracing for Pandemic Response: The Roles of Cultural Worldviews and Technology Awareness

Abstract. The use of digital contact tracing (DCT) during the COVID-19 pandemic serves as a prime example of how government agencies and public authorities are leveraging information technologies to address complex societal challenges. However, during the period in which the study was conducted, the rate of voluntary adoption of DCT among individuals in the United States proved to be both low and uneven, falling short of the critical mass required for effective containment of viral transmission. Despite previous research suggesting that individuals’ willingness to use DCT is primarily driven by their perceived benefits and privacy concerns, and that DCT awareness could help individuals make more informed adoption decisions, the factors contributing to the mixed perceptions of DCT remain unexplored. Drawing on cultural cognition theory, we propose that individuals perceptions of risks, specifically privacy concerns in our context, and perceptions of benefits regarding DCT can be influenced by their cultural worldviews. Furthermore, the influ ence of DCT awareness on individuals’ perceptions of DCT is moderated by their cultural worldviews. This occurs because individuals tend to seek out and interpret information in a manner consistent with their existing worldviews. Based on a survey of 1,014 U.S. residents, our study demonstrates that cultural worldviews characterized by two dimensions, group, emphasizing collective responsibility in addressing personal needs, and grid, focusing on behavioral regulation based on values and attitudes pertaining to social hierarchies and established norms, significantly affect both perceived benefits and privacy concerns related to DCT. These perceptions, in turn, impact individuals’ intention to use DCT. Furthermore, individuals with a high-group worldview or a low-grid worldview exhibit a stronger positive association between DCT awareness and perceived benefits, and a stronger negative association between DCT awareness and privacy concerns. Our findings further reveal that cultural worldviews not only influence individuals’ biases in selecting media outlets for technological information but also shape their risk–benefit assessment of other emerging technologies designed to tackle societal issues. In summary, this study highlights the pivotal role that cultural worldviews play in understanding public support for information technologies aimed at addressing complex societal challenges.

## 1. Introduction

Information technologies (IT) have been developed and used by government agencies and public authorities to address various societal issues such as disease containment, public surveillance, and citizen identity management (Milakovich 2022). Digital contact tracing (DCT) is one such example. It uses mobile devices to trace the spread of infectious diseases, thereby enabling the identification of exposed individuals, facilitating timely notification, and offering treatment recommendations (Danquah et al. 2019, Zhang et al. 2020). Although these technologies may possess robust features and undergo quality assurance, their effectiveness often hinges on public involvement and support. Concerns about the risks and doubts about the benefits of the technologies can impede public support (Chen 2017, Riemer et al. 2020).

Over the past two decades, several lethal diseases emerged, including H1N1, Ebola, Zika, and most recently, the COVID-19 pandemic (Wilder-Smith and Osman 2020). These viruses are highly contagious and demand swift responses. DCT is among the tools put forth to contain such outbreaks. Compared with manual contact tracing, DCT has the advantages of prompt reporting of fresh cases and immediate notification of exposures. Researchers have speculated that if 80% of mobile phone users, constituting 56% of the population, had engaged in DCT during the COVID-19 pandemic (Altmann et al. 2020, Zhang et al. 2020), the technology could have had a significant impact. It might have arrested the spread of the virus, saved lives, averted lockdowns, and expedited economic recovery.<sup>1</sup> In the United States, 24 states and the District of Columbia released DCT apps for COVID-19 in 2020 (Online Appendix A). However, voluntary use of the apps was low and uneven at the time of the study, with less than 29% of Americans planning to use them (Hecht-Felella 2021). This fell short of the critical mass needed for effectively controlling the spread of the virus.

Public opinions on DCT were mixed, as evidenced by the debates surrounding its benefits and privacy risks. Although some argued that DCT could contribute to the greater good by flattening the infection curves and helping to suppress the pandemic, others expressed concerns about the potential for data breaches and misuse of personal information (Rowe 2020, Williams et al. 2021). Despite claims from health professionals and technology developers that DCT in the U.S. incorporates features to preserve user privacy and safeguard their identities while monitoring potential COVID-19 exposures, debates over its benefits and privacy risks continued to be fueled by news outlets and social media platforms (Crothers 2020, Ingram 2020, Amann et al. 2021). People were wary that their contact histories and health statuses might be accessed by unauthorized entities and misused for financial gain. Additionally, there were fears that the government might use the data to monitor citizens movements and behaviors, evoking concerns of a bigbrother initiative (Bichell 2020, Crothers 2020). These discussions and constituent concerns prompted legislators in certain states to obstruct the implementation of DCT apps (Hecht-Felella 2021).

Cultural cognition theory (Kahan 2012, Johnson and Swedlow 2019) posits that individuals’ perceptions of risks, such as privacy risks, and benefits associated with emerging technologies are shaped by their cultural worldviews. These worldviews encompass general social, cultural, and political values, as well as perceived relationships with society (Slovic 1997). The interpretation of risks and benefits by individuals often reflects their socially located beliefs and worldviews. These beliefs largely stem from their roles and experiences within social hierarchies, institutions, and communities (Summerton and Berner 2003). These beliefs may influence how risks and benefits are perceived and interpreted. In this way, perceptions of risks and benefits are not merely objective realities dictated by the technological features; they are social constructs that are culturally motivated and rooted in one’s worldviews. Hence, cultural cognition theory provides a viable theoretical foundation for understanding the varied perceptions held by the public regarding DCT for pandemic response. In the context of COVID-19, studies suggest that cultural worldviews influence individuals’ support for countermeasures like social distancing and selfquarantine (Liu and Yang 2021, Savadori and Lauriola 2021). However, the effects of these worldviews on individuals’ perceptions of DCT and their subsequent intention to use it remain unexplored.

Previous literature in information systems (IS) suggests that when individuals are provided with information about a new technology, they gain understanding of its features and capabilities and form well-founded, mindful decisions (Thatcher et al. 2018). Technology awareness, representing the amount of information one has about the new technology, is a key factor in the adop tion process. Fostered by persuasive communications such as positively framed messages (Angst and Agarwal 2009), beneficial use scenarios (Breward et al. 2017), priming on IT capability (Thatcher et al. 2018), and fear appeal (Johnston and Warkentin 2010), technology awareness may lead to better acceptance of the technology. However, given the heated debates on the benefits and privacy risks of DCT, media contents about DCT from various sources have been opinionated. Under this circumstance, individuals tend to select sources and information that align with their existing beliefs and per ceive the information as credible if it confirms rather than contradicts their beliefs (Kahan et al. 2009, Kahan 2012, Newman et al. 2018). This motivated bias in human cognition is known as selective exposure (Freedman and Sears 1965, Frey 1986). We contend that the influence of technology awareness on DCT perceptions depends on an individual’s cultural worldviews.

Therefore, this study examines how cultural world views, alongside DCT awareness, impact individuals risk–benefit perceptions about DCT and subsequently impact their use intention. We characterize cultural worldviews along two dimensions, group and grid, following the framework of cultural cognition theory (Kahan et al. 2007, 2015). The group worldview gauges the extent to which individuals believe that addressing personal needs should be a collective responsibility rather than an individual one. It is measured using the individualism–communitarianism scale, wherein a high-group worldview emphasizes collective values (i.e., communitarianism) and a low-group worldview emphasizes self-interests (i.e., individualism). The grid worldview captures the degree to which individuals believe that behaviors should be regulated based on values and attitudes pertaining to social stratification and deviance from prevailing norms and roles. It is measured using the egalitarianism–hierarchy scale, wherein a high-grid worldview emphasizes the authority of social elites (i.e., hierarchy) and a low-grid worldview emphasizes equality, fairness, and justice (i.e., egalitarianism).

We propose that cultural worldviews influence perceived benefits and privacy concerns about DCT. Perceived benefits then promote the intention to use DCT, whereas privacy concerns inhibit the intention. Additionally, the effects of DCT awareness on perceived benefits and privacy concerns are dependent on cultural worldviews. Based on a survey involving 1,014 U.S. residents, our findings illustrate that the group worldview substantially increases perceived benefits of DCT and decreases privacy concerns. The grid worldview reduces perceived benefits of DCT but has a weak effect on privacy concerns. Our results also affirm the moderating effects of cultural worldviews on the relationship between DCT awareness and perceived benefits, as well as between DCT awareness and privacy concerns. For individuals with a high-group worldview or a low-grid worldview, their DCT awareness is more positively associated with perceived benefits and more negatively associated with privacy concerns.

To explore the potential impact of cultural worldviews and DCT awareness on existing DCT users’ intention to continue using the apps, we surveyed alongside our main study a separate sample of 138 respondents who installed a DCT App. We successfully replicated the negative relationship between the group worldview and privacy concerns, and the moderating effect of the group worldview on the association between DCT awareness and privacy concerns. The results suggest that cultural worldviews may be most influential when risks or benefits of a technology are uncertain. Although DCT users have direct experience with the benefits of the apps, their concerns about the potential misuse of personal data collected by the apps, akin to the concerns of nonusers, might endure and not be mitigated by their use experience.

To reinforce the significant role of cultural worldviews in selective exposure, we undertook a supplementary study to explore the association between cultural worldviews and individuals’ biases in selecting media outlets for technological information. The findings affirm the association: individuals with a high-group worldview exhibit a preference for left-leaning outlets (e.g., CNN, NBC News, New York Times), whereas those with a highgrid worldview display a greater inclination toward right-leaning outlets (e.g., Fox News, New York Post, Washington Times).

To ensure the generalizability of our primary findings in other technological contexts, we extended our investigation to examine how cultural worldviews impact individuals’ risk–benefit assessments of other technologies (such as technology-assisted surveillance and smart meters) aimed at addressing societal challenges yet bearing potential privacy risks. Consistent with the main study, individuals with a high- (versus low-) group worldview perceive more benefits than risks from these technologies, and those with a high- (versus low-) grid worldview perceive more risks than benefits. The findings underscore the significance of cultural worldviews in understanding individuals’ diverse support for the use of IT to address public issues.

Section 4 describes our research method. Section 5 presents the results of hypotheses testing and supplemental studies. Section 6 delves into the theoretical contributions and practical implications.

## 2. Research Background and Theoretical Foundations

Smartphone-based DCT technology offers a seamless, instant, automatic, accurate, anonymous, and cost-effective approach to tracing and notifying new cases of infectious diseases (Ferretti et al. 2020, Rowe 2020). The operational principles of DCT are well documented in existing literature (Ferretti et al. 2020, Zhang et al. 2020, Simko et al. 2022). DCT apps for COVID-19 in the United States rely on the exposure notification system jointly developed by Goo gle and Apple (Riemer et al. 2020). This technology uses Bluetooth-proximity tracing embedded in both iOS and Android operating systems, enabling iPhone and Android phone users to run state-sponsored DCT apps on their mobile devices without divulging their identities. Table A.1 in Online Appendix A lists the names of the DCT apps introduced by health authorities in 24 U.S. states and the District of Columbia at the time of the study.

Previous studies have drawn on the privacy calculus model (Smith et al. 2011) to explore the influence of individuals’ risk–benefit perceptions of DCT on their use intentions (see Online Appendix A for a review). Perceived benefits of DCT can be personal $( \mathrm { e . g . , }$ , immediate exposure alerts, enhanced peace of mind in the absence of exposure) and societal (e.g., enabling exposed individuals to take precautions and prevent potential virus transmis sion to others). Although some studies evaluate personal and societal benefits as distinct constructs (Trang et al. 2020), others treat them as an integrated one (Hassan doust et al. 2020, Walrave et al. 2020). Conversely, individuals’ concerns about the potential misuse of data collected by DCT apps (Hargittai and Redmiles 2020, Walrave et al. 2020, Frimpong and Helleringer 2021) act as a significant obstacle to the adoption of DCT. Despite progress in understanding the consequences of risk– benefit perceptions about DCT, the literature seldom investigates the factors that shape these perceptions.

The IS literature has explored factors that affect individuals’ risk–benefit perceptions of IT. These factors include system attributes such as information quality and system quality (Liang et al. 2017), as well as user perceptions like trust beliefs (Breward et al. 2017) (see Onlnie Appendix B for a review). Among these factors, researchers emphasize the importance of technology awareness, such as familiarity with beneficial use scenarios (Breward et al. 2017), exposure to persuasive messages (Angst and Agarwal 2009), and fear appeals (Johnston and Warkentin 2010). The premise is that if individuals are provided with more information about the features or benefits of the technology, such as its functions, usability, and potential outcomes, they will become more mindful of its capabilities and form a more positive attitude toward its use (Thatcher et al. 2018). However, these studies seldom consider how individuals’ attitudes may change if they can select the sources of information and interpret the information autonomously, particularly in situations where media content involves opinionated discussions and heated debates about the pros and cons of the technology, as evident in the case of DCT apps. Moreover, few studies have explicitly explored how technology awareness might alter individuals’ risk perceptions of the technology (such as privacy concerns). Compared with the benefits, the downsides of the technology, such as privacy risks to end users or society, often remain unexplored in the literature. Further investigations are needed to understand the underlying factors that influence individuals risk–benefit perceptions and the role of technology awareness in shaping those perceptions.

## 2.1. Cultural Cognition Theory

Cultural cognition theory elucidates how cultural worldviews can explain individuals’ conflicting attitudes and behaviors toward technologies and societal issues that have potential public implications (Kahan 2012), particularly conflicts in their risk–benefit assessments (Douglas and Wildavsky 1982, Slovic 1997). According to the theory, an individual’s risk–benefit assessment is “a socially constructed attribute, rather than as a physical entity that exists independently of the humans who assess and experience its effects” (Bradbury 1989, p. 381). Assessments made by individuals regarding a technology or societal issue reflect their personal value choices or cultural worldviews.

It is vital to emphasize the distinction between cultural cognition theory and the cross-cultural cognition paradigm. Although the cross-cultural cognition paradigm views those cultural variations through a societal or national lens (Hofstede 1991), cultural cognition theory focuses on how disparities in values give rise to intrasocietal conflicts. These conflicts often manifest in varying localized contexts, both temporally and spatially, underscoring the intricate nature of this theory (Kahan 2012).

As previously mentioned, cultural cognition theory delineates two dimensions of cultural worldviews (refer to Table 1): the group dimension, which pertains to societal commitment and is quantified through the individualism–communitarianism scale, and the grid dimension, which denotes the degree of regulation within or beyond the group and is measured using the egalitarianism–hierarchy scale.

Individuals with a high-group worldview, identified as communitarians, exhibit a stronger inclination toward communitarianism and place less emphasis on individualism. They prioritize shared responsibility and the pur suit of the common good as essential and morally justifiable goals, advocating for governmental interven tions that align with these principles. In contrast, individuals with a low-group worldview, referred to as individualists, place less emphasis on communitarianism and more on individualism. They believe that social outcomes stem from individual efforts rather than collec tive endeavors. According to their perspective, people should manage their affairs autonomously, navigate personal setbacks, and reap the rewards of their individual efforts. For individualists, endeavors requiring collective efforts and governmental interventions contradict deeply ingrained personal values.

In general, individuals with a high-group worldview tend to exhibit a heightened appreciation for technologies that advance collective well-being (Kahan et al. 2009, 2012; Johnson and Swedlow 2020). Conversely, those with a low-group worldview are more attuned to the risk associated with technologies that might potentially erode individuality, consequently diminishing their perception of the technologies’ benefits. With respect to public health challenges, individuals with a high-group worldview display greater concerns about the risks posed by infectious diseases than those with a low-group worldview, and they favor more stringent measures to control the pandemics (Johnson and Swedlow 2020).

Individuals with a high-grid worldview, referred to as hierarchists, exhibit a greater tendency to embrace hierarchy and place less emphasis on egalitarianism. They support social hierarchies (such as those based on caste, race, gender, or various forms of socioeconomic status) and established structures like governments, healthcare systems, and regulatory bodies. They believe that these structures establish a natural societal order that should operate without external intervention. In contrast, individuals with a low-grid worldview, known as egalitarians, exhibit a reduced proclivity for hierarchy but a heightened commitment to egalitarianism. They believe these social structures perpetuate unwarranted and arbitrary inequalities that should be rectified through innovative technologies.

Typically, individuals who subscribe to a high-grid worldview perceive assertions concerning societal risks, including pandemics, as criticisms directed at the higher echelons of society. Consequently, they are more inclined to disregard such statements (Kahan et al. 2015). In contrast, those with a low-grid worldview prioritize principles of equality and justice and display a heightened responsiveness to public risks compared with those with their high-grid counterparts. They advocate for the oversight of hazards and measures to alleviate threats to public welfare (Johnson and Swedlow 2020).

Research conducted in various technological domains provides support for the theory. For example, studies on public opinions regarding government-funded initiatives, such as wind and solar power projects (Sposato and Hampl 2018) and green energy programs (Cherry et al. 2019), demonstrate that individuals with a low-grid worldview and those with a high-group worldview are most supportive of these endeavors.

## 2.2. Technology Awareness as the Consequence of Selective Exposure

Individuals often acquire insights into the pros and cons of using DCT through the sources and information content they choose. At the time of the study, evidence regarding the efficacy of DCT in mitigating the spread of COVID-19 remained inconclusive. Some experts criticized the inaccuracy in Bluetooth proximity tracing (Amann et al. 2021). Concerns also emerged that, aside from low adoption rates, some users had inaccurately reported their test results through the apps (Hecht-Felella 2021). Media outlets and content further contributed to divergent perspectives on DCT. An article from CNN suggested that DCT apps could assist employers in establishing a safe work environment by notifying employees about breaches of social distancing (Vasel 2020). NBC News criticized state governments for the delayed launch of DCT apps and praised tech firms efforts in combating the pandemic (Ingram 2020). In contrast, an article from The Washington Times painted a more pessimistic portrait of DCT, highlighting challenges tied to human errors (Bichell 2020). Fox News raised concerns about the potential susceptibility of DCT apps to ransomware infiltration (Crothers 2020).

When individuals have access to a large volume of information on the Internet, they tend to gravitate toward information that aligns with their existing beliefs (Fischer et al. 2008) and insulate themselves from opposing viewpoints through personalizing curation (Sunstein 2009). For example, they often engage in online communities that are fairly homogeneous in terms of values and viewpoints (Dahlberg and Siapera 2007), which affirm their perspectives (Wilhelm 1999). Moreover, they tend to avoid rectifying errors or misconceptions. These “reinforcing spirals” (Zhao 2009, Feldman et al. 2012) create significant barriers to the effective communication of scientific information to the public (Kahan 2015).

As a prevalent manifestation of selective exposure, individuals tend to reject information that challenges their existing beliefs, such as culture worldviews, to avoid cognitive dissonance (Zaller 1992, Lodge and Taber 2013, Newman et al. 2018). Often, their acceptance or rejection of sources or information depends on how well these align with their beliefs (Hart et al. 2009). Ambiguous information is frequently interpreted in ways that align with their beliefs, and information that is consistent with their beliefs is more easily recalled than inconsistent information (West and Turner 2004). Indivi duals may even distort new information to make it conform to their existing views (Hart et al. 2009). Research on cultural cognition shows that cultural worldviews influence how individuals process information from news sources, scientists, and public officials, which in turn shapes their support for or opposition to specific policies (Rachlinski 2021). Therefore, cultural worldviews may influence individuals’ exposure to information about using DCT, potentially introducing bias into their understanding of its benefits or risks.

## 3. Hypothesis Development

Drawing on the previous literature, we develop a research model to examine how cultural worldviews influence individuals’ perceptions about DCT and subsequent use intention, as well as the role of DCT awareness in this process. Figure 1 illustrates the research model and hypotheses.

## 3.1. Main Effects of Cultural Worldviews

We anticipate that individuals with a high-group worldview, characterized by greater communitarianism and less individualism, would likely perceive DCT as offering more substantial benefits. Recent research indicates that communitarians hold a heightened perception of the risks associated with COVID-19 compared with individualists because they believe the pandemic has a society-wide impact (Savadori and Lauriola 2021). This predisposes them to be more inclined toward adopting protective measures like personal hygiene and social distancing. In line with their support for green energy initiatives aimed at combating climate change and safeguarding humanity (Sposato and Hampl 2018, Cherry et al. 2019), we expect that communitarians would also view DCT as contributing to the collective good and providing benefits for both individuals and society. Conversely, individuals with a low-group worldview may be less likely to see DCT as beneficial, especially if they perceive themselves to be at a lower risk of contracting COVID-19 (Dryhurst et al. 2020, Savadori and Lauriola 2021).

In terms of privacy concerns, historical perspectives have often associated privacy rights with individualism, emphasizing the idea that privacy serves as a safeguard against governmental surveillance: a viewpoint particular prominence in the United States (Minkkinen et al. 2017). Given that effective responses to public health crises, like pandemics, often require collective efforts that may intrude on individual liberties (Rachlinski 2021), those with individualistic beliefs could have reservations about potential infringements on their privacy rights. In contrast, communitarians stress the significance of “group privacy” (Reviglio and Alunge 2020) and “positive rights” (Minkkinen et al. 2017), which harmonize the seemingly paradoxical elements of autonomy and social influence. In this framework, individual and society are not considered opposing entities; instead, society actively shapes individuality. As a result, individuals with a high-group worldview are likely to be more receptive to the potential privacy risks associated with DCT than those with a low-group worldview. Therefore, we propose the following.

Hypothesis 1a. The group worldview is positively associ ated with perceived benefits of DCT.

Hypothesis 1b. The group worldview is negatively associ ated with privacy concerns about DCT.

We expect that individuals with a high-grid worldview will perceive fewer benefits from using DCT to combat COVID-19. Conversely, those with a low-grid worldview are likely to see more benefits. The COVID-19 pandemic has accentuated social inequalities, notably causing disproportionate unemployment and fatalities among seniors and marginalized groups (Liu and Yang 2021, Perry et al. 2021). DCT could avert lockdowns and potentially aid in economic recovery for these vulnerable populations. In this context, a low-grid worldview could enhance the perceived personal and societal benefits of using DCT, akin to the greater support for climate change mitigation observed among egalitarians compared with hierarchists (Sposato and Hampl 2018, Cherry et al. 2019). In contrast, individuals with a high grid worldview often endorse a belief in a natural societal order and are skeptical of the need for governmental or public policy interventions (Rachlinski 2021). Such individuals are more likely to attribute a person’s COVID-19 infection to individual factors rather than systemic failures or lapses in authority (Savadori and Lauriola 2021). As a result, individuals with a high-grid worldview are inclined to minimize the societal impacts of COVID-19 (Siegrist and Bearth 2021) and may resist preventive measures like social distancing and selfquarantine (Liu and Yang 2021, Savadori and Lauriola 2021).

Addressing privacy concerns associated with DCT, individuals with a low-grid worldview tend to advocate for standardized privacy norms and emphasize individual control over personal information (Minkkinen et al. 2017). DCT apps collect contact information automatically and anonymously, allowing users to update and willingly share their test results. This functionality empowers users to actively participate in containing the virus, which aligns with egalitarian values of equality and social justice. Thus, such individuals are generally less concerned about privacy and more willing to share personal information, a disposition consistent with their openness to sharing adverse personal experiences (Pilafova 2011). Conversely, those with a high-grid worldview are often apprehensive that technologies like DCT, which gather and distribute information broadly, could erode the control exerted by authoritative figures over that information, potentially destabilizing established social hierarchies (Smith et al. 2011, Kaptchuk et al. 2022). We therefore propose the following.

Hypothesis 2a. The grid worldview is negatively associated with perceived benefits of DCT.

Hypothesis 2b. The grid worldview is positively associated with privacy concerns about DCT.

## 3.2. DCT Awareness and Cultural Worldviews

People often exhibit a form of inertia when faced with the prospect of adopting new technologies, typically hesitating to depart from the status quo (Polites and Karahanna 2012). This resistance can, however, be mitigated through effective communication about the technology’s features and benefits. This perspective resonates within the research of DCT adoption. For example, Prakash and Das (2022) contend that clear articulation of DCT benefits can most effectively be communicated through welldesigned awareness programs. Similarly, acknowledging the public’s limited understanding and prevailing misconceptions about the privacy features of DCT, Zhang et al. (2020) advocate the essential role of public education campaigns. They argue that these campaigns should be launched prior to state deployments to highlight the privacy-preserving aspects of DCT.

The previous arguments assume that the information accessible to individuals can be consciously shaped to enhance their understanding of a technology, in this case, DCT apps. This tailored information aims to promote adoption by highlighting the technology’s advantages and mitigating concerns (Zhang et al. 2020, Prakash and Das 2022). However, as discussed in Section 2.2, the media landscape surrounding DCT is far from monolithic. Various outlets provide a range of perspectives on DCT, some advocating for its benefits (Ingram 2020, Vasel 2020), whereas others warn against potential privacy intrusions and the specter of a “big brother” surveillance state (Bichell 2020, Crothers 2020). This variety of viewpoints, each uniquely framed, could lead to a diverse array of public perceptions about DCT.

Research on cultural cognition shows that individuals with a strongly held cultural worldview tend to gravitate toward media sources and content that resonate with their existing beliefs (Newman et al. 2018, Ofori-Parku 2020). For instance, a survey of 1,904 U.S. residents conducted by Newman et al. (2018) shows that egalitarians and communitarians are more inclined to consume media from left-leaning outlets, which often affirm environmental issues (Feldman et al. 2012). Conversely, hierarchists and individualists typically favor right-leaning media sources, which are more likely to question such environmental claims. Moreover, even when presented with balanced or neutral content, individuals often assimilate the information in ways that conform to their cultural worldviews (Ofori-Parku 2020, Rachlinski 2021).

When it comes to DCT, the landscape of media content offers a wide array of conflicting viewpoints on its advantages and drawbacks (Amann et al. 2021). Individuals tend to selectively expose themselves to sources and media content that align with their existing beliefs while dismissing or avoiding those that diverge. Consequently, their engagement with information about DCT often becomes skewed or one-sided. Such biased exposure can hinder and even distort facts about DCT, as people tend to assimilate information in a manner that aligns with their cultural worldviews rather than correcting their misperceptions. Therefore, the effect of DCT awareness on perceived benefits and privacy concerns may be contingent on an individual’s cultural worldviews.

Specifically, individuals with a high-group worldview may exhibit a preference for, and attribute greater significance to, sources and media content that emphasize the societal benefits of DCT, echoing sentiments found in articles from CNN and NBC News. These sources and media content affirm their beliefs in the collective good of using this technology, whereas concurrently alleviating their privacy concerns through the highlighting of DCT’s privacy-preserving features (Vasel 2020). As individuals consume more information, their exposure to these types of sources and content increases, thereby reinforcing their positive perceptions of DCT (Zhao 2009, Feldman et al. 2012). Conversely, individuals with a low-group worldview may be drawn to sources and media content that accentuate potential risks (Crothers 2020). Exposure to such content might not enhance their perceptions that the benefits of DCT are personally relevant but could instead heighten their privacy concerns.

Individuals with a high-grid worldview are more likely to align with sources and media content that emphasize the privacy risks and limitations of the technology, as illustrated by articles from The Washington Times and Fox News. They may be apprehensive that improper use of DCT, highlighted in The Washington Times article discussing human errors (Bichell 2020), could potentially disrupt established social structures, including the public health system. Conversely, individuals with a low-grid worldview are more inclined to endorse sources and media content that discuss how

DCT fosters social equality by facilitating information sharing among fellow citizens (Vasel 2020).

In summary, as individuals become more aware of DCT through exposure to more information about it, their focus on its benefits or risks may hinge on their cultural worldviews. These worldviews could predispose them to prefer, and be influenced by, certain types of sources and media content over others. We therefore propose the following.

Hypothesis 3a. For individuals with a high-group worldview, their awareness of DCT is associated with more perceived benefits of DCT than individuals with a low-group worldview.

Hypothesis 3b. For individuals with a high-group worldview, their awareness of DCT is associated with less privacy concerns about DCT than individuals with a low-group worldview.

Hypothesis 3c. For individuals with a low-grid worldview, their awareness of DCT is associated with more perceived benefits of DCT than individuals with a high-grid worldview.

Hypothesis 3d. For individuals with a low-grid worldview, their awareness of DCT is associated with less privacy concerns about DCT than individuals with a high-grid worldview.

## 3.3. Privacy Calculus in DCT Use

Perception of DCT’s personal and societal benefits can serve as a catalyst for its use (Hassandoust et al. 2020, Trang et al. 2020, Prakash and Das 2022). Personal benefits of using DCT include receiving rapid notifications, gaining access to doctors, obtaining directions to test facilities, and acquiring self-quarantine instructions (Kahn 2020). Using DCT can also reduce household transmission of the virus, which may exceed transmission rates from other contacts by up to 10-fold (Lei et al. 2020). Swift notification of a positive test result empowers family members to take prompt actions to mitigate the risk of infecting other household members, especially the elderly. Such information is critical, especially in cases of asymptomatic individuals, and plays a pivotal role in curbing the spread of the virus. Subsequently, the social and economic impacts may be minimized (Altmann et al. 2020, Zhang et al. 2020). Those who see these communal benefits are more inclined to use DCT (Trang et al. 2020, Redmiles 2021).

Privacy concerns are significant barriers to user adoption of DCT apps. Research shows that individuals worry about their personal information being collected and potentially misused, leading them to think twice before adopting the technology (Altmann et al. 2020, Trang et al. 2020, Zhang et al. 2020, Prakash and Das 2022). Therefore, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 4. Perceived benefits of DCT are positively associated with an individual’s intention to use it.

Hypothesis 5. Privacy concerns about DCT are negatively associated with an individual’s intention to use it.

## 3.4. Control Variables

We incorporated three categories of control variables for perceived benefits, privacy concerns, and intention to use DCT. The first category pertains to information and appraisal about COVID-19 and contact tracing. Individuals with more information about COVID-19 and contact tracing methods may see more benefits in using DCT (Walrave et al. 2020, Zhang et al. 2020). Thus, we controlled for awareness of COVID-19 and awareness of contact tracing methods. Besides subjective awareness, individuals armed with accurate information about COVID-19 better understand the risks and benefits of using DCT (Rowe et al. 2020), affecting DCT acceptance. Following Calvillo et al. (2020), we controlled for the accuracy of information related to COVID-19. Furthermore, we controlled for perceived risk of COVID-19 (including vulnerability to the virus and severity of illness), as individuals who are more aware of the risk are more likely to appreciate the benefits of DCT and be willing to use it (Walrave et al. 2020).

The second category encompasses one’s ability and willingness to use technologies in general. We accounted for technological self-efficacy, capturing a person’s perceived ability to use technology without external assistance (Trang et al. 2020), as well as personal innovativeness in IT (i.e., PIIT), which reflects a person’s inclination to be an early adopter of new information technologies (Agarwal and Prasad 1998).

The third category relates to perceptions about DCT quality. Trust is a crucial precursor to technology adoption (Breward et al. 2017), and it is thought to reduce privacy concerns and encourage acceptance of DCT (Altmann et al. 2020, Prakash and Das 2022). False-positive and false-negative alert rates are both indicators of product quality perception (Kaptchuk et al. 2022). We accounted for trust in state government, concerns regarding false-positives, and concerns regarding falsenegatives.

Last, in line with previous research, we controlled for demographic factors such as gender, age, income, and educational level when analyzing DCT use intention (Hassandoust et al. 2020, Trang et al. 2020, Prakash and Das 2022).

## 4. Research Method

We conducted a survey targeting U.S. residents to empirically test the research model during the COVID-19 pandemic. As not all states had developed DCT apps at the time of this study (see Table A.1 in the online appendix for a list of DCT apps in United States), for the respondents in states that had released DCT apps, we used the actual app names and their respective developers (i.e., state health authorities). For the respondents in states without an app, we used a fictional name (COVID Alert) and authority (Health Department in your state).<sup>2</sup> In accordance with prior research (Trang et al. 2020, Zhang et al. 2020, Kaptchuk et al. 2022), we crafted a vignette for the survey. The vignette, along with the questionnaire items, was reviewed by several university employees to ensure optimal wording and readability. The final vignette reads as follows:

The [Health Department in your state] developed a mobile app called [COVID Alert] to trace and notify one’s exposure to COVID-19 cases. The app uses a smartphone’s Bluetooth signals to detect if another phone with the app is nearby (i.e., less than 6 feet away). If another phone with the app is found, the app uses an algorithm to generate an identifier to anonymously represent the found phone and stores it for two weeks. The app periodically checks if any of the stored identifiers is associated with a positive test for COVID-19. If so, the app will notify the user about being potentially exposed to the virus.

The survey protocol received approval from the institutional review boards (IRBs) of the universities affiliated with the authors. Following prior recommendations (Lowry et al. 2016) and building on previous studies (Trang et al. 2020, Wong et al. 2021), we implemented procedural measures to mitigate the potential common method bias. These measures included the use of varied response formats for survey questions (see examples in Online Appendix D), reverse-worded items, attention checks, and comprehension checks. Additionally, we incorporated a commonly used marker variable known as blue attitude (Simmering et al. 2014) into the survey.

To mitigate the potential effects of respondents’ social desirability bias during the survey, we followed exemplary studies in our design (Trang et al. 2020, Wong et al. 2021). We highlighted in the consent form that no personally identifiable information would be gathered and that all data collected would remain anonymous and would be stored securely on the researchers’ work computers (Wong et al. 2021).

We established filters at the outset of the survey to prescreen participants based on their geolocations, ages, and smartphone use. We excluded those not residing in the United States, those without smartphone use, or those who were younger than 18 years old. We also used the Captcha to block robots and implemented the ballot stuffing feature to restrict super users (Lowry et al. 2016). Three attention checks were inserted at different places in the survey, and those who failed the checks were excluded from the analysis. A comprehension question was given after the vignette, and those who answered the question incorrectly were asked to review the vignette to ensure they read and understood its content.

The survey unfolded as follows. After completing the Captcha and the consent form, participants moved on to answer screening questions. Those who passed the screening phase then responded to questions concerning their information related to COVID-19; their awareness of COVID-19, contact tracing method, and DCT; their level of trust in state government; and their cultural worldviews. We also assessed participants’ perceptions of COVID-19 risk. Next, we inquired whether the participants had installed a DCT app, and presented the vignette to those who had not yet installed a DCT app.<sup>3</sup> This was followed by a set of questions about the app described in the vignette, including perceived benefits, privacy concerns, concerns about false-positives and false-negatives, and intention to use the app. We also inquired whether app use was mandated by state orders or organizational policies. Finally, participants responded to questions about their perceived technological selfefficacy, PIIT, and demographics.

Participants were recruited through CloudResearch’s MTurk Toolkit, which facilitated the recruitment of high-quality participants on MTurk using features such as duplicate IP block, suspicious geocode block, verified worker country location, and CloudResearch-approved participants. These features enhanced the overall data quality for online surveys (Peer et al. 2022). Following the standard pay rate for MTurk workers at the time, we offered compensation to participants for their time. We excluded 21 participants whose use of DCT was mandated by state orders or organizational policies based on their responses. The data set for hypothesis testing includes 1,014 responses from participants in 41 U.S states and the District of Columbia (see Online Appendix C).

## 4.1. Measurements

We adopted measurement items from existing literature (see Online Appendix D). Where applicable, participants were asked to provide ratings on a five-point Likert scale, ranging from one (strongly disagree) to five (strongly agree). Items measuring cultural worldviews were developed by Kahan et al. (2007, 2015), and these items exhibited superior psychometric properties and larger effect sizes than alternative measures (Xue et al. 2014, Johnson and Swedlow 2019). These items have also been applied in a range of studies (Kahan et al. 2009, Newman et al. 2018, Sposato and Hampl 2018, Cherry et al. 2019, Liu and Yang 2021, Savadori and Lauriola 2021). We measured DCT awareness through selfreported exposure to DCT-related information (Kahan et al. 2009). We adopted items for perceived benefits of DCT from recent COVID-19 literature (Hassandoust et al. 2020, Walrave et al. 2020). Privacy concerns were measured using items from the privacy literature (Xu et al. 2011). We measured intention to use DCT using items derived from Altmann et al. (2020) and Trang et al. (2020).

Regarding the control variables, we adapted the items measuring perceived risk of COVID-19 from Johnston and Warkentin (2010), which included both perceived vulnerability and perceived severity. Items for awareness of COVID-19 and awareness of the contact tracing method were based on the aforementioned DCT awareness items. We used 10 true-false items to measure the accuracy of information about COVID-19, sourced from Azlan et al. (2020), and calculated the scores with one point awarded for each correct answer. We measured self-efficacy using the items from Trang et al. (2020), and PIIT using the items from Agarwal and Prasad (1998). Trust in government, concerns about false positives, and concerns about false negatives were each assessed using single-item measures from the COVID literature (Altmann et al. 2020, Kaptchuk et al. 2022). We also collected demographic information using single-item measures.

## 5.3. Robustness Tests

We conducted a series of supplemental analyses to ensure the robustness of the results.<sup>8</sup> The details are presented in Online Appendix G. First, we tested whether the correlation between group and grid could distort the results. To address this issue, we used the Gram-Schmidt procedure (Golub and Van Loan 2013) to orthogonalize grid with respect to group. Our goal was to examine how the portion of variance in grid, not shared by group, might influence the model. We found that all results remained consistent, except that grid did not moderate the relationship between DCT awareness and privacy concerns. This finding implies that while there is some shared variance between group and grid, it does not significantly affect the outcomes of our model, aside from the moderating effect of grid on the relationship between DCT awareness and privacy concerns.

Next, we conducted a series of tests to examine whether the consolidated difference between perceived benefits and privacy concerns, calculated based on their latent scores and representing the literal outcome of the privacy calculus, could be used in lieu of the two distinct risk and benefit perception constructs in the model. A cross-tabulation test based on whether perceived benefits surpass or fall below privacy concerns shows that the difference has a significant positive effect on use intention. Individuals who perceive more benefits than privacy concerns are more willing to use DCT. A CB-SEM analysis based on the difference score (i.e., perceived benefits – privacy concerns) shows that the score mediated the effects of group and grid on use intention. A multigroup analysis based on the difference score further shows that both group and grid moderate the relationship between DCT awareness and the difference score. Collectively, these tests confirm the effect of the privacy calculus in driving DCT use intention.

Research on risk perceptions often employs a single item to gauge individuals’ comprehensive risk–benefit evaluations of emerging technologies (Slovic 1992, Kahan et al. 2009). In line with this, we investigated whether the single-item measure for risk–benefit assessment could yield comparable results. We substituted both perceived benefits and privacy concerns in the research model with the following survey item: $^ { \prime \prime } \mathrm { O n }$ the whole, the benefits of the app will outweigh the risks.” The model explains 44% of the variance in the holistic risk–benefit assessments and 62% of the variance in use intention. The results, as detailed in Online Appendix $\mathrm { G } ,$ are consistent with our findings. However, the moderating effect of group, although in the anticipated direction, is less significant.

Last, we used Multivariate Analysis of Variance as an alternative method to test the moderating effects of cultural worldviews, given the correlation between perceived benefits and privacy concerns. The results are consistent with our findings: For individuals with either a high-group worldview or a low-grid worldview, the effect of DCT awareness on perceived benefits is more apparent, as is the effect of DCT awareness on privacy concerns.

## 5.4. Cultural Worldviews and Continuance Intention

Our main study focuses on the influence of cultural worldviews on the intention to use DCT. In an extension of this focus, we explored how these cultural worldviews might also impact the intention to continue using the app among individuals who had already installed it. We collected the data alongside the main study and received 138 valid responses from individuals who had installed DCT. These responses make up approximately 12% of the total 1,152 respondents, a proportion that is consistent with the generally low utilization rate of DCT (Hecht-Felella 2021). They were not included in the analyses for the main study.

Online Appendix H provides details on our methodology and data analysis. The model accounts for 23% of the variance in perceived benefits and 40% in the intention to continue using DCT: both significantly lower than in the main study. It also accounts for 27% of the variance in privacy concerns, which is a result closer to that of the main study. Although the group worldview significantly influences users’ privacy concerns and moderates the impact of DCT awareness on these concerns, neither group nor grid has a significant effect on perceived benefits or on moderating the relationship between DCT awareness and perceived benefits.

The results imply that cultural worldviews may exert a stronger influence on the adoption of novel technology among individuals lacking personal experience with it. This phenomenon is likely due to the uncertainty about the potential risks and benefits of the technology. As users interact more extensively with the technology and gain hands-on experience, the previously unclear benefits of the technology become more concrete. However, the hands-on experience might not entirely dispel uncertainties about how the technology handles and safeguards personal data. Consequently, privacy concerns could persist, akin to the concerns experienced by nonusers. Nonetheless, due to the constrained sample size of DCT users, a comprehensive comparison between users and nonusers regarding the impact of cultural worldviews was not feasible in this study. Future research may delve into this topic.

## 5.5. Cultural Worldviews and the Bias in Media Choice for IT Information

In developing Hypothesis $3 , \ \mathrm { a - d } ,$ , we focused on the notion that individuals are likely to acquire information about DCT from sources and content that align with their cultural worldviews. To provide further support for this argument, we examined whether group and grid influence respondents’ selection of news outlets from which they frequently obtained information on IT. We selected 15 outlets (CNN, Huff Post, New York Times, CBS, NBC News, Guardian, BBC, NPR News, AP News, New York Post, Wall Street Journal, Washington Times, Fox News, BlazeMedia, and CBN News) based on the AllSides Media Bias Chart.<sup>9</sup> These outlets include three from each of the five categories: left, lean left, center, lean right, and right. Respondents reported how often they accessed information about information technologies from each outlet using a five-point Likert scale, ranging from one (never) to five (always).

We used the rank-order centroid method (Barron and Barrett 1996) to derive weights for each news outlet based on its position along the left-to-right spectrum. The weights were then used to aggregate individual ratings to generate a score for media choice. A higher value of this score suggests a preference for left-leaning media, whereas a lower value suggests a preference for rightleaning media. The calculated bias in media choice ranges from �9 to 15, with a mean of 1.63 and a standard deviation of 2.84. Notably, the score shows significant correlations with group $( r = 0 . 4 6 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ) and grid $( r =$ $- 0 . 5 0 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$

The linear regression analysis, controlling for age, gender, education, and income, reveals that both the group $( \beta = 0 . 2 2 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ) and the grid worldviews $( \beta = - 0 . 3 9 ,$ $p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ) significantly influence media choice for technological information. In our sample of 1,041 respondents, individuals with a high-group worldview tend to favor left-leaning media, whereas those with a high-grid worldview show a stronger inclination toward rightleaning media. These findings align with previous research on news outlet preferences for climate change information (Newman et al. 2018).

We further observed discernible correlations between the bias in media choice and perceived benefits, privacy concerns, and intention $( r = 0 . { \bar { 3 } } 1 , - 0 . 2 3 ,$ , and 0.31, respectively; $p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . However, when we introduced bias in media choice into the research model (Figure 1) as an antecedent to perceived benefits, privacy concerns, and use intention (excluding DCT awareness), the effects of bias were all insignificant $( \beta = 0 . 0 2 , 0 . 0 2 ,$ , and 0.004, respectively; $p { > } 0 . 1 )$ ). Without controlling for cultural worldviews on perceived benefits and privacy concerns, the effects of bias in media choice on these two constructs became significant $( \beta = 0 . 1 8 \mathrm { a n d } - 0 . 1 2 ,$ respectively; $p <$ 0.001). Additionally, the correlation between bias in media choice and DCT awareness was insignificant $( r =$ 0.03, $p { > } 0 . 1 )$ , suggesting individuals who prefer leftleaning media and those who prefer right-leaning media exhibit comparable levels of DCT awareness. These findings imply that individuals, guided by their cultural worldviews, tend to seek out and engage with information sources and media content that resonate with their existing beliefs about DCT.

## 6. Discussion and Conclusions

Our study shows the significance of cultural worldviews in explaining diverse support for public IT use, particularly for pandemic response via DCT. Our findings highlight that cultural worldviews, characterized by group and grid, greatly influence perceived benefits and privacy concerns regarding DCT. These factors, in turn, shape intention to use DCT voluntarily. Perceived benefits and privacy concerns mediate the relationships between cultural worldviews and DCT use intention. Our study delineates the pathways through which cultural worldviews affect IT adoption.

Additionally, individuals with a high-group worldview or a low-grid worldview exhibit a more positive association between DCT awareness and perceived benefits, as well as a more negative association between DCT awareness and privacy concerns. This phenomenon can be attributed to selective exposure that is driven by individuals’ cultural worldviews. This notion is supported by our supplemental analyses, which show the link between individuals’ cultural worldviews and their bias in media choices for technological information, as well as the limited impact of this bias in explaining perceived benefits and privacy concerns when cultural worldviews are accounted for.

Furthermore, our supplemental analyses demonstrate the role of cultural worldviews in shaping privacy concerns of existing DCT users, which consequently influence their intention to continue using the technology. The broader impact of cultural worldviews was also observed in risk–benefit assessments of other emerging technologies addressing societal issues.

## 6.1. Contributions to Research

Our study makes three significant contributions to the IS literature. First, it underscores the paramount importance of cultural worldviews in influencing individuals risk–benefit assessments on DCT and other technologies. Although the IS literature has extensively explored risk–benefit assessments of technologies, most studies have focused on the impact of technological features (Online Appendix B) and have limitedly explored the role of individual differences in shaping these assessments. Particularly notable is the scarcity of research investigating the emergence of conflicting views on technologies designed to address societal issues. Our study offers insights into how cultural worldviews shape the risk–benefit assessments of such technologies and, consequently, the intention to adopt them. It thus contributes to explaining the origins of divergent viewpoints on the use of IT for public purposes.

Our study underscores the importance of cultural worldviews for the successful implementation of IT aimed at addressing societal issues. As technologies such as biometric identity authentication (Breward et al. 2017) and national identity verification (McGrath 2016) continue to emerge, an opportunity exists to delve deeper into the influence of cultural worldviews across various contexts of IT use. For example, Warkentin et al. (2017) found that individuals’ intention to use smart meters to combat climate change were relatively weak (mean � 3.02, on a five-point Likert scale), similar to the level in our study (mean � 2.41). Cultural worldviews may help explain this lack of interest. Our study offers an illustrative example of how to incorporate cultural worldviews within the realm of IT adoption research.

Second, this study introduces a novel perspective to the IS literature by revealing how cultural worldviews interplay with technology awareness, shaping individuals perceptions of a technology. This extends IS research on technology adoption, which traditionally hinges on the premise that technology awareness informs users about the benefits and corrects their misperceptions about the risks of the technology. Our study challenges this assumption from a selective exposure perspective, revealing that individuals are guided by their cultural worldviews when obtaining and interpreting technological information, thereby reinforcing their existing beliefs about the technology. Our supplementary study provides additional evidence for selective exposure, demonstrating that cultural worldviews influence individuals’ bias in selecting media for technological information and that such bias has limited power in explaining perceived benefits and privacy concerns. These results present a novel theoretical perspective in understanding the impact of technology awareness, particularly in the context of opinionated discussions on the benefits and risks of the technology.

Third, this study offers a new perspective on the formation of privacy concerns, contributing to the literature on privacy calculus (Smith et al. 2011). The existing literature has explored various antecedents to privacy concerns, including privacy experiences, privacy awareness, personality differences, demographic differences, and cultural factors (Smith et al. 2011). In particular, studies that focus on cultural differences in privacy concerns often rely on the roles of Hofstede’s (1991) indices of national culture. To the best of our knowledge, our study is among the first demonstrating the significant influence of cultural worldviews on privacy concerns of both potential and existing technology users. Although user experience with the technology may reduce uncertainty about its benefits, we argue that potential privacy violations stemming from the use of the technology pose an ongoing risk to individuals. Cultural worldviews are key to understanding these perpetual concerns. Our study introduces a new theoretical lens rooted in cultural cognition, which enriches our understanding of how individual values shape privacy concerns.

## 6.2. Implications for Practice

Recognizing the pivotal roles of cultural worldviews in steering technology assessments and adoption bears significant implications for the promotion and design of technologies. Our study underscores the importance of cultural worldviews in facilitating effective communication and fostering acceptance of emerging technologies like DCT. Organizations and government agencies often resort to risk–benefit appeals to promote technological solutions to societal issues, but they often underestimate the impact of cultural worldviews on individuals risk–benefit perceptions of these solutions. As Kahan et al. (2015) note, attempts to disseminate sound information about societal risks can backfire when such claims become infused with antagonistic cultural meanings, leading to conflict rather than consensus. Similarly, Amann et al. (2021) demonstrate that conflicting media messages about the role and modalities of DCT apps can create skepticism and lead to decreasing public trust in these technologies. Our study reveals that because individuals are likely to be exposed to and interpret information in line with their cultural worldviews, the mere presence of risk–benefit information about a proposed solution may not change their opinions and may instead further polarize their viewpoints.

Hence, effective communication of emerging technologies such as DCT should not only be informative, but also compatible with the cultural worldviews of the recipients. The information should be presented in a manner that accounts for the diverse viewpoints of the target audience (Kahan 2015). For example, positioning DCT as a means to achieve personal freedom and autonomy from the virus, instead of emphasizing improved disease control, may resonate with individualists by highlighting personal relevance and benefits. For egalitarians, communication could stress societal justice and privacy preservation rather than expert recommendations that trigger resistance to authority and social elites (Song 2014). Additionally, media campaigns aimed at increasing public familiarity with DCT (Zhang et al. 2020) should use a variety of media outlets so that the messaging can reach a broad audience.

Previous research (Trang et al. 2020) contends that to achieve widespread acceptance of DCT apps, designers must prioritize optimal specifications that account for the different benefits, privacy features, and user conveniences appealing to various user segments. The research deemphasizes traditional predictors of technology adoption, such as attitudes, traits, and demographics, viewing them as largely unalterable and therefore irrelevant. While we agree with the fundamental insights from Trang et al. (2020), our research highlights the crucial role of cultural worldviews in shaping user acceptance. Cultural worldviews shape users’ evaluations of an app’s benefits and risks, acting as cognitive filters that may compromise objective judgments about its design and usability. As a result, some users may be unwilling to engage with the app if it lacks features aligning with their values.

As an initial step, we recommend that designers integrate features that appeal to a broad cultural spectrum. For instance, features with community-centric information about virus control (i.e., societal benefits) may attract those with a high-group worldview, whereas features assuring data security and user control could appeal to more individualistic users. By crafting an app that recognizes and caters to these diverse cultural worldviews, designers can build a platform that appeals to the widest possible audience, thereby maximizing its social utility and adoption rates.

In addressing privacy concerns, app designers should enhance the transparency of data collection and storage processes and offer users control over their own data with the ability to delete personal information or remove their accounts. These features could be especially important for people who prioritize individual freedoms over collective benefits. The technology should address privacy concerns thoughtfully, in a manner consistent with diverse cultural worldviews.

## 6.3. Limitations and Future Studies

Several limitations could be addressed in future research. First, the model could be expanded to include the influences of other value systems such as personal values (Schwartz 1996). Also, this study mainly focuses on DCT apps and a single use scenario within the United States, assuming that the values embedded in designing such apps are consistent. Given that different technologies may be designed for different purposes and may embody heterogenous value systems, further research is needed to explore how the match between users’ cultural worldviews and the values embedded in app design affects user behavior.

Second, future research could consider other barriers to DCT adoption, such as long-term risks (Rowe 2020) and digital inequality (Rai 2020). For example, smartphone use rates are lower among seniors, who are more susceptible to numerous public health threats (Simko et al. 2022). Investigating how DCT impacts such populations could be an interesting avenue for future research. Further, considering the evolvement of the virus and the change of the circumstances like vaccine effectiveness, longitudinal studies are needed to explore the dynamic nature of individuals’ intention to use DCT apps over time.

Third, this study uses CB-SEM to test the relationships between the theoretical constructs. Future research could use the response surface method (Chau et al. 2020) to explore potential polynomial relationships among these constructs. Besides, our model shows weak effects of DCT awareness on perceived benefits and privacy concerns (Figure 2), despite their insignificant correlations (Table E.2). This may imply a potential “suppressor effect” (Fiedler 2021) of DCT awareness on the predictability of cultural worldviews. Future research may be conducted to gain additional insights in the roles of DCT awareness in driving the adoption of emerging technologies.

Fourth, it is important to note that cultural worldviews and political partisanship (such as the liberal–conservative scale) are distinct concepts. Previous research has shown that cultural worldviews encompass more diverse value dimensions than partisanship (Rachlinski 2021) and have greater predictive power regarding individuals’ views on environmental and technological issues (Kahan et al. 2007,

Kahan 2012, Newman et al. 2018). Regarding DCT, Zhang et al. (2020) find no difference between Republicans and Democrats in the United States in their support for the technology. However, further research could explore how partisanship, along with cultural worldviews, may influence the use of DCT apps.

Fifth, future research could examine how personal experiences might alter the effects of cultural values on technology perception, building on the findings of our supplementary study on DCT users. For example, previ ous studies have produced mixed findings regarding the effects of Hofstede’s (1991) national culture indices on users’ privacy concerns (Milberg et al. 2000, Bellman et al. 2004). Although researchers attribute these discrepancies to methodological factors (Bellman et al. 2004), our find ings from the supplementary study imply that a possible avenue for exploration could be whether the mixed effects of national culture indices, alike those of cultural worldviews, may arise from users’ personal experiences with the technology, particularly concerning privacy. These personal experiences might include the types of data being collected or incidents of data misuse. It may be crucial to systematically investigate whether these privacy-relevant encounters with technology act as bound ary conditions that influence the impact of national culture on privacy concerns, as they did for the influence of cultural worldviews on DCT users.

Last, our study examined the moderating effects of group and grid separately, in line with previous studies (Dabholkar and Bagozzi 2002). Questions such as which moderating effect is stronger (and why), and whether there is a three-way interaction with DCT awareness, are beyond the scope of this study but could be explored in future research.

## 6.4. Conclusion

Information technologies have been introduced as solu tions to tackle a diverse array of societal issues, including swift responses to public health crisis. The success of these technologies depends on public support and participation, yet individuals often hold differing and opposing views on the benefits and risks of the technologies. To understand the reasons underlying this conflict, we conducted the study in the context of using DCT fo the COVID-19 pandemic. Our findings highlight the key roles of cultural worldviews in shaping perceived benefits and privacy concerns, and their subsequent impact on individuals’ intention to use DCT. We further suggest that cultural worldviews may lead to selective exposure to technological information, making the impacts of technology awareness on risk and benefit perceptions contingent on cultural worldviews. Therefore, a proper integration of cultural meanings and information content may better motivate individuals to support the public use of emerging technologies.
