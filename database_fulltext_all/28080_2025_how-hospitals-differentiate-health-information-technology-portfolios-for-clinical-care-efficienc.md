# How Hospitals Differentiate Health Information Technology Portfolios for Clinical Care Efficiency: Insights from the HITECH Act

Abstract. Hospitals have implemented health information technology (HIT) for clinical care to address rising operating costs in recent years. We integrate behavioral and institutional perspectives to explain how hospitals differentiate technological search relative to industry peers (i.e., search differentiation) for HIT portfolios. In the context of the U.S. healthcare industry, we theorize that hospitals’ search differentiation for HIT results jointly from idiosyncratic learning in response to cost-based performance shortfalls and isomorphic pressures in relation to changing policy uncertainty as the Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health (HITECH) Act has unfolded. Based on a panel data set from 3,319 hospitals in 2007–2014, we demonstrate that when costs increase relative to aspiration level, a hospital differentiates its search for HIT by exploring more novel technologies for clinical care relative to peers. As policy uncertainty declines from the conceptualization phase to the enactment phase of the HITECH Act, a hospital’s search differentiation for HIT increases to a greater extent in response to cost-based performance shortfalls as lower uncertainty reduces the need to imitate peers’ search. As policy uncertainty further declines from the enactment phase to the enforcement phase of the HITECH Act and reaches its lowest level, however, the hospital’s search differentiation for HIT increases to a smaller extent in response to cost-based performance shortfalls because of policy incentives and professional norms to promote implementation of common technologies. Overall, we provide a more holistic picture of how uncertainty in a dynamic regulatory context intertwines with organizational learning from performance feedback in shaping search differentiation.

## Introduction

Hospitals have had to address a decline in the operational efficiency of clinical care as manifested by rising operating costs. With accelerating health information technology (HIT) targeted at clinical care, there has been an increasing push for hospitals to achieve cost savings and efficiency gains through HIT implementations (Menon and Kohli 2013, Burton-Jones and Volkoff 2017). Although the U.S. government has spent close to \$30 billion in the past several years to promote HIT implementations such as electronic health records (EHRs; Melville et al. 2004, Herman 2016), hospital operational processes exhibit significant efficiency problems. To address such gaps in operational efficiency, hospitals can select and deploy an HIT portfolio to innovate their operational processes (Fichman et al. 2011, Salge et al. 2015) and reduce clinical care costs (Buescher et al. 2004, Paulus et al. 2008).

Though imitating peers’ HIT portfolios is one straight forward option, hospitals may not always want to follow the technological search trajectory of their peers, especially when their operational efficiency is far below that of those peers. By differentiating an HIT portfolio with new technologies earlier than peers, a hospital facing larger cost gaps will be more willing to take risks in fueling its process innovations in clinical care with cutting-edge HIT capabilities to improve operational efficiency. Implementing an HIT portfolio that has been widely used by peers in an industry is not the most effective solution to narrow any gaps because only resources specific to an organization can contribute to its relative advantage (Carr 2003). In practice, hospital managers will look for riskier but potentially more effective HIT portfolios when their hospitals fall short in operational efficiency by leveraging formal and informal interpersonal channels, government/ industry reports, data from professional and government organizations, and market intelligence to understand peers’ HIT implementations and search for alternative options. The information collected on HIT initiatives and peer performance is a social benchmark for hospitals to calibrate their efficiency relative to peers and to inform their decisions to implement HIT based on which technologies have or have not been widely adopted in peers’ IT portfolios.

Drawing on the behavioral theory of the firm (BTOF), a substantial body of strategic management literature has documented that organizations search for solutions in response to performance shortfalls—that is, performance below aspiration level that defines whether a performance outcome is satisfactory (Cyert and March 1963, Argote and Greve 2007).<sup>1</sup> When performance decreases relative to aspiration level, organizations search for solutions such as research and development investment (Greve 2003a), strategic alliances (Baum et al. 2005), divestitures (Shimizu 2007), mergers and acquisitions (Iyer and Miller 2008), capital investments (Souder and Bromiley 2012), risk taking (Shyam Kumar et al. 2014), market entry (Ref and Shapira 2017), and new product introductions (Ren et al. 2022). Information systems (IS) researchers have also drawn on BTOF to examine how organizations respond to performance shortfalls by searching for IT solutions. As a notable example, Salge et al. (2015) showed that hospitals increasingly invest in HIT in response to performance shortfalls. Similarly, Anand et al. (2020) documented that hospitals’ performance shortfalls trigger search for decision support systems, especially those with business intelligence and analytics. More recently, Dong et al. (2021) demonstrated that the relationship of performance shortfalls and IT investment is contingent on corporate governance.

However, prior IS researchers have primarily focused on how much was invested in IT in response to performance shortfalls (i.e., intensity of technological search), so much less is known about what IT organizations invest in and when they do so in response to performance shortfalls (i.e., direction of technological search).

Given the variety in HIT targeting different aspects of patient care in the healthcare context to reduce operating costs, hospitals may differentiate in their search for a portfolio of technologies to address performance shortfalls. Two hospitals investing similar amounts in HIT may implement different technologies at different times. There is hence a need to move IS research on technological search from the earlier stages of resource allocation to later stages of IT implementation. The direction of technological search regarding the extent to which an organization implements a specific technology earlier than its peers—a reference group of competing organizations in the same industry (Gavetti et al. 2012, Dong 2021)—needs to be understood. We call such organizational exploration of technological search by implementing technologies earlier than peers as search differentiation. A better understanding of search differentiation for technology is theoretically and practically important because (1) past IS research on technological search primarily focused on IT investment intensity in response to performance shortfalls, but it is crucial to understand what technologies are implemented and when to remedy performance gaps; (2) past IS research on technological search paid less attention to external institutional governance, whereas an institutional perspective is particularly useful to explain search differentiation and needs to be integrated into our understanding; and in the healthcare context, (3) new knowledge on search differentiation can inform not only decision making in hospitals but also policymaking for healthcare.

Two distinct theoretical perspectives with limited reconciliation have appeared in prior literature to explain how an organization conducts technological search relative to peers. On the one hand, BTOF proposes that organizations learn from performance feedback in comparison with aspiration level (e.g., social benchmark based on peer performance), which leads to unique trajectories of technological search according to specific organizational needs (Cyert and March 1963). On the other hand, institutional theory suggests that organizations are embedded in the institutional environment where isomorphic pressures create social convergence that drives their trajectories of technological search in an isomorphic manner (DiMaggio and Powell 1983). We suggest that BTOF does not provide a holistic picture to understand search differentiation because the theory primarily focuses on technological search in response to performance shortfalls based on an organization’s specific aspiration level. Whereas BTOF acknowledges the tendency of decision makers to avoid uncertainty in the environment (Cyert and March 1963), institutional theory better explains the substantial external constraints on technological search because of uncertainty of policy interventions (DiMaggio and Powell 1983). Regarding how organizations search for technologies relative to peers, institutional theory provides a complementary view of how institutional mechanisms can direct technological search by organizations. The institutional environment could be changed by governments through targeted policy interventions, which are salient in the healthcare context, to facilitate or elicit a particular behavior of hospitals such as technological search (Field 2006). In contrast to firm-specific search processes as suggested by BTOF, isomorphic pressures arising from policies can play an important role in promoting or inhibiting social convergence against organizations’ search differentiation. In light of the plausible complementarities of these two theories regarding the social angle explaining an organization’s trajectories of technological search relative to peers, we draw on both perspectives to investigate hospitals’ search differentiation in the HIT domain.<sup>2</sup>

Our theoretical development integrates insights on technological search from both BTOF and institutiona theory to understand how cost-based performance shortfalls and policy uncertainty jointly drive search differentiation. In our research context, operating costs represent a key performance goal indicating a hospital’s operational efficiency.<sup>3</sup> Consistent with our objective to understand a hospital’s search differentiation for HIT relative to peers and the social nature of the institutional lens, we focus on social comparison with explain how a hospital responds in searching for a better HIT portfolio to address cost-based performance shortfalls in line with the prior IS literature (e.g., Salge et al. 2015). This is particularly suitable for theorizing based on BTOF in the healthcare context because hospitals are highly regulated at the industry level and tend to make decisions in light of peers’ actions. Although the premise of policy interventions plausibly affecting technological search is derivative of institutional theory, it is important to extend our understanding by considering policy intervention as a dynamic process, progressing from the phases of conceptualization to enactment to enforcement, rendering different levels of policy uncertainty in the institutional context where organizations search for HIT. Therefore, we consider policy uncertainty—that is, environmental instability related to diverse understanding of and opinions on a policy from stakeholders (Baker et al. 2016) such as decision makers of hospitals, physicians, nurses, and the medical community—as a key timevariant contextual factor pertaining to institutional pressures, which interacts with hospital-specific performance shortfalls to shape search differentiation for HIT.<sup>4</sup> As a major policy targeting HIT implementations in hospitals, we use the Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health (HITECH) Act as the focal policy intervention in this study. The HITECH Act primarily aims to promote hospitals adoption and meaningful use of HIT for reducing the costs of patient care (U.S. Department of Health and Human Services 2017), consistent with our focus on operating costs as the performance goal of hospitals to improve operational efficiency of clinical care.<sup>5</sup>

In our empirical study, we focus on the U.S. healthcare industry. We combine data from multiple sources to construct a large-scale panel data set of 11,316 hospital-year observations from 3,319 hospitals in 2007–2014. Our research design enables us to examine how a hospital’s search differentiation for HIT, manifesting as implementations of new technologies relative to peers in its HIT portfolio, is collectively determined by cost-based performance shortfalls and policy uncertainty across temporal phases of the HITECH Act. Overall, we contribute a synthesized understanding of how hospitals undertake technological search relative to peers, in which their search differentiation for HIT is jointly shaped by idiosyncratic learning and isomorphic pressures.

## Search Differentiation for HIT

Increasing demands of the patient population and economic challenges as manifested by the rising costs of patient care have made it imperative for hospitals to redesign their clinical processes for clinical care—both what is done (i.e., what care is provided) and how it is done (i.e., when, where, and by whom care is delivered). To this end, HIT has been useful to clinicians in discovering, modeling, analyzing, measuring, and improving direct and indirect care processes (Carter 2015). Research has shown that HIT implementations can enhance a hospital’s efficiency in terms of operating costs (Agarwal et al. 2010, Fichman et al. 2011). Specifically, HIT can help hospitals reduce expenses associated with patient records, improve workflows by reducing the need for staff, and streamline diagnostics, which can improve productivity by eliminating inefficiency. For example, the implementation of a decision support system reduced unnecessary blood transfusions and associated costs (Perez et al. 2007). Therefore, we focus on HIT portfolios pertaining to information processing and coordination in clinical care processes for (1) bar coding, (2) electronic documentation, (3) order entry, (4) result viewing, and (5) decision support.

In line with the idea that HIT implementations enable cost reduction, BTOF implies that technological search conducted by hospitals is unique to their specific cost-saving needs. Hospitals facing greater cost gaps relative to peers are more willing to search for new technologies earlier than peers as a potentially more effective solution to reduce costs. Hospitals are also likely to be more risk-taking in searching for solutions and more willing to explore new technologies when experiencing greater cost gaps (Greve 2003a). For example, a hospital might choose to deploy radiofrequency identification tags on prescriptions to track medication distribution earlier than peers if it faces a large cost gap. As a result, hospitals differentiate in technological search not just with respect to what technologies are implemented but also with respect to when to implement these technologies (Spetz and Baker 1999). In other words, hospitals can differentiate in their search for HIT and change of HIT portfolios to remedy large cost gaps and build a competitive edge by implementing new technologies that peers have not yet widely used.

Accordingly, we define search differentiation for HIT as a hospital’s implementations of new health information technologies for clinical care relative to the technologies implemented by peers. Compared with the prior IS literature (e.g., Salge et al. 2015, Anand et al. 2020, Dong et al. 2021), our focus on search differentiation is unique in capturing the direction of search relative to peers, rather than the intensity of search relative to peers. This focus is particularly suitable for our research given that we take a social angle, integrating behavioral and institutional perspectives, to understand hospitals’ search for HIT relative to peers. By implementing new technologies earlier than peers, a hospital experiencing larger cost gaps will be more willing to take risks and fuel its HIT-enabled process innovations with cutting-edge technologies to improve operational efficiency of clinical care. Although imitating peers’ HIT portfolios is a straightforward option, implementing new technologies that have not yet been widely used by peers can potentially provide the most effective way to narrow gaps. Even if a hospital imitates the HIT portfolios of top performers, their technologies may not be the most effective for improving its operational efficiency.<sup>6</sup> This is because effective HIT-enabled process innovations must be grounded in an organization’s own facts and specifics (Swanson and Ramiller 2004). Simply following others’ HIT portfolios misses firm-specific resources and capabilities and idiosyncratic learning and therefore can be less effective.

Consider the example of Emory Hospitals. As an Atlanta-based healthcare leader, it is actively embracing digital technologies to address operational efficiency gaps. Emory’s strategy leverages generative artificial intelligence to streamline recording clinical notes by instantly transcribing medical dialogues between patients and physicians, ensuring efficient capture and seamless integration of these notes into the electronic medical records system.<sup>7</sup> This initiative underscores Emory’s focus on harnessing innovative digital technologies to enhance the efficiency of clinical care documentation. Alongside other hospitals, Emory gathers insights on peer practices and HIT trends through various channels, interpersonal networks (including board members and employee transfers from peer institutions) as well as government and industry reports from entities like the Office of the National Coordinator for Health Information Technology, the Healthcare Information and Management Systems Society, the American Hospital Association (AHA), and the Medicaid program. Market intelligence from vendors and consulting firms further informs understanding of operational efficiency and HIT-enabled process innovation. This comprehensive approach helps hospitals like Emory formulate their aspirations for improving operational efficiency. Through the adoption of cutting-edge technologies, they move beyond merely replicating the technologi cal choices of others. This enables them to envision and fundamentally transform clinical care processes, resulting in a relative advantage. Emory’s digital initiative exemplifies this approach to configuring HIT portfolios by hospitals, striving for enhanced operational efficiency in novel ways.

## Cost-Based Performance Shortfalls and Search Differentiation for HIT

Although prior research has documented that performance shortfalls can increase IT investment (Salge et al. 2015, Dong et al. 2021), intensity of investment does not capture other important aspects of organizational response to performance shortfalls. As mentioned earlier, it is important to consider what technologies are implemented and when relative to peers: two hospitals with similar amounts of HIT investment can considerably differentiate their HIT portfolios in terms of what technologies are implemented and when. Following the logic of BTOF, when a hospital experiences cost increases relative to aspiration level stemming from peers’ cost levels, it increases its search for HIT to save costs by improving operational efficiency in clinical care processes. We suggest that the hospital is more willing to implement new cutting-edge technologies earlier than peers to deliver efficient care, eliminate unnecessary testing, and systematically streamline delivery of care, all of which have greater potential to reduce operating costs. For example, clinical reports reviewing can save time by locating laboratory reports more readily and exchanging patient information more quickly, a decision support system can save time by standardizing treatment protocols for particular conditions, and a computerized physician order entry (CPOE) system can reduce costly errors when prescribing medication. In response to cost-based performance shortfalls, the hospital will not only increase its HIT investment as documented by prior literature (Salge et al. 2015, Dong et al. 2021) but, more importantly, will also implement new technologies earlier than peers to change its HIT portfolio. We argue that implementing technologies before peers is risky but also likely to be effective in remedying a large cost gap. Driven by a large gap between costs and aspiration level, the hospital aiming to be more cost-efficient is likely to take higher risks by differentiating its HIT portfolio to leverage scarce but advanced technologies earlier than peers. In light of greater cost-based performance shortfalls, a hospital is likely to deploy an HIT portfolio of newer technologies that peers have not yet widely used. Therefore, we hypothesize the following.

## Policy Uncertainty of the HITECH Act

Institutional theory suggests that an organization’s institutional environment can influence organizations scope of actions, including how they conduct technological search (Meyer and Rowan 1977, DiMaggio and Powell 1983, Oliver 1991, Scott 1995). In particular, policy interventions can facilitate or constrain organizational decisions and actions either by incentivizing organizations or penalizing them for lack of legitimacy and compliance (Williamson 1985, Levy and Spiller

1994, Scott 2008). Policy uncertainty has been found to play a salient role in affecting technology investments by organizations (Baker et al. 2016, Weigelt and Shittu 2016), whereas how policy uncertainty influences tech nological search jointly with performance shortfalls has not yet been examined. Given the regulated nature of healthcare, we suggest that policy uncertainty can influence hospitals’ search differentiation for HIT (Chiasson and Davidson 2005). Whereas BTOF acknowledges the tendency of decision makers to avoid uncertainty in the environment (Cyert and March 1963), institutional theory maintains that the external constraints on technological search can be substantial because of uncertainty of policy interventions (DiMaggio and Powell 1983). In our research context, policy uncertainty of the HITECH Act, a policy intervention that targets HIT implementations by hospitals to reduce costs, can play a critical role in how hospitals differentiate their search for HIT within a changing regulatory context. Rather than being static, policy uncertainty over the temporal progression of the HITECH Act from the conceptualization phase to the enforcement phase can affect a hospital’s search differentiation for HIT in conjunction with cost-based performance shortfalls. Next, we describe policy uncertainty associated with the temporal phases of the HITECH Act (see Figure 2).

Conceptualization Phase. This phase of the HITECH Act began when politicians and policymakers put forth ideas, reflecting diverse views on the policy. The act first gained momentum as part of a presidential election campaign promise by the candidate Barack Obama (New York Times 2007). During his campaign and inaugural addresses, he pledged to improve the quality of patient care and drive down costs by streamlining process inefficiencies through HIT (Obama 2009). The HITECH Act is considered to be one of the most significant regulatory overhauls of the U.S. healthcare system (Cohen et al. 2015), using a program of reimbursements to incentivize HIT implementations by hospitals (Cohen et al. 2004). President Obama fulfilled his election pledge when the bill was introduced to the House of Representatives in early January 2009 and passed in February 2009 (U.S. Congress 2009). We bound this first phase to before 2009, defining it as the conceptualization phase characterized by indefiniteness about the act, with many opportunities for the policy to be defeated. A proposed bill could stall in committee with debates among stakeholders, or it could be thwarted in either chamber of Congress (House or Senate), or it could make it to the president’s desk only to be vetoed (USA.- Gov 2021). Thus, in the conceptualization phase, there was high policy uncertainty associated with the HITECH Act (Milliken 1987, Hoffmann et al. 2009).

Enactment Phase. The HITECH Act became law when it was passed by Congress and signed by President Obama in 2009 (U.S. Congress 2009). However, new laws take time to implement, sometimes years. We bracket 2009–2011 as the phase when the policy was enacted but not enforced. Although hospitals affected by the legislation were being made aware of the contents of the HITECH Act, they were not yet subject to its terms and could not receive incentive payments (HITECH Act 2009). The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) invited public comments on its proposed rules regarding the criteria to qualify for incentive payments, the calculation of incentive payments, and payment adjustments under Medicare for hospitals failing to meet requirements (Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services 2010). During this phase, the public as well as hospitals and other professional healthcare organizations had the opportunity to express concerns or support for aspects of the incentive program. After an open period of public comments, CMS aggregated the responses and published a rule on the final requirements (Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services 2010). Therefore, policy uncertainty decreased from the conceptualization phase to the enactment phase as the terms of the policy became much clearer to all stakeholders.

Enforcement Phase. This final phase began with the enforcement of the HITECH Act—that is, the law was fully executed—and hospitals were able to receive incentives payments. Though the first final rule establishing the incentive program guidelines was published in 2011, hospitals would not receive incentive payments until 2012 (Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services 2012). The incentive program evolved in stages in the enforcement phase: Stage 1 for data capture and sharing (2011–2014), Stage 2 for advanced clinical processes (2013–2014), and Stage 3 for improved outcomes (2015). Prior to each stage, proposed rules were published, received public comments, and were adapted (Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services 2010, 2012, 2014). Although some rules were debated via public comments, the policy had become very clear at the beginning of the enforcement phase with convergence in understanding, representing the lowest level of policy uncertainty in the policy’s temporal progression.

## The Moderating Role of Policy Uncertainty

Drawing on institutional theory, we claim that a hospital’s search differentiation for HIT in response to cost-based performance shortfalls depends on the policy uncertainty of the HITECH Act. Given the differences in level of policy uncertainty across the three phases of the HITECH Act, a change in policy uncertainty arising from a transition from one phase to another may strengthen or weaken a hospital’s search differentiation for HIT driven by cost-based performance shortfalls. Conceptualization Phase to Enactment Phase. A policy begins with conceptualization, a phase with high policy uncertainty due to ambiguous contents and unclear outcomes. Institutional theory suggests that when there is high uncertainty in the institutional environment, mimetic isomorphism is a dominant coping mechanism for organizations (DiMaggio and Powell 1983, Oliver 1991). In the context of the HITECH Act, prior to the passage of the law (pre-2009), there was high policy uncertainty stemming from the ambiguity of the contents of the act, as well as the possibility of its stalling or failing in legislative committee. To cope with high policy uncertainty, a hospital imitates peers in its search for HIT: decision makers use various channels to learn what technologies are implemented by peers to reduce costs and implement commonly deployed technologies in its HIT portfolio, rather than explore less-deployed new ones. For instance, a hospital seeking to reduce its cost gap relative to aspiration level may learn that a majority of its peers have deployed CPOE to effectively coordinate the flow of orders across multiple departments and proceeds to deploy CPOE to cope with high uncertainty as to what technologies may be promoted by the HITECH Act. The coercive and normative mechanisms through which isomorphic change occurs are unlikely to have a strong influence in this phase, because implementing the policy is in its infancy, with extensive debates and without compliance mechanisms and professional norms. In sum, the mimetic mechanism makes hospitals’ HIT portfolios more homogeneous.

As the HITECH Act transitioned from the conceptualization phase to the enactment phase in early 2009, the structure and contents of the policy were clearer to hospitals. With a decline in policy uncertainty, there is a reduced need for hospitals to mimic their peers in search for HIT. Moreover, the coercive and normative mechanisms were not materially influenced because compliance and professional norms were still developing. Accordingly, hospitals experiencing cost-based performance shortfalls imitated peers’ HIT portfolios to a lesser extent, promoting greater search differentiation as the HITECH Act transitioned. Therefore, we expect hospitals to increase search differentiation for HIT in response to cost-based performance shortfalls as policy uncertainty declines between the conceptualization phase and the enactment phase. We thus hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 2a. With a decline in policy uncertainty from the conceptualization phase to the enactment phase, a hospital’s search differentiation for HIT in response to cost-based performance shortfalls increases.

Enactment Phase to Enforcement Phase. The enact ment phase represents the period between the passing of the law and the implementation of compliance mechanisms such as incentives and penalties. In this phase, the policy is reviewed and discussed through an iterative process such as proposed rules, public opi nions, and final rulings to reach consensus. Although the conditions to which hospitals will be held were established,<sup>9</sup> the HITECH Act in this phase did not include coercive pressures because hospitals did not yet have to comply with the policy, and professional norms were in development with professional organi zations expressing support for or against provisions of the policy.<sup>10</sup> As the HITECH Act progressed from the enactment phase (2009–2011) to the enforcement phase (beginning in 2012), policy uncertainty reached the lowest level as agreement was reached on the contents of the act, further weakening the need to mimic peers HIT portfolios. Instead, coercive and normative mechanisms escalated and became the dominant isomorphic mechanisms.

In the period from 2012 onward, the incentive program of the HITECH Act was fully implemented and hospitals incentivized to implement certain promoted technologies. Specific contents of a policy and its incentives and penalties have been launched to promote hospitals’ adoption of specific technologies (Steinhauser et al. 2020). Thus, in hospitals’ search for HIT to address cost-based performance shortfalls by changing their HIT portfolios, incentive payments can orient their search toward promoted common technologies. Furthermore, the norms were more developed after 2012, as professional associations and social groups of physicians, nurses, and the medical community publicly endorsed the deployment of the technologies specified by the HITECH Act. The maturation of professional norms also reinforced hospitals’ search for HIT toward those promoted technologies widely used by other hospitals in response to cost-based performance shortfalls. Therefore, we expect that with growing coercive and normative pressures from the enactment phase to the enforcement phase, hospitals HIT portfolios become more similar when addressing cost-based performance shortfalls as policy uncertainty further declines to the lowest level. We thus hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 2b. With a further decline in policy uncertainty from the enactment phase to the enforcement phase, a hospital’s search differentiation for HIT in response to cost-based performance shortfalls decreases.

## Methodology Data

We constructed a panel data $\mathrm { s e t } ^ { 1 1 }$ from multiple archival sources: (1) the AHA annual survey, (2) the AHA IT survey, and (3) the CMS report. From the AHA annual survey, there were, on average, 6,305 hospitals responses each year, and from the AHA IT survey, there were, on average, 3,989 hospitals responses each year. We merged these two data sets based on the AHA hospital ID number and augmented the merged data with data from CMS using a hospital’s Medicare provider ID number. Our final data set with an unbalanced panel structure contained 11,316 observations from 3,319 hospitals in 2007–2014, which provided comprehensive information on HIT portfolios implemented by hospitals and their financial and demographic information. Taken together, the time span of our data captured the years before and after the HITECH Act suitable for testing our hypotheses. ## Measures

Search Differentiation for HIT. We operationalized our dependent variable based on HIT items (i.e., specific technologies in the area of HIT) extracted from the AHA IT survey (see the e-companion, Table EC-1) and imple mented in a hospital’s portfolio,<sup>12</sup> allowing for (1) bar coding, (2) electronic documentation, (3) order entry, (4) result viewing, and (5) decision support to reduce the costs of patient care. These computerized systems support clinical workflows by allowing for discovering, modeling, analyzing, measuring, improving, and optimizing direct and indirect clinical care processes (Carter 2015), saving costs by facilitating better information pro cessing and coordination (Agarwal et al. 2010, Fichman et al. 2011). Specifically, we used these items to create a Saidin index, which is a weighted sum of the number of technologies implemented in the hospital, where the weight of each technology is its relative novelty in the industry as the percentage of hospitals that have no implemented the technology (Spetz and Maiuro 2004). The Saidin index is an appropriate measure of search differentiation for HIT because it increases with the addition of new technologies to a hospital’s portfolio, which have greater weight in computing the index (Spetz and Baker 1999). The earlier and the more a hos pital implements new technologies yet to be implemented by peers, the more the hospital differentiates its technological search for HIT relative to peers. This weighting allows the Saidin index to account for not just the quantity of technologies but also the novelty of tech nologies implemented by a hospital in comparison with peers. In other words, the Saidin index considers how a hospital’s technological search differs from its peers’ in terms of the speed and level of HIT implementations. $$

We then used these weights to compute the Saidin index $s _ { i , z , t }$ for hospital i in group z in year t:

$$
s _ {i, z, t} = \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {K} a _ {k, z, t} \tau_ {i, k, z, t}.\tag{2}

Cost-Based Performance Shortfalls. Although many financial aspects of hospitals are opaque, hospitals must keep beds, equipment, operating rooms, supplies, and staff to treat patients (Breyer 1987, Gaynor and Anderson 1995). Some of these aspects are generally fixed (beds, equipment, and operating rooms) over time; however, supplies and labor (staff) are more flexible and can fluctuate with varying levels of operational efficiency. Thus, the hospital’s reducing costs comes down to reducing operational expenses and increasing operational efficiency. The components necessary for operating a hospital in a given year can be broken down as payroll and employee benefits, which are labor-related expenses of the hospital; supply and other expenses, which are continual and thus vary with hospital operations; and depreciation and interest expenses, which account for the costs of wear and tear on the fixed assets and the interest payments made by the hospital, respectively. Accordingly, we measured a hospital’s operating costs as total facility expenses with six different components, as listed in Table 4.

BTOF suggests that an organization’s aspiration level is a unified performance goal, whereas social aspiration comes from the average performance of its reference group (Cyert and March 1963). One problem with using social aspiration to measure aspiration level is that when performance exceeds peer performance, it suggests that the organization is likely to lower its performance level back to that of its peers (Bromiley 1991). This unrealistic assumption is relaxed by replacing social aspiration when self-performance slightly exceeds peer performance (Bromiley 1991, Bromiley and Harris 2014). This is consistent with BTOF suggesting that organizations have limited managerial attention (Cyert and March 1963, Dong et al. 2021), which switches between a unified aspiration level equal to the average performance of peers when performance is below peer performance and more challenging selfperformance when performance is above or equal to peer performance. The rationale is that organizations with performance below peer performance need to catch up with peers, and organizations with performance above peer performance do not tolerate lower performance, but rather improve their own performance (Bromiley 1991, Deephouse and Wiseman 2000,

Park 2007). We therefore chose to measure aspiration level as switching attention between social aspiration and (slightly higher) historical aspiration under suitable conditions (Dong et al. 2021), and used social aspiration only as an alternative measure in a robustness check (Salge et al. 2015).

Bromiley (1991) and Bromiley and Harris (2014) used a 5% increase in an organization’s prior performance as the performance goal when its performance is above peer performance and found the results to be insensitive to moderate variations in this parameter. Recent simulation research confirms the robustness of results with varying values of this parameter (Dong 2021). Drawing on the BTOF literature, we used 0.95 to represent a 5% cost reduction and varied this parameter to 0.90 (10% reduction), 0.85 (15% reduction), and 0.80 (20% reduction). Policy Phases. We focus on the HITECH Act as the policy intervention (see Figure 2 for a timeline of the HITECH Act). As mentioned earlier, the period before 2009 was the conceptualization phase of the HITECH Act as the bill was proposed, introduced to Congress, and vigorously debated. The next phase of the HITECH Act was enactment in 2009–2011, when the policy was passed into law, but hospitals could not yet directly benefit from it. After 2011 was the enforcement phase of the HITECH Act, because the policy was then in full force, and hospitals could now receive incentive payments. We coded three dummy variables to capture these phases of the HITECH Act, with a value of one if a given year fell into a particular phase and zero otherwise.

We cross-validated the assumption that policy uncertainty progressively decreases over these phases by examining the proposed rules, public comments, and final rules published by CMS.<sup>17</sup> There were three major proposed rules linked with the Medicare and Medicaid programs’ Electronic Health Record Incentive Program.<sup>18</sup> We limited our examination of public comments to those made by hospitals and professional healthcare organizations and thus used the term “public comments” to mean only those made by hospitals and professional healthcare organizations and not individual citizens. Across the three proposed rules, we found a decrease in public comments submitted (2010: 951 submissions; 2012: 615 submissions; 2014: 591 submissions). This provided initial support for diminishing policy uncertainty associated with diverse opinions and perceptions across phases. We further conducted text mining analysis (Baker et al. 2016) on the final published rules, which include responses to submitted comments.<sup>19</sup> Across all three rules, we observed convergence on the various aspects of the policy. We came to this conclusion by examining uncertainty-related keywords that indicate how CMS responded to public comments such as “agree,” “accept,” “address,” “amend,” “clarify,” “disagree,” “discuss,” “modify,” and “suggest.”<sup>20</sup> For example, “[CMS] Response: We disagree. We believe these are fundamentally different types of information” (Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services 2010, p. 44367), “[CMS] Response: … We address the potential barrier of limited Internet access in the comment regarding a broadband exclusion below” (Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services 2012, p. 54009), and “[CMS]

Response: We agree that some clarification is necessary regarding what we meant by ‘not able to fully implement’” (Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services 2014, p. 52920). Across the uncertainty-related keywords, we observed a clear overall decrease in keyword usage as a percentage of total words used in each final rule; in 2010 it was 5.98% on average, in 2012, 1.88%, and in 2014, 0.69%.<sup>21</sup> This gave us further evidence of convergence to a shared understanding of the contents of the policy requirements, and thus a decrease in policy uncertainty surrounding the contents of the policy. All of this taken together supported that our identified phases, bounded by distinct policy events (passing of the HITECH Act and the start of incentive payments), are reasonable proxies of policy uncertainty.

Control Variables. We specified several variables pertaining to hospital characteristics as control variables: Teaching Status (whether the hospital is a teaching hospital; Jha et al. 2005), Nonprofit Status (whether the hospital is nonprofit or for profit; Jha et al. 2005), and Rural Location (whether the hospital is in a rural or urban location; Goldman and Dudley 2008). We also controlled Percent Medicare Days measured by total Medicare days per total inpatient days (Reiter et al. 2012), Caseload as a proxy of the quality of patient care measured by total admissions per full time personnel (Bly et al. 1986), and Capital Expenses as logged expenses to acquire assets such as buildings, remodeling projects, equipment, and properties (Dranove et al. 2014). We controlled Hospital Size, measured by the logged num ber of general hospital beds (Goodstein et al. 1994), and Physician Staffing, measured by the logged number of full-time physicians on the hospital payroll (Miller and Tucker 2014), Additionally, we controlled Research Focus if the hospital has an organized research program in any of the following areas: basic research, clinical research, community health research, or research on innovative healthcare delivery (Miller and French 2016). We controlled the Number of Hospitals in System as the logged number of hospitals in a hospital’s system that may vary after mergers and acquisitions (Miller and Tucker 2014),<sup>22</sup> Physician Ownership Status measured by a binary variable indicating whether the hospital is physician owned in whole or in part by physician or a physician group (Dranove et al. 2014), insurance providers measured by whether a hospital has a formal written contract that specified the obligations of each party with a health maintenance organization (HMO) or preferred provider organization (PPO) (Miller and Tucker 2014), and Change in Leadership measured by whether there was a change in the chief administrator from the previous year (Alexander et al. 2007). For finan cial variables, we controlled Financial Slack measured by current cash on hand and in banks to total current liabilities (Greve 2003a),<sup>23</sup> Fixed Assets as the logged original value of land and physical properties consumed in economic activity by the hospital less accumulated depreciation and amortization, and Net Patient Revenue as the logged gross revenue minus contractual allowance and discounts on patient accounts. Finally, we included year dummies controlling for other industrywide shocks in each specific year .<sup>24</sup>

## Robustness Checks

Endogeneity Tests. Although the hospital fixed effects model specification with a large set of control variables can safeguard against endogeneity, possible omitted variables can still bias our results. Despite it being possible to address endogeneity by using instrumental variables, the BTOF literature provides no clue on valid instruments, given the complex nature of performance shortfalls. In a robustness check, we followed prior studies (Salge et al. 2015) and employed a system general method of moments (GMM) estimator by leveraging internal instruments from a dynamic panel model (Blundell and Bond 1998; also see the e-companion, Table EC-2). Because our moderator phases are binary variables representing the various time periods of the HITECH

Act, we could only progressively run system GMM to estimate the effect of cost-based performance shortfalls on search differentiation for HIT by restricting a model to each phase. Model A represents a model based on the full sample, Model B represents a model for the subsample in the conceptualization phase, Model C represents a model for the subsample in the enactment phase, and Model D represents a model for the subsample in the enforcement phase. We used one-year lags of the regressors and the control variables as instruments and found the effect of cost-based performance shortfalls on search differentiation for HIT to be statistically significant and different across phases, as we had theorized (p < 0.01 in chi-square tests). To directly rule out reverse causality, we forward-lagged search differentiation for HIT by one year (see the e-companion, Table EC-3). The effect of cost-based performance shortfalls on search differentiation for HIT in the subsequent year is still statistically significant, yet slightly smaller than in the original model; thus, endogeneity is not a significant concern for our results.

Alternative Measures for Search Differentiation for HIT. As an alternative measure to the Saidin index, which considers both the speed and level of HIT implementations, we used the count of technologies in an HIT portfolio to measure the level of HIT implementation only. Using a negative binomial regression model specification, we found the results to be consistent with the main results (see the e-companion, Table EC-4). We also expanded the Saidin index to include additional external coordination technologies (see the e-companion, Table EC-1, 10 items in total) from the AHA IT survey, including electronically exchanging patient demographic information, clinical care summaries, laboratory results, medication history, and radiology reports with hospitals both inside and outside their systems, to compute a more comprehensive Saidin index. Using the new Saidin index again produced results consistent with the main results (see the e-companion, Table EC-5). Taken together, these results based on alternative measures support that our original Saidin index is valid for capturing the level and scope of a hospital’s HIT implementation. To confirm that search differentiation for HIT as measured by the Saidin index can reduce subsequent operating costs, we conducted an analysis using search differentiation for HIT as the independent variable on operating costs for one and two years later. We found a negative relationship between search differentiation for HIT and operating costs in subsequent periods (see the e-companion, Table EC-6), further supporting that search differentiation for HIT, which is driven by cost-based performance shortfalls and policy uncertainty, can indeed reduce future operating costs.

Alternative Measures for Cost-Based Performance Shortfalls. Following the BTOF literature (Greve 2003a, Anand et al. 2020), we first split cost-based performance shortfalls into two separate measures when costs were above and below aspiration level. Consistent with the main results, we found support for Hypothesis 1 fo both measures. Interestingly, we also found support for Hypotheses 2a and 2b when costs were above aspiration level—namely, more serious cost-based performance shortfalls (see the e-companion, Table EC-7). Next, we constructed multiple aspiration-level versions by chang ing the parameter of self-performance improvement from 0.95 (i.e., aspiration level to reduce costs by 5%) to 0.90, 0.85, and 0.80 (i.e., aspiration levels to reduce costs by 10%, 15%, and 20%, respectively). Consistent with the BTOF literature (e.g., Bromiley and Harris 2014, Dong 2021), we found the results to be insensitive to these variations in this parameter (see the e-companion, Tables EC-8, EC-9, and EC-10). Furthermore, as an alter native to our aspiration level measure of switching attention between social and (slightly higher) historical aspirations, we used social aspiration only in calculating cost-based performance shortfalls.<sup>26</sup> Again, we found consistent results in support of all hypotheses (see the e-companion, Table EC-11). Our results demonstrate that historical aspiration alone is not sufficient, and that switching and social aspirations drive a hospital’s search differentiation for HIT relative to peers. This is consistent with our premise that social comparison determines the degree to which a hospital differentiates by searching for new technologies earlier than peers, rather than self-comparison with its own performance history. Finally, we normalized cost-based performance shortfalls by the number of beds and, alternatively, the number of inpatient days. We again found that the results were insensitive to variations in this change (see the e-companion, Tables EC-12 and EC-13).

Sensitivity Analysis for Reference Groups. In the BTOF literature, a reference group is an organization’s industry or a group of other similar organizations (Bromiley and Harris 2014, Dong 2021). Thus, we ran several robustness checks using alternative reference groups based on hospitals’ nonprofit status, rural location, and affiliation with the same system under the assumption that hospitals with given characteristics will focus on other hospitals sharing those characteristics as the reference group when differentiating technological search and formulating aspiration level. Specifically, we recreated cost-based performance shortfalls with aspiration level and search differentiation for HIT using nonprofit hospitals, rural hospitals, and hospitals in the same system as alternative reference groups and then conducted the analyses.<sup>27</sup> With respect to cost-based performance shortfalls having a positive effect on search differentiation for HIT, we found supporting evidence for Hypothesis 1 across all alternative reference groups. With respect to the moderating effects of phases, we also observed similar patterns supporting Hypotheses 2a and 2b across all alternative reference groups (see the e-companion, Tables EC-14, EC-15, and EC-16, as well as Figures EC-1, EC-2, and EC-3). For aspiration level, we also considered hospitals’ ambition if they target top performers in the industry as the reference group (Dong 2021). We set the average performance of top performers above the 90th percentile as the reference group to recreate cost-based performance shortfalls and found that it produces more pronounced increases in the coefficients while supporting all hypotheses (see the e-companion, Table EC-17).

Randomized Years for Policy Phases. Because the dummy variables for policy phases were coded based on years, we tested the validity of our measure with a simulated random assignment of years to each hospital in our sample while keeping the number of years per hospital the same as in the real data. This involved creating placebo year distributions to compare with the real data. For example, consider hospital 1 with eight years of data in our sample. To generate its placebo year distribution, we randomized the eight years for this hospital from a uniform distribution without replacement (e.g., 2007 in the real data becomes 2010 in our placebo, 2008 in the real data becomes 2007, etc.). We then estimated our model with phases coded based on the new randomly assigned years. We ran this random procedure independently 500 times and found that the moderating effects of phases based on randomized years are not statistically significant (zero included in 95% confidence intervals). This simulation allowed us to create the counterfactual hypothesis and validate our measure for phases of the HITECH Act that capture the theorized changes of policy uncertainty rather than randomness.

Alternative Model Specifications. We also evaluated the robustness of the hospital fixed effects mode specifications. The Hausman test for Models A–D indicates that we should reject the null hypothesis that a random effects model specification was appropriate because the p-values for Models A–D were all less than 0.0001 (see the e-companion, Table EC-18). However, for completeness, we also provide the results of a hospi tal random effects model, which are largely consistent with the results of a hospital fixed effects model (see the e-companion, Table EC-19). Moreover, we checked how standard errors were clustered because there may still be some unexplained variations in our dependent variable that could be correlated across observations (Wooldridge 2002). We alternatively clustered around hospital service areas (HSAs), which were originally defined by the National Center for Health Statistics. An HSA is a single county or cluster of contiguous counties that are relatively self-contained with respect to hospital care. We found the results to be consistent (see the e-companion, Table EC-20).

We also used two alternative approaches to test Hypotheses 2a and 2b. In the first approach, we varied the baseline to create the dummy variables, where we specified one of the phases as the baseline and two dummies to differentiate membership in the other two phases against this baseline. In doing so, we evaluated three models varying conceptualization, enactment, and enforcement phases as the baseline, respectively (see the e-companion, Table EC-21 for details). In the second approach, we restricted the sample to two consecutive phases, with the preceding phase as the baseline. In doing so, we restricted the analysis to two split samples for the first and the last two phases only (see the e-companion, Table EC-22 for details). Both additional approaches produced consistent results, supporting Hypotheses 2a and 2b.

## Discussion

Our findings extend BTOF research in the HIT context by looking into how hospitals differentiate in search for HIT rather than intensity of search to change their HIT portfolios in response to cost-based performance shortfalls. More importantly, our findings reveal that the aforementioned relationship is conditional on isomorphic pressures with changing policy uncertainty, thereby elaborating the BTOF explanation with insights from institutional theory. Our findings support our theorization of the conditional and dynamic nature of institutional uncertainty, particularly policies, in altering the influence of cost-based performance shortfalls on search differentiation for HIT. With a decline in pol icy uncertainty in moving from the conceptualization phase to the enactment phase of the HITECH Act, hospitals increasingly differentiated search for HIT in response to cost-based performance shortfalls, resulting in imple mentations of newer technologies relative to peers in their HIT portfolios. However, with a further decline in policy uncertainty in moving from the enactment phase to the enforcement phase of the HITECH Act, hospitals decreasingly differentiated search for HIT in response to cost-based performance shortfalls, resulting in implementation of more common technologies similar to peers in their HIT portfolios. Overall, our results have important theoretical implications for hospitals’ search differentiation for HIT and for understanding how the dynamic process of policy development affects search outcomes, offering three main contributions.

## Theoretical Implications

First, this study extends IS research rooted in BTOF by focusing on understudied search differentiation for IT. By revealing how cost-based performance shortfalls can motivate a hospital to differentiate search when changing its HIT portfolio, we offer new insight into IT investment decisions (Salge et al. 2015, Anand et al. 2020, Dong et al. 2021). Whereas prior researchers have shown that performance shortfalls can trigger a hospital’s investment in IT, we extend this understanding by demonstrating the role of performance feedback in guiding a specific hospital’s search for new technologies that mav support more effective cost savings to improve efficiency, such as search differentiation for HIT. In our research context, assessing performance shortfalls requires monitoring the average cost level of clinical care as well as HIT implementations of its peers via various channels available to decision makers in organizations, as indicated in prior literature (Leiponen and Helfat 2010, Ølholm et al. 2015, Srinivasan et al. 2021). The information collected from diverse formal and informal channels on HIT initiatives and peer performance is a social benchmark to calibrate the relative costs of the hospital and inform its decisions to implement HIT based on what technologies have been widely adopted in peers’ IT portfolios. Different from prior studies focused on the intensity of IT investment driven by performance shortfalls, our research uncovers how performance shortfalls influence what technologies are chosen and when they are implemented in IT portfolios. We extend the concept of the IT portfolio in past research (e.g., Xue et al. 2012) by moving from intensity of IT portfolios to direction and timing of changing IT portfolios. Our use of search differentiation can unveil system-level insights into what and when specific technologies are added to IT portfolios that intensity-based conceptualizations (e.g., IT investment intensity) do not capture. This is important because two organizations investing similar financial resources in IT may yield two distinct IT portfolios comprising totally different technologies Therefore, viewing the issue through the lens of search differentiation offers a compelling perspective for future research to explore the composition of HIT portfolios. This includes examining the specific technologies integrated into these portfolios, looking at the timing of their incorporation, and producing insights on diverse IT choices made across different firms.

Second, we contribute a novel institutional perspective by illuminating the evolutionary nature of the pol icy landscape and isomorphic pressures arising from changing policy uncertainty in shaping external con straints on technological search in the healthcare con text. Using the lens of uncertainty stemming from the institutional environment (Baker et al. 2016, Weigelt and Shittu 2016), we explain how hospitals’ search dif ferentiation for HIT is constrained by policy interventions. Using the HITECH Act as the context, we show that as policy uncertainty changes with the temporal progression of policy development, hospitals differentiate search to a distinct extent in response to cost based performance shortfalls. Notwithstanding that our study contextualizes a policy intervention in the healthcare industry, the theoretical implications for the changing nature of policy uncertainty over the course of policy development could be applicable to other policy areas if they have similar evolutionary life cycles.<sup>28</sup> Our findings provide important implications for new policy developments: as policy uncertainty declines from the conceptualization phase to the enactment phase, hospitals decreasingly imitate each other because the policy does not promote any specific choices for HIT implementation and has also not been enforced with incentives or penalties, allowing hospi tals to differentiate search for new technologies to address their specific cost gaps. However, when the policy moves from the enactment phase to the enforce ment phase and policy uncertainty further declines, hospitals increasingly search for common technologies incentivized by policy and reinforced by professional norms, leading to a convergent search direction in response to cost gaps. A new policy can strongly promote deployment of desirable technologies, especially when uncertainty is low.<sup>29</sup> In advancing a dynamic view largely missing from past policy research, we present a fresh theoretical approach for researchers to uncover the deep structure of how technological changes in organizations and specifically hospitals unfold as policymaking evolves over time. Researchers may draw on this dynamic view to better understand how diversity in new technologies (versus a common base of technologies) could be promoted through pol icy intervention, because our findings imply that orga nizations may be triggered to explore diverse new technologies to solve problems when policy uncer tainty is moderate, and to exploit a common base of technologies when the policy and its accompanying incentives and penalties are enforced.

Finally, researchers can broadly apply our theoretical framework to understand organizational decisions related to technological innovation across different contexts other than healthcare. In doing so, we respond to a call for incorporating institutional theory with BTOF, which could be particularly fruitful for deepening our fundamental understanding of organizational decision making in technological innovation (Audia and Greve 2006, Gavetti et al. 2012). Although BTOF provides an important explanation of technological search through a process for solving firm-specific performance problems (i.e., problemistic search), it primarily discerns how search is triggered by performance shortfalls in idiosyncratic learning processes. Despite BTOF acknowledging uncertainty avoidance through negotiating with the environment, little is known regarding external constraints on search from institutional pressures. The institutional environment can be altered through policy interventions specially designed to promote a desired goal for how organizations behave in their search for technologies, allowing institutional theory to offer complementary insights into how institutional pressures influence search over time. We are among the first to empirically demonstrate these behavioral and institutional mechanisms interact to drive organizational decision making, with a unique focus on search differentiation as a joint outcome of idiosyncratic learning based on social comparison and isomorphic pressures leading to social convergence. Our study highlights that a social angle provides a common foundation to bridge complementary theoretical insights and theory integration. Our findings shed light on the scope of search implied by BTOF stemming from the institutional environment—the evolutionary policy landscape involving uncertainty—in explaining organizational decision making, requiring a multitheoretical perspective above and beyond BTOF. Only once we understand idiosyncratic learning and isomorphic pressures and how they interact can we form a more holistic picture of how organizations make strategic decisions regarding technological innovation.

## Managerial Implications

Insights from our study are critical to hospital administrators and managers working in the healthcare industry, who are increasingly being called upon to implement a hospital’s HIT portfolio to enhance efficiency and reduce costs. First, hospitals can direct their technological search with an external orientation based on performance feedback compared with peer performance by scanning the industry environment (Salge et al. 2015, Dong et al. 2021). When facing high demand to save costs, a hospital can differentiate its search toward newer technologies in support of an effective HIT portfolio. Constructing a reference group meaningfully based on industry peers or other hospitals that are similar in status, location, and group affiliation is a good strategy to derive an aspiration level against which performance feedback is compared to inform technological choices (Dong 2021). We suggest that hospital managers become more “extroverted” in monitoring external information to learn about technological innovation fo the industry and their organization (Tambe et al. 2012). They can do so by leveraging their formal and informal networks and other information sources such as government agencies, professional associations, and consulting companies, paying particular attention to the evolving cost levels for clinical care of their peers and possible dig ital solutions to cost issues. In making strategic decisions on technological search, hospital managers can also proactively scan and monitor the evolution of the policy, understand the degree of policy uncertainty, and adjust their HIT portfolios in terms of difference from or similarity to the technologies of their peers.

The insights from our findings are also likely to interest healthcare policymakers at the federal and state levels. Healthcare policymakers need to recognize that the reactions of hospitals will vary along with changing uncertainty in new policy developments. In particular, hospitals can alter their technology portfolios when they address performance problems under policy interventions and, more importantly, even prior to when legislation is formally signed into law. Novel explorations of technological innovation with high diversity in search across hospitals are most likely to occur when policy uncertainty is moderate, whereas at-scale adoptions of similar technologies across hospitals can already take place because of mimetic behaviors when policy uncer tainty is high during early policy development or when policy uncertainty is low when it is enforced. Because new policy developments take years in many countries, policymakers may want to promote target technologies by highlighting notable hospitals that have successfully implemented these technologies as role models for other hospitals to follow in early policy development (Angs et al. 2014). Coercive and normative forces come into play as policy uncertainty later decreases, providing another good opportunity to promote target techno logies by enforcing incentives and penalties under a legal framework. Policymakers could also monitor and leverage high search differentiation for HIT in the enactment phase of policy developments as unique learning opportunities to monitor successful cases from “field experiments” conducted by hospitals. They can learn from such field experiments to identify new tech nologies once they prove effective and promote them in the enforcement phase. Consequently, healthcare pol icymakers could consider the changing uncertainty of new policy developments when promoting desirable technological innovation in different ways over time.

## Limitations and Future Research

This study has some limitations. First, we chose an already heavily regulated industry undergoing a policy intervention that targets HIT implementation (i.e., the HITECH Act). Although the healthcare industry has been widely used as the research context of prior studies to test BTOF (Salge et al. 2015, Anand et al. 2020) it is also meaningful to generalize our findings. Researchers can look into contingencies stemming from other healthcare policy interventions such as the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act or the Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program, and outside the industry, such as the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), exploring their interactions with performance shortfalls driving search differentiation for HIT. Second, researchers could look more closely at temporal changes in various policy mechanisms such as incentives and penalties. For example, the GDPR also differs from the HITECH Act in terms of compliance mechanisms: the GDPR uses only penalties for noncompliance, whereas the HITECH Act used incentives at the beginning to encourage compliance and then switched to penalties for noncompliance. In institutional theory, policies tend to push organizations toward homogeneity through mimetic, coercive, and normative isomorphic pressures using various compliance mechanisms. We encourage future work that explores these isomorphic pressures and mechanisms in more detail and how they can be selectively deployed to promote sustainable technological innovation. Third, we focus on operating costs as a performance goal for operational efficiency, which is particularly meaningfu in our research context where hospitals are struggling with increased costs, and the policy intervention that we study is relevant to implementing HIT for improving cost efficiency. Researchers can explore other performance goals such as survival rates (Salge et al. 2015) and length of stay or revenue and profitability (Anand et al. 2020). Fourth, our operationalization of search differentiation for HIT is based on the Saidin index. This measure is particularly useful in capturing different technological choices made by organizations in terms of first mover advantage. Researchers may develop and consider alternative measures. Last but not least, our data were collected in 2007–2014 as the HITECH Act unfolded. Although it is appropriate to use this time period covering the life cycle of the HITECH Act across all three temporal phases, our data do not fully reflect recent progress in hospitals’ technologica search. Researchers may collect more recent data by choosing other policies to examine our findings.

## Conclusion

We showed how the changing uncertainty of the policy environment can influence responses by hospitals to address cost-based performance shortfalls via search differentiation for HIT. Specifically, we offered empirical evidence of the connection between cost-based performance shortfalls and a hospital’s search differentiation for HIT. We also identified how this relationship changed based on uncertainty associated with temporal phases of a policy intervention through the example of the HITECH Act. More broadly, these insights expand on the interconnected nature of the behavioral and institutional mechanisms that jointly drive search differentiation for solutions to address performance shortfalls.
