Abstract. With the rapid prevalence and massive user growth of social media platforms, efficiently targeting potential customers on these platforms has grown in importance for companies. Enhancing the likelihood that a social media user will engage with brand post holds profound implications for online marketing strategy design. However, predicting customer engagement on social media comes with its own set of challenges. In this work, we design a graph neural network model called the graph neural network with attention mechanism for customer engagement (GACE) to predict customer engagement (like/comment/share) of brand posts. We exploit large-scale content consumption information from the perspective of heterogeneous networks and learn latent customer representation by developing a graph neural network model. We examine GACE using a large-scale Facebook data set, and the comprehensive results show significant performance improvement over state-of-the-art baselines. Furthermore, we conduct an interpretability analysis, which sheds some light on the explanation of the proposed model. To illustrate the practical significance of our work, we provide examples to quantify the economic value of improved predictive power using a cost-revenue analysis in the context of targeted marketing.

## 1. Introduction

Social media platforms offer customers an unparalleled level of convenience for interacting with content generated by brands. Well-documented research shows that effective engagement strategies enhance brand awareness, strengthen consumer relationships, improve satisfaction, and drive profitability (Kumar and Mirchandani 2012, Santini et al. 2020). A recent survey revealed that 67% of consumers have purchased products influenced by social media content.<sup>1</sup>

Despite extensive research on engagement drivers, like social influence and entertainment, there remains a notable gap in studies predicting customer-brand engagement, specifically forecasting interactions with brand posts on social media. Predicting customer engagement offers multiple benefits for brands. It optimizes brands targeting strategies by enhancing their efficacy, reducing costs, and preventing customer annoyance because one unsuccessful engagement may lead to a 13% chance of annoyance for consumers, causing their discomfort and distrust toward brands (Sun et al. 2022). Accurate predictions empower brands to enhance customer awareness through tailored post advertisements, a crucial element of social media marketing (Hutter et al. 2013). It also al lows brands to evaluate and pretest various marketing strategies before deployment. By analyzing expected engagement rates, customer size, and characteristics, brands can tailor their strategies to align with their goals (Eliashberg et al. 2000). Furthermore, accurate engagement predictions facilitate direct promotions to likely engaged customers, thereby boosting brand sales, satisfaction, loyalty, and commitment (Park et al. 2016).

Several approaches have been developed to address the customer engagement prediction problem (Chen and Pirolli 2012, Aldous et al. 2019). These methods typically involve a two-step process: first, identifying predictive factors and then, applying them in analytical models, such as logistic regression, decision trees, and support vector machines. However, these conventional approaches have many drawbacks. First, they require extensive feature engineering or labor-intensive humanlabeling processes for quantifying factors. Second, they inherently separate the factor identification and engagement prediction into two stages, potentially diminishing workflow efficiency.<sup>2</sup> As a result, there is a compelling need to explore and develop more efficient and integrated methods that streamline the extraction of relevant factors and their direct utilization in customer engagement prediction. Such methods could enhance the performance and efficiency of engagement prediction while also simplifying the overall process.

In this work, we propose the graph neural network with attention mechanism for customer engagement (GACE), a novel deep learning framework designed to predict customer engagement on social media platforms (i.e., whether a customer will engage with a brand’s post by liking, sharing, or commenting on it). GACE is built upon heterogeneous customer-brand networks (CBNs), which capture a wealth of contextual information by categorizing entities into customers, brands, and posts. It goes beyond simple binary links between users and posts, and it incorporates rich contextual information among diverse entities, such as the specific brand associated with a post, users who engage with other posts from the same brand, and the relationships among different brands.

We extract information from the CBN to characterize the complex customer engagement behavior in the form of metapaths, which are defined as path patterns like $A \to B \to C \to \ldots ,$ where $A , B ,$ and C are different node types and the arrows represent specific types of relationships (edges) between them. In the context of CBNs, metapaths are sequences of relations connecting different object types, including customers, posts, and brands. These metapaths capture rich semantic information within the network. Importantly, unlike previous models that construct heuristics-based or random metapaths (Wang et al. 2019, Gong et al. 2020), here we construct metapaths based on the prior customer engagement literature to represent three driving factors of customer engagement: similar-user factor, brand loyalty factor, and brand affinity factor. As we will discuss later, these theory-driven metapaths help the GACE model gain much better prediction performance and generate clearer business insights than those atheoretical metapaths.

To operationalize these metapaths, we design a graph neural network (GNN) model that maps the heterogeneous entities and their relationships into a latent space while preserving their semantic connections. The model aggregates information via manifold embedding and integrates various customer engagement factors from multiple metapaths. We enhance information aggregation using a feed-forward attention layer to prioritize the model’s focus on relevant metapaths. Additionally, we incorporate a sequential learning framework that adapts to changes over time by processing CBN from different periods. This dynamic approach, supported by a time decay function, ensures that recent interactions weigh more heavily, reflecting the evolving nature of customer engagement. Lastly, fully trained user representations are combined with latent post features and fed into a multilayer perceptron (MLP) for engagement prediction. Therefore, whenever a new post is introduced into the CBN, the model promptly predicts the corresponding customer engagement outcome.

We evaluate GACE’s performance using a large-scale data set from Facebook featuring 666,188 engagements between 91,342 consumers and 35,665 posts from 47 brands. GACE is evaluated against several state-of-theart baselines for predicting customer engagement. The results reveals that GACE significantly surpassed these baselines, showing improvements of 7.43% in accuracy, 3.86% in precision, 6.99% in recall, 5.91% in F1 score (F-measure), and 4.92% in AUC (Area under the curve). Our comprehensive experiments test the sensitivity, robustness, and effectiveness of GACE’s components and design choices, such as metapaths and post content encoding. We also examine the impact of various metapaths using the attention mechanism, providing insights into the driving factors behind engagement behaviors. Additionally, we assess the economic impli cations of our model by analyzing costs and revenues to illustrate the practical benefits and substantial monetary value that GACE offers to brands in their online marketing efforts. This not only underscores the financial gains but also highlights the model’s practical relevance and efficacy.

Our study makes several contributions. First, we tackle the intricate problem of predicting customer engagement from a network-centric perspective. We introduce GACE, a novel deep learning model that utilizes a heterogeneous customer-brand network developed from social media engagement data. This network incorporates customers, brands, and posts along with their complex relationships, which enrich the contextual information and enhance prediction accuracy. Second, in line with the design principles highlighted in Abbasi et al. (2024), our study contributes to artificial intelligence design research by developing and validating the GACE model, which incorporates domain-specific knowledge derived from customer engagement theories to construct theory-driven metapaths. Third, by rigorously testing the model’s effectiveness using realworld data and conducting extensive experiments, our work adheres to design science research guidelines (Abbasi et al. 2024), ensuring both theoretical robust ness and practical relevance. Furthermore, the attention weights associated with these theory-guided metapaths offer interpretable insights, uncovering the key factors influencing the engagement behavior of individual users. Lastly, we provide compelling evidence of the potential monetary value that our model can bring.

2. Preliminaries and Problem Formulation In this section, we introduce the customer-brand networks and their metapaths followed by the definition of the engagement prediction problem.

## 2.1. Heterogeneous Customer-Brand Networks

Definition 1 (Heterogeneous Customer-Brand Network). $\mathrm { A }$ heterogeneous customer-brand network is defined as $G = ( \bar { V } , E )$ , where V and E represent nodes and edges, respectively (i.e., entities and their relationships). The heterogeneous network also has an associated node-type mapping function $\psi : V \to N$ and an edge mapping function $\phi : E \to \Gamma ,$ , where N and Γ represent the predefined node and edge types, $| N | +$ $| \Gamma | > 2$

A substantial body of research underscores the significance of comprehending how customer behavior evolves over time (Ren et al. 2008, Ahn 2010, Huang et al. 2015, Alfian et al. 2019). Therefore, we construct a CBN sequence to model the evolving customer engagement behavior over distinct time intervals (see Figure 1). Specifically, we segment the CBN chronologically, each corresponding to a nonoverlapping time frame to capture engagement and relationship information for that period. In each CBN, there are two types of links. First, binary links are established between customers and posts when customers engage with those posts and between posts and brands to indicate their associations. Additionally, weighted links are used to signify the affinity between different brands.

## 2.2. Metapaths and Theoretical Background

In a CBN, two objects can be connected via multiple semantic paths, which are defined as metapaths. Formally, we define metapath-related concepts below.

Definition 2 (Metapath Schema). Given a heterogeneous network $G = ( V , E )$ with a node-type mapping function $\psi : V \to N$ and an edge mapping function $\phi : E \to \Gamma ,$ a metapath schema is defined as $\Phi : N _ { 0 } { \xrightarrow { \Gamma _ { 0 } } }$ $N _ { 1 } { \stackrel { \overset { \overset { } { 1 } _ { 1 } } } { \to } } \cdots { \stackrel { \overset { } { 1 } _ { l - 1 } } { \to } } N _ { l } ,$ where l denotes the $l ^ { t h }$ step of the metapath schema. Here, $\{ N _ { 0 } , N _ { 1 } , . . . N _ { l } \} \in N$ and $\{ \hat { \Gamma _ { 0 } } , \Gamma _ { 1 } , \hdots \Gamma _ { l - 1 } \hat  \} \in \Gamma$ represent different types of nodes and edges, respectively. Given a metapath schema, there are multiple different metapaths under the schema.

Definition 3 (Metapath-Based Neighbors). Given a focal node $v _ { 0 }$ and metapath schema $\Phi$ in a CBN $G ,$ we denote its one-step neighbors of $v _ { 0 }$ as $N _ { \Phi } ^ { 1 } ( v _ { 0 } ) = V _ { 1 }$ where $V _ { 1 }$ is an ensemble of all connected nodes $v _ { 1 }$ from single metapaths under the schema Φ. In the same vein, the two-step neighbors of node v are represented as $N _ { \Phi } ^ { 2 } ( v _ { 0 } ) = V _ { 2 } ,$ , where $V _ { 2 }$ contains all one-step neighbors connected to $V _ { 1 }$ under the same schema $\Phi .$ The process repeats until reaching the end of the defined metapath schema Φ.

In a CBN, selecting relevant metapaths for understanding customer engagement is challenging and requires domain expertise. Many existing metapaths are based on general heuristics without a theoretical foun dation, which can obscure model comprehension and practical application. Using metapaths from unrelated tasks is not feasible because of the need for domainspecific knowledge of customer behaviors. To overcome these issues, we review the literature and design three theoretically supported metapaths to represent the key drivers of customer engagement behaviors.

• Similar user factor. User engagement research recognizes that users with similar past preferences often exhibit similar future behaviors. This aligns with the sociological concept of homophily, where individuals with common traits tend to build connections (McPherson et al. 2001). This is particularly relevant on social media, where users frequently interact with others who share similar interests. This principle underpins userbased collaborative filtering in recommender systems (Chen et al. 2019). To model customer preferences effectively, we consider the behavior of users with similar historical interests, leveraging shared preferences, such as mutual product likes (Yang et al. 2014). In the CBN, we propose the metapath schema User � Post � User � Post (UPUP) to model this, assuming that if users $u _ { 1 }$ and $u _ { 2 }$ liked the same post $p _ { 1 }$ and user $u _ { 2 }$ likes another post $p _ { 2 } , u _ { 1 }$ is likely to favor $p _ { 2 }$ because of their shared preferences.

• Brand loyalty factor. Numerous studies show that brand loyalty is a key motivator for customer engagement. This suggests that a customer’s previous engagement behaviors indicate their brand awareness, preference, and loyalty (De Vries and Carlson 2014, Barreda et al. 2015, Sakas et al. 2015). Such preferences often lead to repeated interactions with the same brand (Oliva et al. 1992). Brand trust plays a significant role in enhancing customer engagement. Brand enthusiasts tend to engage more actively with brands on social media (Azar et al. 2016, Dimitriu and Guesalaga 2017). Our method captures this by defining the “brand loyalty factor.” We use the metapath schema User � Post � Brand � Post (UPBP) to model customer preferences toward brands. For example, $u _ { 1 }$ engaging with $p _ { 1 }$ from $b _ { 1 }$ implies a likelihood that $u _ { 1 }$ is likely to engage with another post, say $_ { p _ { 2 } , }$ from the same brand $b _ { 1 }$

• Similar brand factor. In social media, customer engagement with brands reflects brand affinity driven by brand perception (Naylor et al. 2012). Research has revealed that customers tend to engage more with brands that they perceive as similar (Naylor et al. 2012). This phenomenon, termed “value homophily,” indicates that customers’ interactions with similar brands express their brand perceptions (McPherson et al. 2001, Culotta and Cutler 2016). Studies have shown that brand attributes significantly motivate engagement behaviors (Pereira et al. 2014, Culotta and Cutler 2016, Hudson et al. 2016). This line of reasoning is also supported by research showing that brand characteristics project the self-identities of brand followers (Escalas and Bettman 2003, Kuksov et al. 2013). Therefore, we introduce the third metapath schema, User � Post � Brand � Brand �

Post (UPBBP), to capture customer interest in similar brands. For example, consider the path $u _ { 1 } - p _ { 1 } - b _ { 1 } -$ $b _ { 2 } - p _ { 2 } ,$ , where $p _ { 1 }$ and $p _ { 2 }$ belong to brands $b _ { 1 }$ and $b _ { 2 }$ . This metapath predicts the likelihood of $u _ { 1 }$ engaging with $p _ { 2 }$ based on the fact that $u _ { 1 }$ has prior interaction with $b _ { 1 } ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s }$ post and the shared affinities between brands $b _ { 1 }$ and $b _ { 2 }$

## 2.3. Problem Formulation

The aim of the proposed model is to predict whether a target customer interacts (like/forward/reply) with a post in the future by leveraging historical interaction data. Specifically, let U, $P ,$ and B denote a set of users, posts, and brands, respectively, where a single customer and post can be represented by $u _ { i } , p _ { j }$ . The CBN sequence representing customer-brand engagement at different time periods is represented as $\{ G _ { t _ { 1 } } , G _ { t _ { 2 } } , \ldots ,$ $G _ { t _ { T } } \}$ . The objective is to predict the interaction between $u _ { i }$ and $p _ { j }$ at the latest time interval $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , t _ { T } )$ denoted by $y _ { i j }$ (zero for no interaction between $u _ { i }$ and $p _ { j }$ and one otherwise).

## 3.3. Attention Layer

Once user representations from various metapaths are obtained, effectively combining these diverse representations is essential as each metapath type reflects different aspects of user behavior. For example, UPUP shows the influence of peer behavior, whereas UPBP reflects brand loyalty. Recognizing that the relevance of metapaths varies by customer, we incorporate an attention layer to assign learned weights to metapaths during training, as illustrated in Figure 4. This layer aggregates entity embeddings for a target node from various metapaths in graph $G _ { t _ { n } } ,$ , allowing the model to dynamically prioritize and synthesize the most relevant information.

To implement the attention mechanism, we first feed the representations learned from each metapath into a one-layer nonlinear neural network. Then, an attention vector $q$ is multiplied by node-level vectors to get the initial importance of each metapath $\mathbf { w } _ { \Phi _ { i } }$

$$
\mathbf {W} _ {\Phi_ {i}} ^ {t _ {n}} = q ^ {T (t _ {n})} \cdot \mathrm{tanh} (\mathbf {W} ^ {t _ {n}} \cdot \mathbf {H} _ {u} ^ {(\Phi_ {i}) (t _ {n})} + \mathbf {b} ^ {t _ {n}}),\tag{5}
$$

where $W ^ { t _ { n } }$ is the weight matrices and $b ^ { t _ { n } }$ is the bias vector. To maintain consistency and comparability across metapaths within the same graph, we use shared parameters.

Furthermore, the weights of metapaths are applied to a softmax layer, wherein the final weight $\beta _ { \Phi _ { i } } ^ { t _ { n } }$ represents the contribution of a specific type of metapath $\Phi _ { i }$ to the prediction task at time $t _ { n }$ . The final user represen tations are then obtained by combining vectors from each metapath type using these weights:

$$
\pmb {\beta} _ {\Phi_ {i}} ^ {t _ {n}} = \frac {\exp (\pmb {W} _ {\Phi_ {i}} ^ {t _ {n}})}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {M} \exp (\pmb {W} _ {\Phi_ {i}} ^ {t _ {n}})} \qquad \pmb {H} _ {u} ^ {t _ {n}} = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {M} \pmb {\beta} _ {\Phi_ {i}} ^ {t _ {n}} \pmb {H} _ {u} ^ {(\Phi_ {i}) (t _ {n})},\tag{6}
$$

where M is the total number of metapath schemas and $H _ { u } ^ { ( \Phi _ { i } ) ( t _ { n } ) }$ is the representation learned from metapath $\Phi _ { i }$ at time $t _ { n }$ . Algorithm 2 shows the attention layer of GACE.

## 3.4. Customer Representations Aggregation with Time Indicator

To capture the evolving nature of customer engagement on social media, we construct a sequence of CBN, reflecting the dynamic interactions between customers and brands (Bolton 1998, Chan and Ip 2011). This dynamic customer behavior is crucial for improving engagement prediction and informed marketing decisions (Allenby et al. 1999). Thus, we integrate a time decay indicator in the GACE model to emphasize more recent engagements, reflecting their more important roles in prediction tasks. This method not only optimizes the learning process but also captures evolving customer behavior effectively.

## 3.5. Prediction and Model Training

Our model aims to predict interaction probabilities, $\hat { y } _ { i , j } ,$ between user $u _ { i }$ and pos $p _ { j }$ within time window $t _ { T } , \mathrm { r a n g { - } }$ ing from zero to one. $H _ { u }$ encapsulates user preferences extracted from various factors. For post representations, we utilize post embedding vectors, $H _ { p } ^ { ( 0 ) }$ , to streamline predictions for newly generated posts. We concatenate user representations $H _ { u }$ and post embeddings $H _ { p } ^ { ( 0 ) }$ , and we feed them into a three-layer perceptron component, where the logistic sigmoid function produces output in the interval of (0, 1) that can be interpreted as a probabil ity. We set ReLU (rectified linear unit) as the activation function and use the binary crossentropy loss function to optimize the model:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \hat {y} _ {i, j} = \text { sigmoid } (M L P (\boldsymbol {H} _ {u} \oplus \boldsymbol {H} _ {p} ^ {(0)})) \\ L o s s = \sum_ {i, j \in Y} (y _ {i j} \log \hat {y} _ {i j} + (1 - y _ {i j}) \log (1 - \hat {y} _ {i j})), \end{array}\tag{7}
$$

where $y _ { i j }$ indicates the true labels $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ , zero or one) and Y includes both positive and negative pairs.

## 4. Evaluation

We conduct comprehensive evaluations to examine the performance of the GACE model. Because of the space limitations, we report a series of additional evaluations of the effectiveness and robustness of GACE’s components and design choices in Online Appendix F.

## 4.1. Data Set

We collected a large-scale data set from Facebook by selecting 47 brands across various categories. We use antisimilarity and prosimilarity strategies in category selection by including brands with both high and low similarity (e.g., sportswear versus clothing stores, car brands versus cosmetics). This approach highlights the GACE model’s flexible compatibility across diverse brand types. Additionally, we use weighted edges to indicate brand affinities and guide the information aggregation process, which eliminate the impact of brand selection on the model performance.

After brand selection, we collect post data from these brands between January 1, 2014 and December 31, 2015, including post identifications (IDs), content, brand IDs, generation dates, and engaged user lists. Only users with three or more engagements were retained, resulting in 666,188 engagement records from 35,665 posts and 91,342 users. We organize these engagements into a sequence of CBNs, segmenting the data into four intervals of six months each, $T _ { 1 }$ to $T _ { 4 } ,$ to capture temporal dynamics in user engagement. Each CBN reflects the relationships and interactions within its respective time window. The model leverages this temporal and relational data to enhance prediction accuracy. For evaluation metrics, we use accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score, and AUC to evaluate the prediction performance. Accuracy measures the proportion of correct predictions made by a model out of the total number of predictions. Precision expresses the proportion of the predicted posts that are indeed engaged by the users, whereas recall indicates the ability of the model to find all potential posts that are engaged by the users. F1 score is a blend of these two measurements. AUC provides an aggregated measure of performance across all possible classification thresholds.

To demonstrate the superior performance of GACE in predicting the customer engagement task, we compare its prediction performance with network embedding methods and graph neural network models, covering both homogeneous and heterogeneous network-based approaches. We select several state-of-the-art approaches from each of these baseline algorithm groups, including multilayer perceptron, DeepWalk (Perozzi et al. 2014), metapath2vec (Dong et al. 2017), GraphSAGE (Graph SAmple and aggreGatE, Hamilton et al. 2017), RGCN (Relational Graph Convolutional Networks, Schlichtkrull et al. 2018), HAN (Heterogeneous Graph Attention Network, Wang et al. 2019), JODIE (Kumar et al. 2019), and MAGNN (Metapath Aggregated Graph Neural Network, Fu et al. 2020).

## 4.3. Experiments and Results

Following the evaluation procedure described above, we examine the prediction performance of the GACE model and baselines. The results are reported in Table 3. Overall, GACE outperforms all seven benchmarks dramatically. GACE significantly outperforms MLP, improving accuracy by 29.356%, precision by 44.318%, recall by 38.226%, F1 score by 39.610%, and AUC by 33.588%. This highlights the importance of constructing the CBN to incorporate structural information among entities. When compared with network embedding methods, GACE shows substantial improvements of over 17.71% in accuracy, 10.30% in precision, 14.87% in recall, and more than 10% in both F1 and AUC. Even against advanced heterogeneous graph neural network methods, like HAN and MAGNN, GACE improves accuracy by 7.43% and precision by 3.86%, and there are further improvements in recall, F1, and AUC. These results underscore GACE’s superiority in customer engagement prediction, and they indicate the effectiveness of the theory-guided metapath design, the time decay mechanism, and the post content encoding approach.

## 4.4. Attention Exploration

We adopt the attention mechanism for two main reasons: to enhance information aggregation efficacy and to provide domain-relevant interpretations of prediction results. By learning weights among different metapaths, the mechanism highlights their importance. To further open the black box behind this deep learning framework, we analyze how attention weights illustrate the interpretability of the GACE model. For instance, we compare attention weights for two random users (user<sub>1</sub> and user<sub>2</sub>) predicted to engage with the same post (post ). Figure 5 illustrates the averaged attention weights of the metapaths trained by GACE for this prediction. For u , the dominant factor is the similar brand factor (UPBBP weights 0.529), whereas for user , it is brand loyalty represented by metapath UPBP (weights 0.439). This example shows that the attention mechanism uses the network context to identify critical engagement drivers.

Although we suggest these interpretations, we remain cautious, acknowledging debates about whether attention weights truly offer explanations (Jain and Wallace 2019). Our approach differs from studies that use atheoretical or random metapaths by integrating customer behavior theories into the design of metapaths, thereby increasing their relevance for identifying key drivers of predictions. This methodological design not only enhances the interpretative power of our model but also moves the conversation forward in the realm of theoryguided attention mechanisms.

## 4.5. Economic Significance of the GACE Model

A growing body of research has explored the positive link between prompting customer engagement on social media and its profound impact on business outcomes, including brand revenue and performance, such as movie revenue (Oh et al. 2017) and offline store visits (Halloran and Lutz 2021). The superior performance of the GACE model highlights its potential for precise customer targeting. The GACE model exhibits a precision score of 0.889. This means that of a group of 1,000 users, we achieve 889 successful targetings and 111 unsuccessful ones. Compared with the baselines, the GACE model significantly enhances value by delivering additional profit.

The GACE model provides a cost-effective solution for identifying potential customers, particularly useful under budget constraints. This is demonstrated through a cumulative gains chart (Figure 6), which assesses the model’s efficiency in business applications, like targeted marketing (Brandenburger and Furth 2009, Burez and Van den Poel 2009, Jaffery and Liu 2009). The chart ranks customers by predicted engagement likelihood, enabling prioritized outreach to those most likely to respond. For example, at point A on the chart, imagine a scenario with 1,000 users, where 500 are interested in the brand’s posts. Normally, reaching all 1,000 might yield 500 positive responses. However, with GACE, contacting just 400 users (40% of total) could capture 325 interested users (65% of interested), significantly reducing outreach efforts and costs. This demonstrates that GACE can help brands reach 65% of their potential customers by targeting only 40% of the user base, thus optimizing marketing spend and enhancing the efficiency of campaigns. Beyond revenue-cost analysis, it is crucial to account for the costs of customer annoyance from unsuccessful targeting. Research indicates that a display ad reaching an uninterested user has a 13% chance of causing annoyance (Todri et al. 2020). False positives, where users are incorrectly targeted, are a key consideration. Given the GACE model’s strong recall performance, it significantly mitigates these costs, as shown in the cumulative gains curve in Figure 6, by effectively identifying uninterested users and reducing the likelihood of annoyance. In summary, the GACE model helps brands save money in online marketing campaigns.

## 5. Discussion

Predicting online customer engagement is a vital yet understudied issue. We introduce GACE, a novel graph neural network model that uses theory-guided metapaths to predict engagement with brand posts. GACE operates through three main steps: constructing theorydriven metapaths in customer-brand networks over time, using a metapath-based GNN for entity representation, and incorporating an attention layer for enhanced prediction. Experimental results on a Facebook data set highlight GACE’s superior performance, excelling in aggregating contextual information, leveraging theorydriven metapaths, enhancing engagement predictions, and providing strategic insights for businesses.

The GACE model enhances social media prediction for online marketing by integrating contextual information within a novel framework for heterogeneous network-based customer engagement prediction. It provides insights into customer behavior and supports better targeting and resource allocation for platforms, like Instagram, Twitter, and YouTube. GACE helps firms target potential customers cost effectively, refine brand strategies, and minimize annoyance costs from disinterested users. Its predictions enable tailored promotions, strategy pretesting, and direct engagement with interested customers, boosting sales, satisfaction, and loyalty. Moreover, GACE enriches traditional recommender systems by incorporating unique features, broadening its applicability and impact.

Our study has limitations. Because of data constraints, we could not include relationships, like followership between users and brands, user friendships, or demographics, in our model, although we used synthetic followership data to partially mitigate this (Online Appendix G). Enhancing the GACE framework with more diverse data could further improve its performance. Additionally, analyzing customers’ comment content and exploring deeper into post content could reveal more about engagement behaviors, presenting valuable areas for future research. Lastly, we have not tested the model’s real-world efficacy, which highlights further research needs.
