# Rethinking Gamification Failure: A Model and Investigation of Gamified System Maladaptive Behaviors

Abstract. Despite the increasing interest in gamified systems and excitement about their potential positive impact on user engagement, a few studies have started to note gamification failures, which can result from user maladaptive behaviors, or behaviors directed at misap propriating gamified systems. In this research, we examine how such maladaptive behaviors can result from design issues of gamified systems and how such behaviors impact task performance. To date, little is known about design issues which may drive users to maladapt, and why they maladapt gamified systems. We systematically conceptualize gamified system mal adaptive behaviors (GSMB) as having two dimensions: technology maladaptation and gami fied task maladaptation. Based on goal-setting theory and self-determination theory, we develop a research model of GSMB. The model depicts three drivers of GSMB: game-task goals misalignment, game-task complexity, and gamification structure injustice, and how they fulfill or frustrate psychological innate needs (i.e., needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness), which in turn drive GSMB. We conducted two studies using different contexts. We tested the model with Study 1 empirically examining users of a gamified system, Pocket Points. With Study 2, we employed a within-subject experiment. By manipulating the design issues, we assessed the fulfillment of psychological needs induced by the gamified system. The results largely support our conceptualization of GSMB and the research model, highlighting the design issues as the main drivers of GSMB, and that the greater the GSMB, the greater the negative impact on task performance. Findings from this research have implications fo both information systems research and gamification practices.

## Introduction

Gamification integrates game design elements into a target system to enhance user engagement and instrumental task outcomes (Liu et al. 2017). Companies are increasingly investing in gamification and using gamified systems for business tasks such as cybersecurity training, Gamification is also being used in personal growth and health applications to, for example, improve fitness, manage diabetes (Asadzandi et al. 2020), and address malnutrition and hunger.<sup>5</sup> Because of the enthusiasm for developing gamified systems for business and individual use, Technavio predicts that the gamification market will grow by USD 27.77 billion from 2021 to 2026.<sup>6</sup>

Beyond the benefits of gamification for business, scholars have also noted that gamification can, for example, increase users’ contributions to online knowledge exchange (Goes et al. 2016), lead to higher job performance (Landers et al. 2017), and encourage employees to expend more effort (Liu et al. 2017). However, re searchers have also suggested that although gamification designers generally seek to promote positive outcomes (Landers et al. 2018), gamified systems may not always motivate the targeted behaviors and can even lead to undesirable behaviors (Callan et al. 2015). Industry sources give a number of potential reasons for these so-called “gamification failures.”<sup>8</sup>

In this study, rather than simply label gamified systems as design failures or successes, we probe the conditions and motivations that may cause users to use gamified systems in ways not intended or anticipated by designers. In this research, we refer to such behaviors as maladaptive behaviors. Evidence exists for users’ maladaptive use of gamified systems (Callan et al. 2015). For example, the SAP Community Network, a gamified knowledge management system, was designed to encourage professionals to share their technical SAP expertise. However, some members created multiple accounts so that they could use one account to ask questions that had already been answered and another account to answer them (Kumar and Herger 2013). By doing so, these users accumulated points without answering legitimate questions, which conflicted with the intentions of the system design—that is, to motivate users to ask/ answer legitimate questions and thereby increase knowledge sharing. Similarly, for the language-learning platform Duolingo, one of the objectives of gamifying the learning task is to help users remember and recall correct terms by offering badges. But reports show that some users use other translating software to answer questions so that they can get more badges and stay on the leaderboard (Mogavi et al. 2022). We refer to these types of behaviors as maladaptive because the gamified system is being used in a way that is not intended to promote learning by users.

We argue that in the gamification context, maladaptive behaviors could have negative consequences on job performance. In the above example, the instrumenta task outcomes (learning a language) targeted by the gamified system may actually be diminished if users focus on achieving the game goals (getting more badges) instead. Because maladaptive behaviors have scarcely been examined in the literature, their impact on instrumental task outcomes and the conditions or design factors and user motivations that may trigger such maladaptive behaviors are not known. It is quite possible that, in certain situations, instrumental task outcomes may not be adversely impacted whereas in others they could be. For example, on learning platforms such as Khan Academy, some users who are highly focused on earning points might nevertheless learn the subject material whereas others might mindlessly click through the materials without learning anything. Therefore, it is important to identify design factors, task parameters, and other elements that might prompt users to engage in maladaptive behaviors. Addressing this issue is important because many organizations are investing in gamified systems; however, if users do not use the system as intended and engage in maladaptive behaviors, it can diminish or defeat the very purpose of gamification. By conducting research on this issue, we can inform design guidelines and advance the theoretical understanding of user interactions with gamified systems. Hence, the goal of this research is to identify the design and motivation factors that drive users’ maladaptive behavior in gamified systems.

To achieve this goal, we first contextualize ideas from Sun’s (2012) work on adaptive system use to define our concept of gamified system maladaptive behaviors (GSMB) as behaviors directed at misappropriating gamified systems.

We use the principles of gamification design from Liu et al. (2017) and view GSMB as having two broad dimensions: technology maladaptation (misappropriating the technology of a gamified system) and gamified task maladaptation (misappropriating the tasks in a gamified system). A gamified system is built on a technology plat form, and it is the technology affordances that dictate what types of game design elements, objects, and mechanics can be used (Liu et al. 2017). We posit that this technology, including its game mechanics and game features, could be misappropriated and used in unintended ways, leading to maladaptive behaviors. Similarly, tasks could be misappropriated and not completed or executed in the desired manner because the user chooses to prioritize earning game rewards. In this research, we attempt to address the following questions surrounding GSMB: (1) Does GSMB exist, and if so, what design issues of gamified systems drive users’ GSMB? (2) How does GSMB influence instrumental task outcomes?

To address these research questions, we leveraged several theoretical foundations used in prior information systems (IS) research and developed a research model to guide our research. We used goal-setting theory (GST) (Locke 1978; Locke and Latham 1990, 2002) because users of gamified systems typically focus on two goals simultaneously—a “game goal” and an instrumental “task goal.” We argue that the main issue underlying maladaptive behaviors is users’ tendency to prioritize game goals. Game goals, such as accumulating a certain number of points, are relatively more specific by design (Deterding et al. 2011), whereas most task goals are relatively more abstract, for example, improving organizational security through training (Silic and Lowry 2020). Because GST posits that specific goals are more likely to be achieved (Tondello et al. 2018), users are likely to gravitate toward game goals. Moreover, game designs are typically tailored to be engaging and captivating and often provide immediate feedback, which may motivate users to focus on game goals over task goals.

We conducted two studies to investigate GSMB. The first study aimed at verifying the existence of GSMB and studying the design issues that influence GSMB. The model posits that system design issues—including gametask goals misalignment (GTGM), game-task complexity, and gamification structure injustice—directly or indirectly trigger GSMB. Based on the findings from Study 1, we conducted a second study based on self-determination theory (SDT) (Deci and Ryan 1985, Ryan and Deci 2000) to further unpack and investigate how users’ psychological needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness, as defined in SDT (Deci and Ryan 2000), mediate the effects of gamification design issues on GSMB. We also investigated how GSMB impact task performance.

The findings from Study 1 largely support our multidimensional conceptualization of GSMB and show that gamification design issues (game-task goals misalignment, game-task complexity, and gamification structure injustice) influence GSMB in various ways. Study 2 examined whether gamified system design issues satisfy or frustrate users’ perceived psychological needs and how this impacts the development of GSMB. We found that satisfying the need for autonomy and competence increases GSMB whereas satisfying the need for relatedness decreases it. Under certain conditions where there is an incongruence between task goals and game goals, as prescribed in the gamification principles (Liu et al. 2017), we found that task performance is adversely affected.

Our research offers several contributions. First, we systematically develop a new construct, that is, GSMB. This new construct captures maladaptive behaviors that people may engage in when interacting with a gamified system. Second, we develop a novel GSMB research model to describe how gamified system design issues cause GSMB and their effects on task performance. The model offers insight into how essential design issues—that is, the misalignment between the game goal and the main task goal, the task and game complexity of the gamified system, and the perceived injustice of the system—lead to unexpected GSMB directly or indirectly via psychological factors and can thereby cause diminished task performance. Our results suggest that the more integrated hedonic gamified elements there are in a system, the more likely it is that maladaptive behaviors will occur. Third, methodologically, we validate a new instrument for measuring GSMB that can be used in future research.

In the next section, we conceptualize and define GSMB and their dimensions. Then, we establish a theoretical framework based on GST and SDT. We then describe the two research models—Study 1 and Study 2—which are based on the theoretical framework and discuss each study’s methods and results. We conclude the paper with a discussion of the results, implications for research and practice, and future research avenues.

## Conceptualizing GSMB Gamification

Gamification is defined as “the incorporation of game design elements into a target system while retaining the target system’s instrumental functions” (Liu et al. 2017, p. 1013). It is believed that the integration of game elements—such as points, badges, leaderboards (PBL), and game tasks—can promote users’ performance of the main instrumental task, such as improving customer relationships or increasing physical exercise. Gamified systems, by their very nature, are systems with instrumental functions that have game design elements layered on top in order to increase fun and engagement with the system (Liu et al. 2017, Landers et al. 2018). Gamification research is vast; many studies have examined the influence of various game elements, thei impact on user motivation, and the interaction pattern of gamified systems (e.g., Santhanam et al. 2016, Suh et al. 2017) with the assumption that dual goals (i.e., game and task goals) are integral to gamified systems. However, despite the many studies on gamification, hardly any have discussed maladaptive behaviors (see Online Ap pendix A) (Goes et al. 2016, Bellman and Murray 2018, James et al. 2019).

To inform the understanding of maladaptive behaviors, we begin by defining the technology and the task, two key components of gamified systems (Bui et al. 2015, Liu et al. 2017). Technology describes the technology infrastructure (e.g., global positioning system (GPS), database, server, etc.) of the gamified system. Tasks can be described in terms of desired outcomes and behavioral requirements (Wood 1986). Instrumental tasks are the main type of gamified system task; game tasks are tasks that exist because of the game element and aim to support the instrumental task. Some research has discussed other components such as game mechanics, for example, the rules of the games—for example, how to achieve badges and game objects such as images, gold coins, videos, etc. (Hunicke et al. 2004, Liu et al. 2017). However, because these components of game mechanics and game objects are highly intertwined and integrated with the technology of the gamified system, we discuss mal adaptation broadly in terms of technology maladaptation and gamified task maladaptation.

## Defining GSMB

We define GSMB as all behaviors aimed at misappropriating a gamified system (including the supporting technology and gamified tasks) that thereby diminish the integrity of the task and/or game.<sup>9</sup> GSMB are driven by the layering of game mechanics on top of tasks and technology. Because users adapt tasks and technology to achieve desired outcomes (Goodhue and Thompson 1995), we believe that GSMB can modify the technology and tasks of gamified systems (Bui et al. 2015, Liu et al. 2017). Hence, we propose two types of GSMB: technology maladaptation and gamified task maladaptation, which correspond to the two main components of gamified systems (Table 1).

Technology maladaptation occurs when users misappropriate technology by modifying and manipulating it—this may involve other technologies as well. Fo example, users could exploit a system by hacking into its database or the game’s progress-tracking technology to improve their score, which is common in digital games (Duh and Chen 2009, Yan and Randell 2009). In the con text of gamification, a common goal of technology maladaptation is to do less work to complete an instrumental task. Gamified task maladaptation occurs when users alter the nature of a task to prioritize a game goal. Gamified task maladaptation does not violate any explicit rules but may violate the “spirit” of the rules and instrumental tasks. For example, to encourage learning, Khan Academy designers award points (Energy Points) that measure effort rather than mastery.<sup>11</sup> Users can earn points simply by completing lessons and watching videos. Therefore, users can quickly earn Energy Points by rushing through materials without actually learning anything. In this scenario, the task goal of learning is not met, even though users are advancing the game goal of accumulating Energy Points. Although such users are doing precisely what is required by the rules, they are violating the “spirit” of the instrumental task (learning the training material). We conceptualize and capture this phenomenon via the concept of gamified task maladaptation.

## Theoretical Foundations

## Goal-Setting Theory and Gamification Goals

Because the main objective of gamification is to make learning more appealing and engaging (Liu et al. 2017), gamification designers generally seek to encourage users to achieve desired task and game goals simultaneously (Liu et al. 2017). For example, if a system designer selects fitness as a task goal for the system users, the designer will likely implement data management features (e.g., step tracking) to gamify the system to help users achieve their fitness goals (James et al. 2019). Users can then typically engage in pursuing gamification objects (e.g., points, badges, leaderboard rankings) that motivate them to improve their fitness (a task goal). Such gamification affordances embed game goals: the point feature suggests that the goal is to accumulate points, and the presence of a leaderboard suggests that the goal is to compete against others for higher rankings. Users partic ipating in the game layer of the system strive to achieve the game goals. However, if gamification objects are not properly designed, designers may not be able to elicit the desired user experience and behaviors, and the task goals may not be achieved. For example, to get more badges or a higher ranking on the leaderboard of a fitness app, users might cheat by letting their dog run with the health tracker to achieve game goals while ignoring the task goal.<sup>12</sup>

Given the focus on goals in gamification, GST (Locke and Latham 1990) serves as a foundation of our work because it helps model the coexistence of game goals and task goals in gamified systems. Locke (1978) argues that goal setting is either implicitly or explicitly recognized as a part of virtually every theory related to work motivation. GST is framed largely in an organizational context. As such, it focuses on conscious performance goals. As summarized in Online Appendix B, GST focuses on a few key constructs: goal commitment, task complexity, and feedback (Wood et al. 1987, Bandura and Wood 1989, Locke and Latham 2002, Latham and Locke 2007). We argue that these factors are essential for gamification contexts to ensure that gamification objects are properly designed.

1. Goal commitment refers to “the degree to which the individual is attached to the goal, considers it signifi cant or important, is determined to reach it, and keeps it in the face of setbacks and obstacles” (Latham and Locke 1991, p. 217). Locke (1968) proposed that people who stop trying when faced with a difficult task, that is, low commitment, have decided that the goal is impossible to achieve; thus, they stop trying to achieve the goal, which reduces performance.

2. Task complexity refers to the cognitive load created by the pursuit of a goal (Landers et al. 2017). Gamification designers must consider three types of task complexity: component, coordinative, and dynamic (Wood 1986). Component complexity describes “the number of distinct acts that need to be executed in the performance of the task and the number of distinct information cues that must be processed in the performance of those acts” (Wood 1986, p. 66). The more dissimilar acts that are involved and/or the more information that must be processed in a task, the more component complexity is involved (Wood 1986, Wallace et al. 2008). Coordinative complexity refers to the degree to which the relationship between input and output is unclear (Wood 1986). When the relationship between inputs and outputs is less clear and nonlinear, coordinative complexity is high (Wallace et al. 2008). Finally, dynamic complexity refers to the degree to which the relationship between task inputs and outputs (i.e., changes in the means-end relationship) is nonstatic (Wood 1986). The greater the change in the relationship between inputs and outputs, the greater the dynamic complexity (Wallace et al. 2008).

3. Feedback motivates users because it makes the relationship between effort and performance clear to users and demonstrates progress toward goals (Locke and Latham 2002, Jung et al. 2010). Feedback provides users with information on the correctness, accuracy, and adequacy of their actions (Earley et al. 1990).

## Self-Determination Theory and Gamification Designs

Whereas GST informs the understanding of how to set goals to increase one’s chance of success, SDT explains how gamification designs satisfy users’ psychological needs. Deci and Ryan (2000) argued that innate psychological needs energize and direct people’s behavior. If people’s needs are satisfied, they will continue to pursue their goals; if their needs are frustrated, they will seek alternative goals (Koestner and Hope 2014), and engage in other behaviors (Deci and Ryan 2000). SDT is especially relevant to our context, as we are interested in how the design of gamified systems satisfies or frustrates psychological needs and how these needs influence GSMB.

SDT suggests that three relevant psychological needs— autonomy, competence, and relatedness—are core drivers that intrinsically motivate people to play games (Ryan et al. 2006, Sepehr and Head 2018) or engage in gamification activities (Groh 2012, Mekler et al. 2017, Sailer et al. 2017). The need for autonomy refers to the desire for volition, control, choice, and psychological freedom when performing an activity (Deci and Ryan 1980, 2000). In games, autonomy is fulfilled by having a high degree of choice in one’s actions (Ryan et al. 2006). However, in gamification, designers often fall into the trap of “overjustification” by using extrinsic rewards (Lepper et al. 1973). Coupled with strict rules that limit one’s choice of actions, extrinsic needs can make users feel they are being controlled, leaving their need for autonomy unfulfilled (Groh 2012). The need for competence refers to the desire for challenge and effectance (Deci and Ryan 2000). Designers understand that much of the fun of games and gamification comes from mastery (Koster 2005), and providing users with a sense of accomplishment and mastery requires well-designed feedback (Groh 2012). Negative or irrelevant feedback signals to users that they have not learned anything and that their efforts were pointless. Finally, the need for relatedness refers to the desire to fee connected to others. In games and gamified systems, this is often achieved by connecting players with a meaningful community (Groh 2012). However, competition is often designed into gamified systems with multiple players, and intense competition can cause winners to dominate the game, frustrating users’ need for relatedness (Perlman 2010).

## Theoretical Framework

Based on GST and SDT, we developed a theoretical framework to examine GSMB, as depicted in Figure 1. Our framework illustrates that design issues in gamified systems can trigger GSMB, which influences instrumental task performance. SDT explains this relationship, as triggers can satisfy or frustrate users’ psychological needs.

We employed a two-phased study to evaluate the validity of our framework. In Study 1 we focused on validating GSMB and their relationships to gamified system design issues, using an online survey of users of Pocket Points (a gamified application that encourages users to abstain from using mobile phones). Our Study 1 findings provide evidence that people engage in the maladaptive use of gamified systems because of design issues. Therefore, we conducted a controlled experiment in Study 2 to examine the psychological factors through which gamified system design issues drive GSMB. In Study 2, we unpacked the mechanisms between the relationships id entified in Study 1 to manipulate different design issues. We then examine how task performance is affected by the varying degree of GSMB under these design conditions. Each study used a different context to crossvalidate our findings in different settings. By coupling online survey data with an experiment, we strengthened the internal and external validity of our findings (Venkatesh et al. 2016).

## Study 1: GSMB and Design Issues Research Model and Hypotheses

The goal of Study 1 was to validate GSMB and examine their relationships with gamified system design issues. Based on GST, we tested a GSMB research model (Figure 2). Specifically, we conceptualized three gamified system design issues as antecedents of GSMB: game-task goals misalignment, game-task complexity, and gamification structure injustice. The three constructs correspond, respectively, to goal commitment, task complexity, and feedback in GST and represent the user perceptions of gamified system design as well as the interactions between the system features and game elements.

Game-Task Goals Misalignment. In the gamification context, we define game-task goals misalignment as the user’s perception that the objectives of the game mechanics and the instrumental task are inconsistent. The task congruence principle, which states that the gamification design elements and the behavioral requirements of target tasks should be aligned (Liu et al. 2017), supports this view. We build on the task congruence principle by arguing that having two misaligned goals make it difficult for users to commit to both. When users face misaligned goals, their self-efficacy to complete both goals is weakened because they perceive the difficulty of simultaneously achieving both goals (Kruglanski et al. 2015). This “multifinal” goal configuration may have a constraining effect—if multiple active goals are antithetical to one another, goal conflict arises (Kruglanski et al. 2015). Because the goal-setting perspective states that goals direct individual actions (Latham et al. 1994), this misalignment of game and task goals weakens the link between task and game goals and thus weakens users perceived self-efficacy regarding goal completion.

We argue that game-task goals misalignment has a positive relationship with both technology maladaptation and gamified task maladaptation. The rationale is that when a game and a task have different goals, people are more likely to perceive the discrepancy and adapt their use of the gamified system to bridge the misalignment. Adaptive behaviors are often triggered by perceived discrepancies (Armstrong and Hardgrave 2007, Sun 2012). Discrepancies arise when “the outcomes of system use are different from what were expected” (Sun 2012, p. 461). In other words, a discrepancy between individuals’ expectations and reality can motivate people to change their behavior to reduce this discrepancy (Hastie 1984). When gamification designers implement game mechanics without sufficiently accounting for task goals (Scho¨bel et al. 2017), users must navigate and reconcile the divergent game and task goals by adapting their behaviors.

When game goals and task goals are misaligned, users may feel frustrated and engage in emotion-focused coping by rationalizing their behavior or redefining their goals (Lazarus and Folkman 1984). For example, individuals might rationalize that it is acceptable to engage in maladaptive behavior because the system is poorly designed. They may engage in technology maladaptation because they attribute the misalignment as part of the poorly designed system (Karjalainen et al. 2019). They view technology maladaptation as acceptable and even necessary to $^ { \prime \prime } \mathrm { f i x ^ { \prime \prime } }$ the misalignment of goals. For example, a customer service center may ask employees to give good customer service, but the center may also create a game goal of reduced breaks. Some employees may find the two goals incommensurable, because they need to take a short break between each call in order to give good customer service. The employee can engage in technology maladaptation by simply muting a call after a call has finished, instead of hanging up. The system will record that the call is still active.<sup>13</sup> In addition, individuals may also engage in task maladaptation by abandoning the more abstract task goal and focusing on the more measurable game goal instead. For instance, a customer service center may create the game goal of receiving a large number of calls. A frustrated employee might abandon the less measurable goal of helping customers and focus on the more measurable goal of achieving a large number of calls to lessen their frustration, especially if the latter is associated with an extrinsic reward (e.g., accumulating points). Game-Task Complexity. Gamified systems have all three types of task complexity specified by Wood (1986):

component (e.g., the number of game elements, game activities, and instrumental tasks required), coordinativ (e.g., the complexity of the rules: the relationship among instrumental tasks, game elements, and goals), and dy namic (the extent and rate of changes of the rules). There fore, we define game-task complexity as the combination of the number of game elements, activities, and instrumental tasks, the number of their relationships, and the rate of change of those relationships. Because gamified systems can be designed with many game elements (i.e., points, badges, rankings, avatars, power-ups, etc.), users may need to keep track of different data. For example, Nike fitness applications not only track user activity but also keep track of their proprietary point system, NikeFuel. Because gamified systems include both game and instrumental layers, they tend to have more component and coordinative complexity, and users need to manage the functions and rules of both layers. Likewise, if gamified systems change their rules frequently (e.g., with updates), dynamic complexity can also be high because users need to frequently update their understanding of the rules.

Game-task complexity can lead to technology maladaptation. When users are faced with complexity, effort and attention may be of limited use; thus, users will try to develop or discover new strategies to achieve thei goals (Latham and Locke 1991). Users experiencing game-task complexity can be thought of as experiencing an extraneous cognitive load, primarily because of poorly designed instruction (Paas et al. 2003). Users may thus engage in adaptation behaviors by using tools innovatively to reduce complexity (Gollwitzer 1999). We therefore hypothesize that

Hypothesis 2. Game-task complexity is positively related to technology maladaptation.

Gamification Structure Injustice. Gamification struc ture injustice refers to the degree to which the game procedure and reward mechanics are perceived as unfair by the users. Poorly designed gamification objects can lead to feelings of injustice (Kapp et al. 2014). Points, badges, and leaderboards provide feedback on whether a standard is being met (Locke and Latham 2002). GST suggests that feedback is an important moderator that makes goal mechanisms more effective (Locke and Latham 2002). Feedback increases people’s task motivation by emphasizing the connection between effort and performance (Locke and Latham 2002, Jung et al. 2010). Therefore, feedback elements are essential in gamified systems because of their motivational benefits (Marczewski 2015).

Although feedback can vary in terms of immediacy and amount (Locke and Latham 2002, Latham and Locke 2007), few gamification studies have considered the fairness aspect of feedback. Game designers use feedback to direct behavior by offering awards for behaviors that meet certain standards defined by the rules of the game. However, the rules in a gamified system may be dictated by limitations in technology or design, leading users to perceive the rules as unfair.

Although more frequently studied in the organizational behavior literature, the concept of justice has been examined in the gamification literature as well (Leclercq et al. 2020). We focus on two dimensions of organizational justice: distributive justice and procedural justice. Procedural justice refers to “the fairness of the evaluation procedures used to determine [performance] ratings” (Greenberg 1986, p. 340). In other words, when rules are applied to everyone equally, procedural justice is upheld. In gamification, game mechanics such as rules can violate procedural justice when not designed properly. For example, a gamified running app that uses GPS to measure distance would not count running on a treadmill as progress, which could lead to perceived procedural injustice. Distributive justice refers to “the fairness of the evaluations received relative to the work performed” (Greenberg 1986, p. 340). Rewards relate to output, whereas work relates to input. If distributive justice is upheld, the ratio between input and output should be consistent across individuals (e.g., points, ranking, etc.).

However, all games involve a certain amount of variability (i.e., player skills, events that occur in the game) (Morford et al. 2014) and game outcomes are always probabilistic. Even if users input the same amount of effort each time, the output could be different each time. For example, a user could receive a different number of points each time they play a game or they might lose rather than win a game even though they expended the same amount of effort. Indeed, because outcome uncertainty contributes to the fun of games, levels of distributive justice that are either too high or too low can make games less fun (Leclercq et al. 2020).

We argue that the greater the injustice perceived in a gamified system, the less likely it is that users will engage in gamified task maladaptation because inconsistency in the structure of a gamified system can obscure the rules and thus reduce the likelihood of users engaging in gamified task maladaptation. If the rules of a gamified system are applied differently to each player or applied differently over time, it is difficult for users to learn how to exploit loopholes. In contrast, when the procedural and distributive justice of a game is perceived as fair— that is, the rules are clear and applied to all users equally—users’ input will lead to a reliable output (Greenberg 1986). In this case, users can learn the rules and push the boundaries of the rules, which may, however, lead to unintended consequences. For example, a recent article on the dark side of gamifying work reported how a leaderboard tracking hotel workers’ productivity was perceived as procedurally and distributively fair in that it applied the same rules to everyone and had a predictable input-output ratio, but it created the unintended consequence of workers avoiding using the restroom out of fear that their leaderboard rankings would drop.<sup>14</sup>

Furthermore, organizational behavior research has shown that perceived procedural and distributive injustice leads to voluntary turnover and withdrawal behaviors (Holtom et al. 2008). When people perceive something as unjust, they may simply stop engaging in the activity. Therefore, users who perceive injustice may simply stop using the gamified system. This effect would be especially applicable to gamified systems designed for voluntary use.

Hypothesis 3. Gamification structure injustice is negatively related to gamified task maladaptation.

## Methods

Data Collection and Study Design. To test the research model, we designed and administered an online survey of Pocket Points users. Pocket Points is an app developed primarily for college students that aims to reduce digital distraction.<sup>15</sup> Digital distraction is a serious problem experienced by many users who find IS use to be a compulsive or addictive behavior that requires conscious effort to curb (Vaghefi et al. 2023). The founding mission of Pocket Points was to “create healthy learning environments” for students with an app that they would want to use every day. Pocket Points users can set a timer in the app that will lock their phone (turn the screen off) for a set amount of time. If users can keep the phone locked until the timer is up, they earn points, which they can redeem for product discounts, and accrue minutes, which improve their leaderboard ranking (see Online Appendix Figure C1 for an illustration of this process using Pocket Points’ interfaces).

Pocket Points is an appropriate app for our research context for two reasons. First, Pocket Points clearly presents dual goals (the game goal and the task goal) to users. For the game goal, the app offers a gamified layer in which users (1) avoid using their phone, (2) earn points based on the time they avoid using their phone, and (3) spend the points they earn on discounts from retailers. The task goal of Pocket Points can be generally described as increasing productivity through curbing digital distraction (for example, through phone-free class or study periods). Second, Pocket Points is a suitable app for this study because its design encourages GSMB. For example, users could accumulate points/minutes on Pocket Points while engaging in digital distraction on their tablet or laptop, indicating technology maladaptation. Users could also engage in gamified task maladaptation by leaving the Pocket Points timer running after completing the instrumental task (e.g., completing homework or sitting through a class without using their phone) because Pocket Points’ game rules discourage turning off the timer early.

Our online survey was designed using Qualtrics and administered on Amazon Mechanical Turk (Mturk), to ensure participant diversity (Goodman and Paolacci 2017) and to access an appropriate sample (i.e., Pocket Points users). This also increases the external validity of the study (Straub 1989). Details on the design and data collection process of Study 1 are provided in Online Appendix C. This process yielded 118 usable survey responses.

Measures. Because GSMB is a new construct, we developed new measures to cover the scope of its definition (Jarvis et al. 2003). We conceived of the two types of GSMB—technology maladaptation and gamified task maladaptation—as separate constructs (Jarvis et al. 2003, MacKenzie et al. 2005). Online Appendix D explains the process through which the GSMB construct was developed, including interviews and Q-sort exercises. The final instrument contained 15 items for GSMB (9 for technology maladaptation and 6 for gamified task maladaptation).

We adopted prevalidated items from existing constructs to measure the remaining constructs: gamification structure injustice, game-task complexity, and game-task goals misalignment. Online Appendix E lists all the items in detail. We used dispositional competitiveness as a control variable for both technology maladaptation and gamified task maladaptation.

## Summary

The results of Study 1 confirmed three out of the four hypotheses, and we found an indirect effect for Hypothesis 1b. We found that game-task goals misalignment had a significant effect on technology maladaptation and a significant indirect effect on gamified task maladaptation, confirming that game-task goals misalignment is a major design flaw capable of causing GSMB. Hence, one cannot assume that gamification will always achieve its designed outcomes because individuals are differently motivated to engage in different behaviors in the same system (Liu et al. 2017). We also found that game-task complexity is positively related to technology maladaptation and gamification structure injustice is negatively related to gamified task maladaptation, which shows that different design flaws can lead to users maladapting differently. Finally, Study 1 demonstrates the existence of GSMB and supports our conceptualization of GSMB.

In relation to the research framework (Figure 1), however, Study 1 left two aspects unexplained—namely, the psychological drivers of GSMB and whether GSMB influence task performance. Hence, we conducted another study to fill in these gaps and enhance our analysis and understanding of GSMB.

## Study 2: Effects of Autonomy,

## Competence, and Relatedness in GSMB

In Study 2, we further clarified the findings from Study 1, examined the effect of GSMB on task performance, and unpacked the underlying psychological mechanisms between game design issues and GSMB. First, to clarify how design issues influence GSMB, we experimentally manipulated the game elements (Landers et al. 2018). The design issues of three GSMB drivers were relatively fixed in the context of Study 1 (Pocket Points). In Study 2, we purposely manipulated the gamified system to allow for different levels of alignment/misalignment, complexity, and injustice. Second, we examined how GSMB influence task performance. Third, based on IS technology diffusion theory, external factors such as design factors influence users’ behavior via their percep tions of that behavior (Fishbein and Ajzen 1975, Wixom and Todd 2005). Therefore, we used SDT, which is fre quently used to examine psychological needs, in order to explore the psychological factors that may drive GSMB. In addition, we tested an alternative model of GSMB: instead of conceiving of it as having two separate dimensions, we modeled it as a second-order reflective con struct in order to focus attention on the impact of the psychological needs identified by SDT on GSMB. Finally, Study 1’s system, Pocket Points, promotes users to limit their interaction with the phone. Therefore, we did not measure interactional injustice. Study 2 requires users to interact with the system in a more involved manner. Therefore, interactional injustice is measured as a dimension for gamification structure injustice (Online Appen dix I provides further details on interactional injustice).

## Research Model and Hypotheses

Based on the research framework (Figure 1) and SDT (Deci and Ryan 1985, 2000), we argue that gamified systems can satisfy or frustrate innate psychological needs. Consistent with Deci and Ryan’s work, in our research model, we define autonomy as the degree to which users per ceive volition, control, choice, and psychological freedom when performing gamified tasks, and competence as the degree to which users perceive challenge and effectance when performing gamified tasks. We define relatedness as the degree to which users perceive the feeling of being connected to others when performing gamified tasks (Groh 2012). Consequently, these innate psychological needs drive users to engage in GSMB to satisfy them (Figure 4). We expect the psychological drivers have consistent effects on the lower-order constructs of GSMB. Therefore, in Study 2, we propose the conceptual model using the high-order construct GSMB and form the hypothesis.

## Game Design Issues’ Influence on Perceived Autonomy

Prior research has shown that autonomy-supportive game features are important to game design (Ryan et al. 2006). Peng et al. (2012) found that game elements such as character creation, choice of how to spend in-game currency, and how to develop one’s character increased autonomy satisfaction, but Sailer et al. (2017) found that simply allowing players to choose a character did not increase autonomy satisfaction. Gamification research also explored ways to increase autonomy. Hanus and Fox (2015) found that gamifying a classroom, by allowing students to earn badges and coins, undermined autonomy satisfaction. They reasoned that in their classroom context, the students were required to earn badges, which can be viewed as “mandatory fun” that lowered students’ agency.

We argue that complexity increases perceptions of autonomy (Amabile and Gitomer 1984, Sia and Appu 2015). Higher task complexity requires more effort, which increases the psychological experience of arousal (Sia and Appu 2015). Specifically, higher task complexity increases one’s interest, involvement, and curiosity, and introduces positive challenges, which indirectly leads to performance creativity (Amabile and Gitomer 1984). When complexity is high, people tend to feel that they have a lot of autonomy because a complex system offers users a repertoire of actions to choose from. To take a game-related example, imagine a card game of five rounds in which two players randomly draw a card from their hands, and the higher card wins that round. This simple card game does not offer much player autonomy. One can add complexity by allowing the players to look at their card hands and allowing players to choose the card to play. This added complexity engages their cognition by offering players the autonomy to choose their cards and engage in more creative play (i.e., sacrificing lower-value cards in earlier rounds to increase the likelihood of winning later rounds). Using the gamified classroom example, if earning badges is separate from students’ coursework, the complexity increases, leading to higher autonomy. Based on the arguments above, we hypothesize that

Hypothesis 4a. Game-task complexity is positively related to perceived autonomy in performing gamified tasks.

However, perceptions of gamification structure injustice can frustrate the need for autonomy. We argue that poor feedback (i.e., unjust game rules that lead to unfair outcomes) makes it hard for users to exercise autonomy. If rules are perceived as unfair, users may feel that their behaviors and efforts will not result in fair outcomes and they may thus experience a sense of helplessness and lack of control. In addition, feedback enables users to draw links between input and output (Locke and Latham 2002, Jung et al. 2010). When such links are not clear and the relationship between behavior and outcomes seems inscrutable, users’ sense of autonomy will be reduced and they may feel that the system is unfair. For example, if a user of a gamified learning system is working toward a specific badge and a system update removes the badge or makes it much more difficult to obtain, the user may perceive procedural injustice because the rules for earning a specific badge changed unfavorably with the update. Likewise, users may perceive interactional injustice if rule changes occur without warning or explanation. Users may view such injustice as an enactment of power, which can frustrate the basic need for autonomy (Lawrence and Robinson 2007) in that users may feel a lack of control in the activity of earning badges. Thus, we hypothesize that perceptions of gamification structure injustice can reduce one’s sense of autonomy:

Hypothesis 4b. Gamification structure injustice is negatively related to perceived autonomy in performing gamified tasks.

Game Design Issues’ Influence on Perceived Compe tence. Game-task goals misalignment and gamification structure injustice both provide feedback to users that may frustrate their need for competence. When game and task goals are misaligned, it is difficult for users to receive feedback indicating effectance. Positive feedback indicating effectance satisfies the psychological need for competence whereas negative feedback indicating a lack of effectance frustrates this need (Deci and Ryan 2000). Because of game-task goals misalignment, it may be difficult or even impossible for users to achieve both goals at once: Whereas users who focus on an instrumental task might receive negative feedback on game performance, users who focus on game performance might receive positive feedback on this but realize that they are not achieving the task goal (e.g., understanding the material). Self-efficacy theory (Bandura 1977) further explains that if users are unable to achieve desired outcomes, they may feel incompetent, frustrating their need for competence.

Similarly, increased gamification structure injustice may make the game rules more difficult to understand because of inconsistencies in rule applications (i.e., procedural injustice) and the input-output ratio across users (i.e., distributive injustice). Rules that are difficult to understand because of their inconsistency may cause users to feel less competent. Likewise, rules that are perceived to be unfair may also cause users to feel less competent because of an unpredictable input-output ratio. Taken together, if the game and task goals in a gamified system are misaligned or the system’s rules are unfair, participants will likely feel that they lack the competence to understand the rules or achieve the desired outcomes. Consequently, we hypothesize that

Hypothesis 5a. Game-task goals misalignment is negatively related to perceived competence in performing gamified tasks.

Hypothesis 5b. Gamification structure injustice is negatively related to perceived competence in performing gamified tasks.

The Influence of Game Design Issues on Perceived Relatedness. Relatedness refers to users’ feelings of connectedness with other users. Gamification features that support cooperation or social support systems may increase users’ sense of relatedness (Liu et al. 2017). However, we propose that high levels of perceived game-task complexity increase users’ need for relatedness in the context of multiplayer games with or without cooperation or social support. High complexity is often perceived as confusing, difficult, and stressful and thus leads people to seek or provide support (Bastian et al. 2018). Working with others is a common and basic response to difficult experiences and leads to higher levels of group identification (Taylor et al. 2000, Whitehouse et al. 2017). Social identity theory further explains why users bond over high-complexity tasks: People tend to classify themselves and others into membership groups (Tajfel and Turner 2004), and shared experiences play a role in facilitating this process (Tajfel 1978). For example, just as students might bond over a difficult course or soldiers might bond over difficult training, game players can bond over confusing or complex game rules even though they are competing against each other, thus increasing their feeling of connectedness with others. This may be one of the reasons why “escape rooms” are powerful team-building exercises (Zhang et al. 2018). We therefore hypothesize that

Hypothesis 6a. Game-task complexity is positively related to users’ perceived relatedness.

Conversely, we believe that game-task goals misalign ment and gamification structure injustice can frustrate the need for relatedness. The misalignment of the game goal and the task goal forces users to choose one goal or another. Therefore, users will gravitate toward either the game goal or the task goal. The division of the user base can lower relatedness. The reason is that the users are not pursuing a common goal. They are pursuing, and motivated by, different goals. Because of the misalignment of the goals, they cannot work together. In a gamified training setting, a user who tries to learn the material can relate very little to a user who maladapts to increase points.

Perceived gamification structure injustice can also frustrate the need for relatedness, arising from elements that trigger social comparison. Ho et al. (2022) showed that competition can often trigger social comparison. The perception of injustice is triggered through social com parison as, for example, users realizing that rules applied to some do not apply to others; some receive more rewards although everyone input the same amount of effort. Furthermore, individuals are intrinsically moti vated to win because losing is associated with low ability or effort (Weinberg and Jackson 1979) and lower levels of perceived competence or self-determination (Reeve and Deci 1996). Therefore, to avoid experiencing the emotions associated with losing, players will often work to become more competitive, and competitiveness can lead to players engaging in more antisocial behaviors in order to defeat others and eventually win (Perlman 2012). Social identity theory further explains this divide among players. People identify themselves relative to in dividuals in other categories (Tajfel and Turner 2004)— that is, high-performing players identify themselves as distinct from low-performing players, and players focused on a game goal tend to distance themselves from players focused on a task goal. Gamification structure injustice may further increase this effect. If players feel that they are being treated unfairly, they may become envious of others, believing that others benefit from the unfairness in the system. Equity theory also states that players compare their own input-output ratio to that of others and that any perceived unfairness can lead to envy (Tai et al. 2012, Ghadi 2018). Envy interferes with positive interpersonal relationships, creates conflict, damages relationships, and thereby reduces people’s perceived relatedness (Krasnova et al. 2015). We therefore hypothesize that

Hypothesis 6b. Game-task goals misalignment is negatively related to users’ perceived relatedness.

Hypothesis 6c. Gamification structure injustice is negatively related to users’ perceived relatedness.

The Influence of Autonomy, Competence, and Relatedness on GSMB. Both satisfaction with autonomy and competence can lead users to engage in GSMB. Several literatures have explicitly linked autonomy and competence with innovativeness. Ahuja and Thatcher (2005) found that perceptions of autonomy increased users’ likelihood of trying to innovate. However, too much autonomy in performing a task may lead to unintended behaviors. Fox et al. (2001) found a correlation between autonomy and counterproductive work behavior, which has some conceptual similarities with GSMB. Similarly, too much competence may also lead to unintended behaviors and encourage people to engage in risky behaviors and deviate from the norm. Luo et al. (2020) also found a correlation between self-efficacy and computer abuse. Users with high levels of perceived autonomy and competence may be motivated to innovate and test the boundaries of the rules and system. Taken together, extant research suggests that higher levels of autonomy and competence in performing game tasks give users more latitude and confidence to engage in GSMB. We thus hypothesize that

Hypothesis 7a. Perceived autonomy in performing gamified tasks is positively related to GSMB.

Hypothesis 7b. Perceived competence in performing gamified tasks is positively related to GSMB.

Players fulfilling their need for relatedness could potentially lead to fewer GSMB because when a person feels related to others performing the same gamified task, they are more likely to be prosocial and less likely to deviate from the norm by engaging in GSMB. Pavey et al. (2011) found that instructions that use words intended to increase relatedness (i.e., “we are here to help and support you”) can increase intentions to engage in prosocial behaviors. This notion is supported by the SDT framework, which suggests that satisfying a particular need leads to behaviors that continue to satisfy that need (Ryan and Deci 2000). In the context of our study, we hypothesize that when users experience high levels of relatedness, they are less likely to engage in GSMB because gaining an unfair advantage over their peers would decrease their sense of relatedness.

Hypothesis 7c. Perceived relatedness is negatively related to GSMB.

Task Performance. GSMB could potentially reduce task performance. Task performance is “a measure of the productivity of a task, holding constant time and thinking costs” (Bellman and Murray 2018, p. 1). Technology maladaptation involves finding workarounds using other technologies or hacking the gamified system. Such deviant behaviors reduce individuals’ task performance by diverting resources away from completion of the task at hand (Bennett and Naumann 2005). Furthermore, the effort directed toward the game goal may compromise the task goal. Given limited time and effort, users may have to choose to prioritize one goal over the other (Kruglanski et al. 2015). When users engage in GSMB, they are prioritizing achieving the game goal, which will reduce the effort they devote to the task goal, thereby compromising their task performance. We therefore hypothesize that

Hypothesis 8. GSMB are negatively related to task performance.

## Method

First, we conducted a within-subject experiment to examine how participants react to different design features. Second, we conducted a follow-up survey to examine how participants’ psychological needs may influence GSMB.

Research Design and Participants. We conducted a within-subject experiment because it removes the effects of individual differences on outcomes and increases statistical power (Shadish et al. 2002). We designed a gami fied online learning system with a series of sequential web pages that participants could use to learn the game rules and familiarize themselves with the European Union (EU) General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). The learning system is designed to simulate a corporate compliance training. We tell participants that we want them to learn the material to ensure they understand that it is a goal of the activity. Following this, participants took multiple-choice quizzes on the GDPR. We selected this topic because our participants were from the United States, making it unlikely that they had extensive knowl edge about this EU regulation. To gamify the task, we designed points, badges, and rankings that participants could earn.

The experiment started with informed consent and instructions, as well as a verbal indication that no identifying information would be collected in order to reduce evaluation apprehension when answering questions about GSMB. The experiment had three rounds. All participants in a given session started each round at the same time. After each round, the scores, badges, and rankings were displayed to the participants. Then, the participants began the next round. The top three participants in each session earned a \$5 gift card to a business of their choosing. We familiarized participants with this gamified learning system in a practice round. We recruited 216 participants to participate in the multiplesession experiment in a computer laboratory. Each session ranged from 3 to 20 participants. The mean number of participants is 10.8; the median is 10. We removed participants who failed the attention check question and those who did not complete the surveys, resulting in a usable sample of 167 (51.5% were female, and users only identified themselves as male or female; 71.26% were between 18 and 24 years old).

Manipulation. As summarized in Table 2, we designed new game rules for each round to manipulate design elements and observe resulting user behaviors. To observe the effects of technology maladaptation and gamified task maladaptation on task performance, the game goals were designed to incentivize users to engage in gamified task maladaptation in rounds 1 and 2 and technology maladaptation in round 3. In round 1, participants were told that points were based on completion time, that is, how long it took them to read the passage and answer the questions. The faster the participants completed the task, the more points they obtained. In round 2, they were told that points were based on both completion time and the number of correct answers. Participants were told that they could attempt to answer each question three consecutive times and that the answer would be revealed after each attempt that returned an incorrect answer. This workaround in round 2 was made clear to the participants. There was no incentive to answer the quiz zes correctly on the first attempt in either of these rounds. In round 1, the points, badges, and rankings were based on completion time rather than the accuracy of the answer. In round 2, participants could exploit the workaround and see the correct answer after an incorrect attempt. Additionally, participants had no incentive to use search engines (technology maladaptation) in the first two rounds because completion time mattered most for points, badges, and rankings. Therefore, we anticipated that gamified task maladaptation would be much more pronounced than technology maladaptation in the first two rounds. In round 3, participants were instructed to focus only on answering the questions correctly and were given unlimited time. In this round, perceptions of game-task goals misalignment were lower because participants had to answer quizzes correctly to gain points and were not constrained by time limits. However, the rules of round 1 and round 2 are designed to induce perceived game-task goals misalignment, because the parti cipants were told that we wanted them to learn about the GDPR.

In each round, we designed one of the questions to be impossible to answer using only the materials provided. Therefore, we expected technology maladaptation to be more pronounced in round 3 because participants would have to use search engines to maximize their points. The rule changes across the three rounds were used to increase the perception of game-task complexity. Furthermore, exactly how points were calculated was hidden from participants to increase perceived game-task complexity and perceived gamification structure injustice. Finally, each round had a question that could not be answered using the provided learning materials. This was used to increase the perception of gamification structure injustice and encourage participants to engage in technology maladaptation by using a search engine to look up the answers.<sup>16</sup> After the experiment, the final scores, badges, and rankings were announced.

## Data Analysis

We separately analyzed the data collected from the within-subject experiment and the follow-up survey. The experiment involved the manipulation of game design issues and repeated objective measures. The data were suitable to observe different levels of GSMB and examine how GSMB affected task performance. The data collected from the separate follow-up survey were subjective and were mostly Likert-scale items on psychological needs. Therefore, the survey data were appropriate to examine the mediation effects of autonomy, competence, and relatedness. We report the data analysis and results in two parts in the following sections.

## Part 1 (Experimental Data): The Effect of GSMB on Task Performance

Measures. We measured task performance by counting the number of questions the participants answered correctly. We only recorded questions with answers in the learning materials. The questions that could not be answered using the learning materials were designed to encourage participants to engage in technology maladaptation and thus were not counted as part of task performance. We measured gamified task maladaptation using completion time in seconds, given that gamified task maladaptation allowed for speedy completion of the task. We measured technology maladaptation using search frequency—that is, the number of questions the participants searched for online in each round. Because some people may not wish to admit that they used workarounds because that could be considered cheating, using observable measures such as completion time and observed maladaptive behavior helped alleviate this concern to some degree. We recorded participants web browsing history in the computer laboratory during the activity and checked for relevant searches. Online Appendix H displays the frequency distribution and percentage count of technology maladaptation and task performance, and the descriptive statistics of gamified-task maladaptation, as well as examples of detailed measures.

The Presence of GSMB. We conducted two separate analyses to examine the presence of technology maladaptation and gamified task maladaptation. To examine whether participants engaged in technology maladapta tion, we used technology maladaptation as the dependent variable, the activity round as the independent variable, and age, gender, and work experience as the control variables. We conducted a within-subject experiment: the same participants were engaged in three different rounds of activities. The measurements are nested within individual participants because their technology maladaptation and gamified task maladaptation were repeatedly recorded three consecutive times. In addition, the dependent variables are either discrete (e.g., technology maladaptation: search frequency) or continuous (e.g., gamified task maladaptation: completion time in seconds). Therefore, we used a multilevel regression model to account for (1) repeated measures and (2) either continuous or discrete dependent variables (Gelman and Hill 2007, Hoffman and Rovine 2007). Multilevel regres sion is superior to repeated-measure analysis of variance (ANOVA) because multilevel regression (1) can easily extend to discrete dependent variables and (2) has relaxed assumptions (for example, does not need to assume equal variance (Cohen et al. 2003)).

We fitted a multilevel negative binomial model (Wooldridge 2002) to account for repeated measures and the count data of the dispersed dependent variable with robust standard errors (SE). ## Summary

Study 2 supported 10 out of the 11 hypotheses. We found that game design issues (i.e., game-task complexity, gamification structure injustice, or game-task goals misalignment) satisfied or frustrated the psychological needs of autonomy, competence, and relatedness in line with the expectations expressed in Hypothesis 4, Hypothesis 5, and Hypothesis 6. In addition, perceived autonomy and competence increased GSMB (Hypothesis 7a and Hypothesis 7b), whereas perceived relatedness (Hypothesis 7c) decreased it. In the controlled within-subject group experiment, we found that the extent of GSMB is important—the greater the GSMB, the greater the negative impact on task performance. This supports our hypothesis that GSMB reduce task performance (Hypothesis 8).

## Discussion

In this study, we propose and show that the dual goals present in gamified systems may lead users to misappropriate and engage in maladaptive behaviors that result in unintended behaviors and outcomes. The tension between task goals and game goals may lead users to pursue one goal at the expense of the other. Therefore, it is critical that designers pay careful attention to the design of gamified systems to achieve, rather than thwart, the intended purpose of adding game elements to an IS. We advance a novel conceptualization called gamified system maladaptive behaviors (GSMB) as potentially occurring because of either technology maladaptation or gamified task maladaptation. Leveraging goal theory, self-determination theory, adaptive system use, and principles of gamification, we conducted two studies to examine GSMB, the potential users’ psychological needs, potential design triggers, and the potential impact of GSMB on task performance. In the first study, we found evidence for GSMB and for design issues that are likely to lead to such behaviors, such as game-task goal misalignment, game-task complexity, and gamification structure injustice. After finding evidence of GSMB in the first study, we then manipulated the design issues of the gamified system in the second study in order to study the effects and found that design issues can frustrate or satisfy users’ psychological needs and thereby cause users to engage in GSMB. Furthermore, we found that the extent of the impact on task performance is dependent on the extent of maladaptation.

Taken together, the findings from the two studies show that GSMB are very common and can be triggered by design issues in gamified systems. In our Study 1 post hoc analysis, we found that gamified system design issues closely correlated with each other. Specifically, the perception of game-task goals misalignment led users to perceive game-task complexity and gamification structure injustice. In addition, regarding gamification design principles, we found that design issues such as gametask goals misalignment are critical (Liu et al. 2017). Our findings also indicate that GSMB can have a negative effect on task performance depending on the design issues (Study 2). It is possible that users who engage in GSMB may focus too intently on winning the game, at the expense of instrumental task performance. Our findings also suggest that when design issues are carefully addressed, users are less likely to engage in maladaptive behaviors and the desired outcomes of gamified systems will be more likely to be achieved. Finally, we found that users’ psychological needs can be either satisfied or frustrated by GSMB; we also found that users tend to prioritize game goals over task goals. Our findings suggest that autonomy and competence are positively related to GSMB, suggesting that (1) people who perceive an ample degree of personal freedom tend to test boundaries and engage in GSMB, and (2) people who perceive high levels of self-mastery tend to have the confidence to engage in GSMB. However, the negative relationship between relatedness and GSMB indicates that when multiple users are performing a task, even as competitors, they are less likely to engage in GSMB, which thereby promotes the desired instrumental outcomes. We theorize that the feeling of relatedness may trigger altruism, which often conflicts with the intention to engage in GSMB.

## Contributions to Theory

Our study contributes to the literature on gamification in several ways. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study proposing the idea of users engaging in maladaptive behaviors in gamified systems. Furthermore, we conceptualize these behaviors with a new construct—GSMB—which identifies two distinct sets of maladaptation that lead to such behaviors: technology maladaptation and gamified task maladaptation. We show that design issues can induce such maladaptive behaviors and thereby underscore the critical role of design in gamified systems. Although paying attention to design is important in any IS design, in gamified systems, it is essential—our findings indicate that design issues can cause people to focus on game goals at the expense of task goals. Our study shows that if the major gamification principle of congruence between game and instrumental tasks is not followed, it can lead to users misappropriating and engaging in maladaptive behaviors that can distract users from the intended design outcomes of gamified systems (Liu et al. 2017). Our research is quite timely—because productivity, education, and training systems are increasingly adding hedonic features (e.g., virtual reality and augmented reality), it is critical to recognize the potential of maladaptive behaviors in such emergent systems (Lowry et al. 2013).

Our model not only includes design elements but also integrates their influence on users’ psychological needs, which allowed us to examine the “how” of maladaptive behaviors. Our findings are illuminating, particularly because most studies suggest that design issues in gamified systems lead to the satisfaction of all the psychological needs described in SDT and that these effects are similar across design elements (Deci and Ryan 2000, Seaborn and Fels 2015, Nacke and Deterding 2017). In contrast, our granular approach to understanding the “how” explains how design elements may not have the same effect on satisfying psychological needs (e.g., whereas game-task complexity increases autonomy, gamification structure injustice decreases autonomy). A couple of gamification studies have begun to question the assumptions of uniform effects on users’ psychological needs, suggesting that a design element added to satisfy psychological needs may lead to a trade-off in that one need is highly satisfied at the expense of another (Mekler et al. 2017, Sailer et al. 2017, Van Roy and Zaman 2019). Our study findings contribute to this emerging body of research and punctuate how important it is to ensure that adequate attention and care are devoted to the design of game elements because they may not uniformly fulfill all the psychological needs described by SDT, which may lead to trade-offs in the satisfaction of psychological needs, potentially triggering maladaptive behaviors.

In recent years, recognizing concerns that gamified systems may not always lead to the intended outcomes, researchers have suggested personalization and tailoring game design to users’ characteristics as a solution (Scho¨bel et al. 2019, Leung et al. 2023). Although this approach could alleviate some gamification problems, our study suggests that a lack of personalization may not be the only reason for gamification failures—indeed, unintended outcomes may occur because of maladaptive behaviors that are caused by other design issues. One fruitful avenue for future research would be to examine gamification failures in relation to factors other than personalization. Our findings suggest that personalization may not be enough—rather, gamification design should also focus on preventing users from engaging in maladaptive behaviors (Oliveira et al. 2023).

To the best of our knowledge, this is one of the few studies that investigate the existence of dual goals (i.e., the game goal and task goal) from a goal theory perspective. Goal theory is a widely used foundation in IS research and gamification to understand goal-directed behavior (Parboteeah et al. 2009, Goes et al. 2016, Land ers et al. 2017). Our study shows that dual goals can create a tension where one goal is prioritized over the other. This dual-goal situation, where there is pressure to fulfill two goals simultaneously, contributes to the literature on goal theory by presenting a new issue—how can dual goals be aligned by design so that users can focus on both goals without compromising either? Dual goals also pose the question of how feedback regarding goals should be aligned and designed.

Most of the existing research on adaptive system use has assumed a positive outcome, suggesting that users adapt to the task at hand (Ahuja and Thatcher 2005, Beaudry and Pinsonneault 2005, Sun 2012). Our study shows that such adaptation may be detrimental or maladaptive from design and organizational perspectives and thus calls for more research on maladaptive system use. We additionally extend the existing research by showing that it is not only design or use triggers that lead to adaptations but also psychological needs that serve as potent motivational drivers.

## Contributions to Practice

It is important that practitioners recognize that it is critical to devote careful thought and attention to gamification design, because of its dual-goal nature. As we show here, simply adding game elements, such as points and badges to learning systems, may actually cause design issues that we describe as game-task goals misalignment, game-task complexity, and gamification structure injustice, which may lead users to pursue game goals rather than task goals and may motivate maladaptive behaviors. For example, some gamified learning systems reward users simply for completing modules rather than for true learning or knowledge acquisition. This creates dissonance, which we refer to as game-task goals misalignment. Users can engage in maladaptive behaviors by getting answers from external technology sources and clicking through modules to satisfy game goals and earn points or badges. This reduces the achievement of the task goal of acquiring knowledge. In using gamified learning systems for education, teachers have become increasingly aware of maladaptive behaviors and often resort to using quiz scores to evaluate knowledge acquisition instead. Given that emergent systems such as hedonic/gamified systems and other multitasking systems are being designed with multiple goals, designers should go to great lengths to prevent the emergence of maladaptive behaviors because of misalignment and tension between different gamified system goals. One such example is the badges Uber drivers can earn, which are “Entertaining Driver,” “Great Conversation,” “Awesome Music,” etc.<sup>17</sup> Attempting to earn these badges can cause drivers to become distracted.

The design issues we identify can help designers evaluate design elements at a more abstract, generalized, and fundamental level. For example, an organization could break down its gamified system outcomes into task goals and game goals and a gamification designer could address any discrepancies between the two types of goals.

This would be applicable across different tasks (e.g., learning, productivity, etc.) and game goals (e.g., points, badges, etc.).

Education is one of the most common applications for gamification (Callan et al. 2015). Because gamifying for education is usually aimed at younger users, designers tend to use features that will engage their target users. However, practitioners are starting to realize certain risks involving gamification, such as overstimulation (students spending too much effort on game goals and mismanaging their time) and reductions in students attention span (students expecting instant rewards).<sup>18</sup> Our research shows that gamified systems may cause learners to ignore learning goals and engage in maladap tive behaviors. We believe platforms such as Coursera and Udemy could emphasize badges less but focus more on displaying metrics that measure progress and learning. Users who voluntarily join these platforms to learn may be more interested in seeing these metrics than badges. For younger users (kindergarten to 12th grade (K-12)) who may experience overstimulation, educators should avoid gamifying too many elements (i.e., having students create characters which they can customize). Instead, simple team-based trivia-style games may suffice, as team-based activities can increase relatedness.

## Limitations and Future Research Directions

Because of our work’s novelty and complexity, we developed many original constructs for our study. Future work is needed to further validate our measures and extend them. For example, because of measurement issues for game-task complexity in Study 2, game-task complexity only included component complexity. Al though there is precedence for complexity being measured as a perception (Nadkarni and Gupta 2007, Sepehr and Head 2018), some prior studies have been able to objectively manipulate complexity (Jiang and Benbasat 2007, Wallace et al. 2008). To address this concern, future researchers might consider developing a highly custom izable app that affords opportunities to manipulate complexity and specific forms of complexity that are more, or less, relevant in different systems.

There are many ways that this study can serve as a catalyst for future research on gamification. This study is the first to propose and present an initial analysis of gamified system maladaptive behaviors, framed using the lens of GST. Future research could use other theoreti cal frameworks to further study and analyze maladap tive behaviors, not only in gamified systems but also in hedonic systems where there is more than one goal. We changed the game design in our second study in order to trigger maladaptive behaviors. Other designs and other game elements could be used in future studies. Future research could specifically focus on game elements such as graphics, storytelling, narratives, and high system interactivity that tend to be engaging, often distract users from task goals, and have greater potential to cause maladaptive behaviors. Although we studied technology and gamified task maladaptation, other aspects such as mechanics and object maladaptation could also be examined.

Our study shows that gamification elements can satisfy users’ psychological needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness in different ways that may involve trade-offs. Future research could explore this issue further and question the assumption that gamification elements are equally adept at satisfying psychological needs. Future gamification research could study positive adaptive behaviors. Although prior research has explored adaptive behaviors (Barki et al. 2007, Sun 2012), scant attention has been devoted to positive adaptation in the gamification context. A model covering both positive adaptive behaviors and GSMB, as well as the relationships between them, could provide a more holistic picture of how users adapt to gamified systems. In summary, our study proposes and reveals the existence and triggers of maladaptive behaviors in gamified systems, which advances gamification theory and practice and opens many avenues for future research.
