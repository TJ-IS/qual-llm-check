# The Impact of Transparency-Inducing Management Information System Use on Employees’ Daily Work Performance

Abstract. Management information systems (MIS) are increasingly used in all types of workplaces. Although they may be introduced to enhance productivity and performance, they frequently fail to live up to these expectations in practice. In this study, we develop a theoretical model based on the theory of effective MIS use and on the transparency paradox to explain how different facets of performance transparency shape the relationship between MIS use and employee work performance. This model acknowledges that an MIS can offer varying degrees and types of transparency about performances, especially as an MIS can enable individuals to clearly understand their own metrics or allow team members to view each other’s performances. This study focuses on the conditions (i.e., self-directed and team-directed performance transparency) under which MIS use is more or less conducive to employees’ work performance. We tested our conceptual model using an experience sampling study of 1,186 daily observations of 141 employees in a sales context. The results highlight that daily MIS use positively relates to daily employee work performance. This relationship is strengthened when one’s own performance is made more transparent to oneself, whereas it is weakened when one’s performance is made more transparent among peers. The theoretical and practical implications of these findings are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

The advancement of information technology and its increasing diffusion in the business world are transforming organizations’ business processes and employees’ working environments (Cascio and Montealegre 2016, Colbert et al. 2016). As part of this digital transformation, firms are continuously and substantially increasing the implementation of management information systems (MIS) to manage customer information, facilitate employee decision making, and improve communication processes (Lam et al. 2017, Steelman et al. 2019). Although it has been found that MIS may raise work performance (Ahearne et al. 2008, Steelman et al. 2019), studies have also demonstrated that many companies fail to achieve their goals after investing in new MIS (Kim and Gupta 2014, Bala and Bhagwatwar 2018, Habel et al. 2024). To better understand the effects of MIS use, previous studies have explored how newly implemented MIS are used and integrated into employees’ work lives (Burton-Jones and Grange 2013, Burton-

Jones et al. 2017, Trieu et al. 2022). This impactful research on effective MIS use has culminated in the for mulation of a theory of effective use (TEU), which considers the use of MIS as effective if the MIS is used “in a way that helps attain the goals for using the system” (Burton-Jones and Grange 2013, p. 633). This seminal theory yielded valuable insights about the “what” and “why” of MIS use’s performance implications for individual employees (Burton-Jones and Grange 2013, Burton-Jones et al. 2017).

To better understand MIS use, the “what" and “why' focus of MIS use’s performance implications can be enriched with a “when” perspective to explore when MIS use elevates the performances of its users and when it might not yield beneficial outcomes. Prior research (Sun et al. 2019, Venkatesh et al. 2022) has examined contingency factors related to MIS use and revealed that the nature of use, task-technology fit, and the features of the MIS can impact the effectiveness of MIS use. One salient feature of MIS is the level of transparency induced by the system to observe how users of the MIS perform (Cicala et al. 2014). Although transparency induction is an important aspect of MIS and has been intensely discussed by scholars and practitioners in the context of technology-enhanced productivity (Tapscott and Ticoll 2003, Singh and Johnston 2019), it has not yet been regarded as a relevant contingency in the use–performance relationship. This oversight is problematic, given the pivotal role of transparency in organizations, with new technologies increasingly enabling individuals to observe, and be observed by, others at work (Bernstein 2017). From a conceptual perspective, it is important to study transparency as a moderator of the MIS use performance linkage, because effects of transparency can be ambivalent and are not necessarily uniformly positive. Semina works on transparency in work settings suggest that different types of transparency can entail either positive or negative effects on work performance (Bernstein 2017). Based on prior conceptualizations of transparencyinducing technology (Cicala et al. 2014), we define transparency inducing as the extent to which applications in the workplace enhance the possibilities for tracking and monitoring employee behaviors and performance. MIS provide large amounts of data on and insights into employees’ work activities and are therefore able to induce higher levels of transparency in the workplace (Da Cunha 2013, Lam et al. 2017), especially regarding the performance and performance-related behaviors of individual employees (Cicala et al. 2014, Singh and Johnston 2019). Such increased transparency not only raises legal and ethical concerns (Bush et al. 2010, Cascio and Montealegre 2016) but also shapes employees’ work environments and might considerably affect their behaviors and performance (Li et al. 2021, Parker and Grote 2022).

To address this research gap, we build on TEU (Burton-Jones and Grange 2013, Burton-Jones et al. 2017) and integrate the concept of the transparency paradox (Bernstein 2012, 2017). From this theoretical foundation, we develop a conceptual model that examines the relationship between effective MIS use and employee performance. This theoretical perspective not only explains the consequences of varying levels of performance transparency (PT), both positive and negative, but also differentiates the distinct roles of the observer and the observed (Bernstein 2012, 2017). Thus, we distinguish two foci of PT: self-directed PT and teamdirected PT. This differentiation has important behavioral consequences that likely affect the outcomes of effective MIS use. Consequently, we propose moderation hypotheses that explore how different foci of PT influence the relationship between MIS use and employee performance, depending on self-directed PT and team-directed PT. In sum, our conceptual model suggests that the effects of individual effective MIS use on employee work performance are contingent on the level and focus of PT. We tested this conceptual model by applying an experience-sampling methodology based on 1,186 daily observations of 141 sales employ ees who rely on MIS in their work activities after a new measure for PT based on an additional sample of 336 salespeople had been validated. This methodology enabled us to map intraindividual changes because of MIS use on employees’ daily work performance in a multilevel model (Mehl and Conner 2012).

This study contributes to theory development regard ing the consequences of employees’ effective MIS use. First, prior theorizing and empirical studies have yielded inconsistent results regarding the effects of MIS use on employees’ work performance. Although some studies have provided evidence for MIS use’s association with improved work performance (Steelman et al. 2019, Venkatesh et al. 2022), others have not (Tarafdar et al. 2014, Roma´n and Rodr´ıguez 2015). By leveraging the TEU (Burton-Jones and Grange 2013), we demon strate that these mixed findings can be explained through the moderating role of PT. Specifically, we argue and empirically illustrate that the performance implications of effective MIS use are contingent on the degree and focus of transparency induced by MIS— thus explicitly resolving contradictions in prior literature by specifying performance transparency as an essential boundary conditions previously overlooked. Integrating transparency into theory development for effective MIS use therefore advances and refines the current conceptual model and deepens our knowledge about the path from effective MIS use to improved employee performance in the TEU model.

Second, we provide clarity regarding the contradictory evidence surrounding the impact of transparency on employees’ work outcomes. Existing research offers mixed perspectives in this regard: some scholars emphasize positive outcomes of transparency, highlighting benefits such as increased accountability, motivation, and task engagement (Claggett and Karahanna 2018, Grover et al. 2018). Conversely, others emphasize negative effects, suggesting transparency induces stress, heightened surveillance pressure, and reduced psychological safety (Bamberger and Belogolovsky 2017, Singh and Johnston 2019). To reconcile these divergent perspectives, our study introduces and empirically tests a differentiation between two distinct types of transparency: selfdirected PT and team-directed PT. By clearly delineating these types, we reveal that transparency’s impact on performance is not uniformly positive or negative but rather conditional on its focus. In sum, our more nuanced exploration of transparency identifies conditions under which transparency enhances versus disrupts performance, resolving existing contradictions by offering a conditional, context-sensitive theoretical model.

Third, past research conceptualized transparency in generic terms, without distinguishing the specific content of transparency (Be´langer and Crossler 2011, Cicala et al. 2014). We therefore contribute by clearly identifying and pointing to the relevance of a specific content of transparency: PT, which we further distinguish it into self- and team-oriented PT. To support this refined conceptualization and the more nuanced exploration, we developed and validated a robust measure for PT that reflects the systematic distinction between transparency directed at the self versus the team. This measure offers a foundation for future research, enabling scholars to investigate PT more rigorously and facilitating clearer theoretical integration and practical application within the MIS context. ## 2. Theoretical Background 2.1. Theory of Effective Use

Organizations invest heavily in digital technology, in many cases without reaping the expected benefits (Kim and Gupta 2014, Steelman et al. 2019). To explain the wide variation in the outcomes of these investments (Ahearne et al. 2008), scholars have moved beyond conceptual models that focus on the antecedents and contingencies of the acceptance and use of MIS (Jasperson et al. 2005, Barki et al. 2007, Straub and Burton-Jones 2007), and have begun to scrutinize when and how MIS can be used to achieve the desired outcomes (Burton-Jones and Grange 2013, Burton-Jones and Volkoff 2017). The primary object of research has not been MIS use in general but how, and how effectively, MIS are used in firms (Burton-Jones and Volkoff 2017, Trieu et al. 2022). A diverse range of conceptualizations of various MIS usage behaviors has been considered in research on postadoptive MIS use (Jasperson et al. 2005, Burleson et al. 2021). For example, studies have explored how employees might broaden the functionalities of the MIS during and after MIS implementation (Burton-Jones and Straub 2006) and examined employees’ inventive uses of the MIS (Ahuja and Thatcher 2005, Li et al. 2013). This research considered how employees adjust their usage patterns (Sun 2012, Schmitz et al. 2016, Burleson et al. 2021) or strive for advanced levels of information systems (IS) utilization, such as infusion use, to enhance their work (Jones et al. 2002, Sundaram et al. 2007).

Based on this rationale, the TEU was introduced by Burton-Jones and Grange (2013), which builds on the foundational principles of representation theory; this theory is founded on the notion that the core purpose of information systems is to support users’ understand ings of real-world systems and their states that are rele vant for them and their tasks (Wand and Weber 1990, Weber 1997). Burton-Jones and Grange (2013) defined effective use as the use of an information system in a way that aids in achieving the system’s intended goals. Moreover, they have described three dimensions of effective use (i.e., transparent interaction, representational fidelity, informed action) to explain which conditions must be met for a user to benefit from an information system. In this regard, it is important that effective MIS use as conceptualized by Burton-Jones and Grange (2013) does not by itself imply improved employee performances. Rather, concerning the three dimensions they state that “these dimensions should increase performance according to representation theory, but whether they do is an empirical question” (Burton-Jones and Grange $2 0 1 3 , \mathsf { p } .$ 642).

The first dimension of effective MIS use, as stipulated by Burton-Jones and Grange (2013), is that a user must be capable of accessing the representations through the system’s interface and physical structure. This transparent interaction is the degree to which a user accesses the system’s representations without hindrance, for example, from its interface. The next dimension of effective MIS use is that a user requires accurate representations. This representational fidelity represents the degree to which the representations from the MIS accurately reflect the real-world systems. The third dimension of effective MIS use, that is, informed action, follows from the accurate representations that enable a user to take action; this informed action is the extent to which a user acts on the representations obtained from the information system to move toward goal attainment (Burton-Jones and Grange 2013).

Importantly, these three dimensions of effective MIS use are distinct from the notion of perceived usefulness (Venkatesh et al. 2003), as they focus on the rewards derived from the way an MIS is used, thereby characterizing effective use as an observable behavior. These three dimensions of effective use are interrelated (Trieu et al. 2022) and have been linked to both efficiency and effectiveness performance outcomes (Burton-Jones and Grange 2013, Trieu et al. 2022). Although the TEU was formulated as a general theory rather than for a specific context, recent research indicates that the theory can be applied and extended to account for context-specific considerations, such as business intelligence (Trieu et al. 2022). Building on the TEU, we conceptualize effective MIS use as providing transparent interaction, representational fidelity, and informed action (Burton-Jones and Grange 2013). Moreover, effective MIS use should improve employees’ work performances (Burton-Jones and Grange 2013, Trieu et al. 2022).

## 2.2. Transparency Paradox and MIS Use

One important feature of MIS, which practitioners and researchers often mention in the context of technologyenhanced performance, is the extent to which MIS use allows for insights into employees’ work activities and performance (Be´langer and Crossler 2011, Da Cunha 2013, Lam et al. 2017). Consider, for example, the quality and quantity of data and functionalities that MIS such as Salesforce or Microsoft Dynamics offer to companies, managers, and employees. Common practical examples of MIS deployment based on such data include the implementation of gamification elements in MIS applications (Liu et al. 2017) or dashboards based on cloud computing that may display daily updated work summaries and performance rankings for employee monitoring (Gibbs et al. 2023). Therefore, MIS could make employees’ work performance more transparent to themselves and others within organizations (Be´lange and Crossler 2011, Baumann et al. 2019, Friess et al. 2024).

Regarding the likely consequences of varying levels of such PT, seminal research on the general effects of transparency in organizations has great informational value. In this regard, the conceptualization of Bernstein (2017) based on a synthesis of research on transparency in management literature provides us with a valuable starting point based on the notion of a transparency paradox (Bernstein 2012). This transparency paradox reflects the situation that increasing levels of transparency in organizations is both likely to enhance and to reduce desirable outcomes like performance and learning, depending on the type and perception of transpar ency. If transparency offers actors in organizations additional information, the outcomes are positive, whereas they would be negative if transparency is per ceived as privacy violation by the respective organizational actors (Bernstein 2012, 2017). Bernstein (2012, 2017) primarily discussed this paradox within the manager-employee relationship, given that his reasoning based on a review of existing studies in the literature that also focused on this relationship. However, the rationale of Bernstein (2012, 2017) behind the paradoxi cal role of transparency can be generalized to other relationships and constellations in organizations. There are likely some variation in the specific consequences of different kinds of transparency and their exact strength fo different constellations of roles in an organization. However, the general assumptions underlying the transparency paradox are highly likely to generally apply, as indicated in the literature on consequences of pay transparency that can be interpreted as outcomes of such a transparency paradox (Bamberger and Belogolovsky 2017, Brown et al. 2022). Therefore, we leverage and extend the transparency paradox concept beyond the manager-employee context. We explore its relevance more generally and in a way that is not confined to the roles of supervisors and employees.

Antecedents and direct consequences of varying levels of transparency, which might also involve issues connected to privacy violation, have been studied in MIS research (Be´langer and Crossler 2011, Be´langer and James 2020). However, the extent to which varying levels of PT induced by MIS affect the outcomes of effective MIS use has not yet been researched. This is problematic, as this kind of transparency is a theoretically relevant variable and characteristic of MIS that is likely to substantially affect the relationship between effective MIS use and performance. On a general level, this is because the increased transparency offered by an MIS for operational control often reduces the privacy of those employees under this control (Bernstein 2017, Singh and Johnston 2019). The relevance of both transparency and privacy—and their potential consequences for employee behavior and performance—has been widely recognized in MIS research (Alder et al. 2006, Posey et al. 2011, Singh and Johnston 2019, Liang et al. 2023), and this scrutiny is likely to further increase substantially given ongoing technological developments and their implementations in organizations (Malik et al. 2022, Manroop et al. 2024). Therefore, knowing about the consequences of different levels and kinds of transparency in workplace settings for the outcomes of MIS use has clear theoretical and practical relevance.

Moreover, the consideration of PT as a moderator of MIS use and employee performance also has a specific theoretical appeal for the application of the TEU to explain the outcomes of effective MIS use. This is because the rationale of the TEU integrates aspects related to transparency in its conceptualization of effective MIS usage. This is most apparent in two of the three core dimensions of effective MIS use: transparent interaction and representational fidelity. Both dimensions imply that some kind of transparency is required for effective MIS use. For transparent interaction, it is necessary that the user of an MIS can access the representations in the system (Burton-Jones and Grange 2013). Representationa fidelity requires that the representations in the MIS accurately reflect the real world (Burton-Jones and Grange 2013, Burleson et al. 2021), for which transparent interaction is required. Hence, TEU suggests that the accessibility of these aspects represented in an MIS is a precursor of effective MIS use. However, this view does not include the aspect of what is represented in the MIS— for example, whether an MIS includes representations of employees’ behaviors and performances, the level of detail of these representations, or the availability of these representations to different kinds of users.

## 2.3. Transparency: Observer and Observed

As a starting point to derive assumptions about the consequences of transparency, Bernstein (2012, 2017) has highlighted the key distinction of whether transparency in an organization is considered from the perspective of the observer (i.e., somebody who gets access to further information due to higher levels of transparency) or if it is considered from the perspective of the observed (i.e., somebody about whom more information can be accessed by others due to higher levels of transparency). Based on these different constellations, either the aspect of additional information (from the observer’s perspective) or the aspect of potential privacy violations (from the observed person’s perspective) plays the dominant role (Bernstein 2012, 2017). Although Bernstein focused his explanation of the transparency paradox on the constellation between supervisors and subordinate, we can use this reasoning more generally in a work-related perspective, in which privacy violation refers to the extent to which others (e.g., colleagues, supervisors, or team members) might access the work-related data of an employee. This definition does not include any issues connected to employees’ private data or information (e.g., private contact details, bank accounts, private communication), which is not considered in our reasoning that focuses on work-related information. This distinction results in different outcomes in terms of psycholog ical states and behavioral responses to PT.

In the first case, where an employee assumes the role of an observer gaining more of their own work-related information, the overall impact of higher levels of trans parency on this employee’s performance is expected to be positive. Bernstein (2017) characterizes this situation as one marked by higher levels of process visibility, wherein observers can access additional information about processes, activities, and their progress. The expected positive effects of transparency are based on the rationale that more and accurate information about one’s own and others’ activities enhances the learning and control of the observing employees, thereby improving their performances (Bernstein 2017). This rationale aligns with the generally proposed view of transparency and its potential consequences. In the context of MIS use, this implies that the higher the level of transparency induced by an MIS, the stronger the positive effect of its use, as more and richer information would further enhance the abilities to learn about and control the observed for the employees who use it.

In the second case, an employee is the observed party. Here, the general consequences of higher levels of transparency, and therefore higher levels of privacy vio lations, are negative (Bernstein 2017). From the perspec tive of the observed, the underlying rationale still assumes that higher levels of transparency enhance the observer’s abilities to learn about and to control the observed. However, in this context, it pertains to the control others exert over the observed and how they can learn from the observed. In this situation, higher levels of transparency make it easier and more likely for others to potentially (mis)judge the observed person’s actions, whereas others might improve their performances by learning from the observed (Bernstein 2017, Raveendh ran and Fast 2021), without necessarily providing a mutual opportunity for the observed to also improve. Based on this reasoning, Bernstein (2017) juxtaposes benefits and risks, respectively, for the abilities to control and to learn due to increased transparency. For an observer, the benefits of enhanced transparency related to learning and controlling come to the fore. They might, for instance, benefit through learning by improving their shared understanding of the performed activities or increase their control through an “observer’s improved awareness of the nature and quality of activities being performed” (Bernstein 2017, p. 239). For an observed employee, the risks of enhanced transparency for learning and controlling are more salient (Bernstein 2017). Such risks may consist of experiencing “varied, unpredictable, or inconstant responses by observers to monitoring data, creating behavior or psychological implications for the observed including [ … ] lower sense of personal control over activities,” or observed employees’ efforts to “learn complex tasks may be misunderstood or stopped prematurely due to an observer’s awareness and therefore the risk perceived by the observed of deviation from organizational norms” (Bernstein 2017, p. 239). This situation is expected to trigger employees to counteract these risks of enhanced transparency and to maintain their privacy at work (Bernstein 2017, Zhang and Ji 2023). Such concealment would negatively affect both the observers and the observed, as the information provided through the MIS would be less accurate, and any learning and controlling based on it would be impaired.

Hence, in the first case, where an employee assumes the role of an observer accessing more information about their own work processes and progress, transparency can be characterized as self-directed transparency. Conversely, when transparency involves making an employee’s information accessible primarily to others, especially members within the team, it can be termed team-directed transparency.

## 3. Hypotheses Development

## 3.1. Consequences of Effective MIS Use for Employee Work Performance

To develop our hypothesis and build upon the TEU (Burton-Jones and Grange 2013), we suggest that the more effectively an individual uses an MIS, the more likely they are to achieve their intended goals. According to the TEU, effective MIS use comprises the dimensions transparent interaction, representational fidelity, and informed actions. Although each dimension of effective MIS use is likely to positively affect aspects of employee work performance, they are assumed to be hierarchically interrelated in the TEU (Burton-Jones and Grange 2013). In this regard, Burton-Jones and Grange (2013, p. 644) emphasize this hierarchy among the three dimensions and explain that “transparent interaction activates the informating potential of an IS, representational fidelity ensures that this potential is positive, and informed action leverages it.” This suggests a sequence of these dimensions that culminates in the key dimension of informed actions, which facilitate outcomes such as employees’ work performances (Burton-Jones and Grange 2013, Bonaretti et al. 2020, Lauterbach et al. 2020). Given the focus and intention of our conceptual model, we focus on this sequence with informed actions as the proximate outcome representing effective MIS use. This is in line with the key proposition of the TEU that “more informed actions will result in more effective outcomes” (Trieu et al. 2022, p. 654).

The first dimension of effective use, transparent interaction, involves seamlessly accessing the representations provided by an MIS (Burton-Jones and Grange 2013, Trieu et al. 2022). An MIS that enables users to interact with it more intuitively also benefits representational fidelity, which refers to the degree to which the MIS provides accurate representations of the task or organizational context (Burton-Jones and Grange 2013). Fo instance, if users need to retrieve information from a system but encounter obstacles due to a confusing user interface, the completeness and accuracy of the information presented are compromised, thus diminishing representational fidelity. Thus, TEU predicts transparent interac tion to enable representational fidelity through improved access to data (Burton-Jones and Grange 2013).

An MIS with high representational fidelity, in turn, ensures employees have reliable, accurate information on which to base their actions and decisions (Burton-Jones and Grange 2013, Burleson et al. 2021). For example, if customer data or other information in the system is not up to date, the facilitated access to this data are only of limited help. However, the TEU assumes that, in addition to being able to retrieve certain information from an MIS, MIS users need to learn how to use the information from the system (Burton-Jones and Grange 2013, Lauterbach et al. 2020, Trieu et al. 2022). This implies that with higher levels of representational fidelity, employees should not only have access to high quality information but also better leverage the system to guide their actions and decisions (Burton-Jones and Grange 2013, Bonaretti et al. 2020). For example, if sales agents engage with appropriate data in the MIS, this could help them find ways to improve customer priori tization. However, if sales agents fail to take such measures or make decisions based on poor data, they may end up investing more time in customers with lower business potential. Thus, representational fidelity enables better-informed actions, that is, take appropriate actions based on accurate representations from the MIS (Burton-Jones and Grange 2013).

Such better-informed actions constitute the core dimension of effective MIS use (Burton-Jones and Grange 2013). Generally, the availability of useful and relevant information will increase the likelihood that employees accomplish their objectives compared with situations in which employees cannot employ such information (Torres and Sidorova 2019). Specifically, informed actions lead to more precise planning, targeted execution, more appropriate responses to situations, and greater competence in action based on the information provided by the MIS (Burton-Jones and Grange 2013, Bonaretti et al. 2020). Moreover, betterinformed actions involve improved identification and leverage of opportunities (Burton-Jones and Grange 2013). For instance, with higher-quality information, a sales manager can effectively and efficiently organize customer visits, anticipate customer needs, and strategi cally cultivate better business opportunities. In this vein, informed actions are also likely to reduce errors in employees’ work and thereby foster employee performance (Burton-Jones and Grange 2013, Trieu et al. 2022). Less errors and better information also increase the efficiency of task execution if less correction and rework is needed. Generally, users can complete tasks more quickly and accurately when they have the right information at their fingertips (Burton-Jones and Grange 2013). Furthermore, informed actions facilitate decision-making when users take informed actions based on the system’s good data (Burton-Jones and Grange 2013, Trieu et al. 2022). For example, a sales manager engaging with relevant and easily accessible information in a customer relationship management application can more accurately assess potential developments of markets and customers. This will allow the sales manager to make better strategic decisions to boost revenues. Ultimately, the ability to take informed actions ensures that users can meet their objectives effectively and efficiently. Consequently, we expect informed actions enabled by effective MIS use to positively influence overall work performance (Burton-Jones and Grange 2013, Bala and Venkatesh 2016, Torres and Sidorova 2019, Bonaretti et al. 2020, Lauterbach et al. 2020, Trieu et al. 2022).

Hypothesis 1. Effective MIS use, in the form of informed actions, is positively related to employees’ work performance.

## 3.2. Moderating Role of Performance Transparency

To theorize about the consequences of PT, as informed by the perspective of Bernstein (2012, 2017) of a transparency paradox, we consider situations in which MISs induce higher levels of PT in two specific ways: (1) a focal employee’s own performance (i.e., self-directed PT) and (2) mutual PT among employees in the same team (i.e., team-directed PT).

3.2.1. Role of the Observer: Inducing Transparency in an Employee’s Own Performance. In the first case, we consider the varying level of PT that an MIS offers for an employee’s own performance. This means that employees are provided with additional information about their own activities, such as through statistics, progress indicators, or predictions of goal achievement based on the current situation. Here, the benefits of the observer’s role are confined solely to employees observing their own performances. Therefore, we expect the enhanced transparency of this situation to primarily benefit employee learning and controlling, as no (additional) privacy violation occurs.

Rather, the additional information available on an employee’s own performance would help the employee more accurately estimate their own performance and progress, which would enable them to better benefit from the MIS, thereby amplifying the positive relationship between effective MIS use and employee perfor mance. Through this transparency, they may learn how to better understand whether and in which regard they need to adapt their activities and perhaps their level of effort (Kinicki et al. 2004, Palmer et al. 2015). In this way, because of the more accurate and richer information accessible through the MIS and the better decision follow ing from this, employees will benefit more from it the more they use it. For example, if the MIS induces a higher level of transparency regarding an employee’s own per formance by offering customer feedback, this will likely enhance the employee’s understanding of customers and their needs (Buell et al. 2017). This offers many opportunities for learning how to improve performance the more the employee uses the system. For example, this enhanced understanding of the customer will help a sales manager even better recognize opportunities in the business relationship with a customer or serve to avoid errors when negotiating with the customer. Similarly, an employee’s more accurate awareness of their own activities and progress makes it easier to coordinate and prioritize activities in ways that agree with their own overarching objectives (DeShon et al. 2004). This enhanced level of control also allows for reflecting on and adapting one’s activities (Buell et al. 2017) and is likely to reinforce the benefits of effective MIS use and further increasing efficiency. In other words, when only the observed party can access the data from an MIS, then MIS use likely has a more stimulating and motivating effect on the employee rather than an intimidating one that might reduce the employee’s perceived autonomy. Thus, we propose the following.

Hypothesis 2. Increasing self-directed PT enhances the positive effects of effective MIS use on employees’ work performances.

3.2.2. Role of the Observed: Inducing Transparency of an Employee’s Performance to Teammates. The second scenario concerns varying levels of PT for others, with employees being observed by team members. Building on the transparency paradox (Bernstein 2017), we expect this use of MIS to negatively affect employee performance. Assuming that being observed raises employees’ awareness of the risks of greater transparency, which might make them fear the invasion of their privacy (Bernstein 2017, Ravid et al. 2023), employees will likely feel the need to protect their privacy in some way. Thus, dysfunctional hiding behaviors will emerge, which might take different forms (Bernstein 2017): For example, employees might not fully or not properly disclose information about their activities in the MIS. Such dysfunctional behaviors would diminish the positive effects of MIS use on work performance because employees would get less useful or even misleading information from the MIS. In such cases, simply increasing the use of the MIS would not improve their performances. Such dysfunctional behaviors might also overload the system if someone were to enter overly detailed or excessive amounts of information into the system to make it harder or impossible for others to easily track their actual activities. Such behavior would undermine the otherwise positive direct relationship between effective MIS use and employee performance. In any case, such hiding behaviors would result in less useful and accurate information in the MIS (Bernstein 2012, Anteby and Chan 2018). Hence, even though enhanced PT among teammates might generally benefit the observers, these advantages are less likely to materialize for an employee when their own work performance is observed by their team members. Thus, in the case when others than the observed themselves can access the data from an MIS, the use of MIS is likely to have an intimidating and inhibiting effect, rather than a motivating and inspiring one.

Moreover, an MIS providing extensive information about the performance of other employees may generally reduce the benefit of actions informed by an MIS for employee performance. First, the information about other employees’ performance and performance-relevant activities may often not be directly relevant for oneself. For example, in a sales team, other team members might manage customers whose needs and characteristics substantially differ from those handled by the focal sales agent. Furthermore, other team members might have to focus on different goals or tasks in their specific roles. In such instances, the information provided by the system can be less relevant or accurate for the focal sales agent and therefore of less help to enhance pivotal aspects like decision making, opportunity recognition, or efficiency. Second, detailed information from and about other team members or coworkers might distract employees from the self-directed information in the MIS that would offer them more opportunities for learning how they need to adapt to their activities and perhaps their levels of effort. By diverting attention, employees may miss opportunities to utilize the rich and accurate self-directed information within the MIS, thereby foregoing potential benefits. Therefore, it becomes less likely that employees can benefit from this information in terms of better decision making in the way proposed by TEU (Burton-Jones and Grange 2013, Bonaretti et al. 2020, Trieu et al. 2022). In sum, we propose the following.

Hypothesis 3. Increasing team-directed PT weakens the positive effects of effective MIS use on employees’ work performances.

## 3.3. Role of Perceived Surveillance

Beyond the moderating effect we anticipate from teamdirected PT on the relationship between effective MIS use and team performance, team-directed PT likely holds additional implications for system users. Specifi cally, when employees know their performance can be more accurately tracked by fellow team members, they often perceive this visibility as a form of monitoring (Bernstein 2017). We propose that this sense of surveillance could serve as a pathway through which certain forms of PT influence the outcomes of effective MIS use. This is particularly relevant for kinds of PT that involve transparency to others, such as the transparency experienced by team members in our study.

This assumption is in line with the conceptual model of the TEU, which includes potential disturbances that are likely to directly affect outcomes (Burton-Jones and Grange 2013). We expect perceived surveillance to represent one such disturbance that likely directly negatively affects performance outcomes. As explained above, based on the transparency paradox (Bernstein 2012, 2017), employees tend to perceive the surveillance by others as a violation of their privacy, which negatively affects the observed employee. To counter this privacy violation, employees will likely attempt to maintain their privacy (Bernstein 2017). In this regard, Bernstein (2017, p. 237) concludes that “increased awareness of being observed triggers an increased desire for privacy.” What these hiding behaviors—such as playing dumb, reporting inaccurate data, or withdrawing from some activities (Connelly et al. 2012, Bernstein 2017, Ghasemaghaei and Turel 2021)—have in common is that they diminish or delay the exchange of information or cause the information to be inaccurate. Moreover, perceived surveillance has been reported to cause reactance and other negative attitudes among employees (Yost et al. 2019, Ravid et al. 2023), which further compromise collaboration and the exchange of information. This increasingly dysfunctional processing of information is likely to hamper employees’ performances. Thus, team-directed PT would enhance the surveillance perceived by employees, which, in turn, would hamper their performances. We therefore posit the following.

Hypothesis 4. There is a positive relationship between team-directed PT and perceived surveillance.

Hypothesis 5. There is a negative relationship between perceived surveillance and employees’ work performances.

## 4. Method

## 4.1. Study Design and Data Collection

To test the hypotheses proposed in our conceptual model depicted in Figure 1, we conducted an experience sampling study in which participants provided daily reports on their behaviors or psychological states over a predefined period, and we combined these daily reports with the participants’ responses to a baseline survey (Mehl and Conner 2012). In contrast to cross-sectional research methods, experience sampling helps researchers gather data on changes in the psychological states of individual participants rather than on general patterns of behaviors or feelings, which may not apply to each participant (Hektner et al. 2007, Mehl and Conner 2012). We selected the experience sampling methodology (ESM) for two reasons. First, research shows that work performance fluctuates substantially over time (Hofmann et al. 1993, Reis et al. 2000). However, previous studies on MIS use and its consequences have investigated its permanent effects and have not considered daily variations in effects on work performance. By employing ESM, we extend the research on MIS use and can account for the within-person variability in performance caused by situational and contextual factors that change over time depending on the daily MIS use. Second, employing ESM reduces the memory bias of participants because the responses reflect immediate reactions to changes in the working environment and are therefore more accurate than one-time measures (Beal and Weiss 2003). In traditional surveys, participants are likely to remember recent events, but they have more difficulty remembering events that are more distant (Vomberg and Klarmann 2021).

To avoid industry-specific biases and to enhance the generalizability of our findings, we recruited participants from 15 different industries, including professional services, retailing, and financial industries. We collected data from sales employees since the field has substantially increased its use of digital technologies in recent years (Ahearne et al. 2008, Tarafdar et al. 2014) and is therefore very suitable for investigations of the determinants of effective MIS use. Specifically, we focused on the most common MIS in this sales context, that is, customer relationship management (CRM) applications. Employees rely on CRM systems to increase sales, improve target marketing activities, and effectively retain, service, and manage customers (Greenberg 2010). These systems integrate functionalities such as contract management, reporting and decision-making tools, and workflow automation (Kostojohn et al. 2011, Zablah et al. 2012). They also enable the enhanced monitoring and control of employees (Li and Mao 2012). Despite the apparent utility of these CRM systems, their implementations frequently fal short of expectations and cause more problems than benefits (Chen et al. 2020).

We recruited our sample via the Prolific platform which is widely used in research. To be included in the final sample, participants had to be full-time employees working in companies with at least 50 employees, employed in a sales role and team context, and regularly using a CRM system. To check whether employees ful filled these criteria, all participants had to complete a questionnaire confirming these background details. Within our sample of respondents who completed the screener questionnaire (N � 552), 263 met the criteria. We collected data in two phases. In the first phase, participants completed a baseline survey that contained measures of variables that are relatively stable over time. In the second phase, which occurred one week after the baseline survey, we employed ESM to capture variables that might vary over time (Beal and Weiss 2003). For 10 consecutive workdays, participants received an email at 6 p.m. that invited them to fill out a short online questionnaire. This questionnaire contained prompts regarding daily measures of employees MIS use, as well as their work performances and other variables to capture daily variations in the data (Beal and Weiss 2003). To match answers to the baseline survey to the daily questionnaires, and to ensure the anonymity of the participants, we asked the participants to enter a personal code at the beginning of each survey. Our final sample comprised 141 sales employees (response rate � 54%), who generated 1,186 completed daily surveys. This corresponded to an average completion of 8.4 daily questionnaires per employee. Among the survey participants, 40.85% were female, the average age was 36.90 years, and the average company experience was 6.75 years. All the participants in our sample had frequent and direct customer contact and indicated an average technology usage of 4.15 (1, very low; 7, very high). For more sample details, please see Online Appendix 1.

## 4.2. Measures, Reliability, and Validity Checks

Online Appendix 2 displays all measures of our analysis. We primarily based our measures on wellestablished scales from existing literature and adapted them to our study context. Unless otherwise stated, we measured constructs with at least three items on sevenpoint Likert scales ranging from 1 (“totally disagree”) to 7 (“totally agree”). Because employees’ MIS use and their work performance vary significantly over time, we assessed them on the daily within-person level. In contrast, we considered PT as being relatively stable over time and measured them in the baseline survey on the between-person level.

4.2.1. MIS Use. We utilized the scale of Trieu et al. (2022) to measure effective MIS use, which served as our primary independent variable. We included the different dimensions of effective MIS use—that is, transparent interaction (i.e., the extent to which the user of an MIS can access its representations seamlessly), representational fidelity (i.e., the extent to which the user of an MIS obtains the required output), and informed decisions (i.e., the extent to which the user of an MIS can leverage the obtained information in their job)—in our model. We adapted the original items to the daily level. Moreover, we shortened the scales to three items per first-order construct to ensure brevity and minimize respondent attrition. This approach is feasible due to the domain-sampling approach of measurement theory, which suggests that shortening scales is possible when prior research has adequately specified the domain of interest, that is, the full set of items that could be included to assess the construct (Little et al. 1999, Vomberg and Klarmann 2021).

4.2.2. Performance Transparency. Because no widely accepted scale exists for our main moderating variable PT, we introduced a novel measure along with our conceptual approach (Bernstein 2017). Specifically, we included two dimensions: self-directed PT (i.e., the extent to which users can evaluate their own performances via the MIS) and team-directed PT (i.e., the extent to which team members can evaluate other users performances via the MIS). As we had to develop the measure for this study, we tested its reliability and validity in a prestudy (see Online Appendix 3). PT and its two dimensions were considered a stable feature of the MIS and thus measured at the between-person level.

4.2.3. Employee Performance Measurement. We measured employee performance according to the extent to which they achieved their sales quotas, which is a key outcome for sales employees in this context, as established in past research (Ahearne et al. 2008). Moreover, based on the theory of effective MIS use, we include decision-making effectiveness and efficiency in our model estimations as additional outcomes (Trieu et al. 2022).

4.2.4. Control Variables. We added a set of control variables to control for the potential effects of omitted variables. At the daily level, we controlled for perceived task complexity and the number of hours worked per day. At the baseline level, we controlled for factors related to the MIS (i.e., MIS usefulness and the voluntariness of MIS use), factors related to the users’ job perceptions (e.g., job autonomy), and contextual factors (i.e., a failure-oriented organizational culture). We derived these categories from the theory on technology acceptance (Venkatesh et al. 2003), which proposes that these aspects may influence employees’ MIS use. Moreover, we controlled for motivational constructs (i.e., openness to experience, learning orientation, performance orientation, and need to belong) that may drive performance (Alavi et al. 2022). We also included demographic variables such as the participants’ gender, and company tenure, because prior research has suggested that these variables may affect employees’ MIS use and work performances (Sykes 2020, Li et al. 2021).

## 4.3. Measurement Validity

4.3.1. Measurement Diagnostics. Using Stata 17, we conducted one confirmatory factor analysis for the daily survey and another one for the baseline study that contained all reflectively measured constructs to assess thei reliability and validity. Our sample size aligns with the recommendations of Anderson and Gerbing (1984, p. 171) and prior research using similar approaches to ours (Benlian 2022). In addition, the relatively many indicators that we used for each factor (Anderson and Gerbing 1984) and their measurement quality (Gagne ´ and Hancock 2006) further support the adequacy of our sample size for the following analyses. For our newly introduced transparency scales, we show (see Online Appendix 3) that our diagnostics are closely replicated in a larger sample (N � 336).

Based on commonly recommended fit indices (Hu and Bentler 1999), we found acceptable model fit for the two analyses (daily: $\chi ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { d f } = 2 .$ .84; CFI � 0.99; RMSEA � 0.04; SRMR � 0.02; Baseline: $\chi ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { d f } = 1 . 6 3 ; \mathrm { C F I } = 0 . 9 1 ;$ RMSEA � 0.07; SRMR � 0.07). Overall, the analysis had satisfactory results: composite reliability, average variance extracted, and Cronbach’s alpha exceeded the recommended threshold values for all constructs (0.70, 0.70, 0.50), and all but one factor loading surpassed a value of 0.60. One item for the control variable “voluntariness of tech use” had a factor loading of 0.55. We retained this item because one item’s deviation from the 0.60 threshold value is still acceptable, and we favored conceptual concerns over maximizing internal consistency when selecting our indicators (Little et al. 1999, Bagozzi and Yi 2012). Moreover, we followed Baumgartner and Weijters (2022) for establishing discriminant validity. First, we checked if the factor correlations are different from one. We constructed a confidence interval around each factor and checked whether the confidence interval includes one (in which case the factors lack discriminant validity because a perfect correlation is a plausible value). For none of the variables in our sample, the confidence interval includes the value one. Second, all constructs exhibited discriminant validity based on the criteria of Fornell and Larcker (1981) (Table 2). Thus, all constructs have more in common with their own measures than with the others measures.

4.3.2. Self-Reported Performance Measure. Our analysis relied on self-reported performance measures, so respondents could have provided inflated answers. However, in our study, such threats are low. First, sales quota achievement is a quasi-objective measure, and research shows that respondents tend to accurately evaluate quasi-objective measures (Rindfleisch et al. 2008, Homburg et al. 2012). Second, systematic over- or under-evaluations do not impact the results of dependency structures (such as correlations or regression coefficients; please refer to Vomberg and Klarmann (2021) for an overview).

4.3.3. Common Method Variance. Because the same sales employee reported both independent and dependent variables, common method variance (CMV) could affect the validity of our results. However, our study design mitigates concerns regarding CMV (please refer to Vomberg and Klarmann (2022) for an overview of procedural measures to mitigate CMV). First, we temporarily separated the measurement of the dependent variable (sales quota achieved) and the key independent variables (i.e., self- and team-directed performance transparency) by one week. Research suggests that temporal separation can help reduce CMV (Johnson et al. 2011). Second, our study focuses on interaction effects (Hypotheses 2 and 3); analytical and simulation studies indicate that CMV cannot artificially create interaction effects but can only attenuate them (Siemsen et al. 2010). Third, we removed common scale properties by measuring most of our independent variables on sevenpoint Likert scales, whereas we gauged sales quota achieved in percentages. Finally, assessing sales quota achievement involves a relatively low level of abstraction because it can be empirically verified, further mitigating CMV (Podsakoff et al. 2024).

Additionally, we applied the marker variable test of Lindell and Whitney (2001), which assesses CMV by using a variable that is theoretically unrelated to the constructs in the model. We then use this marker variable to construct an adjusted correlation matrix and test the significance of the new correlations. Specifically, we used sales employees’ number of children as the marker variable. This marker variable had a correlation of 0.01 with the sales quota achieved. We based the correlations on the baseline and the first day of the daily survey, because these two surveys were conducted with the shortest time gap between them. All previously significant correlations remained significant at the 5% level. Therefore, CMV is unlikely to influence our findings. Furthermore, Gaussian copula terms (discussed in the section on Robustness Checks and Online Appendix 6- 2) provide additional safeguards against CMV because CMV represents a form of endogeneity (Sande and Ghosh 2018).

## 4.4. Analytical Approach

Our data set comprised 1,186 daily observations nested in 141 sales employees.<sup>1</sup> We accounted for repeated measurement—the nesting that is inherent in our ESM—by clustering the standard errors on the level of the sales employee. Additionally, we accounted for time trends through day-fixed effects. Specifically, we estimated a structural equation model using maximum likelihood estimation with cluster-robust standard errors using Stata 17. Prior to the analysis, we meancentered the independent and moderating variables to facilitate the interpretation of interactive effects (Spiller et al. 2013).

Some bivariate correlations raise the question of whether multicollinearity could affect our results. Such concerns are alleviated firstly by the fact that we can demonstrate discriminant validity (e.g., via the Fornell-Larcker criterion in Table 2); if the constructs are clearly measured differently, concerns about multicollinearity are low (Grewal et al. 2004). Second, we calculated the variance inflation factors for all variables. All variance inflation factors are below the recommended threshold of five, further dispelling concerns about possible multicollinearity. Simulation studies also show that multicol linearity concerns must be evaluated in conjunction with other study factors (Mason and Perreault 1991, Grewal et al. 2004). The results of our construct measurement show that all constructs have a high degree of reliability. In such a situation, even a high degree of multicollinearity could be tolerated (Grewal et al. 2004). Furthermore, the observed t-values and our sample size attenuate multicollinearity concerns (Mason and Perreault 1991). ## 5. Results

## 5.1. Hypothesis Testing

Table 3 presents the results of the estimation of the structural equation model. Hypothesis 1 predicted positive effect of MIS use on salespeople’s daily work performance. In line with Hypothesis 1, we find that the MIS transparent interaction increases MIS representational fidelity $( \gamma _ { 1 1 } = 0 . 8 1 4 ,$ , standard error $( \mathrm { S E } ) \stackrel { - } { = } 0 . 0 1 6 , p <$ 0.01), which in turn increases informed actions $( \beta _ { 2 1 } =$ 0.415, $\mathrm { S E } \ = \ 0 . 0 5 8 , \ p \ < \ 0 . 0 1 )$ . The effect of informed actions on daily work performance is positive and significant for the dependent variables daily sales quota achieved and decision effectiveness (daily quota achieved: $\beta _ { 7 2 } = 0 . 1 6 2 , \mathrm { S E } = 0 . 0 6 4 , p < 0 . 0 5 ;$ decision effectiveness: $\beta _ { 6 2 } = 0 . 1 7 7 , \mathrm { S E } = 0 . 0 5 2 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . However, the effect of MIS use on decision efficiency was not significant. We elaborate on this finding in Section 6. In Hypothesis 2, we predicted that self-directed PT amplified the effect of MIS use on daily work performance such that the positive effect of MIS use grows with increasing levels of such transparency. The results confirm Hypothesis 2, because we find a positive and significant interactive effect between MIS use and selfdirected PT for daily quota achieved and decision effectiveness and marginally for decision efficiency (daily quota achieved: $\bar { \beta _ { 7 2 \times 2 } } = 0 . 3 2 6 , \mathrm { S E } = 0 . 1 2 1 , \bar { p } < 0 . 0 1 ,$ decision effectiveness: $\beta _ { 6 2 \times 2 } = 0 . 1 9 7 , \mathrm { S E } = 0 . 0 8 7 , p <$ 0.05; decision efficiency: $\beta _ { 5 2 \times 2 } = 0 . 1 4 3 , \mathrm { S E } = 0 . 0 8 3 , p <$ 0.10). In Hypothesis 3, we argued that team-directed PT attenuates the positive effect of MIS use on work performance. The results of our model estimation provide support for this hypothesis because the interactive effect between MIS use and team-directed PT is significant and negative for daily quota achieved and decision effectiveness and marginal for decision efficiency (quota achieved: $\beta _ { 7 2 \times 3 } = - 0 . 2 8 3 , \mathrm { S E } = 0 . 0 9 6 , p < 0 . 0 1 ,$ ; decision effectiveness: $\beta _ { 6 2 \times 3 } = - 0 . 2 3 6 , \mathrm { S E } = 0 . 0 7 5 , p < 0 . 0 1 ;$ ; decision efficiency: $\beta _ { 5 2 \times 3 } = - 0 . 1 3 9 , \mathrm { S E } = 0 . 0 7 7 , p < 0 . 1 0 )$ Importantly, as the interaction diagrams in Online Appendix 4 show the positive effect of MIS use and work performance grows with increasing self-directed PT and is attenuated with team-directed PT.

In Hypothesis 4, we suggested that team-directed PT increases perceived surveillance, which was supported $( \gamma _ { 3 3 } = 0 . 3 3 9 , \mathrm { S E } = 0 . 0 8 5 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ ). However, we do not find significant negative effects of perceived surveillance on either quota achieved, decision effectiveness, or efficiency, which leads us to reject Hypothesis 5.

## 5.2. Robustness Checks

We conducted a series of robustness checks to ensure the rigor of our empirical results. We have (1) accounted for temporal issues (Online Appendix 5), (2) addressed potential endogeneity (Online Appendix 6), (3) replicated results with alternative measurements for effec tive MIS use and PT (Online Appendix 7), and (4) validated our measures for self-directed and teamdirected PT with a holdout sample (Online Appendix 3). For detailed results of these robustness checks, see the online appendices.

## 6. Discussion

## 6.1. Implications for Theory and Research

The findings of this study have implications for research and theories regarding the consequences of MIS use. First, our findings further add to the emerging research that examines not only the performance effects of MIS use but also effective MIS use (Trieu et al. 2022). Ou research applies a comprehensive conceptualization of effective MIS use that encompasses transparent interaction, representational fidelity, and informed action (Burton-Jones and Grange 2013). Ours is one of the first studies to utilize the recently developed measure of effective MIS use developed by Trieu et al. (2022), and our findings emphasize that a greater extent of daily effective MIS use generally improves daily performance, which reinforces the overarching performanceenhancing assumptions of the TEU. Testing various performance outcomes fostered a nuanced understanding of effective IS use. Our results reveal differential effects on effectiveness versus efficiency outcomes. Although effective IS use influenced both quota achievement and decision-making effectiveness, it did not show a significant relationship with decision-making efficiency. This finding, although initially surprising, aligns with prior research indicating that transparent interaction is a better predictor of efficiency, whereas informed actions more accurately predict effectiveness (Trieu et al. 2022, Namvar et al. 2023). We also contributed to refining the TEU by demonstrating that PT affects the relationship of MIS use and employee performance. By delineating this aspect, we effectively developed the understanding of the precise contexts in which MIS enhances or potentially dampens employee performance. Despite inconsistent findings in prior studies, our nuanced perspective of the TEU supports the notion that MIS use, although largely beneficial, can be complex.

Second, the current study extends this field of research by revealing that PT significantly affects effective MIS use. The interplay of MIS use and PT and its subsequent impact on employee performance is a pivotal junction that further helps to disentangle the complex nature of transparency that has been demonstrated by inconsistent (Claggett and Karahanna 2018, Singh and Johnston 2019) or insignificant findings (Cram et al. 2022). By considering the multifaceted nature of transparency—self-directed and teamdirected PT—we show that the influence of transparency on IS use and performance is not uniform. Instead, transparency’s effects depend on the dimension and perspective at play. The differentiation in Bernstein (2012, 2017) between the observer and the observed illuminates the complexities of transparency within organizations. This dichotomy accentuates the contrasting emotional and behavioral implications for individuals based on their roles in the transparency spectrum. For the observer, heightened transparency might enable empowerment and enhanced decision-making capabilities informed by the richness of the data (Bernstein 2017). Conversely, for the observed, this transparency can translate into a perceived invasion of privacy that potentially causes defensiveness or even counterproductive behaviors aimed at preserving their autonomy (Bernstein 2012). This latter view aligns with the notion that transparency can foster surveillance, which potentially signals to employees that they are not trusted unless observed (Ball 2010, Bernstein 2017). Such a dynamic can influence an employee’s behavior as they weigh the implications of being observed.

Finally, beyond the insights into the nuances of IPT, our study heeds the academic appeal for more precise instruments in the domain of MIS research. There is a need for robust and nuanced scales to dissect and analyze MIS use and its effects, especially given the evolving nature of MIS in organizational realities (Trieu et al. 2022). Thus, our study relied on the transparency paradox (Bernstein 2012) to develop a new measure of capturing PT. This measure is a new tool for researchers that allows a systematic investigation of PT and supports further in-depth explorations within and beyond the realm of MIS in organizational studies.

## 6.2. Practical Implications

Our study has important, actionable implications for managers and companies. Despite considerable investments in MIS, including CRM systems, the anticipated performance outcomes of MIS use are often unrealized (Chen et al. 2021). Therefore, it is important for managers to understand when MIS use results in the desired benefits and when it fails. Our research underscores that the outcome of MIS deployment hinges significantly on PT and that managers should recognize that not all transparency is created equal. Transparency’s influence on whether employees can benefit from an MIS varies based on the employee’s role on whether they are the observer or the observed. For instance, managers could openly discuss instances when sharing performancerelated information across team members might be per ceived as an intrusion of privacy. Managers might also actively manage the information accessible through the MIS. Additionally, fostering open communication can help address any concerns or misconceptions to ensure that self-directed transparency is a tool for empowerment rather than a source of anxiety. Managers could also establish zones of privacy within their organizations to effectively navigate the paradox of transparency. Zones of privacy are specific areas or contexts within the workplace where employees are shielded from constant monitoring (Bernstein 2012). When employees know they are under constant surveillance, they may withhold information, report inaccurate data, or avoid certain tasks to protect their privacy. These behaviors can significantly detract from performance and hinder genuine transparency. Implementing zones of privacy can mitigate the negative effects of such visi bility (Bernstein 2012, 2017). In so doing, managers could determine which activities and tasks could benefit from reduced monitoring and set clear boundaries fo these zones. Furthermore, managers should specifically communicate the purpose of these zones to emphasize that they should foster collaboration, experimentation, and innovation.

Our study focuses on the employment of CRM systems in sales contexts. CRM tools are designed to provide comprehensive data on customer interactions, sales pipelines, and overall sales performances (Greenberg 2010). The richness of the data available to salespeople allows them to fulfill their job requirements efficiently and effectively because they can retrieve reliable and timely information that fosters their understandings of and responses to customer needs (Au et al. 2008), but the transparency offered by CRM systems can have ambiguous consequences. Although this transparency can empower salespeople with actionable insights and help them understand their sales trajectories, customer preferences, and areas of improvement, the constant monitoring could be perceived as intrusive and could affect a salesperson’s morale and, subsequently, their performance. Given our research, sales managers should strategically employ and communicate the best uses of CRM systems. Managers should emphasize that the system is primarily a tool for selfimprovement, rather than a mere control mechanism, and assist employees in recognizing its beneficial aspects. Furthermore, fostering a team environment that promotes collaboration over competition can encourage teams to utilize the information from the MIS for guidance and mutual support rather than succumb to counterproductive hiding behaviors.

## 6.3. Limitations and Directions for Future Research

This study has some limitations, which may provide opportunities for future research. First, from a methodological perspective, the research design was based on the self-reported performance measures of sales employees. However, these ratings may be affected by social desirability. Although we controlled for the potential autocorrelation of measures of MIS use and worker performance to mitigate this possibility, future studies could validate our results using performance ratings from sales managers or by relying on objective performance data, as has been done in other studies (Kra¨mer et al. 2022).

Second, we focused on a contingency (i.e., PT) that reflects the features of MIS and how they impact the relationship between employees’ MIS use and their individual performances. Beyond such MIS features, there are further potential contingencies worth studying, such as the characteristics of the task environment or the task itself (Morgeson and Humphrey 2006) that have not received sufficient attention in the literature beyond pioneering studies (Rai and Hornyak 2013, Kuegler et al. 2015, Zhang 2017). Extending this research would also allow researchers to study the interplay between different types of contingencies. For example, an MIS that induces higher levels of PT might be particularly helpful or detrimental for effective MIS use depending on the characteristics of the work environment (e.g., work climate) or the task (e.g., task complexity).

Third, even though ESM involves several points of measurement over time, the analysis is still correlational in nature. We therefore cannot exclude the possibility of reverse causality. In such cases, those individuals who consider themselves high performers may use technologies even more intensely (Buehrer et al. 2005). However, reverse causality is less pertinent to our conceptual model and study design—because the participants rated their daily MIS use and daily work performances, it is unlikely that they derived their MIS use from their daily work performances. Reverse causality is more likely to affect cross-sectional studies with broader views of MIS use and worker performance.

Fourth, because perceived surveillance did not directly affect performance outcomes, future research could investigate the conditions under which surveil lance is perceived as supportive and a form of care vs. when it is viewed as a lever for control (Sewell and Barker 2006). Such research could explore various contingencies such as organizational culture, the nature of the work, or the communication strategies used to explain the purpose of the surveillance. For instance, future research could investigate the role of economic incentives and resources in shaping employee perceptions of surveillance and examine how various forms of organizational capital—such as cultural, social, and economic capital (Hsieh et al. 2011)—moderate the effects of surveillance on performance. Researchers could examine whether a collaborative organizational culture or strong interpersonal networks mitigate the negative impacts of perceived surveillance. Understanding these factors could help clarify the nuanced relationship between surveillance and performance and offer insights into how organizations can design surveillance that enhances performance

Finally, we investigated the effects of MIS use on work performance at the level of individual sales employees. However, many tasks in organizations are undertaken by teams (Mathieu et al. 2019), and MIS use is likely to change the nature of teamwork (Zhang et al. 2013, Bayerl et al. 2016). Teams frequently work on complex and nonroutine tasks, and PT may reduce freeriding in teams, but it may also result in potentially dysfunctional competitive behaviors among team members. Future studies could explore how performances differ between teams that effectively and intensively use MIS and those that forego using digital technologies, as well as which contingencies affect such performances. In sum, we hope that our find ings encourage future studies to deepen the understanding of the effective use of MIS.
