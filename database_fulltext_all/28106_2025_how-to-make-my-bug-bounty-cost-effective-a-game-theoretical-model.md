# How to Make My Bug Bounty Cost-Effective? A Game-Theoretical Model

Abstract. To mitigate the threats from malicious exploitation of vulnerabilities, an increasing number of organizations across different industries have started incorporating bug bounty programs (BBPs) in their vulnerability management cycles. Whereas a BBP attracts external security researchers to facilitate the discovery of vulnerabilities in organizations information technology systems, it also increases the risks after the vulnerabilities are discovered. To deal with the trade-offs, organizations need to understand how to design an optimal bounty and evaluate the total cost of a BBP depending on several key factors. The industry is motivated to understand how the bounty and total costs are impacted by (i) the characteristics of the organization (e.g., security posture and patching complexity), (ii) security researchers (e.g., the heterogeneity among security researchers and their number), and (iii) other factors such as the legal framework surrounding the BBP. However, because there is a lack of formal analyses regarding these issues, we use game-theoretical models to shed light on relevant questions and provide several useful results and managerial insights. First, although an organization’s patching complexity and the bounty act as substitutes, the relationship between security posture and the bounty is not necessarily substitutive or complementary. Furthermore, having a larger number of or more capable security researchers does not necessarily imply an increased bounty or lower total costs. Moreover, whereas the prevalent business belief is that an increased level of legal protection offered to the security researchers increases the cost of the BBP, we find that neither the cost of the BBP nor the offered bounty necessarily increases or decreases. This nuanced finding depends on different types of costs incurred because of the inherent vulnerability itself and costs related to possible leaks out of the BBP. Our study provides insights to security professionals, organizations, and policymakers in designing cost-effective BBPs.

## 1. Introduction

Worldwide spending on information security has significantly increased in recent years and is expected to surpass \$198 billion by 2025 (Muncaster 2022). Faced with growing information technology (IT) security risks and increasingly stringent data-protection regulations, organizations across different sectors are seeking cost-effective cybersecurity solutions to improve their security performance strategically (Kumar and Mallipeddi 2022). One emerging solution involves outsourcing vulnerability identification, which is an essential part of cybersecurity management. This approach goes beyond mere experimental interests, and it is endorsed by guidelines from reputable institutions. For instance, the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) updated its cybersecurity framework and advocated organizations to consider establishing processes to receive and deal with vulnerabilities submit ted from external sources (Ellis 2021). Meanwhile, many organizations are tapping into the security researcher community through bug bounty programs (BBPs) (Statista 2021). A 2023 report indicates that the global bug bounty market is valued at \$1,130 million (The Express Wire 2023), and the market is expected to reach \$2,363 million by 2027 (Business Research Insights 2023).

The benefits of BBPs are evident. Similar to general crowdsourcing programs, a BBP attracts attention and collects innovative outputs from a group of external participants (Sridhar and Ng 2021). In a BBP, security researchers discover and report previously unknown security vulnerabilities in an organization’s IT infrastructure o digital products. Upon receiving these reports, organizations can patch the vulnerabilities to mitigate potential damage from cyberattacks.

Many organizations across different sectors have been recognizing the values of BBPs. Firms with sufficient IT resources, such as Facebook, Google, and Apple, among other prominent IT firms, have their own BBPs.<sup>1</sup> Public organizations regarded as usually lagging in technology development also organize BBPs, such as several federal agencies (Harper 2023). Furthermore, in 2020, the COVID-19 pandemic accelerated bug bounty adoptions among organizations and expanded the size of participating security researchers (Bugcrowd 2020). Although an increasing number of organizations and security researchers have become involved in BBPs, many practitioners doubt whether a BBP is always beneficial to organizations and even argue that BBPs may backfire and expose organizations to higher risks and losses (Arrow and Newman 2018). In effect, many BBPs collapse after about a year (Chickowski 2022), which may be attributed to their flawed design because many BBPs are designed without considering their cost-effectiveness (Schulz 2023).

Designing a cost-effective BBP requires setting the appropriate bounty amount and understanding how key factors influence its cost-effectiveness. Despite the prevalence of BBPs in practice, there is a dearth of formal analysis examining these issues. We fill these gaps in the literature by formally investigating how the characteristics of organizations, security researchers, and the legal environment impact a BBP’s optimal bounty and cost-effectiveness based on the vulnerability management and crowdsourcing framework. Specifically, we examine how these measures are impacted by the key factors in a BBP, including the security features of the organization (Cassin 2020, Marino 2020), the characteristics of security researchers (Mumtaz et al. 2020), and the legal underpinnings of the BBP (Haworth 2021). Based on our findings, we provide practical implications for organizations, IT security professionals, and policymakers.

## 1.1. Motivation

NIST security standards and many security professionals emphasize the significance of receiving vulnerability reports from the public, advocating for organizations to carefully manage the process (National Institute of Standards and Technology 2020). One proactive way is incentivizing the public to send vulnerability reports by establishing a BBP. To better manage a BBP, it is crucial to recognize that a BBP has crowdsourcing features. However, a BBP in our context further requires tight integration of the crowdsourcing effort and the BBP organizer’s patching effort. Hence, a BBP in our paper is beyond crowdsourcing and plays a pivotal role in vulnerability management (Cassin 2020). Therefore, it is imperative to consider the dynamics in a BBP’s costeffectiveness, as it is closely tied to an organization’s ability to derive benefits from and sustain the operation of a BBP.

Building on the vulnerability management and crowdsourcing paradigm, our study incorporates (i) various costs and benefits related to the vulnerability of the BBP, that is, expected prediscovery cost, the bounty itself, and the expected postdiscovery cost; (ii) the organization’s security characteristics, that is, patching complexity and security posture; (iii) the security researchers’ characteristics, such as their productivity, heterogeneity, and group size; and (iv) the legal regime.

A BBP helps an organization lower the risks by facilitating vulnerability discovery. This is because a BBP incentivizes a group of security researchers to help the host organization identify security issues in its IT applications or infrastructure. Hence, the wisdom of crowds can compete with the rate at which the inherent vulnerabilities of the software are exploited (Anderson and Moore 2006). This is important because vulnerability exploitation can lead to severe consequences.

For example, Equifax’s 2017 data breach was due to the exploitation of an unpatched vulnerability in the consumer complaint web portal (Fruhlinger 2020). This breach has been deemed as entirely preventable by simply discovering and patching the vulnerability in a timely manner (Whittaker 2018). With the help of security researchers, a vulnerability might have been identified. Therefore, a BBP can lower the expected damage cost, that is, expected prediscovery cost, before the vul nerability is discovered.

Although the benefits of a BBP are well-recognized, beyond offering the bounty per se, the BBP may incur costs induced by the risk of leaking the vulnerability. Specifically, providing incentives in a BBP increases the overall search effort among the participating security researchers (Zhao et al. 2017). As a result, such an increased level of scrutiny is likely to cause leaks out of the program (Crews et al. 2018). These leaks do not have to be in the form of actual submissions or the proof of concept (showing how the vulnerability can be exploited) as any related information about a vulnerability can be disastrous for the organization. We define this cost as the expected postdiscovery cost, which is one of the important considerations of the organizations that run BBPs.

Considering the trade-offs in a BBP, organizations need to be strategic in designing the monetary incentives. Specifically, they need to consider three main categories of factors. The first category includes organizations’ security features, including patching complexity and security posture. In contrast to typical crowdsourcing programs, a BBP serves as an integral component of enterprise vulnerability management, in which an organization’s prompt response and remediation are crucial (August and Tunca 2006, Ransbotham and Ramsey 2012). Thus, incorporating the organization’s patching efficiency is vital in BBP design and analysis. The efficiency hinges on two factors: patching complexity and security posture. Patching complexity is influenced by the organization’s IT system or infrastructure and represents the complexity of remediating discovered vulnerabilities. For instance, patching vulnerabilities in core IT systems is more complex because they create a longer downtime or operational problems (Shein 2022). Because patching complexity determines the baseline of patching time, it substantially affects BBP’s costs and payoffs. For example, without accounting for patching complexity, Apple’s BBP incentivized security researchers to search for its vulnerabilities. However, Apple was unable to patch them quickly. Consequently, in 2021, security researchers disclosed the unpatched vulnerabilities they discovered, leaving Apple exposed to heightened risks (Campbell 2021).

Another critical feature is the organization’s security posture representing the established IT security control capability (National Institute of Standards and Technology 2014). A high level of security posture is generally a long-term endeavor that requires consistent security investment and training (Petersen 2019). Therefore, this metric represents the overall security capability and affects the organization’s efficacy in dealing with vulnerabilities. Along with the patching complexity, the security posture determines the realized patching duration and further impacts various costs (such as the damage cost and the postdiscovery cost that are discussed earlier), thereby influencing the BBP’s performance. Neglecting these factors could lead to the failure of the BBP. As a security practitioner pointed out, “One of the big systemic issues is the fact that many bug-bounty programs are implemented irrespective of the maturity of the underlying cybersecurity program’s practice. That means asset visibility, vulnerability management, developer training, and more” (Chickowski 2022, para. 7). Therefore, assessing and examining the roles of organizational security features in BBP has nontrivial implications.

Similar to crowdsourcing, a BBP organizer (respectively, an organization) wants to incentivize a group of security researchers to discover a vulnerability. Hence, to organizations, the characteristics of participants are critical in determining the performance of crowdsourcing programs (Ales et al. 2019). The productivity of security researchers can directly affect the outcomes of BBPs. However, security researchers are not homogeneous regarding their productivities because BBPs usually attract security researchers with various backgrounds and skill sets (Hata et al. 2017). When designing the reward in BBP, the network of security researchers argue that some organizations do not consider participants’ skill levels or productivity and offer bounties that fail to attract security researchers, leading to delays in vulnerability discoveries (e.g., see HackerOne 2023). Although this heterogeneity is being discussed in practice, there is limited understanding regarding how it impacts BBPs (e.g., see Zhao et al. 2017). Furthermore, the number of participat ing security researchers in a BBP affects their incentives and realized performance. As Linu’s law claims, “Given enough eyeballs, all bugs are shallow” (Raymond 1999, p. 1). Importantly, these characteristics exert different dynamics in a BBP because of the potential leakage risks. As discussed by many security experts from practice, without considering the number of participants and the associated competition in a BBP, the rewards provided by organizations may not sufficiently incentivize security researchers to search for vulnerabilities, resulting in an ineffective BBP (e.g., see Hammond 2021). Therefore, it is necessary to carefully examine the characteristics of secu rity researchers to design a more effective BBP.

Security researchers play a crucial role in identifying vulnerabilities early, yet they face significant legal uncertainties (Gamero-Garrido et al. 2017). The ambig uous line between ethical and malicious hacking cre ates a potential chilling effect on these researchers because of hacking laws (Whittaker 2018). This is because their search for vulnerabilities often involves intrusive actions, such as unauthorized access or han dling sensitive data, which could be misconstrued as illegal activities or policy violations (Khalid 2023). This misunderstanding may lead to researchers not receiving the promised bounty (Smalley 2022). The legal protection strengthens the legitimacy of ethical hacking, thereby increasing security researchers’ probability of receiving the bounty because their effort is less likely to be considered as hacking (Nidecki 2023). Therefore, the extent of legal protection for security researchers (referred to as legal protection hereafter) is crucial for their incentive to seek out vulnerabilities in BBPs. Con versely, for organizations, this legal protection could heighten vulnerability exposure risks as it may increase the chances of undisclosed vulnerabilities if researchers are legally shielded. This issue is a subject of intense debate in industry circles and legislative bodies. For example, the 2022 amendment to the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, which criminalizes unauthorized IT system access, was designed to bolster legal safeguards for vulnerability hunters (Department of Justice 2022). However, many are concerned that this change migh escalate risks for those receiving vulnerability reports (Greig 2022). The legal environment surrounding ethi cal hacking can impact the risks for both organizations and security researchers. Neglecting to consider the legal factors when designing a BBP reward can result in either the failures of the BBP (e.g., see Sardaryzadeh 2023) o expose organizations to higher risks (e.g., see Porup 2018). Given these tensions and ongoing debates, understanding the impact of legal protection on BBPs is of considerable practical importance.

In summary, we investigate the role of organizational characteristics, security researchers’ characteristics, and the legal regime on the optimal bounty and the realized total cost of BBPs. In the next section, we discuss our research questions and contributions in detail.

## 1.2. Research Questions and Contributions

Despite the growing prevalence of BBPs across various organizations, the role of BBPs in the vulnerability management paradigm has seldom been investigated. Furthermore, limited studies examine how primary factors affect BBPs’ cost-effectiveness. To fill these gaps, we raise several questions related to how these primary factors, including the organization’s security features, various characteristics of security researchers, and legal implications on ethical hacking, influence the optimal bounty amount and the cost-effectiveness of the BBP.

First, we examine the roles of organizations’ security features, including patching complexity and security posture. These attributes are critical for the performance of a BBP within the broader context of the vulnerability management paradigm (O’Donnell 2018, Marino 2020). In practice, each organization possesses different digital assets, each associated with varying levels of patching complexity, which directly impacts the baseline patching time. Specifically, the higher the complexity is, the more time it would take to remediate or patch the vulnerability. Furthermore, an organization’s security posture can affect the realized patching time. This is because patching efficiency is positively associated with the overall IT security control capability (Souppaya and Scarfone 2022). Moreover, security posture impacts the realized base level of damage cost. A strong security posture signifies effective IT security controls capable of mitigating a larger portion of the damage cost. However, the precise impact of these security attributes on the bounty remains unclear. Therefore, we ask the following: does having a higher patching complexity or a weaker security posture imply a higher bounty? Proposition 1 addresses this question. One may think that an organization with high patching complexity or weak security posture should provide a higher bounty because it should benefit more from a BBP. However, our results reveal that this conjecture does not necessarily hold.

Second, similar to crowdsourcing programs, security researchers participating in the BBP exhibit varying productivity levels and total numbers. Given the productivity levels and the number of security researchers, each security researcher exerts effort to search for a vulnerability, which is critical for realizing the benefits of BBPs (Sridhar and Ng 2021). Among these security researchers, some are experts with higher levels of pro ductivity, whereas others are novices with significantly lower productivity levels, and a whole spectrum of security researchers is in between. Notably, the increased levels of the lower bound (i.e., novices) and upper bound (i.e., experts) of productivity have oppo site impacts on the heterogeneity among security researchers. Furthermore, the number of security researchers is important in the vulnerability discovery process. Motivated by the quote “given enough eyeballs, all bugs are shallow” (Raymond 1999, p. 1), which suggests that increasing the bounty can reduce the total cost of vulnerability management (i.e., enhance BBP performance) when more participants are involved in the BBP or when the security researchers exhibit higher levels of productivity. Indeed, the economics literature suggests that the benefit of the reward increases in the number of participants (e.g., see Boudreau et al. 2011). However, these issues are unclear in the context of BBPs, in which crowdsourcing is only part of the vulnerability management life cycle. Therefore, we first ask the following: does having a crowd of (i) security researchers of higher heterogeneity in productivity or (ii) a larger size imply a higher bounty? Then, a followup question: does having a crowd with these changed characteristics correspond to a lower total cost for the BBP? We address these questions in Propositions 2–5. The practice is inclined to think that an organization would be better to provide a higher bounty if security researchers’ productivity is high and the number of them is large because the crowd can be more efficient in searching for a vulnerability (Raymond 1999). Furthermore, some also believe that having a crowd with those characteristics can always improve crowdsourcing performance (Xie et al. 2014). Nevertheless, our findings demonstrate that these arguments are not necessarily correct in the BBP context.

Furthermore, legal tensions in BBPs are significant because of the sensitive nature of security vulnerabilities (Elazari 2018). To what extent security researchers can be protected under various regulatory regimes can profoundly affect a BBP’s payoff for organizations, considering that organizations may incur additional costs from unintended violations or infractions within BBPs (Crews et al. 2018). To gain a better understanding of the economic impact of such protection, we ask the following question: does a higher legal protection for security researchers imply a lower bounty and an increased total cost for the BBP? We address this question in Proposition 6. Interestingly, our findings reveal that depending on the nature of various security attributes, an organization may need to offer a higher bounty or may derive greater benefits from a BBP when the level of legal protection increases.

## 1.3. Literature Review

Our study is related to organizational cybersecurity management, bug bounty programs, and innovation crowdsourcing. In this section, we position our work with respect to these literature streams. Figure 1 summarizes how our study differs from the literature.

## 1.3.1. Organizational Cybersecurity Management.

Information systems literature examines how organizations make decisions in cybersecurity management in both in-house and outsourcing settings. In in-house settings, organizations need to evaluate their risk profiles to decide the optimal IT security investment (Gordon and Loeb 2002, Lee et al. 2016, Dey et al. 2022). When outsourcing security management, organizations should design appropriate contracts to align incentives with security service providers to obtain better outcomes (Lee et al. 2013, Cezar et al. 2014, Hui et al. 2019). Based on the two general settings, the literature explores specific security contexts, such as vulnerability management (Cavusoglu et al. 2008), security monitoring (Ji et al. 2016), and network security (August and Tunca 2006).

Among these literature streams, our study is most closely related to vulnerability management. Whereas this literature examines how organizations react to vulnerability disclosures (Cavusoglu et al. 2007, Arora et al. 2008) and deploy security patches (Cavusoglu et al. 2008, August and Niculescu 2013) in both in-house and outsourcing settings, they mainly consider the organizations as passive recipients of vulnerabilities. Hence, there is a gap in understanding how organizations can seek unknown vulnerabilities in their systems by incentivizing a crowd. This question is particularly pertinent in practice given that unidentified vulnerabilities bring in substantial risks, and crowdsourcing is regarded as an effective way to facilitate vulnerability discovery. Therefore, our study addresses this gap by exploring vulnerability crowdsourcing as a proactive defense strategy for organizations and endogenizing vulnerability discovery time. To achieve this goal, we provide a framework to inform organizations about designing incentives based on primary contextual factors, including characteristics of organizations, security researchers, and the legal regime. Our Propositions 1–3 and 6 provide new insights regarding these issues and contribute to the literature.

1.3.2. Bug Bounty Programs. Several studies explore the cost-effectiveness of BBPs. First, a BBP benefits organizations by leveraging a wide pool of researchers to accelerate vulnerability discovery and reduce security costs (Schechter 2002). Furthermore, organizations may only need to reward the first discoverer of a vulnerability (Malladi and Subramanian 2019), often incurring less expense than hiring full-time security professionals (Walshe and Simpson 2020). Moreover, a BBP expands access to IT security talents, which is particularly valuable for small and medium enterprises (Sridhar and Ng 2021). These studies justify the prevalence of BBPs across various industries. However, several studies point out the inefficiencies in BBPs and propose improvements in policies (Gamero-Garrido et al. 2017, Zhao et al. 2017, Malladi and Subrama nian 2019).

Whereas existing research discusses the cost effectiveness of BBPs from different perspectives, none of them considers the patching step. Yet, in practice, patching a vulnerability is a necessary step in addressing the vulnerability and affects the ultimate security outcome (Cavusoglu et al. 2008). More importantly, to organizations, the interplay between several external factors and patching time plays a substantial role in affecting the BBP’s cost-effectiveness. To gain a better understanding of the security context, we consider crowdsourcing vulnerability discovery and vulnerability patching in the same model and investigate how security researchers and legal regimes affect the total costs of BBPs. Propositions 4–6 provide new insights for evaluating the cost-effectiveness of BBPs.

1.3.3. Innovation Crowdsourcing. Literature on innovation crowdsourcing spans information systems, operations management, and economics. Our study aligns closely with two main topics in this stream. The first concerns the design of optimal incentive schemes in allocating prizes among crowdsourcing participants. For instance, whether rewarding multiple participants or only the best participant (i.e., winner takes all) depends on the organizer’s objective (e.g., best performance or expected performance) or the project’s attributes (e.g., cost function types) (Moldovanu and Sela 2001, Terwiesch and Xu 2008, Ales et al. 2016, Stouras et al. 2021). The second topic explores how the characteristics of participants affect their performance. In particular, many studies indicate that the total number of participants subtly affects their effort level (Terwiesch and Xu 2008, Ales et al. 2016). Furthermore, participant heterogeneity in terms of productivity or skills distinctly impacts the crowdsourcing performance (Zhang et al. 2019).

A BBP includes crowdsourcing because the organizations want to expedite vulnerability discovery—a process of generating innovative outputs (Ransbotham and Ramsey 2012)—by providing incentives to a group of security researchers. Hence, “there is a defined crowd with a clear goal and a defined benefit for both the worker and requester” (Fryer and Simperl 2017, p. 3). Building on existing models and industrial practices, we focus on a winner-takes-all award scheme, in which only the best participant wins the prize. However, previous crowdsourcing models and settings are not fully applicable to BBPs for two primary reasons. First, because of the risks of vulnerability exposures stemming from security researchers’ efforts in the program, designing a BBP requires organizations to consider the trade-off when incentivizing security researchers. This differs from most crowdsourcing models, which focus on maximizing the efforts of participants (Ales et al. 2016, Stouras et al. 2021). Second, unlike most crowdsourcing tasks, the realized costs of a BBP depend on organizational security features. Therefore, it is necessary to incorporate these features when examining the BBP context. As a result, the impact of security researchers’ characteristics on the BBP’s design and performance is highly dependent on security attributes. This aspect has not been investigated in the literature. Therefore, our findings, particularly Propositions 2–5, extend the crowdsourcing literature by investigating how participants’ characteristics affect the optimal incentives and total costs in BBPs.

## 2. Model

In practice, a BBP involves a group of security researchers and an organization that we capture in our model. Because the productivity of each researcher cannot be observed, we consider a static game with incomplete information. Next, we discuss how we characterize model components in detail.

## 2.1. Security Researchers

Security researchers are independent individuals who make efforts to discover a security vulnerability for the benefit of the organization. In practice, they are incentivized to exert efforts in searching for vulnerabilities in response to the monetary incentives organizations announce in their BBP (Laszka et al. 2018). Whereas the major advantage of a BBP lies in the power of the crowd as a whole, a BBP’s overall performance needs to be inferred from the characteristics of individual security researchers. In a BBP, each security researcher determines the level of the researcher’s effort depending on the expected payoff. Furthermore, the expected payoff is contingent on the probability that the security researcher is the first to submit a valid vulnerability report (Bugcrowd 2015). Lastly, the expected payoff also depends on the likelihood that security researchers will not face a backlash from the organization and indeed receive the bounty (Gamero-Garrido et al. 2017). Next, we explain the details.

2.1.1. Productivity-Based Project Model. The main task for security researchers is searching for a certain vulnerability.<sup>2</sup> Therefore, their performances are their vulnerability discovery times. To characterize their performances, we use a productivity-based project model in which a security researcher’s performance is a prod uct of two factors: productivity and effort level.<sup>3</sup> Each security researcher i is endowed with productivity $( \mathrm { i . e . , } a _ { i } )$ in discovering a security flaw. This productivity is related to the level of security skills. Security researcher i makes efforts $e _ { i }$ to improve the researcher’s performance in searching for vulnerabilities. For example, security researchers usually spend time conducting (basic) forensic analysis, trying different attacking methods, or figuring out potential remediation (Zhao et al. 2017). Their performance $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , g _ { i } )$ can be written as $g _ { i } = a _ { i } e _ { i } $ , which is similar to the tournament literature $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ ., see Ko¨rpeog˘lu and Cho 2018). Furthermore, security researchers use the same strategy in the symmetric Bayesian game, which is in line with the literature (e.g., see Ales et al. 2021, Stouras et al. 2021).

Because the main purpose of a BBP is to facilitate vulnerability identification, the discovery time of each security researcher can be regarded as the researcher’s performance, which is the focus of the tournament literature (e.g., Terwiesch and Xu 2008, Archak and Sundararajan 2009). The performance is negatively associated with vulnerability discovery time (i.e., the vulnerability is discovered earlier when performance is better). Hence, we characterize the vulnerability discovery time as ${ \scriptstyle { \frac { T } { 1 + a _ { i } e _ { i } } } } ,$ , where T denotes the vulnerability discovery time without a BBP. The characterization suggests that the reduction in the earliest discovery time is with respect to the benchmark time T and that the vulnerability discovery time is nonnegative.

Furthermore, a security researcher incurs a unit cost of c when exerting effort. Therefore, each incurs a total effort cost $e _ { i } c .$ : Organizations have no incentive to announce that a specific vulnerability in their system is discovered but unpatched because the disclosure can lead to the exploitation of the vulnerability (HackerOne 2011). Therefore, the effort of a security researcher is taken as a lump sum (not continuous) in our model regardless of whether the researcher wins the bounty or not, and each security researcher determines the effort level based on the researcher’s expected payoff.

2.1.2. Tournament Among Security Researchers. A BBP is similar to a winner-take-all tournament. In other words, only the participant who has the best performance can win the bounty. In the BBP setting, only the security researcher who is the first to find the vulnerability can win the bounty. Therefore, a security researcher’s expected payoff in participating in a BBP can be represented as E(reward ∗ Probability(1st)). Given that a reward (i.e., a bounty) is public information, it is necessary to derive the winning probability.

A security researcher’s winning probability depends on the researcher’s and others’ performances. Similar to tournament settings, we consider that the number of security researchers (i.e., n) and the distribution of productivity Q (i.e., the productivity follows a uniform distribution, which is $\bar { Q } \sim U [ \underline { { a } } , \overline { { a } } ] )$ ) are common knowledge (e.g., see Terwiesch and Xu 2008, Stouras et al. 2021).<sup>4</sup>

2.1.3. Legal Risks in Participation. A BBP has high levels of legal uncertainty for security researchers because of the gray area in ethical hacking. As a result, being the fastest security researcher does not necessarily mean the researcher will receive the reward. Consequently, the legal uncertainty can affect the perceived award in participating in a BBP. This is a debated topic in the BBP context (Gamero-Garrido et al. 2017). Because of the legal risks of participating in BBPs, security researchers may be discouraged from searching for a vulnerability. Although some BBPs have certain policies aiming to mitigate this concern, the legal scope is ambiguous (Elazari 2018). For instance, some programs state, “You may not … attempt to gain unauthorized access to any solution or to networks connected to it, or to content stored or delivered through it … including by hacking, spoofing or seeking … ” The definitions of “authorization” and “hacking” are rather unclear (Porup 2018). Some BBPs adopt safe harbor clauses, promising not to pursue legal action against security researchers. Therefore, security researchers may face different levels of legal protection when participating in different BBPs. It adjusts the security researchers beliefs that they will indeed receive the bounty even if they are the first to discover the vulnerability. The legal protection l also impacts organizations in a nuanced way. At one extreme, if there is no legal protection $( \mathrm { i . e . , ~ } \bar { l } = 0 )$ , security researchers do not participate in the BBP as they consider it a witch hunt. At the other extreme, if there is complete protection $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , l = 1 )$ they would exert higher levels of effort but would not be concerned about leaks out of the program, leading to a higher likelihood of vulnerability leakage (Neumann and Hudson 2021), which is a significant concern to the firm. Hence, the parameter l, which depends on the environmental factors (such as legislation and industrial standards), also impacts the costs of the firm that we discuss in detail in Section 2.2.3.

2.1.4. Payoff Function. In summary, a group of security researchers search for the vulnerability and set their effort levels depending on the perceived reward, productivity, effort costs, legal protection, and other participants efforts. ## 2.2. Organizations as BBP Planners

Vulnerability management is essential in controlling information security risks in organizations (Barillon 2019). It mainly involves vulnerability identification and remediation (Cavalancia 2020). BBP, as a crowdsourced security solution, can leverage external help and facilitate vulnerability identification. The organization needs to decide on the amount of bounty if it holds a BBP as a layer in its vulnerability-management process. Along with the bounty cost, the organization incurs two other types of costs: expected prediscovery cost and expected postdiscovery cost.

2.2.1. Bounty Cost. In a BBP, a bounty is a reward (generally of a monetary nature) provided for the discovery of a vulnerability to a group of security researchers (Malladi and Subramanian 2019). We denote the bounty by b, which is a decision variable for the organization. As discussed earlier, setting a proper bounty is an important strategy to elicit efforts from security researchers. For example, if the amount is too low, security researchers would exert lower levels of effort, leading to a delay in the identification of the vulnerability. Also, as explained in Section 2.1.3, although the first security researcher that identifies the vulnerability might not be awarded the bounty because of the legal framework on which the bounty is based, we consider that there will at least be one security researcher who identifies the vulnerability and acts within the legal scope of the BBP. Hence, we focus on the BBPs in which the bounty is awarded to a security researcher.

2.2.2. Expected Prediscovery Cost. Vulnerabilities can be leveraged to illegally access organizations’ networks and systems, causing substantial damage. From the perspective of vulnerability management and organizational security management, several key factors impact the magnitude of these damages, including the organization’s security posture, the vulnerability’s earliest discovery time, and the base damage cost.

2.2.2.1. Organization’s Security Posture. An organization’s security posture is critical to its security performance. It is defined as “the security status of an enterprise’s network, information, and system based on information security resources (e.g., people, hardware, software, policies) and capabilities in place to manage the defense of the enterprise and to react as the situation changes” (Dempsey et al. 2012, p. B-13). To assess security posture, NIST defines several cybersecurity frameworks to provide various security metrics in key areas, such as risk-management processes, security technologies, and implementation (e.g., National

Institute of Standards and Technology 2014). Based on the cybersecurity framework, a variety of related standards and models $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , International Organization for Standardization; Information Technology Infrastructure Library; SysAdmin, Audit, Network and Security; Forrester Research) are established. These standards and models share similar characteristics and enable organizations to evaluate their security posture that we denote with $\beta ,$ where we consider that $\bar { 0 } \leq \beta \leq 1$ : The lower the $\beta ,$ the stronger the organization’s security posture. In many settings, increasing the IT capability to tackle a vulnerability in a short-term horizon is not possible because hiring IT security staff that can understand the inner workings of the software application or infrastructure is generally a long-term endeavor (Petersen 2019). Therefore, in our main model, we consider that the security posture is constant and exogenous. However, in Online Section EC.3.4, we endogenize $\beta$ and find that our results are qualitatively similar to those of our main model.

As a proxy for the organization’s overall security capability, security posture can substantially impact the efficacy of vulnerability management in several main aspects. We first introduce its impact in the prediscovery period. Generally, a strong security posture is negatively associated with the rate of damage caused by the exploitation of the vulnerability. The main reason is that a strong security posture indicates that an organization has a smaller attack surface (also known as the attack vector), which means that effective measures are adopted to provide a higher level of protection (Robinson 2017). For instance, effective network segmentation can prevent attackers’ lateral movement, thereby lowering the dam age loss (Andress and Leary 2016). We denote the unit damage cost per unit time with $c _ { a } .$ Therefore, we can present the damage cost as $\beta c _ { a }$ because $\beta$ (i.e., security posture) represents the extent of realized damage cost.<sup>5</sup> This damage cost accrues through the prediscovery period of the vulnerability.

2.2.2.2. Earliest Discovery Time. The life cycle of a vulnerability consists of two main phases: the prediscovery phase and the postdiscovery phase. The prediscovery phase refers to the duration from announcing the bounty to receiving the first vulnerability report (i.e., 0 to I). In BBPs, the expected earliest discovery is determined based on the vulnerability’s properties (such as the complexity of searching for them) and security researchers’ expected best performance. Speci fically, we characterize the vulnerability’s benchmark discovery time by T. In other words, the vulnerability can be discovered at time T without a BBP. Furthermore, security researchers’ expected best performance is determined by all of their performances. During the postdiscovery period, the organization may incur damage costs and leakage costs before the vulnerability is patched.

2.2.3.1. Postdiscovery Damage Cost. The organization can start the patching process only after discovering the vulnerability. Because the vulnerability has not yet been fixed, the damage cost still accumulates during the patching process. However, the organization can now use quick workarounds to partially mitigate the risks (and, hence, costs), and the effectiveness of the workarounds is based on its security posture $( \mathrm { i . e . , } \beta )$ Therefore, the damage cost that accrues now is at a different scale than the cost during the prediscovery period $\mathrm { ( i . e . , } c _ { a } \beta$ per unit time). 2.2.3.2. Postdiscovery Leakage Cost. Besides, the legal protection offered to the BBP participants (by law) is one of the most debated topics in the BBP context (Gamero-Garrido et al. 2017). For many organizations, patching is costly and is believed to have negative impacts on productivity. However, the friction in patching can be substantially lower if the organization has clear maintenance plans to implement effectively. ## 2.3. Sequence of the Game

In a BBP, an organization announces a bounty for uncovering the vulnerability in its IT infrastructure or digital products. Observing the bounty, a group of security researchers exerts effort to identify the security flaw simultaneously. Upon identifying the vulnerability, they submit a report to inform the organization of the vulnerability. We capture all the dynamism discussed so far in a two-stage game. Specifically, in the first stage, the organization decides on the amount of the bounty. As a response, in the second stage, each individual security researcher decides how much effort to exert in response to the announced bounty and the competition dynamics among the security researchers. In particular, the second stage consists of two main phases: the prediscovery phase and the postdiscovery phase. The prediscovery phase is between time 0 and $I ,$ which is the first time when the vulnerability is reported by a security researcher. Next, the organization starts to patch the reported vulnerability at time $I ,$ and it takes ωβ (i.e., patching time) to fix the vulnerability. Therefore, the patching time determines the length of the postdiscovery phase. The end of this phase implies that the vulnerability is fixed and causes no threat. ## 4. Results and Managerial Insights

To better understand how an organization designs a bounty and how to improve the cost-effectiveness of a BBP, we present our results based on how the bounty and the performance of the BBP are impacted by the characteristics of (i) the organization, (ii) security researchers, and (iii) the legal underpinnings.<sup>10</sup>

## 4.1. Does Having a Higher Patching Complexity or a Weaker Security Posture Imply a Higher Bounty?

Vulnerability management consists of two main stages: vulnerability discovery and vulnerability remediation as previously discussed in the motivation section (Ransbotham and Ramsey 2012). Two organizational security characteristics are critical to vulnerability management, that is, security posture and patching complexity. A firm’s security posture $( \mathrm { i . e . , } \beta )$ represents an organization’s overall security capability. It impacts both stages of vulnerability management by directly affecting damage cost and patching time. A firm’s patching complexity $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , \omega )$ also affects patching time, and it is affected by the organization’s IT system size and the types of its digital assets as fixing vulnerabilities that lie in sophisticated and distributed IT systems are argued to be more complex than fixing other vulnerabilities (Souppaya and Scarfone 2022). For instance, vulnerabilities in core IT systems may create a longer downtime or cause more operational problems (Shein 2022).

An increasing number of organizations in different industries, including big tech firms $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , Microsoft, Google), traditional businesses $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , in healthcare or retail), and government agencies (e.g., Department of Defense, Air Force) establish their BBPs. This highlights the heterogeneity across organizations that run BBPs. In effect, practitioners advocate that organizations need to assess their own security features when incorporating BBPs in vulnerability management (e.g., see Cassin 2020, Marino 2020). However, there is no formal analysis of how to design a bounty based on these characteristics. Therefore, we ask the following: does a higher patching complexity or a weaker security posture imply a higher bounty? Proposition 1 addresses this question.

Proposition 1. (i) If the patching complexity of a vulnerability $( i . e . ,$ ω) is higher, the organization prefers a lower bounty. ## 4.2. How Do Security Researchers Impact the Dynamics of the BBP?

The main goal of a BBP is to incentivize security researchers to search for vulnerabilities. In turn, the characteristics of security researchers, namely, their productivity, het erogeneity, and total number, affect the dynamics of the BBP. In this section, we explore these issues.

4.2.1. Does an Increase in Security Researchers’ Productivities or Total Number Imply a Higher Bounty? In the broader context of crowdsourcing, a group of participants can make significant contributions to projects in terms of generating innovative outputs (Boudreau et al. 2011). Similarly, in a BBP, a group of security research ers work on the same IT infrastructure or digital products, facilitating the discovery of vulnerabilities. Specifically, two characteristics of security researchers are vital for the success of a BBP: their (i) productivity distribution and (ii) total number. Given the distinct collaborative dynamics and inherent risks that differentiate a BBP from conventional crowdsourcing, this topic is discussed in practice (Haynes and Sadeghipour 2021). Yet there is no guidance in the literature regarding the role of security researchers’ characteristics in how an organization designs its BBP. Hence, to fill this gap and provide guidance to organizations, we ask the following: does having more productive security researchers in the BBP correspond to a higher bounty? We address this question in Proposition 2. The thresh olds $\hat { c } _ { a 1 }$ and $\hat { c } _ { p 1 }$ are provided in the Online Appendix.

First, the enhancement of novices’ productivity may not benefit the costeffectiveness of the BBP if the unit postdiscovery cost is high, which is in contrast to the expectations in practice (e.g., see Harrington 2021). Organizations should consider if they are vulnerable to high losses in the postdiscovery phase before enhancing novices productivity. Nevertheless, we also find that enhancing experts’ productivity always leads to lower total costs. Therefore, organizations may consider providing advanced tools that are more geared toward security experts. For example, the release of technical details of other similar vulnerabilities or the focal organization’s IT infrastructure reveals some deep features, which can be better leveraged by security experts to increase productivity (Giboney et al. 2016).

Furthermore, organizations face trade-offs in having a smaller or larger number of security researchers participating in their BBPs in practice. Our findings in Proposition 5 suggest that a higher number of security researchers may not be beneficial if the security researchers have similar productivity $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ , the heterogeneity among the productivity levels of security researchers is low), the number of security researchers is low, or the unit postdiscovery cost is high. These settings are also observed in practice. For example, when a BBP attracts security researchers of similar productiv ity, such as for cross-site scripting vulnerabilities (Owens 2018), any endeavor of the organizations for increasing the number of participants would be futile compared with BBPs in which security researchers present higher levels of heterogeneity in productivity. Even though the heterogeneity is high, the organization needs to consider if it is more likely to experience loss in the postdiscovery cost. If so, the organization may prefer a small size of security researchers to have a more cost-effective BBP.

The findings contribute to the literature on BBP by examining the total costs during the vulnerability life cycle in the BBP context. Furthermore, they inform crowdsourcing literature by formally analyzing how characteristics of security researchers (such as their heterogeneity and number) and their interaction with information security factors (e.g., the unit postdiscovery cost) affect the total cost of BBPs, highlighting the unique factors in the BBP context as we discuss in Section 1.3.3.

## 4.3. Does a Higher Legal Protection for Security Researchers Imply a Lower Bounty and an Increased Total Cost for the BBP?

As discussed in the motivation and modeling sections, a BBP does not guarantee that the security researcher who correctly identifies the vulnerability first will be rewarded the bounty because the researcher’s efforts may be considered illegal hacking. This issue is a debated topic in the BBP domain (Haworth 2021). Therefore, the legal protection offered to security researchers in BBP adjusts the security researchers’ beliefs that they receive a bounty if they are the first to discover the vulnerability. Indeed, recently, many security professionals have advocated that establishing a safe harbor for security researchers would be beneficial to protect security researchers (Lynch 2021).

However, although offering protection for security researchers is beneficial for them, doing so may impose threats to the organization after the vulnerability is discovered (Neumann and Hudson 2021). The offered level of protection may be determined based on the industrial context in some cases. Furthermore, lawmakers and government agencies are also interested in the legal protection issue (Pfefferkorn 2020). Some BBPs have certain policies aiming to provide legal protection to $\mathrm { B B P }$ participants, and the U.S. Department of Justice has enacted laws for BBPs. However, the legal scope is still ambiguous (Elazari 2018, Department of Justice 2022), and to our knowledge, there is no formal analysis that investigates if providing higher levels of legal protection to security researchers (i) results in a lower bounty or (ii) worsens the cost-effectiveness of the BBP. For managers, understanding the interplay between legal protections and BBP effectiveness is key to vulnerability management. They should integrate legal considerations into their cybersecurity strategies, ensuring that BBPs remain an effective tool for mitigating losses caused by vulnerabilities. In a similar manner, policymakers should consider the impact of legal protections on the cybersecurity ecosystem. Whereas enhancing legal protections for security researchers is crucial, it’s also important to balance these protections with the needs of organizations. Policymakers should engage with security researchers and organizational stakeholders to develop well-rounded policies.

Although legal protection for security researchers is one of the topics debated in the literature (Gamero-Garrido et al. 2017), no prior work in the literature considers this effect. With Proposition 6, we fill this gap in the literature on organizational cybersecurity investment and BBPs by examining how protecting security researchers’ ethical hacking affects the organization’s bounty and the total cost of its BBP.

## 5. Discussion and Conclusion

The future of cybersecurity requires close collaboration between security researchers and organizations. As NIST notes, “coordinated vulnerability disclosure life-cycle” is a core practice in the cybersecurity framework (Barrett 2018). This highlights that organizations should be more open to external security researchers and adjust their strategies in vulnerability management accordingly. Therefore, it is imperative for organizations to gain a better understanding of the benefits and costs of crowdsourced security solutions. However, there is limited formal analysis of the topic, particularly from the perspective of crowdsourcing and vulnerability management. Our study fills the gap by using game-theoretical models that capture characteristics of security researchers, organizations, and legal protection for security researchers. Hence, our work sheds light on how these characteristics impact the important dynamics in BBP, such as the bounty design and costeffectiveness of the program.

## 5.1. Academic Contributions

Our study contributes to three main streams of literature. First, it enhances organizational cybersecurity management literature. Specifically, we investigate vul nerability crowdsourcing as a proactive defense strat egy for organizations and endogenize vulnerability discovery time. Building on previous research in vul nerability management (Cavusoglu et al. 2007, 2008; Arora et al. 2008; August and Niculescu 2013; Sen et al. 2020), we provide a framework to guide organizations in designing BBP-related incentives based on primary contextual factors, including characteristics of organi zations, security researchers, and the legal regime.

Furthermore, our study contributes to the bug bounty program literature by incorporating two main stages into our models: crowdsourcing vulnerability discovery and vulnerability patching. Unlike previous studies that focus solely on vulnerability discovery (Gamero-Garrido et al. 2017, Malladi and Subramanian 2019), our models con sider the life cycle of vulnerabilities, offering new insights into the cost-effectiveness of BBPs.

Lastly, our models complement innovation crowdsourcing literature by incorporating the distinctive characteristics of a BBP. Unlike most crowdsourcing models, which aim to maximize the efforts of partici pants (Ales et al. 2016, Stouras et al. 2021), a BBP involves organizational security features and vulnerability exposure risks. Our study captures these unique and important factors and provides implications for BBPs that extend beyond general crowdsourcing.

## 5.2. Practical Implications

Our study provides practical implications on how an organization manages its BBP as a layer of its vulnerability management to achieve better cost-effectiveness. We first discuss the impact of the organization’s security characteristics on its BBP. In particular, in the context of vulnerability management and BBPs, the roles of organizational patching complexity and security posture are essential. This is because, unlike most crowdsourcing programs, dealing with a vulnerability requires coordination between the organization and security researchers. Therefore, the organization needs to assess its security capability when designing a BBP as many security practitioners advocate (O’Donnell 2018). Specifically, if the organization experiences high patching complexity, such as hospitals (Davis 2017), it needs to announce a lower bounty. This is because high patching complexity can exacerbate the exposure effect of the bounty because the vulnerability leakage risk increases with patching time. Furthermore, if the organization faces the challenge of having limited security resources and professionals, it is, interestingly, beneficial for the organization to provide a lower bounty if the patching is nontrivial. The practical implication is that BBPs may not substitute for organizations security characteristics. Therefore, organizations must carefully evaluate their patching complexity and security postures when designing a BBP.

Besides the organizations themselves, a group of security researchers is the primary driver of a BBP’s performance (Sridhar and Ng 2021). Therefore, the organization needs to strategically design the bounty based on the dynamics in security researchers’ productivity levels and their total number. First, if security researchers are more productive (e.g., they learn from prevalent ethical hacking tutorials; Laszka et al. 2018), the organization may not necessarily need to provide a higher bounty if the unit postdiscovery cost or unit damage cost is high. This is because high-cost environments suggest that the bounty’s incentive effect cannot offset its exposure effect. Furthermore, although many BBPs provide hacking tutorials and testing software (HackerOne 2019), an increased level of novice productivity may increase the BBP’s total cost if the unit postdiscovery is high. However, increasing expert productivity always reduces the total cost of the BBP. This suggests that organizations may need to disclose more high-level features on their products and IT infrastructure so that security experts can increase their productivity. This finding also aligns with the recommendations of domain experts, for example, see Giboney et al. (2016).

Furthermore, the total number of security researchers is also important in a BBP because “given enough eyeballs, all bugs are shallow” (Raymond 1999, p. 1). However, if there were more security researchers, interestingly, the organization may not try to leverage their efforts even more by providing a higher level of bounty. In particular, we show that the organization prefers a lower bounty if the security researchers are relatively homogeneous in their productivity levels. Furthermore, our results also suggest that a larger crowd of security researchers may not always be beneficial from a cost-effectiveness point of view. Our findings suggest that (i) if security researchers are homogeneous in their skills, (ii) the existing number of security researchers is low, or (iii) the unit postdiscovery cost is high, involving more of them in the BBP increases the total cost. For example, if the organization’s product is at an early stage and it has severe vulnerabilities that may be easily identified, security researchers usually have similar levels of skills as they are searching for the low-hanging fruit. In that case, the organization is better off with fewer participants. These results resonate with the fact that some organizations prefer to have a small group of security researchers to work on vulnerabilities when their products are relatively immature (i.e., in beta or prereleased versions) (GitHub 2021).

Furthermore, we explore how legal protection impacts the design of the bounty and the BBP’s costeffectiveness. In particular, the legal protection for security researchers is an important aspect, and recent efforts are being made at both industry and government levels (i.e., see Lynch 2021, Department of Justice 2022). Whereas many believe that enhancing legal protections for security researchers might increase risks for organizations (Neumann and Hudson 2021), our results suggest that, in the case of an increased level of legal protection, organizations may want to offer more generous bounties if the potential damage cost that a vulnerability may cause is not substantial. Furthermore, legal protection may even improve the total costeffectiveness of the BBP if the organization’s IT infrastructure is not substantially sophisticated or the organization has strong security capability. Therefore, industrial associations and policymakers need to consider these factors when designing standards and legal frameworks.

## 5.3. Future Research Opportunities

There are several major research opportunities in the bug bounty setting that we investigate. First, there is value in empirically examining the financial returns and costs associated with BBPs, particularly with respect to the nature of the organization. Moreover, future researchers could analyze social welfare within the BBP context by examining the net benefits obtained by organizations, security researchers, and other stakeholders (such as users, multiple firms, or bug bounty platforms). Lastly, considering the dynamic roles of security researchers could be intriguing. Specifically, upon discovering a vulnerability, a security researcher faces choices: reporting the vulnerability to the organizer, exploiting it, or selling it in the vulnerability market. Although our models incor porate the risks that security researchers may unintentionally disclose the vulnerability they find, future studies can model security researchers’ payoff functions across these aforementioned decisions.
