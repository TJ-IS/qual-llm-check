# Uncovering the Neural Processes of Privacy: A Neurally Informed Behavioral Intervention to Protect Information Privacy

Abstract. People are increasingly aware that their information is being tracked online. Although people generally self-report privacy to be important to them, in practice they often disclose far more private information than their self-reported privacy preferences denote. This “privacy paradox” could be better understood by uncovering the neural processes underlying the privacy calculus: weighing the risks against the benefits of disclosure. We assess the neural processes shaping privacy tradeoffs to characterize the neural mechanisms underlying privacy tradeoffs to design behavioral interventions that help people make better decisions that align with their privacy preferences. In Study 1, we used functional MRI (fMRI) to identify the neural correlates of the privacy calculus while partici pants integrated information about the benefits and risks of sharing information. fMRI results revealed that activation in the medial prefrontal cortex and the caudate nucleu tracked the perceived attractiveness of the benefits, while activation in the insula, amygdala, lateral prefrontal cortex, and temporoparietal junction tracked the perceived intru siveness of sensitive privacy questions. The lateral prefrontal and dorsomedial prefrontal cortices integrate these signals to guide information disclosure, and this prefrontal-parietal network was previously implicated in directing one’s attention. Interestingly, these fMRI findings suggest that attentional processes at the time of choice guide people’s decisions whether to disclose private information. We leveraged these brain insights to design and test a neurally informed behavioral intervention to help people protect their privacy (Study 2). Our results show that altering the timing when information is presented precisely at the time of decision (specifically within a second) directs attention to privacy risks versus benefits, therefore discouraging participants from liberally disclosing their private information. Identifying the underlying neural processes of privacy helps elucidate the privacy calculus and sheds light on the privacy paradox and guides the design of neurally informed behavioral interventions to help people protect their privacy.

## 1. Introduction

Privacy is touted as one of the major challenges of the information age and online markets (Smith et al. 2011, Tene and Polonetsky 2012, Gopal et al. 2018). People often share private information online, such as their photos, names of family and friends, their demographics, browsing behavior, and personal interests. People’s private information becomes vulnerable in the online environment given the relative ease of collecting, aggregating, and analyzing large-scale data with advanced data mining and machine learning techniques. Studies (Smith et al. 2011, Wang et al. 2013, Sweeney et al. 2018) show that sharing private information online exposes people to many serious risks, either from marketing firms, social media platforms, or third parties. Misusing private information may have serious consequences for people, such as economic and social discrimination (Acquisti et al. 2015); therefore, protecting information privacy is a fundamental prerequisite for the development of a safe online environment, as noted by many academics, industry leaders, and regulators (Malhotra et al. 2004, Be´langer and Crossler 2011, Sutanto et al. 2013).

A main reason people share their private information is because they believe that they may benefit from doing so, such as receiving personalized content (e.g., customized ads, location-specific promotions, and loyalty rewards). However, disclosing private information exposes people to privacy risks, such as losing control over their sensitive private information or private information being shared freely with others (Featherman and Pavlou 2003). Privacy risks include minor nuisances from unwanted advertising, embarrassing privacy breaches, discrimination from employers and other entities, or even identity theft. There is a tradeoff between risks associated with disclosing private information and the benefits from such disclosure (Boyd and Crawford 2012), a calculation often termed the “privacy calculus” (Dinev and Hart 2006). There is a tendency among people, however, to take arguably excessive risks with their privacy in exchange for relatively modest rewards (Hann et al. 2007, Acquisti et al. 2013, Kokolakis 2017). In this study, we seek to prevent people from taking excessive risks with their privacy, especially in exchange for meager rewards by understanding the neural correlates of privacy concerns and decisions about disclosing private information using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and designing behavioral interventions to prevent them from needlessly disclosing their sensitive private information.

Because privacy and information disclosure are theoretically related (Westin 1968, Altman 1975), it is reasonable to expect that people who claim to be concerned with their privacy will not liberally disclose their sensitive private information online. However, although the privacy literature shows that people generally selfreport that privacy is important to them (Barnes 2006, Debatin et al. 2009), their information disclosure is excessive relative to their privacy preferences (Acquisti and Gross 2006, Youn and Hall 2008, Debatin et al. 2009). This dissociation between self-reported privacy concerns and actual behavior is referred to as the “privacy paradox.” Many explanations for the privacy paradox have been offered in the privacy literature, such as low awareness of privacy risks (Debatin et al. 2009, Boyd and Crawford 2012), confusion about the information protection environment, failure to adjust privacy settings (Debatin et al. 2009), or ignorance of how private information is handled as privacy policies are often accepted without even being read (Milne and Culnan 2004, Good et al. 2007). In the present work, we seek to shed light on the privacy paradox by examining the neural origins of why people disclose their private information online while self-reporting their privacy to be important to them. By elucidating the neural processes that underlie the privacy calculus, we seek to shed light on the elusive privacy paradox by delving into the brain areas that are activated when people disclose their private information, despite the apparent risks of disclosure. We then leverage these neural insights to design an intervention that helps people protect their privacy.

Prior literature has shown that behavioral interventions can be effective to encourage (or “nudge”) people to make choices that better align with their own stated preferences without restricting their freedom to pursue other options (Thaler and Sunstein 2008) and thus repre sent a soft-paternalistic approach. Accordingly, appropriate privacy-related behaviors, informed by theory and empirical results, were shown to benefit both people and society by creating a safe and trustworthy environment. In this study, we draw from the neural insights on the privacy calculus and the privacy paradox from the fMRI data, and we seek to contribute to the privacy liter ature and practice by designing a behavioral interven tion that better aligns people’s behavior with their stated privacy preferences to help overcome the privacy paradox. Specifically, our primary thesis is that leveraging the underlying brain functionality involved in the privacy calculus can inform behavioral interventions to encourage people to protect their private information.

Much of the prior work on privacy relied on selfreported measures of privacy concerns, which may be limiting for several reasons. First, people often lack insight into their own beliefs (Gross and Acquisti 2005), making it difficult to reliably assess their privacy concerns, leading them to misreport their true privacy preferences. Second, respondents may assume the experimenters have certain expectations about the importance of privacy, and they may change their responses to align with the study’s perceived intent (termed “demand effects”), thereby enhancing their self-reported privacy concerns (and thus creating the observed privacy paradox). To alleviate these biases, we use brain imaging to measure privacy concerns, thus complementing the use of self-reported measures to capture privacy concerns (Dimoka et al. 2011). Accordingly, we use fMRI to investigate the neural correlates of privacy concerns, that is, where privacy concerns are mapped into the brain. We seek to cap ture the neural processes that underlie people’s pri vacy concerns and shape their decisions to disclose private information.

Integrating theories from the privacy literature with insights from the neuroscience literature, this study has two main aims: (1) we seek to break new ground in our theoretical understanding of the privacy calculus and the privacy paradox by identifying the neuropsychological mechanisms that underlie people’s decisions to disclose their private information online, and (2) using the knowledge we derive from the literature and fMRI results, we design a novel behavioral intervention that encourages people to avoid exposing themselves to excessive privacy risks from information disclosure and behave consistently with their stated privacy concerns to overcome the privacy paradox. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the neural correlates of privacy by seeking to directly measure people’s privacy concerns and rewards with brain imaging (fMRI). We make a contribution by leveraging knowledge acquired from the brain to guide the design of a neurally informed behavioral intervention to help people protect their privacy.

We conduct three experiments. In a pilot study, we examine whether stressing potential disclosure risks may encourage people to protect their private information and how the participants’ weighting of risks and benefits correlate with their real-world privacy behaviors. We show that simply highlighting the potential risks of information disclosure before the decisionmaking process does not prevent people from disclosing their private information. Importantly, the pilot shows that people’s behavior in the experimental task predicts their actual privacy behavior online (i.e., realworld information disclosure on Facebook) which enhances our studies’ external validity. In Study 1, we identify the neural mechanisms underlying privacy decisions and uncover how people weigh tradeoffs regarding their privacy using fMRI. The fMRI study identifies areas of the brain typically involved in threat detection and harm avoidance, including the anterior insula and amygdala, which show greater activation for more intrusive information requests. Importantly, activation in brain regions implicated in choosing between conflicted responses and orienting attention, including the lateral prefrontal cortex and parietal cortex, correlate with willingness to disclose private information. These neural findings imply that attentional processes may shape disclosure decisions. Building on this insight from the fMRI data, in Study 2, we develop and test a novel behavioral intervention to help protect people’s privacy. Specifically, we alter the timing of when information is presented to direct people’s attention and make the privacy risks of information disclosure more salient to them. By directing attention to the intrusiveness of the information requested, our intervention increases people’s willingness to protect their privacy. The timing of this intervention is essential, as the attention is directed at the moment of choice to stress the risk of sharing private information. We demonstrate that this neurally informed behavioral intervention actually promotes the respondents’ protection of their private information.

Our work makes three key contributions. First, we contribute to the information privacy literature by investigating the neural correlates underlying people’s decisions to disclose their private information, elucidating the psychological mechanisms underlying decisions regarding whether to disclose private information. In doing so, we contribute to the neuroscience literature (Dimoka 2010, 2012; Venkatraman et al. 2015; Vance et al. 2018; Meservy et al. 2019) by uncovering the neural correlates of privacy and helping overcome common limitations of self-reported measures that are predominant in the behavioral privacy literature. Second, we contribute to the privacy literature by designing and testing a novel, neurally informed behavioral intervention to help people protect their privacy. We demonstrate that directing people’s attention to privacy risks by altering the timing of when the information is presented at the time of their decision (within a second) encourages people to protect their private information. Third, we shed light on the elusive privacy paradox by demonstrating that the precise timing of when the privacy risks are presented matters. Using timing to redirect attention at the time of choice makes privacy risks more salient than they would be versus if assessed at a different time, thereby altering the privacy calculus. This focus on the privacy risks helps people to overcome the privacy paradox and protect their privacy.

## 2. Conceptual Background, Theory, and Hypotheses

First, we integrate key findings from the privacy litera ture on privacy concerns and related theories. Second, we integrate relevant findings from the neuroscience lit erature to hypothesize the processes that underlie people’s privacy decisions.

## 2.1. Privacy

The privacy literature has focused on privacy concerns and theoretically and empirically developed the antecedents and consequences of privacy concerns (Smith et al. 2011). People’s privacy concerns mainly stem from the risk of unauthorized use of personal data by third parties (Culnan and Armstrong 1999, Malhotra et al. 2004, Hong and Thong 2013) and individual differences in preferences, attitudes, and models (Ackerman and Mainwaring 2005). Antecedents of privacy concerns include privacy experiences (Smith et al. 1996), privacy awareness (Malhotra et al. 2004), personality differences (Xu 2007), gender (Hoy and Milne 2010), demo graphic differences (Culnan and Armstrong 1999), and culture (Dinev and Hart 2006). Xu et al. (2008) devel oped an integrative model to specify the drivers of privacy concerns to show privacy concerns to be formed from personal characteristics and situational cues. In terms of effects of privacy concerns, several studies found that privacy concerns affect people’s privacyrelated intentions and privacy-related behaviors (Jahan gir and Begum 2007, Kumar et al. 2008, Shin 2010, Gopal et al. 2018). For instance, previous research has demonstrated that privacy concerns are negatively associated with online purchase intentions and actual purchases (Eastlick et al. 2006, Tsai et al. 2011).

One major privacy theory is privacy calculus, which posits that people’s decisions to disclose private information depend on the outcome of a risk-benefit analy sis or “calculus” (Dinev and Hart 2006). For instance, studies have found that monetary incentives positively affect people’s private information disclosure behavior (Hann et al. 2007, Hui et al. 2007). Privacy risks inhibit, while privacy-related rewards enhance, people’s intentions to share their private information (Pavlou 2003, Pavlou and Gefen 2004). Personalization benefits often trump their privacy concerns (Chellappa and Sin 2005, Sutanto et al. 2013). Privacy calculus theory is consistent with a related “fair exchange” theory (Culnan and Bies 2003) that states that people are willing to share their private information if they perceive enjoying benefits in return. Accordingly, people would be more willing to disclose their private information if the benefits are perceived to be more attractive to them, whereas they would be less willing to disclose their private information if the questions are deemed more privacy intrusive to them.

People’s actual information disclosure rarely reflects their self-reported privacy concerns (Acquisti and Grossklags 2005, Dinev and Hart 2006, Youn and Hall 2008, Jiang et al. 2013), a dissociation between behavior and attitudes often referred to as the “privacy paradox.” The literature shows that people generally report privacy to be very important to them (Barnes 2006, Debatin et al. 2009); however, their actual behavior does not often reflect their stated privacy concerns (Acquist and Gross 2006, Youn and Hall 2008, Debatin et al. 2009). One common explanation of the privacy paradox is that people are not fully aware of the consequences of their behaviors (Acquisti et al. 2015). This explanation is supported by studies that demonstrate that people rarely read privacy policies about information use practices (Tsai et al. 2011). Theoretically, people’s uncertainty about the consequences can be affected by information asymmetry, bounded rationality, and psychological deviations (Acquisti and Grossklags 2005, Acquisti et al. 2017). Information asymmetry limits people’s knowledge about the effects of their behaviors, while bounded rationality and limited attention reduce their ability to process all relevant information (Acquisti and Grossklags 2005). Understanding the underlying psychological processes that shape people’s decisions about privacy tradeoffs may help encourage safer information disclosure behaviors in real life. Another explanation for the privacy paradox is that people may not be aware of their privacy concerns, and their self-reported privacy concerns do not reflect their true preferences (Gross and Acquisti 2005). People may lack insight into their own perceptions and cannot reliably assess their own privacy concerns, thus downplaying their severity when asked to self-report their privacy concerns. Even if people are aware of their privacy concerns, personality traits such as laziness may affect the disclosing behavior. For instance, Wirth et al. (2021) find that lazy individuals are more likely to self-disclose than less lazy ones given their lack of energy to transfer privacy concerns into actual privacy behaviors. Different from awareness, laziness alleviates the negative effect of privacy concerns on disclosing since individuals are unwilling or desire to use as little energy as possible to protect their privacy, instead of lack of attention to potential privacy risks.

Efforts to help people disclose their private information in a manner that aligns with their reported privacy preferences have focused on raising awareness of the risks of privacy breaches (Awad and Krishnan 2006, Tsai et al. 2011, Xu et al. 2012, Crossler and Belanger 2019). By making people aware of potential privacy risks, researchers aim to resolve the privacy paradox and encourage people to protect their privacy. Studies regarding the effect of raising awareness of the risks on the privacy behaviors have shown mixed results (Thaler and Sunstein 2008, Tsai et al. 2011, Brough et al. 2022). One stream of studies showed that raising awareness of the risks of sharing private information may increase privacy concerns and in turn discourage information disclosure (Tsai et al. 2011, Adjerid et al. 2013). This line of research would thus predict that raising awareness of the risks of disclosure would lead people to be less likely to disclose their private information. Because awareness of potential privacy risks increases people’s perceived risks of information disclosure, we would expect people to be less likely to disclose their private information. Accordingly, one would expect that educating people about privacy risks beforehand may help to reduce their willingness to disclose private information by preventing them from taking excessive privacy risks.

However, another stream of literature in other decision-making domains shows that interventions that help to increase people’s awareness of risks such as education do not often improve decision making (Thaler and Sunstein 2008). Studies have demonstrated that merely educating people about potential risks does not change behavior. For example, in the privacy domain, Buckman et al. (2019) show that salient privacy risk information does not alter people’s privacy valuation in a realistic website setting. In the health domain, education of risks often does not lead to behavior change (Nichols 1994, Kelly and Barker 2016). These interventions may even backfire: Brough et al. (2022) demonstrate that a salient privacy notice decreases customers registration rate, even though it ostensibly offers greater data protection. Also, as our neurophysiological findings in the present study reveal, people are sensitive to the intrusiveness of the information requested at the time of choice, which explains why education prior to the decision is futile. Accordingly, one would expect that educating people about privacy risks beforehand would not alter their information disclosure. Taken together, there is no consensus in the literature whether educating people about privacy risks has a major effect, something that we also empirically examine in our pilot study.

Prior studies have shown that people’s personality differences shape their privacy concerns, which in turn affect their online disclosure behaviors (Smith et al. 2011). Many individual differences have been examined in the literature, such as gender (Sheehan 1999), extroversion (Lu et al. 2004), social awareness (Dinev and Hart 2006), and intellect (Bansal et al. 2010). For instance, young and lesseducated people are less likely to be concerned about their privacy and are more likely to share private information online (Culnan 1995, Blank et al. 2014). Hence, people with diverse privacy preferences may have various weightings of benefits and risks during their decisionmaking process. If a person believes benefits are more important, the person may be more likely to disclose private information, in general. Otherwise, if the person believes risks are more salient, the person may be less likely to disclose private information in general. Hence, we anticipate that people’s weighting of the intrusiveness of risks will reduce their real-world information disclosure behavior based on their individual preferences, whereas the weighting of the attractiveness of benefits, also based on their individual preferences, will increase their real-world information disclosure behavior. In this paper, we confirm that respondents’ privacy preferences in our experimental task track their actual online disclosure of private information.

## 2.2. Pilot Study

We conduct a set of three studies: an initial pilot study, an fMRI study, and a behavioral experiment testing an intervention designed based on the fMRI results. The pilot study explores how people’s behavior in a laboratory task correlates with real-world online privacy behavior and examines how stressing the risks of sharing private information might affect people’s information disclosure behavior. In the pilot study (see online appendix), we manipulated privacy awareness to evaluate whether stressing the risks of information disclosure at the beginning of the study might help resolve the privacy paradox and change how people evaluate information about the risk of sharing their sensitive private information during their privacy calculus. Many efforts to help people disclose their private information in a manner that aligns with their reported privacy preferences focus on education (Thaler and Sunstein 2008, Tsai et al. 2011, Acquisti et al. 2017). By providing people with information regarding the potential risks, the hope is to increase their privacy concern and encourage them to better protect their private information. Moreover, the pilot study benchmarked how individual differences in the weighting of risks and benefits in the pilot experiment correlate with their actual privacy behaviors (e.g., disclosing personal information on Facebook), thereby establishing that decisions in the experimental task predict realworld privacy disclosures online to enhance our studies external validity.

Findings from the pilot study indicate that the weighting used when participants integrate the attractiveness of the benefits and the intrusiveness of the questions tracks their real-world online privacy behavior. Specifically, we observed a positive correlation between the effect of benefits on decision making in the task and the disclosure of their private information on Facebook $( r = 0 . 3 2 , ~ p < 0 . 0 0 5 ;$ Figure S2 in the online appendix), indicating that those who are more affected by the benefits of information disclosure in our experiment are also more likely to disclose their private information in real life. These pilot results indicate that the decisions made in the laboratory study predict real-world privacy disclosures online (Facebook), providing external validity for the task used in our research. Also, educating people about the risks of sharing private information does not alter their actual behavior. Thus, trying to protect people’s privacy by stressing the possible risks well in advance of the decision-making process does not affect their behavior. We therefore turned our attention to elucidating the neural processes underlying decisions about disclosing private information (Study 1). These neural insights were subsequently leveraged to develop and test a neurally informed behavioral intervention to help consumers protect their private information (Study 2).

## 2.3. Cognitive Neuroscience of Privacy

Cognitive neuroscience, as a discipline, focuses on understanding the neural correlates underlying psychological processes. Over the last 25 years, since the widespread adoption of neuroimaging tools, cognitive neuroscience has made marked progress in illuminating how the brain supports how people think, feel, or make decisions (Posner and DiGirolamo 2000, Mather et al. 2013). Neuroscience methods have often been used in the information systems (IS) literature (Dimoka et al. 2011, 2012; Dimoka 2012; Turel et al. 2014; Randolph and Pierquet 2015; Safi et al. 2015; Vance et al. 2018; Meservy et al. 2019). Studies using neuroimaging tools, such as fMRI and EEG, showed their potential for understanding how people process security messages (Anderson et al. 2015a, b, 2016), predicting security-related behaviors (Vance et al. 2014), and encouraging secure behavior in cyberspace (Warkentin et al. 2015). Notably, IS researchers (Randolph et al. 2006, Riedl et al. 2010) have touted the value of using neuroscience methods to create a safe and secure online environment, and many applications have appeared in the IS literature (Anderson et al. 2015b). Yet, the cognitive neuroscience literature has not examined how people make decisions regarding their privacy, and there are no studies on the underlying neural circuitry of privacy. However, given previous cognitive neuroscience findings, we expect decisions involving privacy to rely upon two primary neural networks in the brain.

First, there is a well-characterized network of brain regions involved in the processing of rewards. We posit that this network would also shape decisions regarding people’s privacy. Tradeoffs regarding privacy involve some rewards to people in exchange for sharing their information, such as faster access, financial payments, or personalized service. When weighing the potential benefits of sharing information, areas typically involved in processing rewards are likely engaged, such as the caudate nucleus and the ventromedial prefrontal cortex. The caudate nucleus has been implicated in the anticipation of rewards and benefits (Knutson et al. 2001, 2007), and the medial prefrontal cortex is involved in representing the subjective value of options (Daw et al. 2006, Rangel et al. 2008). Both regions therefore are putatively involved in representing and responding to value and rewards. In the context of decisions about privacy, these brain areas likely represent the attractive aspects of disclosing private information in exchange for certain rewards, such as loyalty rewards and other perks. Accordingly, these brain regions are likely to be activated when people make judgments about potentia benefits. In summary, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 1a. The activation of the brain’s value regions (e.g., caudate nucleus and medial prefrontal cortex) is positively associated with the perceived attractiveness of benefits.

Second, a network of brain areas that track potential risks and support harm avoidance is likely to be recruited when people make decisions about disclosing their private information. This brain network is typically activated in response to negative emotional events or stimuli (LeDoux 2000, Phelps 2006). This network centers on two key neural structures: the amygdala and the insula. The amygdala is a subcortical neural structure that has been implicated in learning about emotional events and avoiding potential harm (Kluver and Bucy 1937; Phelps and LeDoux 2005; Phelps 2006; Mobbs et al. 2007, 2009). The insula has also been implicated in representing information about risk (Preuschoff et al. 2008). As disclosing private information involves potential risks and harm arising from information disclosure, we anticipate that these brain regions will mobilize to represent concerns about these potential risks and help people recognize and avoid potential harm to them from a breach of their private information online. Our analytic neuroimaging approach will also allow us to detect additional brain regions that may represent this information beyond these two key regions identified in the literature. By integrating the privacy with the cognitive neuroscience literature, we propose the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 1b. The activation of the brain’s harm avoidance regions (e.g., amygdala and insula) is positively associated with intrusiveness.

Finally, our analytic approach enables us to examine how different regions across the brain support the final decision about whether to disclose private information. By characterizing the neural regions that track individual decisions to disclose private information, we can gain insights into the likely psychological mechanisms underlying those decisions. Illuminating these processes can help guide the design of neurally informed behavioral interventions to help consumers protect their private information.

Building on the findings from the pilot study, fMRI Study 1 identifies the neural mechanisms underlying privacy and uncovers how people weigh tradeoffs regarding privacy. The main goal of the fMRI study is to understand the neural processes underlying people’s decisions to disclose private information in order to inform the design of appropriate behavior interventions. To preview the findings from the fMRI study, we find a network of neural regions to be engaged when people were deciding whether to disclose personal information. These brain regions are putatively engaged when directing attention among competing representations (Mac-Donald et al. 2000, Botvinick et al. 2001, Corbetta et al. 2008). Drawing insights from Study 1, Study 2 designs and tests a novel behavioral intervention to protect people’s privacy. Specifically, Study 2 aims to design effective interventions that better align people’s behavior with their stated privacy concerns to help overcome the privacy paradox by drawing people’s attention on privacy risks at the time of making decisions. By directing attention to privacy concerns at the time of choice, this intervention leverages the psychological mechanisms uncovered in Study 1 to help people protect their privacy. In Study 2, we draw on insights from our neuro imaging findings (Study 1) to examine the effect of directing people’s attention on online information disclosing behaviors. In contrast to the intervention in the pilot, which merely informed consumers about privacy risks related to information disclosure, Study 2 was designed using the insights from Study 1 to alter peo ple’s psychological processing while making their privacy decisions.

## 3. Study 1

In the pilot study, we found that people integrate information about both the risks and benefits of information disclosure when deciding whether to share their private information. Specifically, the attractiveness of the benefits is positively associated with willingness to disclose pri vate information, while the intrusiveness of the questions is negatively associated with willingness to disclose private information<sup>1</sup> (online appendix). Our results are consistent with the theory of privacy calculus (Dinev and Hart 2006), where people’s decision making is based on a risk-benefit calculation. However, the psychological processes underlying this calculation remain unclear in the literature. In Study 1, we sought to identify the neural mechanisms underlying privacy and uncover how people weigh tradeoffs regarding their privacy. Specifically, we replicated the pilot study with all participants using identical experimental procedures while undergoing fMRI to identify both how privacy concerns are represented in the brain and the neural mechanisms underlying decisions to disclose private information.

Importantly, our design enables us to observe the extent to which these proposed brain networks, along with other brain areas, are engaged while people are making decisions whether to disclose their private information. That is, when people make tradeoffs about whether to disclose private information, we can distinctly detect whether the brain regions that track the attractiveness of the benefit offered, or the intrusiveness of the information requested, are engaged during their decision making. In our focal task, we separately identify these brain regions in localizer tasks. During these localizer tasks, people are only responding to the attractiveness of the benefits or the intrusiveness of the information. We then examine reactivation of these same regions when people are making tradeoffs about sharing their private information to receive certain benefits. The use of localizer tasks here avoids concerns regarding reverse inference, as the brain regions are localized in distinctly separate tasks. We intend to characterize the brain regions that are associated with people’s decisions to disclose their private information to identify the neural correlates of these privacy-related behaviors and inform potential behavioral interventions to effectively manage the disclosure of private information.

## 3.2. Study 1 Design and Procedure

In Study 1, participants provided informed consent, and all experimental procedures were approved by the University Institutional Review Board. Next, the participants complete a series of tasks that consisted of two phases. In Phase 1, participants rated the attractiveness of benefits and the intrusiveness of questions separately, with the order counterbalanced (Tables S1 and S2). For the attractiveness of benefits, participants were required to view 20 potential benefits of disclosing private information (e.g., “free shipping,” “personalized product recommendations”) to third parties and rate their attractiveness on a four-point Likert scale from “Not at all attractive” to “Extremely attractive.” For the intrusiveness questions, participants viewed 60 questions about their personal information (e.g., “What is your social security number?,” “Have you ever taken nude pictures of yourself?”) and rated how intrusive each question is on a four-point Likert scale from “Not at all intrusive” to “Extremely intrusive” (see the online appendix for stimuli). Figure 1 presents the interface of the experimental task in the pilot study and Study 1.

In Phase 2, on each of 120 trials, participants were presented with one of the intrusive questions and one of the corresponding benefits they previously rated (during Phase 1). Participants were asked to specify how willing they would be to honestly answer the question displayed to receive the benefit using a four-point Likert scale from “Not at all willing” to “Extremely willing” (As shown in Figure 1, Phase 2).<sup>2</sup> Participants were then thanked for their participation and debriefed about the experiment.

## 3.4. fMRI Analyses

For each participant, we collected both an initial structural image of the brain (to localize their anatomy) and a series of functional scans to measure brain activation during their responses in the experimental tasks. Functional and anatomical images were acquired with a 3.0-T Siemens Magnetom mMR scanner using an eight-channel head coil. High-resolution T1-weighted anatomical images were collected (TR � 1,600 ms; TE � 2.51 ms; FOV � 23.2 cm, slice thickness � 0.90 mm). Fortytwo contiguous axial slices covering the full brain were acquired along the AC-PC with a gradient-echo planar free induction decay (EPI-FID) sequence (T2\*weighted: FOV � 23.6 cm; slice thickness � 3.0 mm; TR � 2000 ms; TE � 20 ms).

The functional MRI data were analyzed using Statistical Parametric Mapping software (SPM8) (Friston et al. 1995). The first six acquisitions were discarded to allow for signal stabilization. Functional images for each participant were corrected for slice acquisition timing, realigned to correct for motion, and coregistered with the participant’s high-resolution structural scan. Due to excessive motion during scanning, fMRI data from six participants were excluded from analyses. Each participant’s bias-corrected high-resolution structural image was normalized to the Montreal Neurological Institute ICBM 152 template using unified segmentation (Ashburner and Friston 2005). The resulting transformation parameters were then applied to the functional images, which were spatially smoothed using a Gaussian kernel of full-width half-maximum 8.0 mm<sup>3</sup>. Functional im ages had a final spatial resolution of 3.0 mm × 3.0 mm × 3.0 mm. Analyses of fMRI data focused on two aims. First, we sought to identify the neural correlates of pri vacy concerns and the neural correlates of the attractive ness of the benefits of disclosure. Second, we sought to examine activation during decisions whether to disclose information to identify which regions were associated with willingness to disclose private information (privacy calculus). Functional images acquired while participants initially rated the intrusiveness of questions (Phase 1) were analyzed according to the assumptions of the gen eral linear model (GLM) with a focus on identifying the areas in the brain whose activation correlates with per ceived privacy intrusiveness. Each participant’s ratings of privacy intrusiveness during this phase were entered as a parametric regressor for each trial (with response time included as a regressor of no interest), and our anal ysis identified the brain areas whose activation covaried with perceived privacy intrusiveness. A similar analysis was enacted for functional images acquired while parti cipants initially rated the attractiveness of disclosure (Phase 1), with their ratings of attractiveness entered as a parametric regressor for each trial (and response time included as a regressor of no interest). Analyses of Phase 2 data leveraged the ratings from Phase 1 to identify not only brain regions whose activations tracked willingness to disclose personal information but that also repre sented the intrusiveness of the information and the bene fits of disclosure. To achieve this, functional images acquired while participants were rating their willingness to disclose private information (Phase 2) were analyzed such that for each trial, separate parametric regressors quantified their willingness to disclose, their earlier rating of the attractiveness of the benefit, and also their earlier rating of the intrusiveness of the information requested (with response time included as a regressor of no inter est). This approach allowed us to identify the neural signals related separately to each participant’s perception of the attractiveness of the benefit, the question’s perceived intrusiveness, and their willingness to disclose private information. SnPM (Winkler et al. 2014) was used to com pute a combined cluster-extent and voxel-height thresh old to correct for multiple comparisons. ## 3.6. Study 1 Discussion

Taken together, the neuroimaging results demonstrate that people do encode the attractiveness of the benefits offered and the intrusiveness of the questions asked. In Study 2, we seek to develop a novel, attention-based intervention based on the fMRI findings of Study 1, and we posit whether it can influence people’s decisions whether to disclose their private information. We first review the literature on choice architecture, then we present our experiment testing our neurally and psychologically informed behavioral intervention.

## 4. Choice Architecture and Privacy

Choice architecture has recently gained interest from both academic researchers and policy makers (Thaler and Sunstein 2008, Johnson 2022). Choice architecture reflects a growing recognition that how options are presented encourages certain behaviors. Just as the design of a building shapes the behavior of its inhabitants (e.g., placement of doors, bathrooms, and stairways), the design of how choices are presented shapes behavior. Choice architecture can steer people toward choices that they––or society––prefer without restricting their freedom (Thaler and Sunstein 2008), representing a soft-paternalistic approach. The efficacy of choice architecture has been shown across a wide range of decision contexts. For example, programs that precommit future wage raises to tax-advantaged savings accounts increase employee savings for retirement (Benartzi and Thaler 2004), establishing environmentally-friendly options as the default increases their adoption (Dinner et al. 2011), and making decisions to become an organ donor as optout rather than opt-in almost doubles the rate of organ donation (Johnson and Goldstein 2003). Choice architecture is also gaining acceptance by governments both in the United States (White House 2014a, b) and abroad (Science and Technology Committee 2011).

With respect to privacy, the privacy paradox points to a disconnect between how people self-report to desire to behave vs. their actual observed behavior (Awad and Krishnan 2006). As in other domains where people’s preferences deviate from their behavior (e.g., losing weight, saving for retirement, exercising), choice architecture interventions may encourage people to make choices that align with their own stated preferences and goals. As most people claim to desire more privacy and an environment that protects people’s privacy is in society’s best interests, choice architecture interventions are a fruitful avenue to promote consumer welfare in general and help people protect their privacy in particular.

One challenge in designing choice architecture interventions is there are numerous methods to impinge upon people’s behavior, presenting myriad options for those seeking to promote their welfare. For example, the privacy literature has proposed numerous behavioral interventions to nudge individuals toward more beneficial choices (Acquisti et al. 2017), such as the presentation of privacy-related information (Wang et al. 2014, Balebako et al. 2015), default choices (Leon et al. 2012), and incentives (Grossklags and Acquisti 2007). However, leveraging insights from the underlying neural and psychological processes guiding behavior can direct choice architects to behavioral interventions that are more likely to have a beneficial impact on desired behaviors, helping both rule out options that are unlikely to be effective and identify promising means of altering behavior that might otherwise be overlooked.

The fMRI findings from Study 1 point to a key promising means to alter consumers’ behavior: directing their attention. Study 1 reveals that a frontoparietal network that has been implicated in directing people’s attention (Corbetta and Shulman 2002) is engaged during decisions whether to disclose one’s private information. However, these brain regions are also involved in other processes, and to avoid concerns related to reverse inference, we need to test this psychological account experimentally. Importantly, identifying that this psychological process is essential to privacy decisions enables us to design a neurally informed interven tion to help protect people’s privacy. Specifically, we build on two key attention theories. The first, bounded attention, asserts that humans’ cognitive capacities are limited (Simon 1955). Rather than optimizing mental computations, the human brain relies on limited access to information and capacity to hold that information in mind during decision making. The second, adjacency, states that people use information mentally close to when they acquire that information attentionally (Costa-Gomes et al. 2001). For example, when you look at a piece of information, such as an intrusive personal detail solicited by a website, you rapidly consider your reaction to that information and immediately include it into your privacy calculus. However, if you shift your attention elsewhere, this information may exert less influence.

These two theoretical principles, combined with the fMRI findings in Study 1, suggest a key means of shaping people’s privacy calculus and, potentially, their behavior: altering the timing of the information that is provided. By presenting information that is key to protecting privacy closer to the time of the decision, an intervention can take advantage of both adjacency and bounded attention to ensure that this key information is being attended to at the time of decision. Critically, information is processed very rapidly by the brain, potentially updating decision processes within one second of appearing (Corbetta and Shulman 2002). Building on the fMRI findings from Study 1, we therefore theorize that manipulating the presentation timing of information regarding intrusiveness would influence people’s choices, making them more likely to incorporate this information into their decision and discouraging the disclosure of private information. Drawing from attention theories, we theorize that presenting the intrusive elements salient within a second from the time of choice helps to direct people’s attention to the privacy risks. This manipulation activates the brain areas involved in the privacy calculus (Hypothesis 1), bringing privacy risks to the forefront at the time of choice and thereby discouraging people from disclosing their personal information. In sum, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 2. Presenting information about intrusive privacy elements one second after the benefits information and at the time of choice will diminish people’s willingness to disclose their personal information.

This neurally and psychologically informed intervention in Hypothesis 2 departs from previous research in two key ways. First, it focuses on the timing of information provision, unlike prior work that has focused on altering the information provided. For instance, Tsai et al. (2011) show that people are more likely to purchase products from websites with annotated privacy icons. However, our study does not seek to manipulate the amount of information provided to individuals or its overall accessibility but rather focuses on the timing by which the exact same information is presented instead. Second, the proposed timing intervention involves a very small shift in the presentation of information: just a one-second difference between the elements presented. Prior work (Table S7) has typically manipulated the timing of information at longer time scales, such as presenting privacy notices in the app store versus during app use (Balebako et al. 2015), or distracting people between when they see privacy relevant information and make privacy decisions (Adjerid et al. 2013). These temporal changes happen on the scale of minutes (Table S7), whereas our manipulation makes changes in presentation of only one second. We adopt this shorter timescale based on the understanding of how information is processed by the brain, which indicates that even very small changes in timing can advantage the use of information during decision making. If competing attention between the attractiveness of the benefit and the intrusiveness of the question is at the heart of how people make choices about sharing their private information, as the fMRI findings from Study 1 suggest, then manipulating the timing of when the intrusive elements are presented should increase their influence on decisions, therein altering the privacy calculus and discouraging disclosure. The proposed manipulation that shifts the attention to intrusive elements at the time of choice could thus alter the privacy calculus to be more aligned with protecting private information, thereby encouraging people to protect their privacy (Hypothesis 2).

## 5.2. Study 2 Design and Procedure

Study 2 generally followed the same experimental procedures as the pilot and fMRI Study 1, with one important exception. Participants were randomly assigned to one of two conditions for the completion of Phase 2. All participants first completed one run of the decisionmaking task in which the benefits of the disclosure to intrusive questions appeared simultaneously, as in our prior experiments (pilot and Study 1). During the second run of the task, we manipulated the relative timing of the presentation of these two pieces of information.

In the Question Salient condition (n � 58), the benefit of disclosure appeared first on the screen for one second before the intrusive question appeared along with the response scale. In the Benefit Salient condition $( n = 5 7 ) .$ the intrusiveness question appeared first on the screen for one second before the benefit of disclosure appeared along with the response scale. This manipulation should increase the focus on the second piece of information presented, because it appears along with the rating scale and should attract the participants’ attention with its later, more salient presentation. We analyzed the data in a similar fashion to the pilot study and Study 1, calculating the extent to which the attractiveness ratings of the benefits and the intrusiveness ratings of the questions from Phase 1 influenced the participants’ decisions regarding whether to disclose their personal information in Phase 2 (see earlier regression equations).

## 5.4. Study 2 Discussion

This study tested a novel, neurally informed intervention that was designed based on the fMRI findings from Study 1. We demonstrate that manipulating the timing of when information is presented alters people’s decisions to disclose their private information. Specifically, presenting the question one second after the benefit in order to appear precisely at the time of choice directs attention to the intrusiveness of the information requested, encouraging people to protect their private information. Stated in a different way, making the private information requested more salient resulted in people weighing the intrusiveness of the information solicited more heavily in their decision to disclose, thus shifting the privacy calculus and overcoming the privacy paradox by encouraging people to behave more in line with their privacy concerns. The proposed behavioral intervention occurs within a second from when people are making their decision, compared with other interventions, such as education, which take place in advance of their decision making.

## 6. General Discussion

## 6.1. Key Findings

In a set of three experiments, our results show that people integrate information about both risks and benefits when deciding whether to share their private information or not. We verified that the attractiveness of benefits is positively associated with, while the intrusiveness of privacy questions is negatively linked to, the willingness to disclose private information. Moreover, we found people’s differences in weighing risks and benefits correlate to their actual privacy behaviors (i.e., disclosing their personal information on Facebook). Importantly, from a practical standpoint, we found a significant positive correlation between people’s weighting of benefits and the pieces of private information actually disclosed on Facebook in their real life. The results indicate that participants’ decisions in the laboratory experiments did predict their privacy disclosures in real life, thus providing external validity for the experimental task used in Stud ies 1 and 2.

In fMRI Study 1, we showed that attractiveness judgments about the benefits activate the brain’s value regions, specifically the medial prefrontal cortex and caudate nucleus. In contrast, we showed that privacy concerns elicit activation in brain areas associated with threat and harm avoidance: anterior insula and amyg dala. Thus, this set of brain areas appears to be activated by privacy concerns. Similar activation due to privacy concerns is also observed in the lateral prefrontal cortex, parietal cortex, and TPJ. These brain regions are impli cated in judging between conflicting responses, assigning value, capturing decision conflict, and directing attention. Importantly, these same brain areas were also activated later when people were deciding whether they would be willing to disclose their private information in order to receive a benefit (Phase 2). This key finding indicates that people are sensitive to the intrusiveness of the information requested, and this process is captured in the brain (Study 1), specifically at the time of choice, explaining why education prior to the decision-making process may not work (as shown in our pilot study).

This key neural finding is what inspired our behavioral intervention in Study 2. By directing people’s attention to the intrusiveness of the information requested at the time of their decision, these same brain regions should be even more engaged (versus regions associated with benefits that rewards activate) and thus encourage decisions to protect private information. Notably, in Phase 2 of fMRI Study 1, we did not identify any significant activation that tracked the attractiveness of the benefits in the brain’s reward areas (medial prefrontal cortex and caudate nucleus), implying that signals related to the benefits are not substantially rerepresented in the brain when people are considering disclosing their private information.

However, we observed that brain activation in areas associated with integrating information and weighing tradeoffs predicted people’s decisions to disclose their private information. These brain regions appear to be critical for integrating information about the benefits and the risks of disclosure, likely related to an internal, neural privacy calculus. This finding elucidates the correlates of the elusive “privacy calculus” in the brain. This network of regions includes aspects of lateral prefrontal cortex that are implicated in selecting between competing goals or actions (MacDonald et al. 2000, Botvinick et al. 2001). This network also encompasses brain regions of both the lateral prefrontal and parietal cortex that are involved in directing attention (Corbetta et al. 2008). This finding suggests people feel conflicted when making privacy decisions, and that attention may play a role in shaping people’s decisions whether to disclose private information. In sum, our findings delineate the neural processes of the privacy calculus using fMRI, and they offer insights into the underlying processes that shape people’s decisions about their privacy. To our knowledge, this is the first neuroscience research to examine how privacy is captured in the brain, and how the privacy calculus is mapped into specific brain areas of rewards vs. harm avoidance, adding to the neuroscience literature.

Given the fMRI results of Study 1, and especially how directing attention to privacy risks may direct people’s decisions to disclose their private information, we designed Study 2, where we found that directing people’s attention to a given factor (specifically privacy risks versus rewards that marketers want to emphasize) can shift the balance in favor of that factor at the time of decision. In contrast to the intervention from the privacy literature tested in the pilot study (educating people about general privacy risks associated with information disclosure), our behavioral intervention sought to alter people’s psychological processing at the exact time when making their decisions about disclosing private information. The results show that using behavioral interventions that enhance the person’s attention to intrusive elements may increase their perceived risks and encourage people to protect their privacy. Our behavioral intervention to make the intrusive questions more salient at the time of decision, guided by the fMRI study that showed that these questions activated the brain’s avoidance areas, was successful in nudging participants to weigh the intrusiveness of the question more in the judgments of whether to share their private information.

## 6.2. Contributions and Implications for Theory

Our study makes three key contributions. First, our study contributes to the privacy literature by identifying the neural correlates underlying people’s decisions to disclose their private information. Several theories on the antecedents of privacy behaviors have been proposed in the literature, including the privacy calculus and the privacy paradox. Our study informs these theories by understanding the underlying neural processes of privacy using neuroscience methods. We identify a network of neural regions related to privacy concerns by examining how people make decisions regarding their privacy. Specifically, we find that two brain areas were activated that positively correlated with people’s selfreported privacy concerns—anterior insula and amygdala. The fMRI results suggest that the intrusiveness of the privacy-sensitive questions appears to be represented neurally, whereas the attractiveness of the benefits is not. These neurophysiological findings inform privacy calculus theory by elucidating the neural correlates of the privacy calculus (that is, where the privacy calculus maps onto the brain), showing that people feel conflicted when making privacy decisions. Critically, we neurally show that presenting people with information in a manner that directs their attention to the intrusiveness of the information requested at the time of choice nudges them to protect their privacy.

Second, prior literature (Thaler and Sunstein 2008 Acquisti et al. 2017) has shown behavioral interventions do encourage people to behave in a way that aligns with their self-reported preferences. Thus, such appropriate privacy-related behaviors could benefit both individuals and society by creating a safe online environment. Along this vein, in this study, drawing on the results from the fMRI study, we design and tes a novel, neurally informed behavioral intervention that encourages people to protect their privacy. Specifically, we found that employing manipulations that focus on changing people’s attention to intrusive elements dur ing the time of their decision-making shifts their pri vacy calculus toward the privacy risks and encourages people to protect their private information. We therefore contribute to the privacy literature by designing a practical behavioral intervention, guided by both our neuroscience results and privacy theories, that aligns with people’s actual behavior and their stated privacy concerns. We demonstrate the efficacy of the behaviora intervention that shapes people’s attention on the pri vacy risks at the time of their decision to disclose thei private information, versus relying on general educa tion beforehand, or asking people about their privacy concerns well before or after they make a decision, thus implying that directing people’s attention to the intrusiveness at the exact time of their decision whether to disclose their private information actually helps people protect their privacy. Our behavioral intervention provides practical guidance to the privacy literature by focusing on altering the timing of when the information is shown instead of the type or quality of information. Our behavioral intervention also helps people to overcome their tendency to take excessive risks with thei private information in exchange for often meager re wards (typically highlighted by marketers at the time of their decision) to disclose sensitive private information to enjoy these benefits. Finally, our study also contributes to the choice architecture literature by designing a neurally informed intervention that helps people to pro tect their privacy by stressing the privacy risks at the time of decision. Accordingly, this research could inform the design of other choice architecture interventions by leveraging the brain’s underlying functionality and b manipulating the timing of when the information is presented.

Third, our study makes a theoretical contribution to the privacy paradox by uncovering how neural processes shape decisions about whether to disclose their private information. The timing of the intervention (within one second from the time of choice) may elucidate the underpinnings of the elusive privacy paradox, which seems to emerge because people’s privacy preferences are typically solicited at a much different time than their actual information disclosure behavior. This key finding about the importance of the precise timing of the decision in privacy-related decisions helps shed light on the privacy paradox and helps explains why educating people about privacy risks, which typically happens well before the privacy decision, has little or no effect at the time of their decision to disclose their private information. We also neurally explain the privacy paradox by showing that people’s privacy behaviors differ from their self-reported sense of the importance of their privacy because of how attention is oriented during their decision making in the brain versus selfreporting their privacy preferences well before or well after their privacy choice. We theorize that making privacy risks salient at the time of choice is what matters versus education before the time of choice or by asking them to self-report how important their privacy is. Specifically, directing people’s attention to the privacy risks precisely at the time of choice ensures that the privacy risks become salient in people’s privacy calculus, thus helping trump the rewards that typically dominate the decision process in practice.

## 6.3. Contributions and Implications for Practice, Policy, and Research Methodology

Our research also has implications for practice, policy, and research methodology. For individuals, our study prescribes practical interventions to protect their private information. When making decisions about disclosing private information, individuals should focus more on the intrusiveness of the information required by marketers or third parties, and consider the corresponding risks associated with the privacy disclosure behavior at the time they are making their decision. Essentially, this attentional redeployment would help people to align their behavior with their privacy concerns and overcome the privacy paradox. Meanwhile, our study shows that simply educating people about the risks of disclosing private information and making them salient outside the decision-making process does not help to protect individuals’ privacy, and emphasis should be paid at the time of the decision by shifting attention to the focal privacy risks.

For public policy, our study informs policymakers to design effective regulations and policies to protect people’s privacy online. Our results show that shifting attention during the actual time of choice in the decisionmaking process is an effective behavioral intervention to help people protect their privacy, while behavioral interventions that rely solely on educating people in general, such as privacy practicums and privacy statements are unlikely to significantly shape safe privacy practices. Policymakers could regulate marketers and third parties to ensure that individuals focus on the privacy intrusiveness of the requested information during the time of choice in consumers’ decision-making process. For instance, many marketers stress the benefits instead of the risks when soliciting customers’ private information online. Accordingly, policymakers could design policies that regulate marketers to show the potential risks of information disclosure along with the proposed benefits, specifically the risks after the benefits, to increase customers’ attention on the potential risks at the time of their privacy-related decision. Collecting fewer consumer data may also benefit firms as they have less sensitive data they need to store and secure.

For methodology, capturing people’s privacy concerns using neuroscience methods help overcome concerns of self-reported measures and helps scholars develop richer theories to predict people’s behavior. The privacy literature consistently shows that people generally self-report their privacy to be important to them (Barnes 2006, Debatin et al. 2009); yet, their actual behavior in the form of disclosure of private information significantly differs from their self-reported privacy concerns (Acquisti and Gross 2006, Youn and Hall 2008, Debatin et al. 2009). This dissociation between self-reported privacy concerns and people’s actual behavior is referred to as the privacy paradox, which we sought to explain with our study. Our approach, using brain data to directly measure privacy concerns, at the time of decision, provides us with more direct complementary measures, which help complement traditional self-reported measures.

## 6.4. Limitations and Suggestions for Future Research

Our study also has limitations that create opportunities for future research. First, besides the current behavioral intervention presented in the paper, future studies could design other behavioral interventions to protect consumers’ privacy online. Drawing insights from the neu roimaging experiment, our study only examines the effects of one behavioral intervention that shifts people’s attention to privacy risks at the time of the decision. Future research could leverage the neural insights to design other effective behavioral interventions to enhance people’s privacy. In addition, our studies’ context focuses on social media sites; future studies could explore the effectiveness of the behavioral intervention in other contexts (e.g., online shopping websites). Our study also focuses on responses from young adults rather than examining a broad age range. However, this population is of particular interest, given 84% of 18- to 29-year-old people use social media and many advertisers seek to target this key demographic (Pew Research Center 2021).

Second, besides privacy protection, future studies could use our blueprint to design other behavioral interventions in other contexts, such as cybersecurity and public health. Our research provides a way to use neural methods to guide the design of behavioral interventions based on the underlying brain functionality. Future researchers could apply the key finding of stressing the corresponding risks at the time of the decision to other contexts, plus identify other important insights based on neuroimaging data.

Third, to use neuroimaging (fMRI) we needed to develop a simple set of privacy decisions that could be repeated many times by participants. However, future research could explore other types of one-time choices for participants. For example, more detail about the advantages of information disclosure could be given to participants, especially at the time of their decision, or additional information about the protections in place, or lack thereof, to safeguard the information could be revealed at the time of decision.

Finally, behavioral parameters estimated from participants’ choices in the laboratory experiment predicted their real-world online disclosures of private information, demonstrating the external validity of our study by showing that the weighting of the intrusiveness of the risks and the attractiveness of benefits track realworld online behavior. Although this finding reinforces our study’s generalizability by creating a link between the neural activation patterns in Study 1 and actual observed behaviors in real life, future research could examine other behaviors in other privacy contexts using a more general population.

## 6.5. Conclusion

Privacy is touted as one of the major societal challenges of the information age and online markets (Smith et al. 2011). Data mining and machine learning methods that examine large amounts of data to identify patterns about individual behaviors pose challenges to people’s privacy and raise privacy concerns. Our research seeks to enhance the trustworthiness of cyberspace by uncovering the neural correlates of privacy concerns and designing and testing behavioral interventions to help people make better choices about their privacy. Using a novel neuroscientific approach, we were able to identify the brain areas associated with the attractiveness of benefits, the intrusiveness of questions related to people’s privacy, and the decision to disclose private information. These results help us better understand people’s decision-making processes of information disclosure and provide more accurate and direct measures for privacy concerns that may complement self-reported measures in the privacy literature. Most importantly, the insights from the neuroimaging study guided the design of behavioral interventions that help people overcome their tendency to take excessive risks with their private information in exchange for meager rewards, specifically stressing privacy risks at precisely the time of the decision, as suggested by the fMRI findings and the neuroscience literature. We hope that the combination of neurophysiological with behavioral experiments undertaken in this research could be used as a basis to explore effective designs of novel behavioral interventions that rely on the brain’s functionality and precise timing of when decisions are made in the brain, and the proposed multimethodological approach that relates the neural mechanisms to real-life behavioral interventions could be applied to other contexts, such as security, fraud protection, financial planning and retirement, philanthropy, exercising, and pursuing healthy lifestyle choices.
