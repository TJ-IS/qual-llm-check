Abstract. Social media platforms, such as Facebook, often display assertive call-toaction ads that encourage direct conversion actions, such as purchasing a product or installing an app. Additionally, these ads can display popularity signals (i.e., “likes”) and social endorsement from friends (i.e., friends’ “likes”). We examine the effectiveness of displaying these different signals on such ads in generating clicks through field tests conducted on Facebook. We collaborated with a mobile app company and conducted a call-to-action ad campaign on Facebook, targeting unique user groups with this type of ads for a mobile app. Our findings reveal that the overall number of “likes” associated with the ad does not impact the user’s decision to click the ad. Additionally, ads endorsed by friends have a lower clicking performance compared with those without such endorsement. Further analyses based on randomized laboratory studies and additional field tests using different products and apps confirm these results and demonstrate that the presence of these cues activates users’ persuasion knowledge of assertive call-to-action ads, resulting in a lower click performance. Furthermore, the negative impact of social cues is particularly pronounced when there is low similarity between users and their friends. These results have significant implications for the design of assertive call-to-action ads on social media platforms.

## Introduction

Social media platforms, such as Facebook, manage users’ social graphs, which can be used to target users and spread content. To monetize these social networks, social media platforms also incorporate ads into users’ newsfeeds. Firms typically promote their content on these platforms through informational ads, which display ad content (including images and text) alongside social cues and may also feature links to landing pages for further information, thereby enhancing brand awareness. Additionally, firms can utilize certain call-to-action (CTA) ads that ask users to take direct conversion actions,<sup>1</sup> such as purchasing a product or installing an app.<sup>2</sup> These CTA ads are assertive and are readily available on social media platforms.<sup>3</sup> The use of assertive language is notably prevalent in media (Zemack-Rugar et al. 2017),<sup>4</sup> and assertive CTA 5 ads are commonly used on social media platforms. However, their effectiveness has not been evaluated. This study aims to fill this gap by investigating the impact of popularity cues, such as “likes,” and peer endorsements, such as friends’ “likes,” on the performance of assertive CTA ads.

Assertive CTA ads are appealing to advertisers as they are persuasive and force users to take immediate conversion actions. However, this kind of persuasion is known to trigger a negative response (Miller et al. 2007, Kronrod et al. 2012, Zemack-Rugar et al. 2017, Chen et al. 2020). Similar to other social media advertisements, CTA ads on social media platforms often showcase their popularity by highlighting the number of users who have expressed their approval through “likes.” Additionally, many social media platforms also provide peer endorsement information by showing the friends of the targeted user who have also “liked” the ad. On the one hand, popularity cues can capture user attention and serve as quality signals for the advertised product, ultimately leading to higher user response and increased sales; similarly, peer endorsements have the potential to enhance ad performance by leveraging social influence. On the other hand, given the negative perception of assertive ads, prominently displaying popularity cues and peer endorsements on the ad may be interpreted as the advertiser’s attempt at manipulation. In such cases, they could potentially harm the ad’s performance. Therefore, it is crucial to investigate whether popularity cues and social endorsements have any impact on the targeted users of assertive CTA ads.

To examine the effectiveness of popularity cues and peer endorsements, our study evaluates the following questions. (1) Do the popularity cues displayed alongside an assertive CTA ad influence targeted users decisions to click the ad? (2) Do peer endorsements influence users’ decisions to click an assertive CTA ad? We answer these questions using data sets from field tests conducted on Facebook in collaboration with a mobile app company that advertises on Facebook as well as four randomized studies conducted on Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk) and Prolific.

In the field test, we conduct a mobile app install ad campaign on Facebook for a five-week period targeting 16 different user groups with varying levels of “likes” and friends’ “likes.” We use the standard interface provided by the Facebook advertiser platform to target users and draw inference from the aggregate performance data typically received by millions of Facebook advertisers as part of their ad campaigns. Note that Facebook prioritizes content delivery to users who are more likely to respond (Lee et al. 2018). To address such potential selection issues, we implement a random bidding procedure and employ an instrumental variable (IV) approach to address endogeneity related to popularity cues. We also validate our findings with an additional instrument to mitigate selection bias for peer endorsement signals and further validate our results with an ad campaign for a different app.

Our empirical results from the Facebook field tests show that the popularity cues associated with Facebook ads are ineffective in leading to clicks. In addition, we find that the presence of peer endorsements has a negative effect on users’ clicking decisions. To further investigate the underlying mechanism, we conduct a series of randomized studies, where we exogenously manipulate the display of popularity cues and peer endorsements. Specifically, in a pilot study (Study A), we verify the feasibility of utilizing the name generator technique (Burt 1984) to acquire subjects’ social network information; in the subsequent studies, we design a web app simulating the Facebook interface, acquire subjects’ friend information through the name generator approach, and observe subjects’ ad browsing behaviors. Across these studies, we find that popularity cues increase the click performance for informational ads but not assertive CTA ads (Study 1); for assertive CTA ads, we also find that popularity cues increase reactance and the ad strategy perception (Study 2). Next, we find that peer endorsements increase reactance and decrease click-through rate for assertive CTA ads but not informational ads (Study 1) and that the negative reaction is likely because of activation of subjects’ persuasion knowledge as the endorsement credibility decreased (Study 2). We also rule out an alternative explanation related to how friends’ similarity levels were assessed (Study B).

The laboratory experiment results validate the field data analyses as the treatment assignment is completely random and allows us to infer the plausible mechanism. (1) Subjects view popularity cues as part of the ad strategy, and this leads to higher reactance and lower click performance. (2) Subjects associate peer endorsements with lower credibility, which increases the reactance and lowers the click performance. (3) Peer endorsements from similar friends increase the credibility and improve the click performance.

Our research makes several contributions. We add to the advertising literature focusing on the user response to assertive ads. Previous studies have focused on differences between assertive and regular ads and the role of factors, such as customer characteristics (Zemack-Rugar et al. 2017) and type of products (Wang and Zhang 2020), on the response to such ads. We extend this stream of literature by showing how the word-ofmouth (WOM) cues on such ads can influence their performance.

We also contribute to the existing social media literature by informing research on the importance of context in harnessing the value of popularity cues and peer endorsement signals. In the context of ads, we show that these signals may not work when there are specific calls to action for installs or purchases because the assertive language can trigger a negative response. In contrast, previous research has shown a positive effect of peer endorsement on informational ads (Bakshy et al. 2012, Huang et al. 2020). Finally, we add to the WOM literature by demonstrating the role of popularity cues in the context of ads. Previous research (Liu 2006, Chen et al. 2011, Kumar et al. 2016) has suggested that WOM signals on organic content increase sales performance. We show that popularity cues associated with CTA ads that are assertive do not increase their performance.

Our research provides managerial implications to social media platforms and advertisers relying on popularity cues and peer endorsements to promote content and enable purchases using assertive CTA ads. Specifically, our results show that popularity cues are inadequate to get consumer traffic for such ads. Similarly, peer endorsements from dissimilar friends can have a negative effect on the clicking of these ads. From a practitioner’s perspective, our results suggest that advertisers should discount such metrics in the evalua tion of their ad performance for assertive CTA ads. This insight is also crucial for social media platforms, like Facebook, which are focused on enhancing the sales outcomes for their advertising partners.

## Related Literature and Theoretical Background

Call-to-Action Ads, Assertiveness, and Reactance Call-to-action ads are designed to persuade users to make purchases, and they often come across as assertive. Such assertive ads are known to generate reactance (Zemack-Rugar et al. 2017) as they create pressure for consumers to comply without choice, and lack of choice can lead to reactance (Shen 2015). Several studies have explored the effectiveness of assertive language in messages within various contexts and among different customer segments. For instance, Miller et al. (2007) discover that controlling language has negative outcome for health messages. Kronrod et al. (2012) demonstrate that assertive language diminishes compliance in environmental messages, particularly when users lack initial conviction. Zemack-Rugar et al. (2017) reveal that assertive ads generate stronger negative reactions from committed customers because of the increased pressure to comply. These assertive messages are also widely adopted by social media platforms in the form of CTA ads. Chen et al. (2020) find that CTA ads on Facebook can lead to recognition of advertising intent and lower intention to share, highlighting the presence of heterogeneity among consumers. Social media platforms often accompany such ads with user-generated signals, such as “likes” and peer endorsements, whose impact on ad performance remains unclear. This research aims to bridge this gap by examining the influence of these signals on assertive CTA ads on social media.

## Effects of Popularity Cues and Peer Endorsements on Organic Content

The extant WOM literature shows that an individual’s decision is influenced by the opinions and preferences of other consumers (e.g., Godes and Mayzlin 2004, Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006, Liu 2006, Dellarocas et al. 2007, Duan et al. 2008). Such WOM can enhance consideration (Gupta and Harris 2010), with WOM from friends being deemed more trustworthy than regular WOM (Rogers 1995). Many studies have found such online social influence because of peer endorsement to play a role in user response when the content is spread organically by the users. These include the adoption of videos (Susarla et al. 2012, Yoganarasimhan 2012), social networking sites (Katona et al. 2011), app installations (Aral and Walker 2011), restaurant visits (Shi and Whinston 2013), paid subscriptions (Bapna and Umyarov 2015), and adoption of music (Dewan et al. 2017).

## Effects of Popularity Cues and Peer Endorsements on Social Media Ads

Some studies have assessed the effect of peer endorsements on Facebook informational ads aimed at creating awareness. These ads typically display ad content (image and text) and may include links or buttons for obtaining more information. A Facebook study (Bakshy et al. 2012) demonstrates the efficacy of peer endorsement information in generating more clicks. Huang et al. (2020) find that peer endorsement enhances click performance across a variety of product categories in display ads. Tucker (2016) uses a Facebook ad cam paign data set of a charity organization to show that social display ads perform better than regular display ads. Despite the concern that ads explicitly promoting social influence could be less effective in generating clicks and subscriptions to newsfeed, these studies confirm that the WOM effect of peer endorsements on display ads mirrors organic content. Nevertheless, these studies focus on informational ads designed for content awareness, where users are not necessarily exposed to specific calls to action for installation or purchase. Furthermore, these studies do not delve into the impact of popularity cues on display ad performance.

## Hypothesis

Building on the established effects of WOM, it is reasonable to anticipate that popularity cues and social endorsements would capture users’ attention and encourage engagement with CTA ads. However, the perception of these cues by users is crucial. Colicev et al. (2019) find that user-generated content on social media primarily aids in creating awareness rather than persuasion. Because CTA ads aim to persuade users to take action, user-generated signals, such as “likes,” may prove inef fective. Additionally, the assertiveness of CTA messages may activate persuasion knowledge, potentially causing users to view the ad information negatively (Campbel and Kirmani 2000). Consequently, users may perceive the ad as manipulative and reflecting the advertiser’s intent (Campbell 1995), which could trigger reactance behavior (e.g., Clee and Wicklund 1980, Brehm 1989). In the context of social media, Boerman et al. (2017) demonstrate that celebrity endorsements on sponsored content perform poorly because of persuasion knowledge. Similarly, with assertive CTA ads, the activation of persuasion knowledge may lead users to perceive popularity cues and peer endorsements as manipulative.

Persuasion can result in a loss of freedom (Miller 2013), and reactance may be further exacerbated with this lack of freedom (Shen 2015). Although peer endorsements are typically associated with the creation of conformity pressures, they tend to be effective in the context of organic content and informational advertisements. However, it is conceivable that assertive CTA ads, by explicitly accentuating this pressure, might evoke a negative response because of the perceived encroachment on individual freedom. Hence, displaying peer endorsements, such as friends’ “likes,” on assertive CTA ads may increase reactance because of perceived pressure to comply. Another potential consequence is the triggering of privacy concerns among users when they encounter peer endorsements. Tucker (2014) finds that insufficient privacy policies led to negative reactance to Facebook ads, an issue attributed to a sense of limited control. Similarly, the presence of peer endorsements in assertive CTA ads could provoke negative reactance and hamper the performance of such ads as users may perceive them as invasive and an unauthorized use of their personal information.

In summary, although prior literature suggests that popularity cues and peer endorsements on ads can positively influence user engagement, their effectiveness on the performance of assertive CTA ads remains uncertain. As the true impact is driven by how users perceive these signals and can point in either direction, we present the following competing hypotheses.

Hypothesis 1a. Popularity cues and peer endorsements have a positive effect on the click performance of assertive CTA ads.

Hypothesis 1b. Popularity cues and peer endorsements have a negative effect on the click performance of assertive CTA ads.

## Facebook Field Test Design and Data Description

We conducted a field test in collaboration with a technology company in India to assess how popularity cues, represented by “likes,” and peer endorsements, represented by friends’ “likes,” affect the performance of Facebook’s “install now” ads for app downloads. We targeted users with these ads and explored the impact of “likes” and friends’ “likes” on ad clicks.

Facebook’s “install now” ads are CTA ads displayed in users’ newsfeeds on mobile devices, and they prompt users to install mobile apps. Notice that at the time of our field test, mobile install ads displayed the total number of Facebook users who have “liked” the ads. Clicking will take them to the app’s install page on the appropriate platform, such as Apple’s App Store or Google’s Play Store, and users can decide if they want to install the app. Facebook excludes users who have already installed the app from the campaign.

## Identification and Estimation

Effect of Popularity Cues. We assessed the impact of popularity cues based on cumulative “likes” on the click performance. Users can “like” ads when they see them, and Facebook combines these “likes” across all target groups and displays the ad with the total count to subsequent users. As the “likes” increase over time, users targeted at different times see varying counts. In addition, user quality and ad performance may relate to popularity as Facebook’s ad placement is determined by bids and expected performance, potentially influencing user selection. However, recall that we specified randomly generated bids for each of our 16 target groups on an hourly basis, making the selected users quality less likely to be correlated across groups with cumulative likes.

To further alleviate any remaining endogeneity concern because of Facebook’s targeting algorithm, we adopted an instrumental variable approach where we used the sum of bids across different ad groups to instrument for cumulative “likes” for each ad for each time period. We subsequently used these estimated “likes” up to the prior period in our click model. Note that we used a single post that appeared in several identical ads and accrued “likes” from all these ads. As the random bid for each ad generates “likes,” a higher sum of bids would suggest higher overall bids across all ad groups, which should lead to more impressions and more “likes” in that period. Further, as the individual bids are random, the sum of bids is also random. As this random sum of bids is generating “likes” in the previous period, it is not likely to be correlated with unobserved variables of a specific ad influencing click decision for that ad in the current period. We also controlled for ad-specific week effects to account for any ad-specific time trends. Specifically, when examining the effect of signals on users’ clicking decisions, we used the number of fitted cumulative “likes” instead of the actual number of cumulative “likes,” where fitted cumulative “likes” in period t are generated using the following expression:

$$
\begin{array}{r} L i k e s _ {g, t} = \alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} \cdot b i d \_ s u m _ {t} + \alpha_ {\delta} \cdot \delta_ {t} + \alpha_ {h} \cdot h _ {t} \\ + \alpha_ {\gamma} \cdot \gamma_ {g w} + \varepsilon_ {g t} ^ {\alpha}, \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

where bid\_sum<sub>t</sub> represents the sum of the randomly generated bids in period t across all groups; $\delta _ { t }$ is the vector of day dummies to control for overall time effects; $h _ { t }$ is a vector of time-of-day dummies, where we separate a day into four total six-hour periods; and $\gamma _ { g w }$ is a vector of dummies corresponding to user ad group g and week w combinations.

An F-statistic of 25 suggests that this instrument is adequate.

Effect of Peer Endorsements. To assess the impact of peer endorsements, we compared the performance of users exposed to ads with peer endorsements with the performance of those exposed to ads without them. Users exposed to our ads belong to one of two distinct groups: one with peer endorsements and one without (Figure A2 in Online Appendix A). Note that user classification into these groups is not random as Facebook’s advertising interface does not allow advertisers to selectively enable peer endorsements for specific users. Therefore, users with friends who have already “liked” the ad would receive the peer endorsement treatment if their friends consent to the use of thei endorsements in ads. Conversely, users without peer endorsements either lack friends who have “liked” the ad or have friends who opt out of having their information used in Facebook ads. These factors could lead to inherent differences between social and nonsocial groups. To address this challenge, we ensured that our findings are not affected by unobservable characteristics by incorporating a separate instrument for the selection of social and nonsocial users in each period (see Online Appendix A for an explanation of the instrument and first-stage results). Our models are similar to the ones used in sponsored search research (Ghose and Yang 2009, Yang and Ghose 2010, Agarwal et al. 2011, Rutz and Trusov 2011, Rutz et al. 2012). Following this line of research, we estimated our clickrate model using a Bayesian approach, applying Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling because of the nonlinear characteristics of our model (Rossi et al. 2005). The priors and conditional posteriors of this model are discussed in Online Appendix B. We ran the MCMC simulation for 80,000 draws, discarding the first 40,000 as burn-in.

This suggests that Facebook “likes” do not increase users’ tendency to click on the ad. Because “likes” represent popularity cues, one would expect “likes” to increase the click performance. However, we did not find any such effect. A possible explanation is that as assertive CTA ads are perceived negatively, users might perceive popularity cues as part of the firm’s advertising strategy. This would induce users’ reactance behavior, leading them to respond negatively to the signal (Clee and Wicklund 1980, Brehm 1989). An alternative explanation for the effect of popularity cues can be that users seeing the ad with a higher number of “likes” are poorquality users because of the selection bias induced by Facebook’s targeting algorithm, where it could be tar geting best-possible users first. However, this concern is unlikely as we controlled for the time effects for each ad. The coefficient for Social is negative and significant, suggesting that the presence of peer endorsement information decreases users’ propensity to click the ad. In our setup, only those users whose friends already “liked” the ad could be targeted with peer endorsement information. Normally, we would expect that a user whose friend has “liked” the ad may inherently have a higher propensity to respond to the ad because of similarity in taste (i.e., homophily) (McPherson et al. 2001). In addition, one would expect endorsements from friends to further influence users to respond to the ad (Rogers 1995). However, we found the opposite effect in our analysis. Thus, our results partially support Hypothesis 1b.

One possible explanation is that the display of peer endorsement information may be seen as a manipulative intent (Campbell 1995), which could trigger reactance behavior (e.g., Clee and Wicklund 1980, Brehm 1989). This could be because of several underlying reasons. One plausible scenario is that users may perceive this as loss of freedom as they are forced to respond to these ads containing peer endorsements, increasing the pressure to comply. Perceived loss of freedom can trigger reactance (Shen 2015), and this may result in a lower click propensity for ads with peer endorsement information. Yet another possibility is that users may associate peer endorsements on assertive CTA ads with loss of privacy. Finally, it is possible that the peer endorsements are from dissimilar friends, indicating that there is no good match between the ad and the user, and this could be why reactance persists. Reactance tends to be high when the content is personalized and holds a low perceived value (White et al. 2008). We later present our exploration of the plausible mechanisms using a series of randomized studies.

Endogeneity of Peer Endorsements. It is possible that unobservable differences exist between social and nonsocial users, which could affect the platform’s decision to select a greater or lesser number of users with social impressions for any specific bid. For instance, the users chosen might be more appealing because of homophily; that is, they resemble their friends who have already expressed interest in the ad. We tackle this selection issue by employing an IV to ascertain user selection. The selection of an ad for a user at any given moment is influenced by the bid and the expected action rate.<sup>6</sup> The expected action rate may be dictated by the intrinsic value of the ad, peer endorsement, and other factors known to the Facebook platform but not visible to us. This rate is also dynamic and evolving as the Facebook algorithm potentially adjusts the value based on the ad’s performance. For example, the algorithm might modify the expected action rate on the observed responses of targeted users to peer endorsement signals.

By basing the selection of social versus nonsocial impressions for each ad on its randomly generated bid while also accounting for the expected action rate, we can mitigate the selection bias. Specifically, we use our random bid as an instrument to determine the probability of an impression being social or nonsocial, and we re-estimate our model. We adjust for the expected action rate by considering the lagged click performance for each ad group at every bid level,<sup>7</sup> recognizing that users’ expected action rates may fluctuate with the bids, and we control for cumulative bids, which we use as an instrument for popularity cues. This method aligns with the approach of Lee et al. (2018), who employed a twostage process to address the endogenous selection of post impressions on Facebook based on known characteristics. First-stage results are included in Table A2 in the Online Appendix (see Online Appendix A for more details). The click-rate estimates are shown in Table 5, column 2, and they are qualitatively similar to our main results.

Repeat Advertising. Users may encounter the same ad multiple times because of Facebook’s ad targeting algorithm, potentially reducing ad effectiveness (Lambrecht and Tucker 2013). First, comparing the ratio of unique to overall impressions, we found that the unique-to-overall ratio is significantly higher for social ads (mean 0.72) compared with nonsocial ads (mean 0.67; $p { < } 0 . 0 1 )$ ). This suggests that nonsocial ads are more likely to repeat, and the negative effect of repeat advertising is likely higher for them; however, our results suggest that social ads performed worse than nonsocial ads. Furthermore, by focusing only on unique impressions and clicks, we still found results consistent with the main findings, suggesting that our results are unlikely driven by repeat advertising (see Table 5, column 3).

Other Robustness Tests. We verified our results using a holdout sample analysis (Table C1 in the Online Appendix), built an alternate model with random effects to account for clustered random assignment (Table C2 in the Online Appendix), and additionally, verified our results using a linear specification (Table C3 in the Online Appendix). Because of space constraints, detailed descriptions of these models along with the estimates are provided in Online Appendix C. We also ran a similar analysis for a different advertising campaign featuring another app (online poker) as an additional robustness test for our main analysis, and we found qualitatively similar results (Tables C4 and C5 in the Online Appendix).

## Randomized Laboratory Experiments

In the field tests, we were unable to exogenously vary popularity cues and peer endorsements, and we were also unable to evaluate plausible mechanisms because of Facebook’s advertising platform restrictions. We overcame these restrictions in a series of randomized studies. Specifically, the goals of these studies were to (1) evaluate whether popularity cues and peer endorsements generated reactance; (2) evaluate whether the similarity of users moderated the effect; and (3) iden tify the likely antecedents for reactance, such as the activation of persuasion knowledge, the pressure to comply, and privacy concerns. Note that such laboratory experiments are often used as a means of studying the underlying mechanism, especially in situations where field data do not allow researchers to pinpoint the detailed behavioral factors (Lambrecht and Tucker 2013, Bleier and Eisenbeiss 2015, Burtch et al. 2018, Chevalier et al. 2018, Buckman et al. 2019, Huang et al. 2019, Melumad et al. 2019, Gershon et al. 2020). Re searchers have used similar experiments in the context of social media (Lowry et al. 2016, John et al. 2017, Cao and Smith 2021).

In these studies, we adopted the name generator method (Burt 1984) to access study participants’ social networks for research. The name generator method has been used by research in the sociology and business literature (e.g., Kumpel ¨ 2019, Stadtfeld et al. 2019, Cao and Smith 2021), and it provides an effective approach for researchers to access individuals’ social network information to infer network characteristics. To assess the feasibility of this approach in the social media context, we ran a pilot study in the form of a randomized survey, where we (1) followed the name generator method to request that survey respondents provide three friends’ names, (2) created a static page resembling a Facebook ad and randomized the display of “likes” and friends’ “likes” endorsing the ad using the friend names provided by respondents, and (3) asked respondents to indicate their ad clicking intention. The results are consistent with what we observed in the field data, and they suggest that the name generator method, in conjunction with randomly displaying friend “likes,” can help simulate peer endorsement signals for research. We present the pilot study results in Study A in Online Appendix D.

## Study 1: Web App Experiment Comparing Assertive CTA and Informational Ads (MTurk)

The goal of this study was to exogenously vary the display of “likes” and friends’ “likes” to investigate the effect of popularity cues and peer endorsements on users’ clicking behavior. Another aim was to compare whether the ad type moderates the clicking behavior by comparing assertive CTA ads with informational ads. The pilot study suggests that the name generator technique can be utilized to gain participants’ social network information, and therefore, we continued to implement this method in the current study. In addition, to increase the realism of subjects’ browsing experience, we built a web app that closely resembles the layout of the Facebook app. This web app displayed a series of three ads (a grocery delivery service ad, a clothing store ad, and an app install ad) in a random order. The subjects could interact with each ad by clicking the ad button, which would display a pop-up window with more information about the product. The subjects could further click a “buy/install” button in the pop-up window to confirm their purchases, similar to how Facebook ads are currently set up. Moreover, we randomly varied the ad message displayed on the ad button by showing “learn more” (i.e., informational) or “buy now” (i.e., assertive CTA) for each ad. This design allows us to compare the causal effect of popularity cues and social endorsements on ads featuring an information message (e.g., those reported in Bakshy et al. 2012 and Huang et al. 2020) and ads featuring an assertive CTA message (e.g., the CTA ads used in our Facebook field tests).

Procedure. We recruited 982 users on MTurk for this study. The subjects first provided at least five friends names. The subjects then proceeded to the web app to interact with all three ads in a random order, where (1) the numbers of “likes” and friends’ “likes” endorsing each ad were randomly displayed and where (2) each ad message was accompanied by a randomly assigned “learn more” or “buy now” button. In the case of friends’ “likes,” the names being displayed were randomly chosen from the names provided by the subjects. After seeing all three ads, the subjects evaluated each ad’s reactance and quality levels, the similarity in preference with friends “liking” the ad, and questions regarding privacy concern (see the Study 1 procedure in Online Appendix E1). Finally, the subjects answered standard demographic questions.

Results. Table E1 in Online Appendix E provides the summary statistics for this experiment. We first analyzed whether the ad type (informational or assertive) moderates the effect of popularity cues and peer endorsements using a logit model with ad and subject fixed effects, as shown in Table 6, columns 1 and 2. We found that friends’ “likes” have a positive effect on the click decision for informational ads (column 1 in Table 6). This finding is consistent with existing research showing a positive effect of peer endorsement signals on user response (e.g., Bakshy et al. 2012, Huang et al. 2020). However, column 2 in Table 6 shows that friends’ “likes” have a negative effect on conversion for assertive CTA ads, consistent with the field studies. We also found that “likes” have a positive effect for information ads but no effect for assertive CTA ads, suggesting that popularity cues are effective only for information ads.

We further explored the role of similarity on the effect of peer endorsement signals. Previous studies have indicated that the negative reactance effect is more pronounced when the influencer (endorser) dif fers from the influenced (focal user) (e.g., Silvia 2005). Conversely, reactance tends to be minimal when the content is personalized and holds a high perceived value (White et al. 2008). Consequently, peer endorsements from friends who share similarities with the viewer may provoke less reactance.

We represented similarity using a dummy variable Similar, which is coded as one when friends with high similarity (greater than two) are shown and zero otherwise. As shown in Table 6, column 3, whereas peer endorsements have a negative effect on click rate, those coming from similar friends actually have a positive effect. This provides evidence that similarity plays an important role in the effectiveness of peer endorsements. Note that the interaction term of friends’ “likes” and privacy concern<sup>8</sup> is insignificant, suggesting that the negative effect of peer endorsements is unlikely driven by privacy concern.

Discussion. The results from this study revealed that the effect of popularity cues and peer endorsements on subjects’ clicking behavior is moderated by the ad type, with informational ads seeing a positive effect but assertive CTA ads seeing a negative effect. One limitation of the current study is that the purchases made were hypothetical and did not incur any cost. ## Discussion and Conclusion

In this research, we investigated the impact of popularity cues and peer endorsements on the click performance of assertive CTA ads through a combination of field tests and randomized laboratory studies. We found that popularity cues alone do not help the user’s decision to click. We also found that peer endorsements have a negative impact on the clicking performance. Through the randomized laboratory studies, we confirmed that popularity cues do not help assertive CTA ads and that peer endorsements have a negative effect on assertive CTA ads but not informational ads. We also found that the activation of persuasion knowledge likely leads to the null effect of popularity cues and the negative effect of peer endorsements in the context of assertive CTA ads. However, the negative effects of peer endorsements can be attenuated if the signals come from friends with similar preferences.

These results are important for several reasons. Our analyses inform the design of assertive CTA ads on social media platforms, such as Facebook; our results show that the use of popularity cues is not effective in generating responses for assertive CTA ads used for conversion. Similarly, showing peer endorsements from dissimilar friends can backfire and lead to a lower performance as compared with ads without any such endorsement information. Although prior studies have found a positive effect of peer endorsements on user responses for informational ads, our results suggest that such an effect does not apply to assertive CTA ads as such ads can induce reactance, and the display of peer endorsements can activate persuasion knowledge and exacerbate the reactance behavior.

From a practitioner’s perspective, our research provides insights into the efficacy of popularity cues and peer endorsements in the context of assertive CTA ads on social media. Although information systems and marketing studies have established the value of popularity cues and peer endorsements in enabling product sales, our results question the efficacy of these signals in the context of assertive CTA ads. Our results suggest that advertisers should carefully consider the value of peer endorsement signals in advertising content. Moreover, advertisers should ignore the “likes” generated by ads as these do not increase user response, and they should not misinterpret these signals when evaluating ad performance. This is especially relevant for advertisers that cannot observe product sales directly through the Facebook interface and must rely on interpreting intermediate signals, such as clicks or “likes,” to infer the ad performance. Additionally, because of its potential negative effect when combined with popularity cues and peer endorsements, advertisers should carefully decide whether to include an assertive call-to-action message in their ads. Lastly, social media platforms, such as Facebook, should consider making the use of popularity and peer endorsements as a targeting option for advertisers. Such an option can be beneficial for advertisers hoping to promote their products through informational ads, but advertisers should unselect this option if the ad is a direct call to action to buy or install.

As with any empirical analysis, our study has several limitations. First, we relied on aggregate data for our field studies, and our analysis was restricted to only two ads, focusing solely on app installs. Consequently, we were unable to observe individual behaviors and attributes, such as similarity and privacy preferences, nor could we validate purchases across other types of products. Additionally, we had to use instruments to address potential selection issues. Although our laboratory experiments attempted to overcome these restrictions, we could not directly observe social networks for our experimental subjects. Future studies should validate our results and mechanisms through randomized field studies on assertive CTA ads for a wide variety of products. It is possible that users may choose to interact with their friends offline after seeing the peer endorsements rather than directly interacting with the ad, potentially leading them to install the app separately. Although this does not alter the implications for the immediate response to the ad, future studies should investigate such effects. Furthermore, future studies should also consider the impact of these signals on conversions. Because we only studied the direct effects of popularity cues and peer endorsements separately, future research should also assess any potential interaction effects. Finally, future research should explore how other user actions, such as comments and sharing, influence the performance of assertive CTA ads.
