Abstract. With growing popularity of e-commerce, nearly every prominent retailer is aiming to turn omni-channel. One crucial decision in this pursuit is the identification of the joint assortment. Despite the importance of this decision, few studies have addressed this issue. In this study, we contribute by examining joint assortment and product prices for a retailer that sells products through both brick-and-mortar and online channels using the locational choice model. We consider two classes of customers such that customers in one class prefer the brick-and-mortar channel and customers in the other class prefer the online channel. Our analysis indicates that the optimal assortment should be thought of as a portfolio of two types of products: customized and omni-channel. Customized products are priced in such a way that they are targeted toward customers who prefer to shop from the channel the products are sold through. In contrast, omni-channel products are priced attractively so that all customers consider buying them. The relative mix of these products depends on how flexible customers are in shopping from the channel they do not prefer and the size of each class. Additionally, we investigate whether the conventional wisdom of selling niche products through the online channel is always optimal. We find that this suggestion may be suboptimal when the online channel has greater cost of including a product in the assortment and fewer preferring customers compared with the brick-and mortar channel. We also develop insights on price selection. As more and more retailer are converting from single-channel to omni-channel, they need to reexamine their assortment strategy across existing and new channels. The models, results, and insights presented in this paper would help them in designing a joint assortment policy across channels.

## 1. Introduction

Over the last decade, selling through multiple channels has become the norm in the retail industry. While brick-and-mortar retailers have added digital channels (or started taking their digital channels more seriously), e-tailers have added physical stores. For brick-andmortar retailers, the motivation to add a digital channel lies in the fact that much of the growth has been concentrated in digital channels in the recent past. For example, in the fourth quarter of 2019, which was the last normal quarter before the COVID-19 pandemic, whereas the total retail sales grew by 3.8%, the e-commerce retail sales increased by 16.7% (U.S. Census Bureau 2020). Customers’ reluctance to visit physical stores during the COVID-19 pandemic further accelerated the growth and adoption of digital channels. At the same time, many pure play e-tailers such as Amazon, Warby Parker, Bonobos, Birchbox, Alibaba, and Casper added physical stores (Walsh 2016, Petro 2017, Doctoroff 2018, King 2023). This move is driven by the realization that despite the growth of e-commerce, a large number of customers still find value in visiting a brick-and-mortar store. That the brick-and-mortar channel remains an attractive channel is also supported by more new stores opening than closing during 2017–2019 (Sabanoglu 2021).

For any multichannel retailer to prosper, coordination across channels is crucial. This is not only necessary to ensure efficient operations, but customers also increasingly demand a seamless experience (Graf et al. 2021). This phenomenon of seamless experience for customers through coordinated operations is known as omnichannel retailing. A key decision for any omni-channel retailer is assortment design. It is an important decision since poor assortment planning may lead to stockouts and markdowns. Despite the criticality of this decision, few research studies have examined it. By developing insights on the structure of the optimal assortment (along with the price for each product) for an omni-channel retailer, we fill an important gap in the existing literature.

## 1.1. Motivation and Research Questions

We consider a retailer who would like to design an assortment to be sold through a brick-and-mortar (BM) store (or BM channel) and the online channel for a product category when customers are open to shopping from both channels.<sup>1</sup> Given a combined assortment for both channels, a customer chooses the product-channel combination that maximizes her utility. We model this selection using the locational choice model in which the utility function of the customer includes the disutility of using a channel and the disutility associated with not purchasing her most-preferred product.

We consider customers to be heterogeneous in their preference for channel. The heterogeneity is modeled by considering two classes of customers such that customers in one class prefer the BM channel and customers in the other class prefer the online channel. Greater channel preference implies smaller channel disutility, so customers who prefer the BM (online) channel face smaller disutility when they purchase a product from the BM (online) channel compared with the online (BM) channel. We consider two scenarios that correspond to all products being equally popular and products with differential popularity. In addition, we model and analyze an extension in which a few customers may purchase exclusively from the BM or the online channel. We analyze both scenarios and the extension using the continuous and the discrete form of the locational choice model.

In every setting, the locational choice model is used to identify the market share for each product in an assortment, which then is used to compute the profit associated with the assortment. The profit function also takes into account the possibility of differential prices for products, differential costs of including a product in the assortment, and operational features such as shipping cost incurred for products sold through the online channel.

We use the model to explore four important research questions. The first research question is motivated from the absence of clear guidelines on the design of joint assortment for an omni-channel retailer. Although many articles in popular media and management journals note the importance of appropriate product selection as the academic research on this topic is limited (Rooderkerk and Ko¨k 2019). Accordingly, we consider the following research question.

Research Question 1. How should an omni-channel assortment be designed, and how does it compare with the optimal single-channel assortment?

We propose a novel framework for composing an omni-channel assortment. This framework embraces differential pricing across products and channels at its core. According to the framework, each product is priced such that it is either customized or omnichannel. A customized product is targeted toward customers who prefer the channel through which the product is sold. In contrast, an omni-channel product is priced attractively so that it can be targeted toward all the customers. The relative mix of these products depends on how flexible customers are in shopping from the channel they do not prefer and the size of each class. For example, as the size of a class increases (due to market expansion), the number of customized products in the assortment is likely to increase. The optimal assortment is thus a portfolio comprising customized and omni-channel products. We refer to this framework as customer-based framework.

The customer-based framework can be linked to another, well-known framework, product-based framework. In this framework, the focus is on product placement such that each product is sold through either one channel or both channels, and the subassortments for the two channels can have complete, partial, or no overlap (Rooderkerk and Ko¨k 2019). Because omni-channel products target customers of both classes, they should be offered through only one channel. Thus, an assortment consisting entirely of omni-channel products is nonoverlapping. In contrast, customized products can be offered through both channels simultaneously. When an assortment contains only customized products and the parameters for both channels are similar, the optimal assortment is completely overlapping. Between these two extremes, the assortment is likely to be partially overlapping.

The presence of omni-channel products in the optimal omni-channel assortment is one way in which it differs from the optimal single-channel assortment. Another crucial difference is in customer coverage. Although the optimal single-channel assortment covers a continuum of customers (Gaur and Honhon 2006), the customer coverage may be patchy with some customers not obtaining positive utility from any product, even though other customers with similar product preferences are covered. Clearly, the optimal omni-channe assortment is far more intricate compared with the opti mal single-channel assortment.

The strategy of differential pricing across channels, which is an important component of the customer-based framework, is also widely used in practice; Wolk and Ebling (2010) find that up to 60% retailers may set different prices in different channels for the same product.

The strategy can lead to significant improvement in profit; Baker et al. (2018) indicate this improvement to be in the range of 2%–5%. In another study, Harsha et al. (2019) estimate the projected profit improvement to be 7% when their proposed multichannel pricing approach is used for one major retail chain. Usually, prices are lower in the online channel, although there can be exceptions. For example, Costco and Walmart have charged higher prices in their online channels (Nassauer 2017). Given the crucial role of price in an omni-channel environment, we ask the following research question.

## Research Question 2. How should prices be set for products in an omni-channel assortment?

We find that although a product should be priced higher than another similar but less popular product when they are sold through the same channel, this relationship between popularity and price may break down when the products are sold through different channels. The reason is that popularity is just one of the many drivers for product price; other drivers such as the number of customers who prefer each channel and the channel’s cost structure may cause a product’s optimal price to be lower than the optimal price for a less popular product sold through the other channel. The result applies to both customized and omni-channel products.

Apart from product popularity, the optimal price is also influenced by channel and customer parameters, as noted previously. As the number of customers who prefer a channel increases, the prices of products sold through the channel also increase. Interestingly, the price for an omni-channel product also increases when the number of customers who do not prefer the channel through which the product is sold increases. The price increase is a reflection of greater demand for the product. However, the price has a nonmonotone relationship with how flexible customers are in purchasing products from their nonpreferred channel. As this flexibility reduces, the price (for an omni-channel product) first decreases to lure them to purchase the product. However, as this flexibility reduces even further, the product changes to customized and the price increases.

The third research question explores another dimension of assortment design. This question is motivated by the conventional wisdom, which is rooted in the wellknown long-tail theory (Anderson 2004), that niche or long-tail products should be sold through the online channel (Meindl and Chopra 2015, Silk 2021). We investigate this belief by considering the following research question.

Research Question 3. Is it always optimal to sell niche products through the online channel?

We find that the advice is true in certain circumstances, but not always. In particular, if the fixed cost of including a product in the assortment is lower and the margin potential (i.e., maximum possible margin) is greater for the BM channel and the number of customers who prefer the BM channel is greater than the online channel, then it is more profitable to sell niche products through the BM channel.

Finally, even though the number of omni-channel customers (who are open to shopping from both channels) is rising by the day, there still are a few customers who strongly prefer to shop exclusively from one channel (Sopadjieva et al. 2017). According to this study, 7% of all the shoppers surveyed are online-only shoppers and 20% are store-only shoppers. The objective of the fourth research question is to probe the effect of the presence of such customers. Accordingly, we ask the following.

Research Question 4. How do the above insights change if a few customers shop exclusively from either the BM channel or the online channel?

We find that the previous insights broadly continue to hold. In addition, we observe common products, which are sold through both channels, act as a strategic tool for the retailer. These products can be used to guide omni-channel customers toward the more profitable channel through appropriately selected prices.

## 1.2. Literature Review

In this section, we briefly review the following two streams of literature that come closest to this paper: (i) assortment planning and (ii) multichannel retailing. We also discuss how we borrow from these streams of literature and highlight our contributions with respect to them. For a summary of our research contributions, see Figure 1.

1.2.1. Assortment Planning. The literature on assortment planning for substitutable products can be broadly divided into four categories depending upon the decisions optimized. The four categories correspond to the optimization of only assortment; assortment and inven tory; assortment and prices; and assortment, inventory, and prices together. Across all categories, the studies have used a wide variety of modeling approaches to capture customer preferences such as the multinomial logit (MNL) model (van Ryzin and Mahajan 1999), Bayesian logit model (Hopp and Xu 2005), nested logit model (Li and Huh 2011), and, of course, the locational choice model. Given our focus, we next summarize a few studies that use the locational choice model.

Using this model, Gaur and Honhon (2006) address assortment planning for a single-channel retailer for both static and dynamic substitution scenarios assuming exogenous prices. (In static substitution, a customer selects a product from the assortment without being aware of or regardless of its availability. In contrast, in dynamic substitution, a customer selects only from available products.) Our study extends this paper for an omni-channel retailer with static substitution. Apart from sales channels, another difference between this study and Gaur and Honhon (2006) is that price is endogenous in our model. Our results show that the omni-channel assortment is substantially different and more complex compared with a single-channel assortment; a detailed comparison is available in Section 4.3.

One study that endogenizes prices while optimizing the single-channel assortment is Alptekinoglu et al. (2012). Compared with it, we contribute by considering a second sales channel. To the best of our knowledge, none of the papers that investigate assortment planning using the locational choice model explores multichannel retailing.

In general, the design of omni-channel assortment has received limited attention in the literature on assortment planning, and the only studies to consider this issue are Dzyabura and Jagabathula (2017) and Lo and Topaloglu (2022). In both studies, the retailer displays a subset of products in the offline channel; the complete set of products is available in the online channel. Furthermore, each product is characterized by a set of features; this is where the two studies primarily differ as they use different approaches. The objective of both studies is to develop optimization algorithms to identify the offline products in two scenarios that correspond to customers purchasing only from the online channel and customers purchasing from both channels. In comparison, our objective is to derive insights for designing an assortment for both channels simultaneously. Another difference is in the customer choice model (locational choice versus MNL-type model).

Excellent reviews of the literature on assortment planning in general and assortment planning for an omni-channel retailer are available in Ko¨k et al. (2015) and Rooderkerk and Ko¨k (2019), respectively.

1.2.2. Multichannel Retailing. The rapidly growing literature on multichannel retailing has probed a diverse set of research questions using a multitude of method ologies. Depending on the methodology deployed, the literature can be broadly divided into two seg ments. The first segment, which is empirical in nature, develops insights on the observed relationship between different channels. Ghose and Yao (2011) compare transaction prices in the online and BM channels and find that the dispersion in prices is lower in the online channel. Similarly, Granados et al. (2012) find that the price elasticity of online customers is greater than the BM-channel customers in the travel industry. In another example, Kumar et al. (2019) study the effect of the opening of a new store on online-channel sales for a retailer and find that a new store leads to an increase in online-channel sales. Physical and online channels have also been compared for service products such as labor time: Gong et al. (2018) study how monetary incentives influence the relative supply of foreign labor in online and physical markets. Two more examples of papers in this segment are Brynjolfsson and Smith (2000) and Ansari et al. (2008).

Conversely, the second segment, which uses analytical methods, usually seeks to derive insights on the optimal decisions. Ofek et al. (2011) identify the equilibrium between two competing retailers that are considering opening an online channel and examine changes in pricing and store-assistance strategies once the online channel is opened. Chen et al. (2015) investigate dynamic pricing for an omni-channel retailer that satisfies customers of both channels from the same inventory. In another example, Gao and Su (2016) study the buy online and pickup in store strategy. They show that it may not be optimal to sell high-volume products using this strategy. Finally, one example of a paper with a different objective is Shi et al. (2019), which proposes a forecasting method for an online-to-offline arrangement.

We also note the existence of a vast amount of literature (using analytical methods) on opening of a second, direct channel by a manufacturer. Key objectives of such papers are to explore the impact of channel conflict on supply chain decisions and to understand the circumstances under which the manufacturer should launch a new channel. The channel conflict arises because the manufacturer, by selling directly to consumers, competes with the retailer(s). As an example, Chiang et al. (2003), a seminal paper on this topic, model a supply chain consisting of a manufacturer and a BM retailer as a Stackelberg game and examine the effect of the manufacturer opening a direct channel on the retail price, wholesale price, and overall demand.

Much of the direct-channel literature models the possibility of differential pricing in the two channels, a feature this study embraces as well. In general, one contributing factor for the differential pricing is the potential for price discrimination among consumers in e-commerce. This feature of e-commerce has led to novel pricing practices and many papers have studied them. As an example, Ho et al. (2017) study a cash-back model, in which an online retailer offers discounts to customers who come to its website through an intermediary.

To the best of our knowledge, the issue of optimal assortment that a retailer who sells products through multiple channels should carry has not yet been studied in this literature. Thus, ours is the first study to derive insights on the structure of the optimal joint assortment for an omni-channel retailer. Before we conclude this section, we note that several articles have appeared in the recent past on practical perspective on omni-channel retailing; see, for example, Rigby (2011), Brynjolfsson et al. (2013), Bell et al. (2014), and Nyquist (2016).

## 2. Model and Notation

In this section, we describe the model when customers are open to purchasing products from both channels, although they may be heterogeneous in their preference for channel. We model heterogeneity in channel preference by considering two classes of customers such that one class has greater affinity for the BM channel and the other class has greater affinity for the online channel.

## 2.1. Setting

Consider a retailer that sells products through both brick-and-mortar and online channels. The two channels are represented by superscripts s (as in store) and $w$ (as in web), respectively. The retailer would like to jointly optimize its assortment for both channels and the price for each product for a single category of horizontally differentiated products.

## 2.2. Customer Choice Mode

Given an assortment, we model the selection of products by customers using the locational choice model such that customers’ most preferred products are located over [0, 1] (Gaur and Honhon 2006).<sup>3</sup> As we noted in the literature review, this model has been widely used in studies on assortment planning. We consider both continuous and discrete forms of the model. The continuous form is applicable when either the number of available product variants is large or when the retailer has flexibility to include any product in the assortment (e.g., when products are private label). Conversely, the discrete form is applicable when the number of available product variants is limited. Although we present the results for the continuous form, we verify them for the discrete form.

The retailer does not know locations of the most preferred product of customers, although it knows the probability distribution. Let this distribution be denoted by F. We consider the distribution F to be either uniform or triangular. The probability density function is denoted by $f ,$ and we interpret f(b) as the popularity of a product located at b. Clearly, when $F$ is uniform, all product specifications are equally popular. Conversely, with a triangular distribution, we can model scenarios in which products are not equally popular.

We use $b _ { i } ^ { j }$ to denote the location of the ith product sold through channel $j \in \{ s , w \}$ , and let $r _ { i } ^ { j }$ be the corresponding price. The products are indexed from left to right, so $b _ { 1 } ^ { j }$ is the location of the left-most product sold through channel $j .$ Let $\mathbf { b } ^ { j }$ and $\mathbf { r } ^ { j }$ be the vectors of product locations and prices, respectively, for products sold through channel $j .$ Furthermore, let $\bar { n ^ { j } }$ be the number of products sold through channel $j .$ It is possible that some or all of the products in $\mathbf { b } ^ { s }$ and $\mathbf { b } ^ { w }$ are common as the same product may be offered through both channels. Let $\hat { \mathbf { b } } = ( \mathbf { b } ^ { s } , \mathbf { b } ^ { w } )$ and ${ \bf r } = \left( { \bf r } ^ { s } , { \bf r } ^ { w } \right)$ be the combined vectors of product locations and prices over both channels.

In our model, the same product could be sold at different prices in the two channels. This type of pricing strategy, which we refer to as differential pricing strategy, is extensively used in practice, though its deployment varies across product categories and geographies (Cavallo 2017). According to the study, while prices are different between the BM and the online channels for drugs 75% of the time, the prices are different only 8% of the time for electronics products. Similarly, although the prices in the two channels are different in Brazil and the United States 58% and 31% of the time, respectively, in the United Kingdom and Canada, this percentage is 9%. Overall, the study estimated the prices to be different 28% of the time.

In implementing the differential pricing strategy, retailers have a multitude of motivations. One motivation could be profit improvement; the strategy can improve profit by exploiting differential price elasticities of customers for the two channels. Another motivation could be to promote one channel. Finally, the differential prices could just be a reflection of different cost structures of the two channels. The strategy is not without disadvantages; the inconsistent pricing may frustrate a few customers (Goad et al. 2015, McKinsey 2019). Despite this concern, the previous statistics show that a sizable number of retailers have found this strategy attractive.

We consider two classes of customers: class s and class w. Customers in class s have greater affinity for the BM channel. Thus, the disutility $A ^ { s , s }$ they experience when they purchase a product from the BM channel is at most the disutility $A ^ { w , s }$ they experience when they purchase the product from the online channel. Similarly, customers in class w experience respective disutilities of $A ^ { s , w }$ and $A ^ { w , w } \leq A ^ { s , w }$ when they purchase products from the BM and online channels. 4

The disutility parameters corresponding to the BM channel, $A ^ { s , s }$ and $A ^ { s , w }$ , arise from a combination of three factors. ## 2.3. Profit

For every product sold through channel $j ,$ the retailer incurs a fixed cost of $G ^ { j }$ for order placement and stocking. Although the order placement process should be identical for the two channels, the stocking and display processes could be different. Usually, stocking and display require greater care in a BM store than in a warehouse for the online channel, which implies that $G ^ { w } \leq G ^ { s }$ . However, for categories in which the appearance of products is important, the retailer may have to spend additional effort while displaying products online. In such cases, $G ^ { w }$ may be greater than $G ^ { s }$ Because either parameter may be larger, we make no assumption on their relative values. For notational convenience, we sometimes take $G ^ { s } = G$ and $G ^ { w } = \delta G ,$ where $\delta \ge 0$

Let $\lambda ^ { k }$ be the number of customers in class $k ^ { 7 }$ As noted before, all customers are open to shopping from both channels. This assumption is supported by the findings of a recent study, in which, of 46,000 shoppers (that the study tracked) around three-fourths (73%) were omnichannel shoppers (Sopadjieva et al. 2017). The COVID-19 pandemic has further accelerated the migration of customers to omni-channel shopping. Another recent study shows that omni-channel shopping for fast moving consumer goods increased by 50% in 2020 (Nielsen IQ 2020). A crucial distinguishing feature between the two channels is the return rate. Due to an inability to touch and feel products and observe all of their features, the return rate in the online channel is usually higher than the BM channel (Ofek et al. 2011). According to this study, although the overall customer returns are approximately 8.7% of the retail sales, the returns in the online channel are 18%–35% of the sales depending upon the product category. Greater returns inevitably lead to greater cost per unit sold. Although, for simplicity, we do not explicitly consider the (differential) return rate in our model, we describe an approach for adapting the disutility and profit models to incorporate product returns in Section EC.1 of the online appendix. The approach follows a framework described in Ofek et al. (2011). Using this approach, the results obtained in this study can be easily reinterpreted to develop additional insights on the impact of differential return rates.

## 2.4. Foundational Results

In this section, we discuss two fundamental properties of optimal single-channel assortments that do not carry over to optimal omni-channel assortments. For both properties, we present updated results.

A key property displayed by any optimal singlechannel assortment is that the union of first choice intervals for the products included in the assortment is a closed interval (Gaur and Honhon 2006). This means that the customers covered by an optimal assortment form a continuum. In other words, if two customers obtain positive utility from a product in the optimal assortment, so will all the customers located in between. This property may not always hold in an omni-channel environment, and the retailer may choose to stock products such that the requirement of some customers will not be met. Thus, the customers covered by the optimal assortment do not form a continuum, and the coverage of the assortment could be patchy. Holes in the assortment coverage usually arise when small segments of customers do not find any channel-product combination acceptable, and it is uneconomical for the retailer to offer additional products through their preferred channel. Similar to the first property, violations to the second property of optimal single-channel assortments are only partial in optimal omni-channel assortments. In the following proposition, we show that the first choice and coverage intervals are identical for at least one class on each side of the product location for a product that is purchased by customers of both classes. (We refer to such products as omni-channel products.)

Proposition 2. Consider any omni-channel product located at $b _ { i } ^ { j } .$ On each side of b<sup>j</sup>, the first choice and coverage intervals are identical for at least one class; that is, $b _ { i } ^ { j , k - } = b _ { i } ^ { j } - L _ { i } ^ { j , k }$ for at least one class k, and $b _ { i } ^ { j , k + } = b _ { i } ^ { j } + L _ { i } ^ { j , k } .$ for at least one class k.

Extensive computational experiments indicate that violations of the second property are not common in optimal solutions. This is even more so when products are offered through both channels, which is the more interesting and plausible scenario from a practical perspective. (In Example 2, all the products are sold through the online channel, and the violations ensure coverage for class s customers.) Any assortment in which the first choice interval is smaller than the coverage interval for at least one product is inefficient for the retailer.<sup>1</sup>

Therefore, through suitable prices and/or product locations, the retailer tries to ensure that the coverage and first choice intervals remain equal for every product.

Because coverage and first choice intervals are usually identical, for simplicity, we present analytical results in the rest of the paper assuming this to be the case. This also keeps the exposition simple. However, most of the results (i.e., Propositions 1–8 and part 1 of Proposition 9) hold analytically even when the first choice and the coverage intervals are not identical. For each result, while presenting its proof in the electronic companion, we mention whether the result extends when the two intervals are not equal, and if yes, then how.

\frac {1}{p _ {i} ^ {j , j}} \left(\lambda^ {j} p _ {i} ^ {j, j} \left(\alpha^ {j} - \frac {p _ {i} ^ {j , j}}{2}\right) + \lambda^ {\ell} (p _ {i} ^ {j, j} - 2 A ^ {j}) ^ {+} \left(\alpha^ {j} - \frac {p _ {i} ^ {j , j}}{2}\right) - G ^ {j}\right).\tag{5}
$$

This function may have up to two local maxima, $\textstyle { \sqrt { \frac { 2 G ^ { j } } { \lambda ^ { j } } } }$ and $\textstyle { \sqrt { \frac { 2 ( G ^ { j } + 2 \lambda ^ { \ell } A ^ { j } \alpha ^ { j } ) } { \lambda ^ { j } + \lambda ^ { \ell } } } } ,$ , depending on whether the product is customized $( p _ { i } ^ { j , j } \leq 2 A ^ { j } )$ or omni-channel $( p _ { i } ^ { j , j } > 2 A ^ { j } )$ . The expression for the first maximum, $\textstyle { \sqrt { \frac { 2 G ^ { j } } { \lambda ^ { j } } } } ,$ , indicates that the optimal market share of a customized product sold through channel j increases with the fixed cost for the channel and decreases with the number of customers who prefer the channel. The expression for the second maximum implies that both these relationships should hold for omni-channel products as well. In addition, it indicates that the optimal market share of such products increases with the cross-channel disutility $A ^ { j }$ and margin potential $\alpha ^ { j } .$ . However, the market share may either increase or decrease with respect to the number of customers in the other class $( \lambda ^ { \ell } )$ depending on other parameters. Recall that an increase in $p _ { i } ^ { j , j }$ makes a product more likely to be omni-channel. $\mathrm { A s }$ we discuss later, the hypothesized relationships between the market share and the parameters broadly hold in the experiments.

3.2.1. Fixed Cost. Greater fixed cost in a channel results in fewer products and hence larger market share per product (Table 3). Larger optimal market share is facilitated through lower margin (price), which makes products attractive to customers who prefer the other channel. Consequently, products are more likely to be omni-channel as the fixed cost increases. For example, in Table $^ { 3 , }$ all the BM-channel products are customized when $G ^ { s } = 0 . 1 5$ . However, when $G ^ { s } = 0 . 3 5$ , these products become omni-channel. We formally state these observations as follows.

Observation 1. As the fixed cost of selling a product through a channel increases, the number of omni-channel products sold through the channel increases. Furthermore, the average price decreases and the average market share per product increases.

Observation 2. As the number of customers who prefer a channel increases, the number of products sold through the channel increases and more products are likely to be customized. In addition, the average price increases, and the average market share decreases.

Observation 4. As the margin potential for a channel decreases, the average price increases and the average market share decreases for products sold through the channel.

Observation 5. As the number of customers who do not prefer a channel increases, the average price for omnichannel products sold through the channel increases, which decreases the average per product market share for such products. In addition, the size of the assortment increases.

Because omni-channel products are sold through only one channel, the subassortments do not overlap when the optimal assortment consists of only them. For example, when the cross-channel disutilities are low for both channels, then it is optimal to offer only omni-channel products through both channels. Because all customers are (somewhat) open to shopping from both channels, there is little to gain by offering any product simultaneously from both channels.<sup>13</sup> In contrast, when customized products are offered, an overlap may occur. In particular, when both channels have similar parameters and all customers have a strong preference for one channel, then the subassortments completely overlap. Between these two extremes, a partially overlapping assortment is likely to be optimal.

4.3. Comparison with Single-Channel Assortment In this section, we discuss differences between the optimal omni-channel and single-channel (as described in Gaur and Honhon (2006)) assortments. As Gaur and Honhon (2006) assume price to be fixed and exogenous, we omit differences that are caused by price endogeneity in our model. The list is as follows.

1. The optimal assortment has a more intricate structure in the omni-channel environment. The intricacy is primarily caused by the presence of omni-channel products in the omni-channel assortment. In contrast, all products in a single-channel assortment are purchased by customers devoted to shopping from that channel.

2. Another difference arises from the distinction between optimal channels for popular and niche products. In an omni-channel environment, while popular products should be sold from both channels, niche products may be sold from only one channel. In a singlechannel environment, all products are sold from only one channel.

3. The optimal assortment for a single channel covers a continuum of customers (or customer preferences). Moreover, all covered customers derive positive utility from exactly one product. In contrast, the optimal omnichannel assortment could be patchy in coverage; that is, there may exist customers who do not derive positive utility from any product, even though surrounding customers are covered. Similarly, some customers may obtain positive utility from two products.

In addition, Alptekinoglu et al. (2012) examine optimal prices and market shares for products with differential popularity for a single-channel assortment. They find that both optimal prices and market shares increase with popularity. However, in an omni-channel context, the optimal price for a product may not be greater than a less popular product sold through another channel.

We first describe the notation and model. Let $\lambda ^ { s } , \lambda ^ { w } ,$ and $\lambda ^ { o }$ be the numbers of customers who purchase only from the BM channel, only from the online channel, and from both channels, respectively. As before, let $\mathbf { b } ^ { s } : = \mathbf { \sigma }$ $( b _ { 1 } ^ { s } , b _ { 2 } ^ { s } , \ldots , b _ { n ^ { s } } ^ { s } )$ and $\mathbf { b } ^ { w } : = \widehat { ( b _ { 1 } ^ { w } , b _ { 2 } ^ { w } , \ldots , b _ { n ^ { w } } ^ { w } ) }$ be the vectors of locations of products offered through the BM and online channels, respectively, where $n ^ { j } = \vert { \bf b } ^ { j } \vert \ \mathrm { f o r } j = s , w$ While selecting a product, each customer considers the set of products available to the class she belongs to. For example, customers that purchase only from the BM channel consider only $\mathbf { b } ^ { s }$ , whereas omni-channel customers consider both $\mathbf { b } ^ { \check { s } }$ and $\mathbf { b } ^ { w }$ . Let $A ^ { j , j }$ be used to denote the channel disutility for customers who are open to purchasing products from channel $j .$ Consequently, the utility of a customer who is located at x and purchases product $b _ { i } ^ { j }$ is equal to $Z - A ^ { j , j } - r _ { i } ^ { j } - | x - b _ { i } ^ { j } |$ . Unlike Sections 3 and $^ { 4 , }$ we allow $A ^ { j , j }$ to be nonzero for generalization. Observe that this customer could either be an omni-channel customer or an only channel j customer.

Let $p _ { i } ^ { j , j }$ and $p _ { i } ^ { j , o }$ be the market share of product i of channel $j , \ j = s , w ,$ among customers that purchase exclusively from channel $j$ and omni-channel customers, respectively. The total expected profit from an assortment, $( \mathbf { b } ^ { s } , \mathbf { \dot { b } } ^ { w } )$ , in which the ith product of channel j is sold at a price of $r _ { i } ^ { j }$ is equal to

$$
\begin{array}{l} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n ^ {s}} \big ((r _ {i} ^ {s} - c ^ {s}) (\lambda^ {s} p _ {i} ^ {s, s} + \lambda^ {o} p _ {i} ^ {s, o}) - G \big) \\ \qquad + \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n ^ {w}} \big ((r _ {i} ^ {w} - c ^ {w}) (\lambda^ {w} p _ {i} ^ {w, w} + \lambda^ {o} p _ {i} ^ {w, o}) - \delta G \big). \end{array}
$$

The complete model is as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \max \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n ^ {s}} \big ((r _ {i} ^ {s} - c ^ {s}) (\lambda^ {s} p _ {i} ^ {s, s} + \lambda^ {o} p _ {i} ^ {s, o}) - G \big) \\ \qquad + \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n ^ {w}} \big ((r _ {i} ^ {w} - c ^ {w}) (\lambda^ {w} p _ {i} ^ {w, w} + \lambda^ {o} p _ {i} ^ {w, o}) - \delta G \big) \end{array}
$$

subject to

$$
\mathbf {b} ^ {s}, \mathbf {b} ^ {w} \in \mathcal {B}, \quad \mathbf {r} ^ {j} \in [ c ^ {j}, Z - A ^ {j, j} ] ^ {n ^ {j}}, j = s, w,
$$

$$
n ^ {j} = | \mathbf {b} ^ {j} |, j = s, w,
$$

where B is as defined in Section 2.3.

The model is difficult to examine analytically, so the results in this section are obtained computationally using a dynamic program. (The program and associated details are available in Section EC.17 in the online appendix.) Using the program, we conduct a sensitivity analysis with respect to six parameters, $A ^ { s , s } , \lambda ^ { s } , \lambda ^ { w } , \lambda ^ { o } , \bar { G }$ and $\delta ,$ for both uniformly and triangular distributed customer preferences. (Sample assortments are available in Tables EC.8 and EC.9 in the online appendix for different values of $\lambda ^ { s }$ and $\lambda ^ { w }$ when customer preferences are triangular distributed and in Table EC.10 of the online appendix for different values of G when customer preferences are uniformly distributed.) The experiments indicate that many insights from Sections 3 and 4 continue to hold. The following is a list of those insights.

• Depending on whether a product is purchased by omni-channel customers, an optimal assortment comprises two types of products. The first type of products is purchased by omni-channel customers as well as customers who only shop from the channel the product is sold through. In contrast, the second type of products is purchased only by customers who shop from the channel the product is sold through. This classification is analogous to the categorization of products into cus tomized and omni-channel products in the previous two sections.

• Optimal price for any product exceeds the price that maximizes its gross profit.

• Optimal price for a more popular product is greater than a less popular product when both products are sold through the same channel.

• Optimal assortment may include common products as well as products that are sold through only one channel.

• Niche products need not be sold only from the online channel, and we see assortments in which it is optimal to sell niche products through the BM channel. Moreover, it is optimal to sell popular products from both channels. Similar to Section 4, the channel with lower fixed cost and more favorable per-product profit (through margin potential and class size) appears to be more suitable for niche products.

Another interesting observation from the experiments (especially, Table EC.9 in the online appendix) is the strategic role played by common products. Such products can be used to maximize the number of omnichannel customers that shop through the channel with greater margin. To see this, consider Table EC.9 in the online appendix, for which the channel with greater margin potential is the BM channel. The trick is to choose prices for common products to ensure greater margin in the BM channel while making sure that omni-channel customers derive greater utility from it. For example, when $\lambda ^ { w } = 2 0$ , consider the common product located at 0.80. The price of the product is 0.17 in the BM channel and 0.16 in the online channel, which implies respective margins of 0.12 and 0.10. Furthermore, because $A ^ { s , s } = 0 . \bar { 0 8 }$ and $A ^ { w , w } = 0 . 1 0$ , every omnichannel customer located in (0.75, 0.85) obtains greater utility from the product sold through the BM channel. The identical product location maximizes the number of omni-channel customers assigned to the BM-channel product.

One consequence of the use of common products to direct omni-channel customers to the BM channel is that the only online customers are not entirely covered. Continuing with the previous example, none of the online customers located between 0.74 and 0.76 is covered, even though all the only-BM and omni-channe customers between 0.71 and 0.80 are covered. As long as the size of the only-online pool is relatively small, the loss of profit from uncovered only-online customers can be ignored. However, as $\lambda ^ { w }$ becomes sizable, the profit loss can become significant. As a result, the coverage for only-online customers increases as $\lambda ^ { w }$ increases. Although we still observe common products, we also see greater assignment of omni-channel customers to online channel products. In Table EC.9 of the online appendix, when $\lambda ^ { w } = 2 0$ , online-channel products have a market share of 24.67% among omnichannel customers, which increases to 35.64% when $\lambda ^ { w } = 6 0$ . To summarize, a key tradeoff for an omnichannel retailer while designing an assortment when some customers are open to shopping from only one channel is the loss of profit by insufficient coverage of the channel with lower margin potential among customers who shop only from that channel versus the loss of profit from omni-channel customers when its coverage is increased.

Finally, the insights presented in this section continue to hold when the discrete form of the locational choice model is used. See Table EC.11 in the online appendix for sample optimal assortments with respect to $\hat { \lambda } ^ { w }$ when customer preferences are triangular distributed.

## 6. Conclusions and Managerial Implications

In this study, we examine the optimal assortment and product prices for a retailer that sells products through both brick-and-mortar and online channels. Customers are heterogeneous in their preference for both channel and products, and we capture this feature through the locational choice model. Given this framework, we build a model to maximize the total profit from both channels by jointly identifying the optimal assortment and price vector.

A list of key actionable insights from the study is as follows:

1. Retailers should think of the omni-channel assortment as a portfolio consisting of (up to) two types of products. The first type (omni-channel products) should be priced in an attractive manner so that even those customers who do not prefer the channel through which the product is sold consider purchasing such a product. The second type of products (customized products) are only targeted toward the customers who prefer the channel through which the product is sold and their price is usually higher.

Omni-channel products should be sold through a channel if the fixed cost of including a product in the assortment is high and the number of customers that prefer that channel is small. Of course, customers who prefer the other channel should also be open to buying products from this channel. In contrast, when the number of customers who prefer a channel becomes large or the fixed cost is small or customers who prefer the other channel do so strongly, then it is optimal to sell customized products from the channel (Observations 1–5). We refer to this approach as customer-based framework, and it can be easily related to the well-known product based framework, which we describe next.

2. In the product-based framework, each product is either sold through one channel or both channels. It results in three forms of the optimal assortment: non, partially, or completely overlapping subassortments for the two channels. It is possible to connect this framework with the customer-based framework. In the customer-based framework, if every product is an omni-channel product, then each product should be sold from one channel, which results in nonoverlap ping subassortments. In contrast, if every product is customized and the two channels have similar parameters, then the subassortments are completely overlapping. Otherwise, the subassortments may partially overlap (discussion following Observation 5).

3. The conventional wisdom, which is rooted in the long-tail theory (Anderson 2004), that niche products be sold through the online channel is not always true. In particular, when the fixed cost of including a product in the assortment is higher for the online channel and more customers prefer the BM channel than the online channel, then selling niche products through the BM channel may generate greater profit than selling them according to the conventional wisdom (Proposition 11). (It is optimal to sell popular products through both channels.)

4. The price for a product should be greater than the price that optimizes the individual profit of the product (Propositions 4 and 7). Furthermore, a more popular product should be sold at a price premium compared with another product with lower popularity when both products are sold through the same channel (Propositions 9 and 10). Moreover, the prices should be chosen so that the market share of the more popular product remains greater than the other product (Proposition 9). The insight that a more popular product should be sold at a higher price, however, does not apply to products sold through different channels; a product with greater popularity may be sold at a lower price compared with a less popular product sold through the other channel. w

These insights hold both when the retailer has complete flexibility to choose products $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , for private label products) and when it has limited flexibility (e.g., when it has to choose which variants produced by a manufacturer to stock). The insights also appear to hold when there exist customers who exclusively purchase from one channel.

Many retailers choose to sell products at the same price in both channels. Modifying our model to ensure identical prices in both channels will be an interesting extension of our work. Another future direction is to examine the optimal assortment when prices could be different across channels but identical for all products sold through a channel.
