# The Power of Conversation: Analyzing the Impact of Starter Response on Backer Accumulation in Crowdfunding

Haoyan Sun,<sup>a</sup> Weijia You,<sup>b,</sup>\* Junchao (Jason) Li,<sup>c</sup> Han Zhang<sup>d,e</sup>

Abstract. The unique dynamics of the crowdfunding process have attracted substantial scholarly attention over the past decade. In this study, we investigate how crowdfunding campaign starters’ response to potential backers in the discussion board influences backer accumulation in reward-based crowdfunding platforms. We suggest that the starter response serves to reduce information asymmetry and signal the positive qualities of the campaign starter(s). Accordingly, we shed light on the threefold implications of starter response in (a) directly attracting more backers, (b) strengthening the herding effect (i.e., the extent to which cumulative backers help attract new backers), and (c) mitigating the goal-attainment effect (i.e., the decline in new backers after campaign goal attainment) in reward-based crowdfunding. Utilizing a unique panel data set of JD Crowdfunding, we found a positive relationship between starter response and new backers the next day. Moreover, our data showed that the volume of cumulative backers was positively related to new backers the next day (i.e., showcasing the herding effect), and the starter response served to amplify this positive herding effect. Last, our analyses revealed that campaign goal attainment was negatively related to daily new backers (i.e., supporting the goal-attainment effect), but starter response served to mitigate this negative effect. We discuss the implications of our findings for the literature on crowdfunding.

## 1. Introduction

Crowdfunding refers to a variety of ways in which people can seek funds for their endeavors by drawing on relatively small contributions from a mass audience, mostly via the internet (Schwienbacher and Larralde 2010; Burtch et al. 2013, 2018). Over the past decade, crowdfunding has rapidly surged and has become an important source of financial support for early stage entrepreneurs, budding artists, and social activists. The crowdfunding phenomenon has intrigued scholars from various fields because of its increasing market value and social influence. Additionally, crowdfunding is unique when contrasted with more traditional financial intermediaries such as angel investors, venture capitalists, and foundations. Understanding what serves to shape crowdfunding backers’ decisions and attract their support has important implications for both academic researchers and practitioners.

Whereas crowdfunding represents a promising platform that helps connect entrepreneurs with a mass audience and thus helps facilitate entrepreneurial success and innovation, it is also plagued by substantial information asymmetry where potential backers often have to make pledging decisions with limited informa tion about a campaign (Chan et al. 2020, Xiao et al. 2021, Jiang et al. 2022). In reward-based crowdfunding platforms, although campaign starters use narratives, pictures, and videos to present their products, business plans, and credentials, potential backers may still be unclear about the quality of the product, as well as the trustworthiness, dedication, and ability of the campaign starters to fulfill what they plan to do. Such information asymmetry and the related uncertainties not only hinder potential backers from making informed decisions but also plague high-quality campaigns if the starters fail to quickly signal the quality of their campaigns/products to potential backers. Existing literature thus has shed light on how a wide range of factors serve as necessary signals of campaign quality and contribute to crowdfunding success (e.g., Mollick 2014, Agrawal et al. 2015, Li et al. 2017, Anglin et al. 2018, Kuppuswamy and Bayus 2018, Chakraborty and Swinney 2020, Xiao et al. 2021).

This research seeks to advance this line of research by investigating the effect of starter response in reward-based crowdfunding platforms. In more traditional investment channels such as angel investors and venture capitals, investors rarely make their investment decisions by only looking at the pitch, business proposals, or product description; instead, they rely heavily on face-to-face interactions with entrepreneurs, where they not only ask questions to reduce information asymmetry about the business plan or product, but also seek to gauge a sense of the entrepreneurial team regarding their capability, dedication, and trustworthiness, which make a fundamental difference in turning a good idea into a successful endeavor. In the crowdfunding platform, such face-to-face interactions with campaign starters are absent. As an alternative, crowdfunding platforms provide a discussion section where prospective backers and campaign starters can communicate with each other in public by asking and answering questions. Given the importance of questions and answers (Q&A) in reducing information asymmetry and influencing decisions from traditional investors, we have reasons to believe that campaign starters’ communications in the crowdfunding discussion board play a critical role in influencing potential backers’ decisions.

Specifically, we suggest that starter response serves to reduce information asymmetry by providing important information of interest beyond the initial campaign pitch to potential backers and signaling positive qualities of the campaign starters. Accordingly, we propose the threefold implications of starter response in crowdfunding backer accumulations. First, we suggest that a greater volume of starter responses can directly influence more people to support and contribute to the campaign. Second, we posit that starter response can strengthen the “herding effect” (e.g., Zhang and Liu 2012, Xiao et al. 2021) in crowdfunding platforms. Crowdfunding represents a classic context for herding behavior, where individuals possess limited private information and try to infer information from others’ behaviors to advise their decisions (Banerjee 1992; Bikhchandani et al. 1992, 1998). As a result, researchers have observed a herding dynamic where more cumulative backers/funds beget more new backers/funds and so on (Zhang and Liu 2012, Colombo et al. 2015, Vismara 2016, Xiao et al. 2021, Jiang et al. 2022). However, the existence and strength of a herding pattern can be subject to a variety of contingencies (e.g., Courtney et al. 2017, Chan et al. 2020, Xiao et al. 2021, Jiang et al. 2022, Chakraborty et al. 2023, Zhang et al. 2023). In this research, we suggest that a greater volume of starter responses serves to strengthen the herding effect in reward-based crowdfund ing platforms. Last, we propose that starter responses can mitigate the “goal-attainment effect” in crowdfunding. The preset goal of a campaign plays an instrumental role in the crowdfunding process, as only those campaigns that exceed the preset goal can collect the pledged funds from backers. Importantly, researchers found that potential backers not only are less motivated to fund a campaign, but also contribute smaller amounts of money to a campaign after it attains its goal (versus before) (Kuppuswamy and Bayus 2017, Dai and Zhang 2019, Li and Wang 2019). We argue that this drop in pledged backers after goal attainment—coined as the goal-attainment effect—can be mitigated when there is a greater volume of starter responses. In sum, this research seeks to shed light on how starter response serves as an important driver and moderator of the backer accumulation on reward-based crowdfunding platforms.

Our empirical investigation in this paper pertains to starter communication with backers on a reward-based crowdfunding platform. The data comprise over 360,000 comments posted on a panel of 1,677 campaigns over 15 months between April 2015 and July 2016. We capture crowdfunding-related attributes and funding outcomes in a daily panel format. We adopt a fixed effect model with an instrumental variable to analyze how starter response influences the daily added new backers, controlling for different types of backer comments, as well as a range of other campaign factors. Our findings first reveal a positive correlation between starter responses and the acquisition of new backers the following day. Additionally, our analysis indicates that the number of cumulative backers is positively associated with new backers on a subsequent day, thereby supporting the herding effect, and this association is strengthened by a higher volume of starter responses. Lastly, we observe that reaching the campaign goal has a negative impact on the daily acquisition of new backers, confirming the goal-attainment effect. However, this negative impact is lessened with a higher volume of starter responses.

Our study makes several contributions as follows. First, it contributes to crowdfunding research by high lighting starter response as an essential signal of campaign quality, which helps attract and persuade backers. Besides its direct impact on backer accumulation, we also reveal (a) how it complements the vital signal of cumulative backers and strengthens the herding momentum in crowdfunding; and (b) how it mitigates the goalattainment effect where backers have lower motivation to support a campaign as the campaign reaches its goal. Although Xiao et al. (2021) investigated how campaign starters’ communicative messages influenced backer accumulation, they combined starter updates and starter responses to backer comments into one variable, analyzing both for-profit and nonprofit campaigns and finding that starter communications weakened the herding effect. We believe that the starter response is unique and different from updates, as it better mirrors the Q&A in more traditional investing channels. Moreover, for-profit campaigns and nonprofit campaigns can exhibit different dynamics as potential backers have different motives (Dai and Zhang 2019). Thus, our research provides a more focused investigation on how starter response influences the crowdfunding outcome among for-profit, reward-based campaigns.

Second, our research contributes to the ongoing investigations of backer/fund accumulation dynamics in crowdfunding. These dynamics have been central to academic inquiries into crowdfunding (e.g., Burtch et al. 2013, Colombo et al. 2015, Vismara 2016, Chakraborty et al. 2023). Although previous research has provided strong evidence on the herding effect (e.g., Zhang and Liu 2012, Zhang et al. 2023) and goalattainment effect (Kuppuswamy and Bayus 2017, Dai and Zhang 2019, Li and Wang 2019) in crowdfunding, researchers also note that these effects’ strengths vary across different contingencies (e.g., Courtney et al. 2017, Dai and Zhang 2019, Li and Wang 2019, Chan et al. 2020, Jiang et al. 2022, Chakraborty et al. 2023). Our research adds to this literature by showing how starter response plays a key role in not only strengthening the desirable herding effect, but also mitigating the undesirable goal-attainment effect.

Third, our research contributes to understanding managerial responses and customer service on digital platforms. Prior studies highlight the importance of responding to customer comments to benefit business performance (Evans et al. 2012, Kumar et al. 2018, Mousavi et al. 2020). This line of research focuses on the context where the platform allows review for products or services and highlights the need to manage customer experience and expectations as an important signal to potential customers. Relatedly, social media has become an increasingly critical platform for businesses to address customer complaints and engage in customer relationship management (Gunarathne et al. 2017, 2018; Kumar and Qiu 2022; Al Balawi et al. 2023; He et al. 2024). Our work extends these findings to the crowdfunding context, demonstrating the value of responding to potential backers’ support, suggestions, criticism, and inquiries in the crowdfunding platform, even when no product or service has been delivered yet. Importantly, our work goes beyond demonstrating the direct effect of managerial responses and showcases how such responses can further influence backer accumulation dynamics (i.e., strengthening the herding effect and mitigating motivation loss). As such, our work provides a more comprehensive understanding of the benefits of managerial responses.

## 2.1. The Crowdfunding Literature

The extant literature on crowdfunding has examined various factors related to crowdfunding success, such as campaign setup (e.g., goals, reward categories) (Burtch et al. 2013), campaign presentation (e.g., video pitch quality, pictures, language in campaign descriptions), and characteristics of the campaign starters (e.g., gender, race, credentials, and social network) (e.g., Mollick 2014, Allison et al. 2017, Greenberg and Mollick 2017, Li et al. 2017, Younkin and Kuppuswamy 2018). Although this line of research substantially enriches our understanding of crowdfunding, much less is known about how the interaction between potential backers and campaign starters can influence outcomes. On crowdfunding platforms, potential backers can leave comments to campaign starters to seek further information (such as product specifics and delivery updates), advise product development, or show support and compliments; campaign starters can, in turn, respond to the comments and engage backers. Extant literature has discussed the importance of communication on digital platforms, and there is rising attention on the impact of communication through the process of crowdfunding. For example, researchers have shed light on the positive influence of “update”— a one-way communication from the fundraiser to eliminate the information asymmetry—on funding outcomes (Lagazio and Querci 2018, Mejia et al. 2019, Yin et al. 2019). Examining the two-way communications between borrowers and lenders on peer-to-peer funding platforms, Xu and Chau (2018) found that borrower (or fund-seekers) response was positively associated with funding success. More recently, Xiao et al. (2021) examined the effect of communicative messages in crowdfunding by combining updates and creator comments and found that the frequency of communicative messages had a positive effect on backer contributions but attenuated the herding effect, whereas these effects varied across different topics. Our research contributes to this line of research by not only demonstrating the importance of starter response as a positive signal for reward-based crowdfunding projects, but also shedding light on how it moderates the backer accumula tion pattern that has long intrigued researchers.

## 2.2. Herding Behavior in Crowdfunding

Our research relates to herding literature (Banerjee 1992; Bikhchandani et al. 1992, 1998) and particularly its exhibition in crowdfunding regarding the backer accumulation pattern. The literature of economics and social psychology has long demonstrated how individual decisions are influenced by others’ decisions/ actions, as individuals engage in observational learning and infer information from others’ behaviors, hoping to help overcome their own information asymmetry, reduce uncertainties, and make better decisions (Banerjee 1992;

Bikhchandani et al. 1992, 1998). For example, customers often choose options marked as “popular,” such as certain dishes in a restaurant (Cai et al. 2009) or online vendors (Tucker and Zhang 2011). Herzenstein et al. (2011) revealed strong herding behaviors in online peer-to-peer loan auctions, as lenders exhibited a greater tendency to bid on an auction with more bidders. Similarly, in microloan markets (Zhang and Liu 2012) and debt-based crowdfunding platforms (Jiang et al. 2022), lenders inferred borrower creditworthiness by observing peer lending decisions, such that well-funded borrower listings tend to attract more funding in the next period. In particular, in reward-based crowdfunding (our research context), there’s substantial evidence documenting the herding effect in backer/fund accumulation patterns (Colombo et al. 2015, Thies et al. 2016, Vismara 2016, Chan et al. 2020, Xiao et al. 2021), as a larger number of cumulative backers/funds at a time point further attracts a greater amount of new backers/funds.

The herding pattern of backer/fund accumulation, however, is not without its critics and counterevidence. To begin with, researchers have found that, particularly with crowdfunding projects that have a stronger focus on prosocial impacts (Burtch et al. 2013, Dai and Zhang 2019), a greater amount of cumulative backer/fund may reduce potential backers’ perceived impact of their contribution and thus crowd out future contributions (Andreoni 1990). Recent studies have also identified convex or U-shaped pledge patterns in crowdfunding (Chan et al. 2020, Chakraborty et al. 2023, Zhang et al. 2023). Relatedly, Kim et al. (2022) revealed different dynamics of the crowdfunding process at different stages, including friendfunding, stagnation, and acceleration. Furthermore, researchers have also started to unveil how the backer/fund accumulation pattern is moderated by certain factors. For example, Colombo et al. (2015) found that the internal social capital proponents have developed inside the crowdfunding community may helped ignite a herding pattern of fund accumulation. Xiao et al. (2021) showed that starter communications could attenuate herding momentum by reducing the informational value of cumulative backers. Jiang et al. (2022) demonstrated that herding momentum was weaker when the pledged backers used real-seeming usernames rather than anonymous ones. Our study of backer accumulation patterns in reward-based crowdfunding projects lends support to the herding effect in crowdfunding and elucidates how starter response serves as an important booster of the herding effect.

## 2.3. Goal Attainment and Motivation Loss in Crowdfunding

The prelaunch fundraising goal of a crowdfunding campaign is crucial in influencing the dynamics of supporter engagement. Crowdfunding platforms predominantly adopt an all-or-nothing funding model, where campaign starters receive the funds pledged only if the project meets or exceeds its financial target within the campaign’s duration. Failure to achieve the goal results in the forfeiture of all pledged funds. Elevated funding goals may deter potential backers due to the increased likelihood of the campaign not reaching its target, thus negating their financial contributions (Mollick 2014). Rational behavior would, therefore, suggest a higher likelihood of support for campaigns that have already surpassed their goals. Conversely, individuals engaged in crowdfunding are also driven by prosocial motives, aiming to contribute to the success of a venture or campaign (Kuppuswamy and Bayus 2017, Dai and Zhang 2019, Li and Wang 2019). When a campaign has already reached its goal, potential backers may perceive their contribution as having a negligible incremental impact, redirecting their support to campaigns with a greater need for funds. As a result, campaigns often experience a deceleration in funding and backer accumulation post-goal-attainment as backers become less likely to contribute, and those who do contribute tend to pledge smaller amounts (Kuppuswamy and Bayus 2017, Dai and Zhang 2019, Li and Wang 2019). This observed decline in motivation and subsequent backing after a goal is reached—coined as the goal-attainment effect— may impede the progress of otherwise successful campaigns by stifling additional fundraising necessary for further development. Although researchers have shed light on when this goal-attainment effect tends to be more pronounced—such as in public-good projects that evoke stronger prosocial motivation (Dai and Zhang 2019, Li and Wang 2019) or in projects that are led by a single starter (Dai and Zhang 2019)—these studies offer limited guidance on what campaign starters can do to counteract the decline in backing post-goal-attainment. Our research aims to illuminate how campaign starters engagement with backer commentary may serve as an effective strategy to mitigate the goal-attainment effect.

## 2.4. Literature on Managerial Responses and Customer Service on the Digital Platform

As we focus on how starter response influences and moderates the backer accumulation pattern, we also relate our work to studies on the impact of business owners’ responses on user satisfaction and business performance. For example, Evans et al. (2012) conducted an experiment to test the effect of owner responses to restaurant reviews, finding that customers were least likely to visit a restaurant with no responses to negative reviews. However, positive and constructive responses from restaurant owners could somewhat correct the bad impression caused by negative reviews. Additionally, Kumar et al. (2018) investigated the management responses to Yelp reviews, showing that they substantially impacted not only the focal business performance, but also nearby business performance. Most recently, Mousavi et al. (2020) analyzed Telecom customer sentiments on Twitter and business owner responses to estimate the parameters in an analytical model, finding that effective digita care requires careful examination and response to customer tweets. Research on online service has shown that management response in online communities serves as a service recovery, significantly impacting customer satisfaction by moderating customer perceptions of justice and fairness (McColl-Kennedy and Sparks 2003, Gu and Ye 2014). This is termed peerinduced fairness, where individuals use peers as a reference (Ho and Su 2009). In a related vein, companies increasingly perform customer service and customer relationship management on social media platforms (such as Facebook and Twitter). Social media offers brands an unprecedented opportunity to engage with customers in real time, providing immediate support and fostering stronger relationships. However, this shift also presents challenges such as public scrutiny, the need for rapid response times, and the risk of neg ative feedback spreading widely. Researchers found that firms are more likely to respond to customers complaints on social media when the complaining customers have more followers (Gunarathne et al. 2017, 2018). Relatedly, Gunarathne et al. (2022) also found that African-American customers are less likely to receive a response to their complaints than otherwise similar White customers. Additionally, companies are increasingly steering interactions toward private messaging to maintain control over customer interactions and minimize public exposure, though customers often prefer public channels to exert pressure on brands (He et al. 2024). Research also suggests that crisis situations can impact firms’ social media engagement strategies, with companies increasing informativeness but reduc ing the timeliness and attentiveness of their responses during crises (Al Balawi et al. 2023). Similarly, in crowdfunding, potential backers could also observe others’ comments and starter responses and develop their perception of the campaign, which further influ ences their funding decision.

## 3. Theory Development

3.1. Impact of Starter Response in Crowdfunding Compared with traditional investment-seeking chan nels such as venture capital and angel investors, crowdfunding backers often make funding decisions with limited information about the product and the entrepreneurial team. Without face-to-face interaction, crowdfunding backers generally lack the chance to ask important questions that help them better develop a sense of the viability of the product/business model, nor can they gauge a good sense of the entrepreneurs characteristics beyond the listed credentials, such as gender, physical look, education, and experiences. As a substitute for face-to-face Q&A with entrepreneurs in the venture capital and angel investor context, the discussion board of a crowdfunding project is an essential channel where starters can reduce information asymmetry by answering backers’ questions and signaling the quality and the credentials of the team.

First, the starter response provides important additional information about the crowdfunding project beyond the initial description. As potential backers review the project description, they may have important questions or doubts—such as product specifics or expected delivery dates—that need to be addressed before they decide to invest. This mirrors traditional investment channels, where venture capitalists and angel investors engage in Q&As with the entrepreneurs beyond their pitch. Research in information systems has shown that Q&A content on platforms connecting service providers and clients can reduce information asymmetry (e.g., Khurana et al. 2019, Peng et al. 2020, Pu et al. 2022). Some potential backers may proactively ask questions on the discussion board and wait for answers, whereas others may skip the project if they have doubts, given the many alternatives on crowdfunding platforms, and they might deem it not worthwhile to devote the attention and time to asking and waiting. Thus, a starter response serves as a crucial means to not only provide additional information and address questions for those who ask, but also, perhaps more importantly, to preemptively resolve doubts for a broader audience who may lack the time or patience to inquire before moving on to other projects.

Second, the starter response serves as a crucial signal of the credibility of the campaign starter(s). As the famous saying goes, “I don’t invest in ideas; I invest in people.” Accumulated research in entrepreneurship has long documented the importance of perceived entrepreneurs’ characteristics in shaping investors’ decisions. Investment decisions concerning early start-ups often hinge heavily on investors’ impressions or perceptions of entrepreneurs, such as their human and social capital (Burton et al. 2002, Shane and Stuart 2002, Baum and Silverman 2004, Hsu 2007, Unger et al. 2011), education credentials (Bernstein et al. 2017), professional roles (Shane and Stuart 2002, Hsu 2007, Unger et al. 2011), and ascribed identities such as gender (Brooks et al. 2014, Kanze et al. 2018, Guzman and Kacperczyk 2019) and race (Younkin and Kuppuswamy 2018). Without faceto-face interaction, starter response to backer comments becomes an essential window for potential backers to gauge a sense of the campaign starter(s), particularly regarding their conscientiousness and openness to negative feedback from customers. Responding promptly to questions and inquiries shows that the starter team is responsible and serious about the project and serving customers. The response practice functions similarly to an online reputation management strategy, building trust and engagement through social interactions with customers (Proserpio and Zervas 2017, Kumar et al. 2018, Khurana et al. 2019). Conscientiousness—the extent to which one is responsible, motivated, and selforganized—is among the most critical personality characteristics that drive higher performance and success, especially in the world of entrepreneurship, where failure is common. Given the challenges and difficulties that lie ahead for new ventures, a more conscientious and dedicated entrepreneurial team is a highly important signal that indicates a greater likelihood of the entrepreneurial team fulfilling their promise (i.e., delivering the product with high quality promptly). Additionally, starter response signals how campaign starters handle negative feedback. While some starters may ignore certain critical comments, viewing them as unhelpful, others may see them as valuable customer feedback for improvement. A more responsive approach to criticism signals the starter’s (or starters’) ability to view negative feedback from clients as opportunities and, thus, their ability to learn and improve continuously. Given the above reasoning, we propose:

Hypothesis 1. Starter response in discussion boards has a positive effect on attracting potential backers to fund the project.

## 3.2. Starter Response Strengthening the Herding Effect

Beyond establishing the starter response’s effect in attracting backers, as it reduces information asymmetry and signals project quality, we believe that starter response can also bolster the effect of other important signals. One of the most documented, dynamic signals of project quality in crowdfunding is how many back ers have already contributed or how much funding has been accumulated at the time of decision for a particular backer (e.g., Burtch et al. 2013, Colombo et al. 2015, Chan et al. 2020, Xiao et al. 2021). Economists and social psychologists found that individuals often look to others’ decisions and behaviors when making their own decisions (Banerjee 1992; Bikhchandani et al. 1992, 1998). With limited private information and subject to information asymmetry and uncertainty, individuals tend to infer the utility of taking certain actions by observing others’ choices, especially when they believe that others’ choices may be guided by useful information. If an individual deems others’ decisions more informative than their own private knowledge, they will likely follow, resulting in herding behavior (Banerjee 1992; Bikhchandani et al. 1992, 1998).

There is ample evidence of herding behavior in reward-based crowdfunding platforms (see Colombo et al. 2015, Vismara 2016, Chan et al. 2020, and Xiao et al. 2021). The number of cumulative backers— prominently displayed on crowdfunding webpages— represents crucial information influencing potential backers’ decisions for two main reasons. First, backer decisions on crowdfunding platforms involve substantial uncertainty and information asymmetry (Chan et al. 2020, Xiao et al. 2021, Jiang et al. 2022). Although investment in new ventures or new ideas generally entails uncertainty, crowdfunding project starters typically provide even less detailed information to potential crowdfunding backers than is given to venture capitalists. Thus, backers with limited private information often rely on cues from observing others’ choices. Second, in most cases, potential backers on crowdfunding platforms may lack the knowledge, expertise, or time to evaluate the technical specifics—both the product’s technological features and the business viability—of a crowdfunding project (Li et al. 2017). According to the elaboration likelihood model of persuasion (Petty and Cacioppo 1981, 1986), when being persuaded, individuals with higher ability and motivation tend to evaluate and scrutinize the directly relevant information in support of a proposal (such as technological specifics and business plan), whereas individuals with lower ability and motivation rely on simple cues. In crowdfunding, the number of cumulative backers serves as a positive and powerful peripheral cue that signals project quality, thereby influencing potential backers’ likelihood of contributing.

We argue that a starter response can help bolster the herding behavior on crowdfunding platforms. As noted, a herding effect often results from the process of observational learning and informational cascade (Bikhchandani et al. 1992, 1998).<sup>1</sup> As previously argued, starter response on discussion boards, by providing additional project details and demonstrating the campaign starters’ positive characteristics, serves as an important signal of the project quality. With this positive signal, the observation of a larger number of cumulative backers further reinforces project quality (Courtney et al. 2017), effectively persuading potential backers to fund the project. However, a larger number of cumulative backers may be less persuasive if potential backers possess a negative view of the project based on low starter response (i.e., leaving questions unaddressed or showing a lack of conscientiousness or openness to feedback). In this case, individuals with negative private information may rely on their own judgment rather than the “wisdom” of the crowd. Therefore, we argue that a strong starter response complements the signal from the pledged backers, strengthening the herding effect on crowdfunding platforms. This complementary perspective aligns with the finding of Courtney et al. (2017) that startup-originated signals (in terms of the use of media and crowdfunding experience) and third-party endorsements (the sentiment expressed in backer comments) can validate and complement each other in driving crowdfunding success. As such, we propose:

Hypothesis 2. Starter responses in discussion boards can positively moderate backer herding behavior, such that the relationship between cumulative backers and newly increased backers is more positive when there are more responses from starters.

## 3.3. Starter Response Mitigating the Goal-Attainment Effect

We posit that starter responses can not only amplify the herding effect, but also alleviate the goal-attainment effect—the decline in new backers as the campaign meets its funding goal. Prosocial motives, which are the wish to have a positive effect on others, have been acknowledged as an important motivator for individual engagement in crowdfunding (Dai and Zhang 2019, Li and Wang 2019). This prosocial motive is prominent not only in contributions to public-good initiatives that benefit the welfare of others, but also in reward-based crowdfunding, where backers expect to receive products or services in exchange for their support. Potential backers often perceive their contribution as more impactful when helping a campaign reach its target (Higgins and Brendl 1995, Heath et al. 1999), thus guaranteeing its success and securing the funds pledged under the common all-or-nothing funding model of crowdfunding platforms. In contrast, once the fundraising goal is met, additional contributions do not affect campaign success. It has been consistently documented that backer engagement and funding peak as the campaign nears its goal, but slow down significantly after the goal is achieved (Kuppuswamy and Bayus 2017, Dai and Zhang 2019, Li and Wang 2019).

This goal-attainment effect presents a challenge to campaign starters who seek to sustain momentum beyond their initial target. Yet, current scholarship offers scant strategies to counter it. We suggest that a starter response to backer comments can alleviate this effect. The genesis of the goal-attainment effect lies in the diminishing perceived social impact by potential backers once a goal is met, but this perception is malleable. For example, Cryder et al. (2013) showed that providing more tangible information about charity activities could enhance potential donors’ perceived impact, thereby increasing their propensity to contribute. Similarly, Toure´-Tillery and Fishbach (2017) observed that donations were perceived as more impactful and were more likely when directed toward geographically proximate beneficiaries. Based on these findings, we posit that campaign starters engagement with backers’ comments may bolster perceived impact in two significant ways. First, when starters actively engage with responses to backers or potential backers after the campaign reaches its goal, it signals to the potential backers—whether asking questions or observing the Q&A—that their potential contributions remain valued. This active engagement counters the belief that postgoal contributions are less meaningful, effectively mitigating the drop in pledged backers after goal attainment. Second, through the discourse generated by backer comments and starter responses, a sense of interpersonal connectedness may emerge, potentially bridging the psychological distance inherent in the digital nature of crowdfunding. Such a sense of connectedness not only emerges in the backers who make the comments and get the response from starters, but also applies to the potential backers who observe the exchange and interaction between campaign starters and the community. This sense of connectedness between the campaign starters and the crowdfunding community—to which the potential backers belong—can enhance potential backers’ perceived impact of their contribution, even after a campaign reaches its goal (Toure´-Tillery and Fishbach 2017), which serves to alleviate the goal-attainment effect.

In conclusion, we contend that starter responses can effectively counteract the diminished perceived impact and the consequent decrease in potential backers’ willingness to contribute to postgoal-attainment. Consequently, an active engagement from campaign starters in responding to backer comments can buffer against the downturn in new backers following the achievement of campaign goals.

Hypothesis 3. Starter responses in discussion boards can mitigate the decline of new backers after campaign goal attainment, such that the negative relationship between campaign goal attainment and daily new backers is less negative when there are more responses from starters.

## 4. Research Context and Data 4.1. Background

Our empirical analysis is based on a data set obtained from JD Crowdfunding.<sup>2</sup> In 2015, JD Crowdfunding raised more than 450 million CNY (i.e., 65 million USD), accounting for 56.3% of China’s market share. In 2017, JD Crowdfunding raised over 2.4 billion CNY (i.e., 350 million USD) and was ranked first in the Chinese crowdfunding market. Similar to Kickstarter, JD Crowdfunding is an all-or-nothing platform, meaning that project starters obtain their raised funds only when the project achieves its funding goal. If the project fails to reach its funding goal or the starter quits during the funding period, all the raised money will be returned to the backers, who receive no product, even if they are still interested. Many starters also offer a lottery category, allowing backers to win a product for a small contribution (e.g., one dollar). During our data-collection period, the JD Crowdfunding platform listed seven main reward-based categories: Journalism, Household Appliances, Entertainment, Health, Tech nology, Design, and Miscellaneous. Each project page contains a project introduction, reward categories, fundraising progress, a discussion board, etc. Figure 2 illustrates a project page for a technology product.

We collected a daily panel data set from April 2015 to July 2016. Our study contains a sample of 1,677 projects that had concluded their crowdfunding campaign, among which 1,446 projects succeeded (i.e., raised more funds than their targeted goal) and 231 projects failed. The fundraising goals ranged from 1,000 CNY (160 USD) to 1 million CNY (160,000 USD), and the fundraising periods varied from 10 days to 60 days. To choose a potential instrumental variable (IV), we are looking to find a variable that may affect the outcome variable only through the endogenous variable, Starter Response, while not being systematically codetermined with the dependent variable. One such plausible IV is the measure of daily average responses provided by campaign starters that share similar campaign type and funding characteristics as the focal campaign starter (Peer\_Response). This approach is motivated by many studies that leverage external variation as an instrument to isolate the causal effect of treatment on outcomes (e.g., Lu et al. 2017), as well as studies in the online crowdfunding context $( \mathrm { e . g . , }$ Burtch et al. 2016, Lin et al. 2022). Yet, there is a possibility that the project starters with similar funding characteristics are potential competitors in the same market; this could also affect the focal starter’s backer attraction. To alleviate this concern, we only included a similar campaign that had already finished fundraising before the focal project had started. To explain the construction of the instrumental variable, we follow a three-step approach. In the first step, based on the project category, project duration, and funding goal, we used the k-means cluster method to construct similar groups of fundraisers. We find that the best k-class is four. In the second step, for each focal starter, we found the subset of the similar starter group based on the starters that had ended the campaign before the launch of the focal project. In the third step, we calculate the average number of starter responses of similar projects that have ended before the focal project’s launch for the same progressing day. This construction of IV theoretically justifies the homogeneity and exclusion restriction, as the instrument used could not directly affect the dependent variable, but only through its effect on the endogenous regressor. To further check whether the ${ \mathrm { I V } } ,$ Peer\_Response, satisfies other properties of IV in 2SLS, we ran multiple tests that check on relevance, weak instrument, and exogeneity. To check if the instrument is relevant and is not weak, we examine the first-stage regression F-statistic, which requires a score greater than 10 to be considered relevant. We used a weak identification test of the ${ \mathrm { I V } } ,$ the Cragg-Donald Wald F statistic. The F statistic is 791.81, which is significantly above the 5% maximal Stock-Yogo critical value of 16.38, suggesting that the instrumental variable has very strong relevance and is not weak (Stock et al. 2002). Because we have a just-identified model, Hansen’s J-test doesn’t work for our purpose of testing for the endogeneity of the regressor. Therefore, we ran the Durbin-Wu-Hausman test. We obtained a significant p-value, indicating that the endogenous regressor is indeed endogenous and the IV approach is necessary, which indirectly supports the exogeneity of the instrument variable.

We summarize the number of starter responses and the corresponding response ratio based on the different types of backer comments in Table 6. As shown in Table 6, the majority of backer comments were in the form of inquiries. Starters responded most frequently (about 61%) to inquiries compared with other backer comment types. Notably, starters responded to criticism and support at the same frequency (both at 0.38).

Our analysis results using different backer contents are presented in Table 7. The results on the main effect and moderating effect of starter response are consistent with our main empirical results. Interestingly, content analysis results provide more nuanced impli cations on the effect of a specific content. Criticism content usually carries negative emotions; thus, the negative effect is consistent with what we find from the one based on negative sentiment. However, support content has a negative effect on the backer attraction. One plausible reason is the so-called diffusion of responsibility in crowdfunding (Kuppuswamy and Bayus 2017), where prospective backers may turn to help other projects when they see the focal project has more existing support. Inquiry comments related to the product or campaign also hurt the funding outcome, likely because the questions raised regarding specific product issues may concern the prospective backer in their funding decision.

5.2.2. Propensity Score Matching on Project Level Outcome. As a robustness check on the main effect of starter response on funding outcome, we perform propensity-score matching based on two matched samples of campaigns; that is, based on a cross-sectional sample of all projects, we identify pairs of projects that are similar in many characteristics, such as project category, project duration, funding goal, whether video is included, whether there is prelaunch campaign warmup, and whether additional external links are included. We create a high-response group (the treatment group) if the project has starter responses at the top 25th percentile, whereas the bottom 25th percentile comprises the control group. We ended up having 426 projects in the treatment group and 428 projects in the control group. We perform one-to-one nearest-neighbor matching without replacement to identify the control and treatment observations in a common support region. To improve the quality of the matching, the caliper was set to 0.001. As a result, we identify 126 projects in the treatment group and 428 projects in the control group on support.

## 6. Theoretical and Managerial Implications

This study offers important implications to the crowdfunding literature, particularly the investigation of backer accumulation patterns in crowdfunding. First, we highlight that, in the absence of face-to-face Q&As used by traditional investors (such as angel investors and venture capitalists), starter responses to backer comments on crowdfunding platforms play an essential role in reducing information asymmetry for potential backers, signaling the credibility of the campaign starter team and fostering interpersonal connections between the campaign starters and potential crowdfunders. Reducing information asymmetry and assuring potential backers of the dedication and capability of the starter team have always been at the core of the inquiry of crowdfunding success. This has become particularly important after many incidents of campaign starters failing to deliver the promised product or delivering products with inferior quality or substantial delays. As the volume of crowdfunding campaigns continues to grow rapidly and crowdfunding backers are perhaps becoming more careful in pledging, communications from campaign starters may play an increasingly important role in influencing crowdfunding outcomes (e.g., Xiao et al. 2021).

Second, we contribute to the existing investigations of backer/funding accumulation patterns in crowdfunding, showcasing how starter response serves to alter the long-investigated herding effect and goalattainment effect. Regarding the herding effect, the existing investigations of crowdfunding backer/fund accumulation patterns have presented mixed evidence, with some documenting a general herding effect (e.g., Xiao et al. 2021, Jiang et al. 2022), some suggesting the existence of a crowding-out effect (e.g., Burtch et al. 2013), and others showcasing a U-shaped pattern (e.g., Chan et al. 2020, Kim et al. 2022, Chakraborty et al. 2023, Zhang et al. 2023). Relatedly, researchers have shed light on how video quality, starter preparedness, and starter passion, as well as situational urgency (Chan et al. 2020), starter communications (Xiao et al. 2021), and contributor anonymity (Jiang et al. 2022) moderate the relationship between prior backer/funding and sub sequent backer/contribution. Note that our findings to some extent, stand in contrast with the findings of Xiao et al. (2021) that starter communication (including updates and responses) attenuates the herding effect, which, as they speculated, occurred because starter com munication reduces the informational value of cumula tive backers. Our investigation presents a different case where the starter response strengthens the herding effect, which is consistent with accumulated research documenting how potential crowdfunding backers are looking for multiple positive signals that provide maxi mized assurance of campaign quality, such that the positive signals often strengthen each others’ effect on crowdfunding outcomes (e.g., Courtney et al. 2017, Li et al. 2017, Chan et al. 2020). In addition, we offer valu able insights into strategies that successful campaign starters can employ to mitigate the goal-attainment effect, thereby maximizing support from the crowd. Attaining the preset goal is crucial for crowdfunding campaigns, as it allows the collection of pledged funds. Nevertheless, research has highlighted a decrease in motivation among crowdfunders, influenced by their altruistic intentions, which can lead to a downturn in new backers and funding postgoal achievement (Dai and Zhang 2019). This goal-attainment effect is recog nized, yet strategies to counteract it remain underex plored. We propose that campaign starters’ engagement with the crowdfunding community via active responses in the discussion boards can create a personal connec tion with potential supporters. This strengthens thei perceived value of contributing, even after the campaign goal is met. Our findings indicate that such active engagement can counter the subsequent drop in new backers, deepening our understanding of the goalattainment effect and offering campaigners practical strategies to optimize financial support.

Third, our work contributes to a broader understanding of the role of managerial responses on digital platforms and customer service on social media. As highlighted in our prior literature review, existing studies have provided consistent evidence of the importance of managerial response to customers in digital platforms in terms of increasing customer satisfaction, attracting new customers, and enhancing business performance. However, how these responses interact with other signals to shape customer behavior and business outcomes remains underexplored. Our study goes beyond the direct effect of managerial responses and sheds light on how it moderates the impact of other signals (i.e., the volume of cumulative customers/backers and the status of goal attainment). Importantly, we find that managerial response not only serves to strengthen the desirable herding effect, but also mitigates the undesirable goalattainment effect due to customers’ motivation loss. As such, our study expands the scope of implications of managerial response and points to the importance of investigating managerial responses, together with other factors.

This study also has important managerial implications for both fundraisers and crowdfunding platforms. Our findings can be generalized to the context in which information asymmetry exists on the twosided platform. We show that online responses can be an effective way to manage consumer-side expectations and signal underlying service quality. Reducing the information asymmetry on the platform also benefits the platform owners, as it will lead to increased adoption of both sides. Thus, starters should promptly respond to backer comments regarding the product and the project starters on the discussion board. Meanwhile, crowdfunding platforms should summarize the discussion board sentiment on the main project page to help potential backers obtain this information more efficiently. For example, platforms could extract tags to indicate high-frequency words or phrases used in the discussion. Also, the platform could provide more efficient tools, like instant messenger, to improve communication. If the misunderstandings regarding the project or its fundraisers could be removed by the communication between prospective backers and fundraisers, fewer negative comments would appear on the discussion board. Previous literature on digita platforms has shown that instant communication, such as live chat, between the two sides of platform users can reduce negative information and positively impact sales and conversion (Tan et al. 2019, Sun et al. 2021). Therefore, platform owners can consider embedding a live chat feature on the crowdfunding platform.

Furthermore, crowdfunding platforms should provide an effective incentive mechanism to encourage page viewers to express their comments and suggestions. It will help the fundraisers improve their projects and help potential backers make informed decisions.

## 7. Conclusion and Future Research Directions

In this study, we have provided insights into how starter response to backer comments can influence crowdfunding performance, delineating not only its direct influence on attracting more backers, but also its role in amplifying herding behavior and mitigating motivation loss after campaign goal attainment. There are several interesting avenues for future research. First, from a methodological perspective, although we have methodologically addressed possible selection biases, our model cannot control all confounding fac tors. A potential avenue for future research is to design a fully randomized experiment on the platform to validate the findings. Also, we do not measure the effect of the specific types of crowdfunding campaigns on funding performance. Future research could study the impact of specific campaign types to quantify the differential impact of starter responses. Second, our data were collected at a time when crowdfunding platforms had just launched the discussion board, and campaign starters had just begun to pay attention to backer communications. As crowdfunding becomes more prevalent, the effect of starter response and the underlying mechanism may have changed. For exam ple, starters could provide a Frequently Asked Ques tions (FAQ) section in the campaign description to address some of the frequently asked questions. In addition, after our data collection, we noticed tha some starters had started utilizing the discussion board for project updates and announcements. They engaged backers by initiating conversations instead of solely responding to questions or comments. Given the limitation of our current data, we are unable to disentangle the responses and announcements, and we encourage future research to do so. Finally, future research could also go beyond the response comments and further look at the backer identity of the com ments to which starters respond or do not respond. For example, Gunarathne et al. (2018) found that air lines only addressed less than half of the tweets directed at them from complaining customers. Fur ther, when the complaining customers have more fol lowers, airlines are more likely to respond to their complaints and respond faster. By the same token, the response to comments from more influential and reputable crowdfunding backers or other campaign starters may have a greater impact on influencing fun draising outcomes. To conclude, we believe that the communication between campaign starters and crowdfunding backers will take on an increasingly important role as crowdfunding continues to grow. We thus encourage future research to further unveil the influence of communication in the crowdfunding context and investigate how campaign starters, crowdfunding backers, and crowdfunding platforms can benefit from such communication.
