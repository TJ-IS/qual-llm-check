# Punished for Success? A Natural Experiment of Displaying Clinical Hospital Quality on Review Platforms

Abstract. The healthcare market faces severe information asymmetry; patients struggle to evaluate the quality of hospitals and make informed decisions about their healthcare. Review platforms (e.g., Yelp) have begun to display the clinical quality of hospitals (alongside consumer reviews) to inform patients about hospital selection. In 2017 and 2019, Yelp introduced a feature with clinical measures of maternity care for hospitals that delive babies in select markets. We study how clinical quality measures displayed on Yelp— especially for those (clinically) high-quality hospitals—influence subsequent patients’ ratings of hospitals. Our difference-in-differences estimation shows that when clinical quality measures are displayed, high-quality hospitals are surprisingly punished with lower sub sequent ratings on Yelp, especially hospitals with low staffing capacity. This novel finding is consequential for hospitals as patient dissatisfaction can jeopardize the federal funding that hospitals receive (CMS.gov). To tease out the underlying mechanism, we queried Safe-Graph’s precise foot traffic data, and we observed a significant patient surge for hospital that have high maternity care clinical scores displayed on Yelp. We used transfer deep learning to show that because of the patient surge, (only) hospitals with high maternity scores that were short staffed received significantly more negative patient reviews and more complaints about key hospital service areas, thus driving patient dissatisfaction and lower ratings. We contribute to theory and practice by elucidating the role of publicly dis playing clinical quality measures in patient (dis-)satisfaction with hospitals.

## Introduction

Patients<sup>1</sup> often struggle to ascertain which hospitals provide high-quality care. This information asymmetry problem has been severe and enduring. In recent years, a significant healthcare reform in the United States has been collecting and publicly displaying clinical quality measures about hospitals, aiming to improve healthcare quality through information transparency (Chandra et al. 2016, Yaraghi et al. 2018). Clinical quality measures of hospitals refer to standardized, comparative, and generally reliable indicators of hospital quality as verified by authorized expert agents (Chandra et al. 2016). Table 1 shows the definition, source, and examples of clinical quality measures. For instance, the maternity care clinical score is an example of a clinical quality measure (henceforth shortened as maternity score). However, nationally representative surveys found that fewer than one in five patients was aware of any accessible (publicly displayed) clinical quality measures (Scanlon et al. 2015). Also, the complexity of the reported clinical quality metrics and a lack of timely exposure to patients (Schlesinger et al. 2015) limit their value. Therefore, despite their importance, patients’ reliance on clinical quality measures has remained limited.

By contrast, many patients use review platforms (e.g., Yelp) to choose hospitals, similar to choosing local businesses (restaurants, hotels) based on the consumer reviews that are publicly available on social media platforms (Peuchaud 2020). Table 1 provides the definition, the source, and examples of patient reviews for hospi tals. Hedges and Couey (2020) found that 72% of U.S. respondents use social media platforms as their very first step in searching for hospitals; Yelp is the most widely used platform. However, judging the underlying clinical quality—such as the treatment plan, clinical intervention, safety grade, and readmission rates—is difficult for most patients because of a lack of relevant medical knowledge (Saifee et al. 2020). Instead of commenting on actual true clinical quality of hospitals, consumer reviews, such as those on Yelp, are predominantly about experiences (Lagasse 2018)—satisfaction or dissatisfaction with nonclinical customer services (see Online Appendix A for several sample reviews written by patients for hospitals).

It is not difficult to rationalize why patient satisfaction has been shown to only weakly correlate to clinical quality measures (i.e., the so-called low-correlation puzzle) (Gao et al. 2012). Online patient reviews may be informative about the friendliness and responsiveness of hospital staff but not necessarily about clinical quality. Perhaps more worrisome is that based on an analysis of thousands of Yelp ratings, the Top 20 U.S. hospitals, as ranked by the U.S. News & World Report expert ranking, received abysmal online patient ratings (Lagasse 2018). We refer to this incongruity as the punish-the-best paradox as if the poor patient ratings are punishing the top-ranked hospitals. This paradox motivates our research.

Healthcare practitioners have alerted that only providing consumer ratings may impair the decision making of patients and urged that clinical quality measures be displayed on popular platforms (Hedges and Couey 2020). Joining the more significant effort to foster a quality-based healthcare market, Yelp launched a new feature that displays maternity scores (e.g., C-section and breastfeeding) on the profile pages of hospitals that delivered babies in California (CA), New York City (NYC), and Long Island (Figure 1). This change on the Yelp platform provided an ideal opportunity to examine the interplay between clinical quality measures and subsequent online reviews that hospitals receive by patients. Maternity scores are good indicators of hospitals’ overall clinical performance for expecting parents and other patients. First, maternity scores are critical to many patients because childbirth is among the top reasons for hospitalization in the United States (Baessler and Schuessler 2003). Second, maternity care requires the expertise of multidisciplinary clinician teams. A high quality level of maternity care indicates high-quality clinical care in several other related departments (Easter et al. 2019). Accordingly, the maternity scores posted on Yelp are arguably a reasonable proxy measure of the overall quality of hospitals.

Online consumer reviews (e.g., on Yelp, Google, and WebMD) have growing importance to hospitals. In 2006, the federal government started requiring U.S. hospitals to administer patient satisfaction surveys (the Hospital Consumer Assessment of Healthcare Providers and Systems (HCAHPS)<sup>2</sup>) in order to receive federal funding (Ranard et al. 2016). Hospital patient ratings on Yelp are generally highly correlated with HCAHPS results (Bardach et al. 2013, Ranard et al. 2016). Therefore, hospitals are incentivized to improve their patient ratings both online and offline.

The potential impact and underlying mechanism(s) of how displaying a high clinical quality score (related to maternity care) affects patient ratings and reviews are poorly understood. On the one hand, clinical quality measures are considered trusted and informative, and they may serve as expert opinions to shape patients evaluations of hospitals (Beckman 1967). Patients lack qualified medical training, and thus, they need expert guidance to assess hospitals. High clinical quality scores displayed on Yelp can guide patients to give positive ratings on Yelp. Also, high clinical quality scores generally mean better clinical care quality; thus, patients are more likely to enjoy positive healthcare outcomes and give more favorable ratings for hospitals on Yelp. On the other hand, patient ratings might also drop following the display of high clinical quality scores on Yelp. The rationale is that higher scores displayed about hospitals might attract more patients to the hospital and put a strain on its limited clinical (staffing) resources, potentially resulting in service delays and deteriorated service to patients; thus, this ultimately leads to adverse patient experiences and hurts the ratings that patients would post online.

Although prior research on hospital reviews focused on the correlation between consumer reviews and clinical quality measures (e.g., Lu and Rui 2018, Kordzadeh 2019, Saifee et al. 2020), we go beyond estimating their mere correlation because without a deeper understanding of the theoretical relationship and underlying mechanism(s), the punish-the-best paradox would still remain a puzzle (Ranard et al. 2016). Accordingly, we aim to shed light on this paradox by answering the following two research questions.

• How will displaying clinical quality measures (about maternity care in our context) affect subsequent patients ratings about hospitals on review platforms?

• What underlying mechanism explains the relationship between clinical quality measures and the subsequent patients’ ratings about hospitals on review platforms?

A natural experiment on Yelp enabled us to examine these two research questions empirically. The focal Yelp feature introduced maternity scores for hospitals offering childbirth in California in 2017 and New York City and Long Island in 2019, whereas the Yelp pages of other hospitals without birthing centers or maternity departments remained unchanged during our observation window. In this study, we used a difference-indifferences (DID) estimation to establish the effect. Our panel data covered all hospitals with this feature in California, New York City, and Long Island. Propensity score matching (PSM) was used to select the hospitals without maternity scores displayed on their Yelp pages in these regions as our control group to limit the ex ante differences between the treatment and control samples.

The maternity scores serve as a valid signal of hospital quality, bolstering the credibility of the treatment effect. The maternity scores of hospitals are significantly and positively correlated with their public rankings of clinical quality that the national news agencies publish; additionally, maternity scores are reasonably correlated with the mortality rate of hospitals.<sup>3</sup> Although the maternity score reflects only a specific aspect of the clinical quality of a hospital, it is by far the only clinical quality measure displayed by Yelp; because of information asymmetry, even a quality signal, as specific as this, might have the potential to exert a significant impact on patients.

We offer three notable findings. First, displaying high maternity scores on Yelp significantly reduced patient ratings but only for hospitals with low staffing capacity; those hospitals with high staffing capacity did not exhibit this negative impact. Second, low maternity scores displayed on Yelp did not significantly change patient ratings. Third, SafeGraph’s geographic footprint data<sup>4</sup> offer rare opportunities to observe patient demand with precision; using the subsample of New York hospitals with the SafeGraph measures, we found that hospitals with high maternity scores (with high or low staffing capacity) had significantly more patient visits after the Yelp change. In contrast, hospitals with low maternity scores did not experience significant changes in patient visits.

These results hint at our proposed underlying mechanism; high maternity scores unexpectedly attract more patients and thus, lead to a patient surge, likely resulting in a staffing shortage for hospitals. When hospitals have low staffing capacity, patients would likely experience longer wait times and poorer bedside manner of the hospital staff, resulting in less satisfactory patient experiences. In contrast, those with high staffing capacity may not be negatively impacted. To further examine the proposed mechanism, we employed transfer deep learning (TDL) to identify reviews that specifically complain about wait times and the bedside manner of hospitals. Our results lend support to this proposed underlying mechanism. We find that displaying high maternity scores significantly increases the number of highly negative ratings (i.e., one star). Even more interestingly, displaying high maternity scores increases the volume of text reviews with complaints specific to wait times and the bedside manner but only for those hospitals with low staffing capacity. In contrast, the volume of one-star ratings and text reviews specifically complaining about wait times and bedside manner remains unaffected at those hospitals with high staffing capacity. Wait times and bedside manner—communication skills, such as vocal tone, body language, honesty, and concealment of attitude—are the two most frequently discussed patient comments on online review platforms like Yelp (Xu et al. 2021). Importantly, these complaints can cost a hospital a significant loss of revenues and market share and can add additional stress on its healthcare workers (Lagasse 2018).

This study makes several important contributions. Our findings suggest a counterintuitive effect; high-quality hospitals, as objectively measured by clinical quality data, are punished by displaying their clinical quality measures on Yelp, at least in terms of poorer subsequent patient ratings. Our analyses of the underlying mechanism revealed that the objective signal of the hospital’s high quality, as captured by clinical quality scores, the hospital’s staffing capacity, and the ensuing patient complaints, led to this surprising effect. Therefore, the findings for the chain effect mechanism can help explain the observed punish-the-best paradox in practice.<sup>5</sup> These results have theoretical and practical significance, offering actionable insights, particularly for high-quality hospitals that seek to prepare better to address potential adverse effects when popular review platforms display their clinical quality metrics in the future. We also add to the literature on online reviews by focusing on the impact of a relatively new feature introduced by review platforms on the future consumer ratings of hospitals. Our finding is practically relevant because U.S. hospitals are increasingly incentivized to improve their patient ratings on review platforms, and low patient ratings could threaten their federal funding. Finally, we add to the burgeoning literature that applies TDL methods onto unique classification problems with text from social media. With a fine-tuned TDL model (i.e., bidirectional encoder representations from transformers (BERT)) that achieves state-of-the-art performance, we showcase how machine learning (ML) and TDL techniques can be leveraged at a granular level of analysis to uncover the causal mechanism underlying a seemingly counterintuitive relationship.

## Related Literature

## Online Consumer Reviews About Hospitals

Although the literature has extensively studied online consumer reviews (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006), we still have little systematic knowledge about the role of online patient reviews of healthcare providers. Patient reviews of healthcare providers are inherently imperfect because consumers generally lack the expertise and credentials of a qualified or board-certified medical professional with multiple years of specialized education and experience. Studies have observed that online patient reviews mainly discuss nonclinical customer experi ences and found that wait times and providers’ bedside manner are the top two aspects of patient dissatisfaction (Gao et al. 2012, Xu et al. 2021). Thus, scholars doubt whether online patient reviews alone can inform clinical quality and outcomes (Saifee et al. 2020). The literature on patient reviews is largely divided. Although Lu and Rui (2018) showed that cardiac surgeons’ ratings on RateMDs are negatively correlated to mortality rates, other scholars have reported very weak correlations in chronic care (Gao et al. 2012, Kordzadeh 2019). We refe to this issue as the low-correlation puzzle.

Some earlier research has examined the impact of online reviews on physicians’ offline demand (Wang et al. 2020, Xu et al. 2021). Specifically, Xu et al. (2021) employed natural language processing (NLP) to extract quality proxies (e.g., diagnosis accuracy, physician knowledge) from review texts and found that these proxies significantly predicted patient choice of physicians above and beyond physician demographics. This literature provides face validity to this study in that quality-based information on review platforms could alter consumer decisions about which hospital to visit as a patient.

Our review of the literature reveals at least two crucial research gaps we seek to fill. First, although most studies explored physicians’ reviews, little research in information systems (IS) has examined online patients reviews for hospitals, with only a few exceptions (Ranard et al. 2016, Xu et al. 2021). Hospitals have more diverse teams of specialists and offer a more comprehensive range of healthcare services (including general and specialized types) than individual physicians. Research has shown that larger and more diverse healthcare teams deliver safer, higher-quality care (Ku et al. 2015). Therefore, more research is needed to better understand the role of hospital reviews. Second, although extant studies have only examined the correlation between clinical quality measures and patient reviews, we dive deeper to examine the possible causal role of displaying clinical quality scores on review platforms in online patients’ ratings about hospitals.

Online patient reviews have become fundamental to hospitals. Since 2006, U.S. federal laws have required hospitals to report HCAHPS survey results to enjoy certain federal payment (Ranard et al. 2016) (see Online Appendix H for information on the HCAHPS survey). The survey centers on the patient (consumer) perspective and collects essential hospital experiences, including communication with hospital staff and the quietness of facilities. As such, HCAHPS is a key component of the data-reporting requirements for hospitals to receive federal payments (CMS.gov), and poor survey results can result in severe monetary penalties for hospitals. A hospital’s performance on the HCAHPS drives up to 25% of its financial incentives (Ranard et al. 2016). Moreover, the Yelp consumer ratings of hospitals were found to highly correlate with their HCAHPS data (Bardach et al. 2013, Ranard et al. 2016). Importantly, Bardach et al. (2013) found a correlation of 0.49, whereas the result of Ranard et al. (2016) was 0.69 after including more recent Yelp data. We also collected HCAHPS data reported by the hospitals in our sample and obtained a consistent (high) correlation (ρ � 0.631, p < 0.001). Therefore, strong incentives exist for U.S. hospitals to improve their patient satisfaction as indicated by the online patient ratings and the HCAHPS survey.

## Clinical Quality Measures and Consumer Decision Making

Clinical quality measures reported by authorized agencies enable comparison of hospitals on care quality, and they are usually published on governmentsponsored healthcare websites (Chandra et al. 2016), such as Hospital Compare (https://www.medicare. gov/care-compare/). Research has shown strong evidence that hospital quality (or proxies thereof) is a major consideration in patients’ choice of hospitals; higher-quality hospitals tend to attract more patients, all else being equal (Varkevisser et al. 2012). Patient demand and market share are susceptible to quality differences among hospitals (Chandra et al. 2016). Varkevisser et al. (2012) found that hospitals that scored higher on quality measures attracted more patients, even if the measures may be an imperfect signal of hospital quality or even though these measures may not be relevant to each patient.

These studies show evidence that patients tend to gravitate toward hospitals with higher care quality as reflected by various clinical quality measures, including the general hospital-wide measures and specialtyspecific ones. Making various quality measures widely available is thus critical to better informing patients on their selection of hospitals. Millions of dollars have been invested in the collection and public release of these measures. Moreover, maximizing the benefits of clinical quality measures would require them to be viewed, understood, and ultimately, incorporated into patients’ decision making. Despite a growing number of quality measures published on healthcare websites, many patients are reported to be unaware of them (Scanlon et al. 2015). Moreover, studies examining the leading healthcare websites (e.g., Hospital Compare, Health Grades, Leapfrog Group) point out that even if patients find them, the provided measures may confuse rather than inform patients (Varkevisser et al. 2012). Kordzadeh (2019) found that the information on Health Grades did not clearly differentiate between high-, medium-, and low-quality hospitals, making it difficult for patients to discern their quality differences. Further, the measures tend to be complex and involve an extensive array of diverse metrics that are sometimes inconsistent or incomparable with each other, thereby adding additional difficulty for patients to use the information (Schlesinger et al. 2015). Therefore, the challenge remains of how quality measures can be made accessible and available to a broader segment of healthcare consumers (patients) so that they are helpful to them and for their decision to select a hospital for their healthcare.

## Signaling Theory and Heuristic Decision Making

Patients typically suffer from not knowing much about healthcare providers and services before their visit (Emons 1997). Signaling theory (Spence 2002) offers a helpful framework to understand better and remedy this information asymmetry. The theory aims to reduce information asymmetry through effective signaling (Spence 2002). A signal is a cue that can convey unique information about the underlying quality (Rao and Monroe 1989). Clinical quality measures released by government-authorized agencies could be considered a credible signal of hospital quality (Chandra et al. 2016). Signal credibility alone, however, is not enough. Signal observability is a critical characteristic of an effective signal. Also called signal visibility or clarity (Rao and Monroe 1989), signal observability refers to the extent to which a signal is readily noticeable and understandable to its intended audience (Rao and Monroe 1989, Dimoka et al. 2012). In our context, clinical quality measures must be readily visible and easy to process by patients in order to be a helpful quality signal and be effective at guiding patients’ choice of hospitals. Popular online review platforms (e.g., Yelp, Google, and WebMD) have become primary information sources for selecting healthcare providers (Peuchaud 2020), and studies show that the majority of U.S. consumers use Yelp as their very first step in searching for hospitals (Ranard et al. 2016). Accordingly, releasing clinical quality measures on Yelp and other review platforms should enhance the observability of signals.

Medical decision making represents a complex decision scenario; research has shown that patients rely on simple heuristics when choosing hospitals (Schlesinger et al. 2015). The decision-making literature has long established that in a complex decision scenario, people are inclined to adopt heuristic strategies to reduce the amount of information they have to process; strategies may be boundedly rational, but they allow for faster decisions (Payne et al. 1993). Thus, easy-to-process and clearly labeled clinical quality measures (about maternity care on Yelp) could serve as a decision shortcut for patients to infer hospital quality. Maternity scores are critical for many patients’ choices as childbirth is among the top reasons for hospitalization in the United States, and the widespread variation in the clinical quality of maternity care is well known (Baessler and Schuessler 2003). Further, maternity scores may serve as a valuable signal of overall hospital quality for the broader patient population. Maternity care—including prenatal and postnatal diagnoses and labor and delivery services—requires the expertise of multidisciplinary care teams, indicating the high clinical quality of the other involved hospital departments (Easter et al. 2019). Hospitals that provide high-quality maternity care may be perceived to be caring for their patients because newborns and women in labor are among the most vulnerable patients (World Health Organization 2019). Additionally, women with positive delivery experiences at a hospital may become loyal patients in the future. Given that, on average, women in the United States give birth to over two babies in their life span, maternity care can potentially impact the decisions of many women and their families about which hospitals to patronize. In contrast, a negative delivery experience may be enough for patients to spread nega tive word of mouth and stay away from the hospital in the future, including for other types of treatments, later in their lifetime.

Empirically, our data reveal that the correlation between the average maternity scores of hospitals from Yelp (the higher, the better) and their mortality rates (the lower, the better) collected from CMS (data. cms.gov) is �0.563 (p < 0.001). Mortality rate is treated as the gold-standard measure of overall hospital quality (Parina et al. 2015). As such, the observed correlation of �0.563 further implies that maternity scores on Yelp can be seen as a reasonable signal for overall hospital quality.

## Role of Clinical Quality Measures of Hospitals on Online Patient Ratings

Earlier, we mentioned that the newly added clinical quality measures on Yelp are maternity scores. According to signaling theory, we anticipate that displaying hospitals’ maternity scores on Yelp, as a credible signal, would affect patients’ subsequent ratings. High maternity scores may positively impact patients’ ratings of hospitals compared with those hospitals with either low or no scores displayed on Yelp. There are mainly two theoretical reasons to a priori expect a positive relationship, which we will explain here.

First, high maternity scores may effectively mitigate the information asymmetry about healthcare quality between hospitals and patients. Clinical quality measures made publicly accessible by Yelp may shape public opinion (Beckman 1967) about hospitals among patients given the challenge for patients to evaluate hospital quality (Saifee et al. 2020). Based on anchoring heuristic theory (Tversky and Kahneman 1974), clinical quality scores for hospitals publicly displayed on Yelp—albeit only on maternal care—can shape patients’ perceptions of the overall quality of hospitals. Anchoring heuristics in consumers’ choice and subsequent word-of-mouth communication have been theoretically discussed and empirically observed in the context of multiple experience goods (e.g., movies, restaurants, and hotels) with similar information asymmetry problems as hospitals (i.e., those whose underlying quality is difficult to evaluate prior to their consumption) (Clement et al. 2007). The same logic is proposed to apply to patients’ ratings of hospitals.

Second, credible signals would positively shape consumers’ perception of hospitals’ service quality, resulting in higher subsequent ratings. High-quality clinical care means better overall clinical outcomes that patients observe. Especially for maternal care, higher maternity scores mean smoother baby delivery. Thus, a smooth birthing process resulting in a healthy baby would make parents happy with the services received, leading to higher ratings for the hospital in the future. In addition, hospitals would be in a better position to emphasize their treatment quality following positive baby delivery outcomes, thereby further reinforcing the patients’ positive impressions about the particular hospital (Hudak and Wright 2000).

Despite these plausible reasons for a positive impact, it may also be reasonable to expect a negative impact on hospitals’ subsequent ratings. We argue that this is possible because of a surge in patient volume. Hospitals that score higher on maternity scores displayed on Yelp are expected to attract more patients if those scores are deemed credible, objective, and accessible signals. Ceteris paribus, patients would likely choose hospitals with high maternity scores. Patients who prefer better-quality hospitals may be unaware of where to find clinical quality measures (e.g., maternity scores). Even if they can locate these measures, it is not easy to understand them and integrate them into an informed decision. The official government websites that publish clinical quality measures contain overwhelming information, which may impede meaningful comparisons. Patients may instead use the readily available and easily interpretable clinical quality measures on Yelp as representative indicators to select hospitals (Schlesinger et al. 2015). As patients gravitate toward hospitals with high maternity scores displayed on Yelp, the surge may strain the service capacity of these (highly rated) hospitals and result in delayed care and longer wait times because of inadequate staffing, thus leading to a less satisfactory patient experience in the future. As noted, most patients opine about their customer service experiences with hospitals on Yelp instead of the underlying quality of clinical care (Ranard et al. 2016). A less satisfactory patient experience could encourage subsequent patients to offer lower ratings and complain more online. Especially in an era when healthcare resources and hospital staff are generally under stress (Haseltine 2021), a short-term surge of patients may likely further restrain hospital staff, therefore resulting in poorer consumer ratings on Yelp.

Given the tension on whether adding high (versus low or no) maternity scores on Yelp would exert a positive (or a negative) effect on hospitals’ subsequent Yelp ratings, we describe how we leveraged a natural experiment on Yelp to investigate this effect empirically and attempted to uncover the main underlying mechanisms to explain this effect.

## Research Methodology Empirical Setting

We tested our research questions using hospital reviews from Yelp. On July 26, 2017, Yelp first added maternity scores to the pages of all 250 hospitals that deliver babies in CA. Then, on May 7, 2019, Yelp introduced the same feature for 60 hospitals that deliver babies in NYC and Long Island<sup>6</sup> (refer to Figure 1). We refer to these two implementations on Yelp as the system change. Our treatment group consisted of hospitals that had the new Yelp feature added. Throughout the study period, we ensured that Yelp only modified the maternity score feature in these markets. In contrast, the Yelp pages of other hospitals (i.e., those without labor and delivery services) did not have this feature. Therefore, they remained unchanged throughout our study period, making them suitable as a control group. We empirically verified that the presence of a maternity center did not systematically differentiate hospitals from those without such a center, thereby addressing potential concerns about selection bias.<sup>7</sup>

## Observational Data and Measurement

We programmatically collected online ratings and text reviews for these hospitals from Yelp. We included all hospitals in CA, NYC, and Long Island with Yelp pages and reviews posted before and after the system change. All hospitals with the Yelp feature were selected. Next, we used PSM to pair each hospital with the feature with a comparable hospital without the feature, resulting in a sample of 620 hospitals. Because of the different years of Yelp’s implementation in the two states, we used the lead-lag design on the pooled data. The pre- and posttreatment periods each consisted of 12 months of data for CA, NYC, and Long Island before and after the respective Yelp changes in those states. We excluded data from the months of intervention and any months when no ratings or reviews were posted for a hospital, resulting in an unbalanced panel data set. Our unit of analysis is at the hospital-month level. We averaged the values for each hospital-month unit to create monthly panel data. Descriptive statistics and a correlation matrix are shown in Tables 2 and 3.<sup>8</sup>

We measured patient ratings using the star ratings ranging from one (negative) to five (positive) posted monthly for each hospital. We measured the number of highly negative ratings using only one-star ratings (discussed further in Identifying the Underlying Mechanisms). Yelp provided four measures of maternity care, all of which were highly correlated to each other. Details of these measures, including relevant Yelp blog announcements, the names of measures, their mean ings, and their specific measurement scales, are in cluded in Online Appendix B. In the main analyses, we averaged these measures to create a hospital’s maternity score; for robustness checks, we also used the lowest score among the four measures to represent a hospital’s final score. To control for other hospital factors, we included the natural logarithm of the number of months since a hospital had a Yelp page until a review was posted (ln(Hospital Age)) to capture hospital age and the natural logarithm of the number of photos in the reviews (ln(Number of Photos in Reviews)) to capture review richness.

## Empirical Estimation and Propensity Score Matching

For our empirical analyses, we used the DID estimation in order to better control for potential omitted variable effect (e.g., hospital quality and location). DID has been extensively used in IS studies (e.g., Greenwood and Wattal 2017, Burtch et al. 2018). Specifically, DID uses longitudinal data from treatment and control groups to estimate the causal effect of a treatment by comparing changes in outcomes over time between these groups.

We executed PSM (Greenwood and Wattal 2017) to further reduce potential bias because differences in the outcome variable between treatment versus control groups might be attributable to factors predicting group assignment rather than to the Yelp change itself. This process ensures that control hospitals in our sample are representative counterfactuals for treated hospitals by selecting a sample of untreated hospitals comparable with those in the treatment group (Caliendo and Kopeinig 2008). For PSM, we used the 1:1 nearest neighbor matching method by pairing one control unit with each treated unit based on criteria including a hospital’s prior review volume, prior average ratings, variance of ratings, hospital age, and the business category displayed on Yelp. These variables were measured prior to the Yelp change. First, our choice of Yelp covariates for matching helps generate a new sample where treatment is orthogonal to these covariates so that they cannot confound the outcomes comparison between treatment and control groups. The benefits of using Yelp variables for matching include more availability, granularity, and variety of Yelp data while potentially avoiding selection bias by not accounting for other hospital variables, such as those published by the CMS,<sup>9</sup> which might differentiate hospitals with maternity centers. We have empirically verified that selection bias is not a concern here. When using our choice of Yelp matching variables, hospitals in treatment and control groups do not differ significantly on a wide range of clinical and operational factors, including hospital size (“total hospital beds”), care team (“total hospital employees on payroll”), care quality (“HCAHPS rating” for patient satisfaction), and key performance indicators, like “mortality rate,” “readmission rate,” and “case mix index” (detailed results are in Online Appendix I). Thus, PSM based on covariates from Yelp should be sufficient to ensure no selection bias among hospitals with and without maternity centers. Second, 1:1 matching is the most commonly used because it results in better balance in terms of closeness to the treated unit.<sup>10</sup> Therefore, a balance check was provided to add credibility to our analysis in Online Appendix J.

## Identifying the Underlying Mechanisms

Patient Volume. As we theorized, our underlying mechanism first hinges on direct evidence of a positive effect of Yelp displaying high maternity scores on the patient volume at these hospitals, and no such effect is expected for low-maternity score hospitals. We use SafeGraph’s precise footprint data<sup>13</sup> as a proxy to measure patient volume visiting the hospitals each month in our data. SafeGraph collects geolocations of a large panel of mobile users who agree to share such data anonymously via third-party app partners. SafeGraph geofences and maintains physical location databases for individual hospitals, retailers, brands, and other places of interest spanning as many categories as coded by the North American Industry Classification System (NAICS).<sup>14</sup> After aggregating and mapping users’ movement data with business locations, Safe-Graph publishes monthly demand proxies (i.e., foot traffic data). These measures for physical retailers are superior to alternate measures used in the prior literature (e.g., overall review volume) because of Safe-Graph’s precise geotracking of mobile devices and the limitation (i.e., self-selection and underreporting) of available competing measures (Hu et al. 2009). Recent academic studies have used and validated SafeGraph’s data as demand proxies (e.g., Mitkina et al. 2022, Wang et al. 2022, Babar et al. 2023).

We adopted two SafeGraph measures: monthly unique visitors and monthly total hospital visits. The

DID results of patient volume are shown in Table 6. Columns (1) and (2) in Table 6 report the results of unique visitors, and we show that high-maternity score hospitals experienced a significant patient surge $( \beta = 1 . 2 5 7 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ ). However, patient volume reduced for low-maternity score hospitals, albeit not significantly $( \beta = - 1 1 . 3 9 2 , p > 0 . 1 )$ ). Similarly, results for total hospital visits in columns (3) and (4) in Table 6 reveal that only high-maternity score hospitals saw a marginally significant patient surge $( \beta = \overset { \_ } { 1 } . 4 6 1 , p < 0 . 1 )$ , but their low-maternity score counterparts saw an insignificant decrease in patients $( \beta = - \bar { 1 } 0 . 9 1 1 , p > 0 . 1 )$ 15

If patient volume surged significantly at the highmaternity score hospitals but did not significantly decrease at the low-maternity score ones, it is not entirely known from where the increased portion of patient volume of the high-maternity score hospitals came. Following the DID analytical framework for causal inference, we have two conjectures. First, despite the insignificant results, the reduced patient volume at low-maternity score hospitals because of the Yelp change may have contributed to the significant increase at high-maternity score hospitals. In other words, some patients might have switched from low-maternity score to high-maternity score hospitals after seeing and comparing the newly disclosed maternity scores on Yelp, although our earlier SUTVA robustness check did not find robust evidence for this explanation. Second, as we reasoned, maternity scores added by Yelp may send effective signals about the clinical quality at hospitals. Without the Yelp change, such signals would not have been easily accessible by patients. New and credible signals about clinical quality provided by Yelp may have caused changes in patient behavior, leading certain patients to visit those hospitals with high clinical quality measures (e.g., maternity scores) more often than they might have otherwise. We remain cautious and presume that such behavior change would only take place for nonemergency and nonurgent care services, such as primary care.

## Patient Complaints of Relevant Hospita Service Areas

The other direct evidence key to our underlying mechanism is to anticipate increased wait times and poorer bedside manner only at those high-maternity score hospitals with relatively low staffing capacity. In contrast, high-staffing capacity hospitals should be unaffected. Because wait times and bedside manner rank highly among patient complaints in online reviews (Xu et al. 2021), ceteris paribus, we should observe an increase in the number of highly negative ratings: more specifically, a significant increase in the volume of patient reviews complaining particularly about long wait times and poor bedside manner but only for those highmaternity score hospitals with low staffing capacity. Although capturing highly negative reviews using onestar ratings is often straightforward, we used transfer deep learning to uncover relevant patient complaints from review text.

Model Architecture. We trained and evaluated three model architectures and used the best model to classify whether a review qualifies as a complaint of wait times or bedside manner (or both); then, we aggregated their volume at the monthly level. These models span a wide range of (legacy to state-of-the-art) ML architectures and were evaluated by average 10-fold crossvalidation metrics. The first benchmark is logistic regression (logitreg), which is widely implemented because of its simplicity (Cao et al. 2020). Technical details are included in Online Appendix D. However, logitreg is limited by its bag-of-words approach, and any information embedded in word order is ignored (Cao et al. 2020).

The next benchmark is the long short-term memory (LSTM) neural network model, a type of recurrent neural network (RNN) and deep learning model (Goodfellow et al. 2016). RNNs may be preferred to logitreg because they can learn additional information from longer data sequences (Goodfellow et al. 2016). Compared with basic RNNs, the hidden layer of LSTMs has additional design features (various gates) that enable the network to overcome computational challenges inherent in RNNs (Goodfellow et al. 2016). Online Appendix E includes additional technical details. Despite these advantages of LSTMs, the inability to understand the rich context of very long texts can limit their perfor mance (Liu et al. 2020).

The last model architecture—BERT—leverages the efficacy of TDL, which has recently become prominent (Devlin et al. 2018). BERT is a powerful language representation model (Online Appendix F details its core features). BERT is particularly suited for training text classifiers with limited labeled training data—a methodology called transfer learning or fine-tuning (Devlin et al. 2018). TDL methods build upon sophisticated pretrained models (unsupervised or semisupervised) to achieve superior performance across a wide range of standard tasks without requiring large amounts of labeled data during fine-tuning (Devlin et al. 2018). We seek to overcome the paucity of training data by leveraging BERT. Figure 2 illustrates the TDL design in this study. Despite the power and efficiency reported about TDL methods, BERT has not been used for specific text classification tasks, like our task.

## Robustness Checks

We conducted a series of checks to test the robustness and eliminate confounders. First, we reran the analyses on the childbirth-related reviews only as the maternity scores directly relate to patients needing maternity care and birthing services. Their feedback on whether a hospital met their specific needs and provided satisfactory patient care should be reflected in childbirth-related reviews, which we identified using the same classification method (i.e., the expert labeling and ML described earlier) that produced 99% crossvalidation accuracy. A review about childbirth, prenatal care, or pregnancy complications was labeled as a childbirth-related review (the review could be a complaint or a compliment). The results for the ratings of childbirth-only reviews reported in columns (1) and (2) in Table 10 indicate a strong and significant negative effect only on the hospitals for which the maternity score is high and staffing capacity is low, consistent with the main results.

Second, the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic might confound the effects of high maternity scores by directing many patients to high-quality hospitals. According to reports from The New York Times and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention,<sup>19</sup> the first COVID-19 case during the early pandemic in New York was confirmed on March 1, 2020. We shortened our analysis window to nine months to remove any potential COVID-19 confounds. For New York data, this means excluding all months beginning March 2020. As shown in columns (3) and (4) in Table 10, after eliminating the potential confounder of the COVID-19 pandemic, the results are consistent with our main findings.

## Discussion and Conclusion Key Findings

Although clinical quality measures and consumer reviews shape patients’ hospital choices, little is known about how displaying clinical quality measures (e.g., maternity scores) on review platforms may affect patients’ ratings of hospitals. Our research examined the effect of adding maternity scores on Yelp on patients’ ratings of hospitals with different levels of quality signals (maternity scores) and resource (staffing) capacity. A counterintuitive finding is that displaying high-quality metrics on review platforms (i.e., high maternity scores) had a significant negative effect (versus the more intuitive positive effect) on patients ratings of hospitals with low staffing capacity. Additionally, hospitals could alleviate these adverse effects by having a higher staffing capacity. Despite the intuition that higher maternity scores could have an anchoring effect that could help raise future patient ratings of hospitals, it is plausible that patients would choose to visit hospitals with high-quality care over others once informed by the visible maternity scores on Yelp. A surge in patient volume is shown to increase patient wait times and worsen the patient experience caused by staff shortage; accordingly, con sumer ratings were shown to decline. Using transfer deep learning to identify reviews containing complaints of long wait times or poor bedside manner helped us to tease out the mechanism underlying what seems to be a paradoxical effect, thus complementing the empirical findings from the observational study. These results show that the volume of patient com plaints on wait times or bedside manner did increase. Taken together, displaying a high maternity score increased the volume of relevant service-specific complaints and negatively affected patient ratings, but this only occurred for those hospitals with low staffing capacity. Interestingly, the observed negative effects on patients’ ratings were alleviated (i.e., were statistically insignificant) for those hospitals with a higher staffing capacity.

## Contributions and Implications to Healthcare Theory and Literature

Our findings add to the limited research that empirically examines the causal effect of releasing clinical quality measures about hospitals on review platforms on patients’ ratings. Our findings reveal that doing so may unintendedly punish high-quality hospitals by lowering their patient ratings, especially for hospitals with limited staffing capacity. Admittedly, hospitals offering quality care with relatively limited staffing capacity should be applauded for their high operational efficiency. However, when popular review platforms suddenly implemented a system change and increased public awareness of hospitals’ clinical quality, those hospitals with high-quality clinical care, albeit with low staffing capacity, were caught off guard. We empirically demonstrate the unanticipated disruption in the service operations of high-quality hospitals after popular review platforms, specifically Yelp, publicly displayed their (positive) measure of high clinical quality. This surprising finding is a novel empirical contribution to the literature.

We also contribute to a growing literature on consumer (patient) reviews of hospitals in healthcare. Extant research has merely focused on testing the correlation between various clinical quality measures and consumer ratings, and the punish-the-best paradox still lingers (Gao et al. 2012, Lu and Rui 2018). We tackle the more nuanced interplay between the two critical types of information on review platforms: consumer (patient) ratings versus expert clinical quality measures. We uncover a potential causal relationship between clinical quality measures and patients’ ratings of hospitals, thus filling in a critical gap in the role of quality measures in affecting the demand for hospitals and healthcare providers (Xu et al. 2021).

This study helps demystify the unsettling puzzle of why the nation’s top hospitals tend to receive poor ratings on social media (Lagasse 2018). Our findings reveal that the causes of low patient ratings for the nation’s top hospitals may include signaling of clinical quality, patient volume surge, and medical resource shortage. Our analyses unveil the underlying mechanism: patient surge. Displaying high clinical quality measures on popular review platforms increases the burden on critical healthcare resources (hospital staff), which induces prolonged wait times and lower service levels, ultimately resulting in lower online ratings from patients. We used transfer deep learning techniques to identify (with high accuracy) patient complaint reviews, specifically about wait times and bedside manner. This finding demonstrates a likely causal link between displaying healthcare expert quality measures and deteriorating patient ratings when a hospital has high clinical quality but low staffing capacity. As such, we contribute to the literature by leveraging patient review texts that include rich details about individual-level patient interactions and qualitative experiences with healthcare workers to validate this underlying theoretical mechanism.

This study also contributes to the literature on online reviews. Previous research has examined the impacts of online ratings and reviews—including the average rating (Li and Hitt 2008, Moe and Trusov 2011, Ma et al. 2013), review volume (Lu et al. 2013), review positivity and negativity (Yin et al. 2016), review writing quality (Hou and Ma 2022), review helpfulness (Ghose and Ipeirotis 2011), and emotions (Ghose and Ipeirotis 2011)—on sales, consumer choice, and subsequent reviews (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006). Although Huang et al. (2017) and Chen et al. (2018) also studied the impact of platform changes on consumer reviews, Chen et al. (2018) focused on comparing single-dimensional versus multidimensional rating systems, and Huang et al. (2017) examined the impact of the platform’s integration with social network services on individual reviewers’ content. However, these studies only examined how the review contents, already on the review platform by prior consumers, impact subsequent reviews. Instead, we focus on how displaying external information about the hospital (clinical quality measures) published by government-authorized agents (the CMS) affects subsequent reviews. Thus, we fill the void on the interplay between diverse information sources (internal versus external) on consumer ratings and subsequent reviews of hospital care.

This study demonstrates the applications of ML and TDL in novel problems of text classification where researchers tend to be challenged by limited high quality labeled data (Weiss et al. 2016). This methodology allowed us to resolve the paradoxical finding that in our context, high scores of clinical quality measures penalize, rather than reward, hospitals regarding their patient ratings. Specifically, we used TDL to better pinpoint patient reviews that complained specifically about wait times or bedside manner, which revealed the underlying mechanism for the adverse effects of displaying positive clinical quality scores of highquality hospitals on review platforms. To our knowledge, this is the first study to leverage TDL to identify the underlying mechanisms of what seems to be counterintuitive observational findings in a healthcare ana lytics context. We are aware of only three IS studies that have used TDL (Liu et al. 2020, Shin et al. 2020, Zhu et al. 2020). Whereas Shin et al. (2020) and Zhu et al. (2020) applied TDL to image and motion sensor data, only Liu et al. (2020) explored TDL on text sequences from video-sharing social media (captions of YouTube videos). Therefore, we extend IS research on the best practices of TDL in emerging NLP contexts, such as our social media text analytics, and specifically, in a healthcare setting.

## Contributions and Implications to Healthcare Practice

This study has practical implications. We recommend that hospital providers and managers look more into patient ratings and reviews on review platforms and social media. They need to form a strategic plan on how to better anticipate patient dissatisfaction and best resolve a patient complaint during the process rather than letting it easily find its way into social media. Anecdotal evidence suggests that healthcare providers have largely disregarded their online reviews and believe that reviews on consumer platforms are less influential than those by government agencies. Our analyses suggest otherwise. Patient satisfaction (e.g., the HCAHPS survey) is becoming more critical to hospitals given the increasing weight allocated by federal governments to make funding decisions (Ranard et al. 2016). A key takeaway from our empirical finding is that customer service and patient experience form the weak spot of the punish-the-best paradox. After knowing that their top-notch care quality metrics are highlighted by review platforms, hospital managers and healthcare workers should be more proactive in communicating with patients about potential patient surge, pay more attention to areas of patient dissatisfaction, and try to resolve patient overcrowding. Hospitals might also hire special service teams to reconcile patients annoyed by deteriorated customer experiences and be ready to deploy more units. They can employ surge staffing through creative approaches that use accelerated staffing processes, such as hiring healthcare workers from neighboring areas, recruiting traveling nurses, and moving retirees or temporary contract employees back into service.

More broadly, top-tier hospitals should pay closer attention to the nontrivial relationships between patient volume, patient ratings, and their increased popularity on online platforms. Patient volume has a significant bearing on hospital revenue, yet disgruntled patients spread negative word of mouth because of patient surge; unprepared hospital staff could also hurt the hospital’s revenue (Ranard et al. 2016). Research has found that higher levels of satisfaction in patient experience resulted in higher per-patient revenues and lower operating costs of hospitals (Giese et al. 2023). We offer suggestions for hospitals to improve their response to unexpected patient demand surges. Following the disclosure of their excellent clinical quality ratings, top-tier hospitals that prepare themselves better for unexpected patient surges may be able to increase revenue instead of seeing it drop by simultaneously improving their patient ratings on such review platforms, thereby increasing patient demand, revenues, and patient satisfaction altogether.

Our findings guide patients’ decision making; when evaluating information on review platforms about hospitals, patients must understand the basic differences between ratings contributed by other patients vis-a-vis \` clinical quality ratings provided by healthcare experts. To the extent that high clinical quality ratings of hospitals can cause their patient ratings to drop because of operational factors at the hospitals (at least tentatively), we advise patients to adjust their weights on the information conveyed by these two types of ratings according to their specific disease symptoms and healthcare needs. New patients with coronary heart diseases who are inclined to visit doctors at a hospital because they have recently seen the hospital being ranked as a top-tier hospital should be cognizant of the potentially increased patient traffic in not only the cardiology department but also, other areas, such as the in-patient department, front desk, and even parking lots. Timely access to treatment by top cardiologists is critical to preventing significant disease progression; thus, patients should weigh relatively more on the clinical quality ratings and less on the patient ratings if the two sources provide conflicting evaluations, especially when it comes to high-risk disease types. In contrast, patients who need a simple diagnosis on a common disease type, such as flu or common cold, and prefer to visit a hospital may gain more value in terms of customer service quality by weighing more on patient ratings in general than on clinical quality ratings of their less relevant specialties.

Finally, our findings guide review platforms (e.g., Yelp) to anticipate better the consequences of adding clinical quality measures for hospitals and healthcare providers. Although adding clinical quality measures should doubtlessly reward high-quality hospitals and benefit patients in the long term, we observed sur prising unintended consequences, even though Yelp announced that it would make these changes on its website well ahead. Quite clearly, simply preannouncing these changes may not be enough to avoid the observed adverse effects; they need to be more proactive in alerting the affected healthcare providers about possible logical consequences (e.g., patient surge) so that (high-quality) hospitals can plan accordingly.

## Limitations and Suggestions for Future Work

As with most empirical studies, this study has limitations. First, we conducted our analysis in the states of California and New York because Yelp only added maternity scores in those markets. These markets have sufficient hospitals and patients, are economically di verse, and give us an opportunity to study patients reactions to adding maternity scores on the review platform (Yelp) over a sustained period of time. Still, caution is needed when generalizing our results to hospitals in rural and remote places. In urban settings where several hospitals exist to service all the residents and each shares a portion of the total patient demand, even a small demand shock to a hospital would be a significant burden for its medical staff. Meanwhile, in a rural setting where a single large hospital serves the entire area, patient demand might be relatively stable, and demand shock is unlikely. Informing patients about clinical quality measures of the only hospital in the suburban area may not result in much change in patient volume. By the same token, hospitals in urban settings tend to meet with more competition, whereas those in rural areas may have less competition. Caution is needed before generalizing our results to hospi tals located in healthcare markets of rural areas with low levels of competition. Second, although the hospi tal’s maternity score is believed to be a reasonable sig nal of its overall clinical quality and although we found that even a weak signal like this could signifi cantly affect hospital operations and patient (dis-)satisfaction, the maternity score is not a perfect signal and should not replace other more direct metrics of clinical quality (e.g., mortality rate). Third, although our results indicate an absence of unaccounted heterogeneity before the Yelp change and although we strived to tease out the main mechanism underlying how the Yelp change affects patients’ ratings, we still are unable to completely rule out other unobserved factors and possible alternative explanations, including expectation disconfirmation of the patients who might be dissatisfied as they compare the relatively poorer actual experiences at the high maternity care-score hospitals (because of patient surge) against their relatively higher expectations before visiting the hospitals. Fourth, we assume that patients primarily rely on review platforms to select hospitals and will, in turn, give ratings and write reviews on review platforms, such as Yelp. However, this assumption may not be true for many patients. Fifth, the issue of online review manipulation can potentially threaten the validity of our results. Although we used the Yelp feature<sup>21</sup> to remove all suspicious reviews flagged by Yelp before collecting our review data, its fake review filtering system may not be perfect and may have resulted in some (albeit a small number of) such reviews in our data. Sixth, patient selection of hospitals might be driven by other factors, such as insurance coverage, hospital news, and other people’s opinions. Our study is only the initial step to unveiling the underlying process of how clinical quality measures affect patients’ hospital choices. Given the paucity of patient data available at the individual level, we were unable to examin whether individual patient decisions are also driven by a multitude of other factors. Seventh, our SafeGraph proxies are not perfect measures of the monthly patient volume of hospitals because a recorded visit or visitor may not necessarily represent an actual patient; other possibilities include a physician, a nurse, hospital staff, and a patient’s friends or relatives. Nonetheless, they may be reasonable-enough measures for our empirical purposes with the DID fixed effect analyses after carefully examining the parallel trends and other necessary assumptions. Therefore, we caution future researchers about (mis-)using the SafeGraph foot traffic data as direct replacements for official data released by hospitals about their patient visits.

This study suggests a promising direction for future research. Future studies can examine whether other (weak, strong, general, specific) clinical quality signals may have a similar or different effect on the operational aspects of hospitals, such as those revealed in our study. The clinical quality signals that we examine relate to maternal care, which may be conceived as having broader implications for patients. Whether clinical quality signals about very specific areas of specialty, such as craniotomy surgery, that should affect a much smaller portion of the population can cause a major patient surge and deterioration of the customer service quality at general hospitals remains an important question because if their effects are much smaller or close to nothing, hospital operational teams need not allocate their limited resources in response to the potential public release of such clinical quality measures. Conversely, if their effects are comparable or much larger, future research may dig deeper into the patient psychologies as there might be other underlying reasons that specialty ratings could have unexpectedly broader impacts.

## Conclusion

Our study sheds light on the implications of displaying hospitals’ objective clinical quality measures on review platforms and the effects on patient traffic, staffing capacity, and patient satisfaction. These effects should be desirable for patients in the long run. Empowered by more accessible objective data about the quality of hospitals, patients should be better off when making hospital choices. Although the infamous punish-the-best paradox may have a negative impact on the customer service of high-quality hospitals, it would help hospi tals to deploy staff to provide high-quality clinical and customer care more strategically. In sum, revealing objective quality metrics of hospitals on Yelp can have desirable societal implications (patients and hospitals in general) because of better decision making for patients and better efficiency of resource allocation for hospitals if appropriately managed ahead of time to address staffing issues.
