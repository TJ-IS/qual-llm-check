# Multidimensional Observational Learning in Social Networks: Theory and Experimental Evidence

Abstract. The prevalence of consumers sharing their purchases on social media platforms (e.g., Instagram and Pinterest) and the use of this information by potential future consumers have substantial implications for online retailing. In this study, we examine how product characteristics and the type of information provider jointly impact purchase decisions in a social network setting. We first propose an analytical observational learning framework integrating the impact of product differentiation and social ties. Then, we use two experimental studies to validate our analytical results and provide additional insights Our key findings are that the effect of learning from strangers is stronger for verticall differentiated products than for horizontally differentiated products. However, the effec of learning from friends does not depend on whether the underlying product is horizontally or vertically differentiated. What is more interesting is the nuanced role of social ties: For horizontally differentiated products, the effect of learning increases with the strength of social ties. In addition, contact-based tie strength is more important than structure-based tie strength in accelerating observational learning. These findings provide motivation for online retailers to generate alternative strategies for increasing product sales through social networks. For example, online retailers offering horizontally differentiated products have strong incentives to cooperate with social media platforms (e.g., Instagram and Pinterest) in encouraging customers to share their purchase information.

## 1. Introduction

The unprecedented growth of social network users in the last decade has resulted in significant increases in the availability of individual-specific information such as holiday pictures, mobile check-ins at restaurants, and information on everyday purchases (Susarla et al. 2012, Bai et al. 2017, Huang et al. 2017, Qiu et al. 2018, Sun et al. 2019a). Consumers shopping through social network channels are increasingly using this information in making their purchase decisions (Newberry 2016, Qiu and Whinston 2017, Li and Wu 2018, Xu and Liu 2018). For example, according to an article in Retail Dive, in 2017, 72% of Instagram users made purchase decisions after seeing something on Instagram, with the most common categories being clothing, makeup, shoes, and jewelry (Salpini 2017). In 2016, 55% of U.S. online users shared that their primary use of Pinterest was to find and/or shop for products (Kim 2016). According to a joint study by Twitter and analytics firm Annalect, around 40% of respondents surveyed indicated that they have purchased an item online after seeing it used by an influencer on Instagram, Twitter, Vine, or YouTube (Swant 2016).

The key research issue explored in this paper is: How do social network users evaluate available prior users’ information in making their own purchase decisions? In order to do so, the traditional observational learning framework<sup>1</sup> is adapted and extended to integrate context-specific aspects such as the types of products offered on social networks and the strength of social ties on such networks. Whereas prior studies on observational learning focus on one-dimensional learning (e.g., consumers infer a products’ vertically differentiated attribute such as quality by observing prior purchase behavior), horizontal product differentiation has not been considered in this context. In addition, the interaction between the strength of social ties and product differentiation in the context of observational learning has also not been explored in prior research.<sup>2</sup>

From a methodological perspective, the contribution stems from developing an analytical framework for multidimensional observational learning such that consumers can infer both vertical and horizontal product attributes through observing others’ actions. In addition, we supplement our analytical results with experimental evidence, and our study is the first providing experimental evidence for multidimensional observational learning.

Managerial contributions and insights are as follows. First, social ties impact the magnitude of observational learning. In the case of strangers, the effect of learning is stronger for vertically differentiated products than for horizontally differentiated products; whereas in the case of friends, the effect of learning for vertically differentiated products is similar to that for horizontally differentiated products. Second, the type of product impacts the magnitude of observational learning. For horizontally differentiated products, the effect of learning from friends is stronger than that from strangers; whereas for vertically differentiated products, the effect of learning from friends is similar to that from strangers.

There is strong support for these analytical results from an experimental perspective. In a follow-up experiment, multiple measures for social ties used across social network platforms are considered. Experimental results are that for horizontally differentiated products, learning effects using the information provided by friends are increasing in the strength of social ties, and this holds regardless of the measure used. In addition, we also find that contact-based measures (recording the number of interactions between an individual and a friend) are more effective than structure-based measures (capturing the idea of different groups, see Gee et al. 2017) to accelerate observational learning.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In the next section, an overview of relevant prior literature is discussed, and key differences in this paper are highlighted. Section 3 highlights the analytical framework and the corresponding structural insights. In Sections 4 and 5, the experimental framing and results are presented and discussed, and finally, Section 6 concludes the paper.

## 2. Literature Review

In this section, we review the relevant literature classified into (i) product differentiation, (ii) search/ experience/credence goods, (iii) information provider dynamics, and (iv) social ties. We also highlight our extensions/contributions as they relate to each of these areas. In addition, since we are using a multimethodology approach to answer our research question, we present our contributions to this stream of literature in observational learning in Section 2.5.

## 2.1. Product Differentiation

In general, single-dimensional observational learning occurs when individuals learn useful information about a single dimension, product quality (vertically differentiated attribute), from observing the actions of previous decision makers (Banerjee 1992, Bikhchandani et al. 1992). Two critical assumptions in this stream of research are:

• Assumption 1: The product is vertically differentiated

• Assumption 2: No social relationship between early and late decision makers.

Our focus on multidimensional observational learning is different from this stream in the following aspects. In terms of modeling assumptions, most prior research in this domain has considered information on a single vertical attribute, such as product/service quality (e.g., Banerjee 1992, Bikhchandani et al. 1992). In contrast to prior literature focusing only on learning information on a single quality dimension, our study integrates learning information on both quality and taste dimensions. In particular, we relax assumption 1 in prior literature on single-dimensional observational learning and consider a more general framing in which products/services could be vertically and horizontally differentiated, for example, movies, music, and restaurants (Chen and Xie 2005, Kumar et al. 2018, Cho et al. 2020, Li et al. 2020). In contrast to the quality dimension (all consumers agree on whether they would prefer more or less of it), consumers may have different opinions about the taste dimension based on horizontally differentiated attributes (whether a product matches a consumer’s taste). By examining the role/impact of observational learning for such general multidimensional product/service settings, our study fills a critical research gap and enriches our understanding of observational learning from both quality and taste dimensions.

Although most of the extant literature focuses on learning for vertically differentiated products, there are a few exceptions: Hendricks et al. (2012) examine learning effects for horizontally differentiated products. Besides horizontally differentiated attributes, we seek to relax assumption 2 in prior literature on single-dimensional observational learning and capture the learning effect within a social network setting. Although Liu et al. (2017) also consider products that could be horizontally or vertically differentiated, their research question is different from ours and considers word of mouth as the information transmission mechanism. Our proposed approach captures learning effects for products that have both horizontal and vertical parameters when the information propagation mechanism is observational learning instead of word of mouth.

The primary result stemming from examining singledimensional observational learning approaches is that agents tend to follow the early decision maker by matching their actions (e.g., Banerjee 1992, Bikhchandani et al. 1992, Smith and Sorensen 2000). Our study on multidimensional observational learning has a different focus and digs deeper into the magnitude of the observational learning effect. Not only do we examine how observational learning is impacted by both vertical and horizontal product differentiations, but we also integrate the relationship between the current and early decision makers defined through social ties. Our results document how the effect of observational learning is impacted by both product differentiation and social ties.

It is worth noting that observational learning is the process of learning through watching others, retaining the information, and then later replicating the behaviors that were observed. In economics, observational learning and rational herding belong to the same stream of literature (Banerjee 1992, Smith and Sorensen 2000, Çelen and Karin 2004) since both correspond to situations where individuals “learn” by observing the behavior of others. Observational learning focuses on the agent behavior of the model. The focal agent does not directly observe a predecessor’s informative signal, instead, the focal agent infers this signal based on the observed behavior of the predecessor. By contrast, rational herding behavior (irrational herding has no direct relationship to observational learning), as proposed by Banerjee (1992) is a possible result of observational learning. Under the setting of observational learning, even a focal agent that is perfectly rational may still exhibit herding behavior. Smith and Sorensen (2000) note that an informational cascade occurs when all individuals ignore their private information in making a decision, whereas, under herd behavior, all individuals make an identical decision, without always ignoring their private information.

## 2.2. Search/Experience/Credence Goods

Depending upon the availability of product attribute information, one classification relates to whether the product/service is a search, experience, or credence good. In general, search goods are those for which consumers can acquire full information on all product attributes before purchase, whereas experience goods are those for which product attribute information is only revealed after purchase and subsequent consumption of the product (Nelson 1970). Unlike search and experience goods, credence goods are those for which product attributes cannot be assessed even after purchase and consumption (Darby and Karni 1973). Consumers of credence goods can observe derived utility ex post, but they are unable to evaluate whether product attributes match their preferences ex ante (Dulleck et al. 2011).

Since product attribute information for search goods can be acquired prior to purchase, it is not surprising that there is limited evidence of using observationa learning for such products. Whereas Hendricks et al. (2012) integrate observational learning in a model of search goods, Li and Wu (2013) compare observational learning for experience versus search goods and find that observational learning has a more substantial effect on sales of experience goods than search goods.

Information goods such as books, music records, movies, and software are regarded as experience goods (Shapiro et al. 1998). Since the quality of experience goods is ex ante uncertain, consumers potentially value the actions of previous consumers as signals for matching preferences or determining product quality. Villas-Boas (2006) analyzes competitive effects in a duopoly setting for firms offering experience goods. There is also an extensive stream of literature that empirically establishes observationa learning as the mechanism for aiding consumer adoption decisions for various experience goods. Examples include software (Duan et al. 2009), crowdfunding platforms (Ward and Ramachandran 2010), movies (Moretti 2011, Lee et al. 2015), digital music market (Dewan and Ramaprasad 2012, Dewan et al. 2017), online books (Wang et al. 2018), and restaurant meals (Cai et al. 2009).

Health services or medical treatment, automotive repair, and various expert services are examples of credence goods (Emons 1997, Beck et al. 2014, Khurana et al. 2019). Prior studies suggest the existence of observational learning for credence goods. For example, Zhang (2010) finds evidence of observational learning in the U.S. kidney market. The decision makers (physicians) in Dai and Singh (2020) anticipate observational learning from their peers while making a diagnostic testing decision. Carrera and Villas-Boas (2015) find that observational learning increases generic drugs’ market share significantly Using Groupon data, Cao et al. (2018) show that credence goods are more affected by deep discounts than experience goods.

In our paper, product attribute information on a good is provided by individuals who have experienced the product. We focus on observational learn ing for horizontally and vertically differentiated experience goods. In particular, we examine the interaction between social networks and product differentiation in observational learning, which has not been addressed in prior studies on observational learning.

## 2.3. Information Provider Dynamics

Guarino et al. (2011) model imperfect observability of other individuals’ actions and assume that a consumer can only observe the aggregate purchase decision of the predecessors. Acemoglu et al. (2011) develop a model for social learning with stochastic sampling. In their paper, the agents know their position and the positions of sampled individuals in sequential decision making. Motivated by the social sharing feature on Facebook, a recent research stream focuses on observational learning in social networks and the role of network topology: People connected in social networks can only observe friends’ choices (Acemoglu et al. 2011, Qiu and Whinston 2017).

Though most studies in social networks consider observing adoption decisions by friends, there is a growing stream of literature that evaluates the informative signals from both friends and strangers. It is worth noting that the term “friend” is used to denote that user type is known or the individuals share some common interests, whereas “strangers” or “community” refers to the rest of the population that is observed. Lee et al. (2015) empirically examine the differential impact of prior movie ratings by strangers versus friends and find that friends’ ratings always induce herding. Zhang et al. (2015) apply a gametheoretic model to study the impact of the size of friend and stranger network on information accuracy and usefulness in aggregation. Ameri et al. (2019) empirically study the impact of word of mouth and observed adoptions on the anime watching decisions within a friend network and a community network (includes the friend network). Sun et al. (2019b) also distinguish between friend and community network. They find that agents tend to conform to the popular choices in a friend network while ignoring the popular choice in the community network. Different from their study, our paper considers how learning is moderated by observing a friend or stranger’s purchase decision for a product that has both horizontal and vertical dimensions.

## 2.4. Social Ties

Granovetter (1973) illustrates that an individual’s decisions are affected by their close friends’ decisions because they trust them. Further, it is argued that weak ties tend to bring in more novel information than strong ties. However, using field experiments, Bakshy et al. (2012) and Aral and Walker (2014) find that a strong tie is more influential than a weak tie in information propagation. Bapna et al. (2017a, b) investigate the effect of social ties on trust formation and forgiveness. Instead of such a focus, we examine how social ties and product differentiation together influence observational learning.

## 2.5. Multimethodology Approach

Although earlier observational learning studies focus on the analytical modeling approach, more recent work has adopted empirical and experimental methods to either establish the presence of observational learning in various contexts or separate the information propa gation method of observational learning versus other confounding mechanisms such as word of mouth (Li and Wu 2018) or saliency effects (Cai et al. 2009). There are several empirical studies assessing learning effects (using archive data) in different contextual settings such as software adoption (Duan et al. 2009), kidney market (Zhang 2010), movie sales (Moretti 2011), online deals (Chen et al. 2011), online microloan markets (Zhang and Liu 2012), and the digital music market (Newberry 2016). Specifically, within the context of social networks, Shi and Whinston (2013) and Qiu et al. (2018) examine learning within location-based social networks using empirical methods. In the latter study, Qiu et al. (2018) estimate an empirical model of restaurant discovery and observational learning, which allows them to separate observational learning from noninformational confounding mechanisms, such as homophily.

In addition to analytical and empirical work, numerous experimental studies provide evidence of observational learning. For example, Cipriani and Guarino (2005) find a significant observational learning effect in a laboratory financial market, whereas Goeree et al. (2007) find that agents tend to pay more attention to their own signals and less to past publicly observed choices in the context of observational learning.

Using a multimethodology approach, this paper extends prior research by exploring how product differentiation and social ties moderate the impact of observational learning. The adoption information is available from two sources, namely friends and strangers. This approach allows us to robustly examine how learning effects vary by product type as well as types of the information provider.

## 3. A Simple Analytical Framework 3.1. Preliminaries

Consider an early consumer j and a late consumer i deciding whether to purchase a product. The product has both vertical (quality) and horizontal (taste) di mensions. In the vertical dimension, all consumers agree on the preference order of an attribute, whereas on the horizontal dimension, consumers have heterogeneous tastes for the same attribute (Chen and Xie 2005).

In the vertical dimension, the quality of the product is unknown and is represented by a binary random variable: $V \in \{ V _ { H } , V _ { L } \}$ , and ${ \cal V } _ { L } = \alpha \dot { V } _ { H } ,$ , where $0 < \alpha < 1$ Following the classical observational learning literature (Bikhchandani et al. 1992), we assume a common prior on the probability that the product is of high or low quality: $\operatorname* { P r } ( V _ { H } ) = \operatorname* { P r } ( V _ { L } ) = \overset { . } { 1 } / 2 . \overset { 3 } { 1 }$ In online $\mathrm { A p \mathrm { - } }$ pendix A2, we extend this simple analytical framework by considering $\mathrm { P r } ( V _ { H } ) = \hat { \beta } ,$ , where $\overset { \cdot } { 0 } < \beta < 1$ , and the analytical insights remain the same.

In the horizontal dimension, consumers i and j have an exogenous taste for the product. Following Chen and Xie (2008), we assume the exogenous taste as a preference matching process. The type of the product is unknown and is represented by a binary random variable: $\Pi \in \{ T _ { 0 } , T _ { 1 } \}$ . Similarly, consumer i’s type is $T _ { i } \in \{ T _ { 0 } , T _ { 1 } \}$ (consumer j’s type is $T _ { j } \in \{ T _ { 0 } , T _ { 1 } \} )$ , which is the consumer’s private information. Assuming a common prior on the distribution of types: The types Π and ${ \bar { T } } _ { i } ~ ( \mathrm { o r } ~ T _ { j } )$ are independently drawn from a Bernoulli distribution with $\mathrm { P r } ( \bar { T } _ { 0 } ) = \mathrm { P r } ( T _ { 1 } ) = 1 / 2$ . If $\Pi = T _ { 0 } , \ T _ { i } = T _ { 0 } \ ( T _ { j } = T _ { 0 } )$ , or $\Pi = T _ { 1 } , \ T _ { i } = T _ { 1 } ( T _ { j } = T _ { 1 } ) ,$ we say that the taste is matched; otherwise, the taste is not matched.

The payoff functions of consumers i and j depend on their purchase decisions, the price of the product, the quality of the product, and whether the taste is matched. If consumer i or j purchases the product, the payoff function is as follows:

$$
u _ {i} \big (\mathrm{or} u _ {j} \big) = V - r \cdot I _ {\{u n m a t c h e d \}} - p,\tag{1}
$$

where $I _ { \{ u n m a t c h e d \} }$ is an indicator function, which takes the value of one if the taste is not matched, and takes the value of zero if the taste is matched, and $p$ is the product price. If the product does not match the consumer’s taste, there is an unmatched cost, r. We assume that the product price, $p ,$ is exogenously given, and we set it to be the consumer’s ex ante expected value gained from the product:

$$
\begin{array}{l} p = \mathbb {E} [ V - r \cdot I _ {\{u n m a t c h e d \}} ] = \frac {1}{2} V _ {H} + \frac {1}{2} V _ {L} - \frac {1}{2} r \\ = \frac {1 + \alpha}{2} V _ {H} - \frac {1}{2} r. \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

Since our focus is not on dynamic pricing in the presence of observational learning (as in Jing 2011a, Garcia and Shelegia 2018), a consumers’ ex ante expected value is also the optimal (ex ante) price charged by the firm given that a consumer will purchase the product only when $\mathbb { E } [ u _ { i } | I _ { i } ] > 0 ,$ , where I is consumer $i ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ information set. This assumption is also consistent with the settings in prior literature on observational learning (Qiu and Whinston 2017).

Prior to making a decision, early consumer j can access binary private signals about the product quality and product types. The signal on product quality can be either $S _ { H }$ or $S _ { L } ,$ and satisfies $\mathsf { \bar { P r } } ( S _ { H } | V _ { H } ) = \mathsf { \bar { P r } } ( S _ { L } |$ $V _ { L } ) = q , { \mathrm { P r } } ( S _ { L } | V _ { H } ) = { \mathrm { P r } } ( S _ { H } | V _ { L } ) = 1 - q ,$ , where $1 / 2 { < } q { < } 1$ The signal on product types can be either $S _ { 0 }$ or $S _ { 1 , }$ and satisfies $\mathrm { \hat { P r } } ( S _ { 0 } | T _ { 0 } ) \stackrel { \cdot \cdot } { = } \mathrm { P r } ( S _ { 1 } | T _ { 1 } ) = w ,$ $\mathrm { P r } ( S _ { 1 } | T _ { 0 } ) =$ $\begin{array} { r } { \operatorname* { P r } ( S _ { 0 } | T _ { 1 } ) = 1 - w . } \end{array}$ , where $1 / 2 < w < 1$ . Note that q and w measure the precision of the signals. Note also that $q > 1 / 2$ implies that if the product quality is $V _ { H } \left( V _ { L } \right)$ consumer j is more likely to receive $S _ { H } \left( S _ { L } \right)$ . Similarly, $w > 1 / 2$ implies that if the product type is $T _ { 0 } ~ ( T _ { 1 } ) .$ consumer j is more likely to receive $S _ { 0 } \left( S _ { 1 } \right)$ . If q or w is higher, the signal is more informative.

The sequence of the game and the information set of each player in each stage of the game are as follows.

. Early consumer j receives private signals about <sup>Stage 1</sup>product quality/types and makes a purchase decision. Consumer $j ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ information set includes the following:

i. Private signal on product quality.

ii. Private signal on product type.

iii. Prior product quality information: the common prior on product quality $\dot { \mathrm { P r } } ( V _ { H } ) = \mathrm { P r } ( V _ { L } ) = 1 / 2$

iv. Prior product type information: the common prior on product type $\mathrm { P r } ( T _ { 0 } ) = \mathrm { P r } ( T _ { 1 } ) = 1 / 2$ , and consumer j knows $j ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s }$ own type, $T _ { j } \in \{ T _ { 0 } , T _ { 1 } \}$

Stage 2. Late consumer i observes early consumer $j ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ decision and makes a purchase decision. Consumer i’s information set contains the following:

i. Early consumer $j ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ decision (purchase or not).

ii. Whether early consumer j is a stranger or late consumer i’s friend (we will define stranger and friend in Section 3.3).

iii. Prior product quality information: the common prior on product quality $\dot { \mathrm { P r } } ( V _ { H } ) = \mathrm { P r } ( V _ { L } ) = 1 / 2$

iv. Prior product type information: the common prior on product type $\mathrm { \hat { P r } } ( T _ { 0 } ) = \mathrm { P r } ( T _ { 1 } ) = 1 / 2 ,$ , and consumer i knows $i ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ own type, $T _ { i } \in \{ T _ { 0 } , T _ { 1 } \}$

Thus, the key difference in terms of information sets for the early consumer j and late consumer i is: (a) late consumer i does not receive private signals on product type and quality; (b) late consumer i has prior purchase information vis-a-vis early consumer\` $j ;$ and (c) late consumer i also has information on whether early consumer j is a stranger or friend.

The rationale why late consumer i does not receive private signals and has less information than early consumer j is as follows. Early consumers are typically the most loyal fans of a brand, and firms target these loyal fans (potential early adopters) with different brand loyalty programs. For example, many firms are sponsoring offline and online brand community events, which provide new product information to a selected group of loyal fans (Muniz and O’Guinn 2001, Jing 2011b, Bapna et al. 2019). These loyal fans are very likely to be early adopters and obtain more product information than late consumers through brand community events. Another example is that many vendors of software and equipment provide online/onsite new product demonstrations and training seminars for a selected group of loyal customers, which makes early consumers obtain more product information than late consumers (Jing 2011b). These examples motivate our assumption that focal consumer i (late consumer) does not receive private signals and has less information than early consumer j.

Moreover, late consumers are, in general, less incentivized to acquire product information than early consumers. The reason is that information acquisition is typically costly. For example, Qiu et al. (2014) show that when information acquisition is costly, people are less likely to acquire information by themselves and are more likely to free-ride on other people’s information acquisition. A similar logic applies here. Late consumers have an incentive to be free riders and do observational learning without paying the cost of information acquisition. Therefore, late consumers may not receive private signals. This model setting (early consumers have more information than late consumers) simplifies our model and allows us to focus on the effect of observational learning. The mechanism of observational learning is important when early consumers have more information than late consumers. In such a case, late consumers can infer useful product information by observing early consumers’ decisions. If late consumers have other information sources, the impact of observational learning on purchases is confounded by other effects.

## 3.2. Analytical Results

Without loss of generality, we consider a generic early consumer j of type $T _ { j } = T _ { 0 }$ . The following lemma characterizes consumer $j ^ { \prime } \boldsymbol { \mathrm { s } }$ decision rule.

Lemma 1. If early consumer j of type $T _ { j } = T _ { 0 }$ receives signals $S _ { H }$ and $S _ { \mathrm { 0 } } ,$ the consumer will always purchase the product; if consumer j receives signals $\bar { S } _ { L }$ and $S _ { 1 } ,$ , the consumer will not purchase the product; if consumer j receives signals $S _ { H }$ and $S _ { 1 } ,$ , the consumer will purchase the product when $\frac { V _ { H } ( 1 - \alpha ) } { r } { > } \frac { w - \frac { 1 } { 2 } } { q - \frac { 1 } { 7 } } ;$ if consumer j receives signals $S _ { L }$ and $S _ { 0 . }$ , the consumer will purchase the product when $\frac { V _ { H } ( 1 - \alpha ) } { r } < \frac { w - \frac { 1 } { 2 } } { q - \frac { 1 } { 2 } } .$

Although only stated for a consumer of type $T _ { 0 } ,$ , the underlying logic of Lemma 1 also applies for a consumer of type $T _ { 1 }$ . The results are that for a representative early consumer j:

• Always purchase the product if the consumer’s private signals favor high quality and matched taste.

• Do not purchase the product if the consumer’s private signals favor low quality and unmatched taste.

• If the consumer’s private signals favor high quality but unmatched taste, the consumer will purchase the product provided the ratio of quality mismatch to the cost of taste mismatch is significant (or the precision of the taste to quality signal is low); else the consumer will not purchase the product.

• If the consumer’s private signals favor low quality but matched taste, the consumer will purchase the product provided the ratio of quality mismatch to the cost of taste mismatch is small (or the precision of the taste to quality signal is high); else the consumer will not purchase the product.

When $V _ { H } ( 1 - \alpha )$ is larger (smaller) relative to r, the quality differential is more significant (less significant) relative to the taste cost, and thus, the product is more (less) vertically than horizontally differentiated. Correspondingly, when $\begin{array} { r } { w - \frac { 1 } { \lambda } } \end{array}$ is larger (smaller) relative to $\begin{array} { r } { q - \frac { 1 } { 2 } , } \end{array}$ , the precision of the signal corresponding to product type is greater (lesser) than the signal corresponding to quality, and thus, there is better information available for the horizontal (vertical) attribute than the vertical (horizontal) attribute.

The intuition behind Lemma 1 is as follows. When both of the signals favor desirable outcomes (high quality in the vertical dimension and matched taste in the horizontal dimension), consumer j will purchase the product. When both of the signals favor undesirable outcomes (low quality in the vertical dimension and unmatched taste in the horizontal dimension), consumer j will not purchase the product. In the following two more complicated scenarios, consumer j receives one signal that favors a desirable outcome, and the other signal that favors an undesirable outcome:

i. Consumer j receives a quality signal that favors the desirable outcome in the vertical dimension and a taste signal that favors the undesirable outcome in the horizontal dimension.

ii. Consumer j receives a taste signal that favors the desirable outcome in the horizontal dimension and a quality signal that favors the undesirable outcome in the vertical dimension.

In these two scenarios, consumer j should make a decision based on the relative weight of the quality and taste components in the payoff function. If the quality component is more important $\frac { V _ { H } ( 1 - \alpha ) } { r }$ is large), consumer j will follow the quality signal: The consumer will purchase the product in scenario (i), and not purchase the product in scenario (ii). If the taste component is more important $( \frac { V _ { H } ( 1 - \alpha ) } { r }$ is small), consumer j will follow the taste signal: The consumer will not purchase the product in scenario (i), and purchase the product in scenario (ii).

## 3.3. Information Provider Dynamics

How do the characteristics of the information provider relate to the decision for a typical consumer? In order to address this issue, we consider two cases. In the first case, this typical consumer observes a stranger’s decision, and in the second case, the consumer observes a friend’s decision. In our model, if two people are friends, their types are perfectly correlated; if two people are strangers, their types are uncorrelated. This setting follows from the prior literature that demonstrates inherent similarities in friends’ personal tastes and characteristics (Aral and Walker 2011, Gu et al. 2014, Qiu et al. 2017, 2018). Our model assumption about friends and strangers captures this phenomenon: friends’ types (tastes) are correlated, and strangers’ types (tastes) are uncorrelated.

3.3.1. Observing a Stranger’s Decision. We assume that before making a decision, late consumer i is able to observe a stranger, early consumer $j ^ { \prime } \boldsymbol { \mathrm { s } }$ decision. Because consumer i and consumer j are strangers, we assume that consumer i does not know consumer $j ^ { \prime } \boldsymbol { \mathrm { s } }$ $\mathrm { t y p e , }$ but knows consumer $j ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s }$ type is drawn from a Bernoulli distribution with $\bar { \mathrm { P r } } ( \bar { T _ { 0 } } ) ^ { \bullet } = \mathrm { P r } ( T _ { 1 } ) = 1 / 2$ . The realization of consumer $j ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s }$ type is $T _ { j } \in \{ T _ { 0 } , T _ { 1 } \}$ . Without loss of generality, we assume that $T _ { j } = T _ { 0 }$ . The same logic follows if $T _ { j } = T _ { 1 }$

Early consumer j has faced a similar decision problem and has made a decision. Early consumer $j ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s }$ payoff function is the same as late consumer i’s: $u _ { j } = V - r \cdot$ $I _ { \{ u n m a t c h e d \} } - p$ . The only difference between the consumers’ decision problems is that early consumer j receives private signals about product quality, $V ,$ and product type, Π. These private signals that early consumer j receives could be interpreted as private information that the consumer has regarding the attributes of the product (Welch 1992). However, late consumer i does not have access to this private information, which indicates that the consumer does not receive private signals about the product quality and type, and does not have access to early consumer $j ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s }$ private signals. This stems from the base premise of observational learning, which is that late consumer i cannot directly observe early consumer $j ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s }$ private signals, but can infer these signals from the early consumer $j ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ decision.

From consumer $j ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s }$ decision (purchase or not), consumer i may learn useful information. In the following two propositions, we present the results of observational learning from a stranger’s decision, which critically depends on whether the product is more quality or taste oriented.

Proposition 1. In the context of learning from a stranger, $i f$ the product is more quality oriented, that $\begin{array} { r } { i s , \frac { V _ { H } } { r } { > } \frac { w - \frac { 1 } { 2 } } { ( 1 - \alpha ) \left( q - \frac { 1 } { 2 } \right) } , } \end{array}$ late consumer i will follow early consumer $j ^ { \prime } s$ decision (a stranger’s decision): Consumer i will purchase the product when consumer j purchases the product; consumer i will not purchase the product when consumer j does not purchase the product.

Proposition 2. In the context of learning from a stranger, $i f$ the product is more taste oriented, that is $\begin{array} { r } { \prime \frac { V _ { H } } { r } < \frac { w - \frac { 1 } { 2 } } { ( 1 - \alpha ) \left( q - \frac { 1 } { 2 } \right) } , } \end{array}$ , late consumer i will ignore early consumer $j ^ { \prime } s$ decision (a stranger’s decision) and use the consumer’s own information to make the decision.

Proposition 1 shows that since the learning effect from a stranger is very strong when the product is more vertically differentiated, late consumer i will completely follow early consumer $j ^ { \prime } \boldsymbol { \mathrm { s } }$ decision. However, Proposition 2 demonstrates that this learning effect from a stranger does not exist when the product is more horizontally differentiated. Note that in Proposition 2, late consumer $i ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ own information is the common prior on product quality and type, which does not include early consumer $j ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s }$ decision. In particular, in the case of Proposition 2, consumer i will be indifferent between purchasing and not pur chasing the product since prior information implies that $\mathbb { E } [ u _ { i } ] = \dot { 0 } .$

Late consumer $i ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ decision in Proposition 1 is the same as early consumer $j ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s }$ decision in Lemma 1, but their strategies are different. Since there is a distinct difference between decision/action and strategy in game theory (Fudenberg and Tirole 1991), in our context, a decision is either purchase or not purchase. In contrast, a strategy is a mapping from a player’s information set to the decision set, which translates to a decision rule rather than a decision. Given the asymmetry in product quality and type information between the early and late consumers (e.g., the early consumer has access to this information whereas the late consumer does not), their similar behavior stems from observational learning effects rather than identical signals. Observational learning allows late consumer i to obtain additional product quality and type information by observing early consumer $j ^ { \prime } \boldsymbol { \mathrm { s } }$ decision, and this, in turn, helps late consumer i make a better decision. This is the primary motivation for observational learning in our model. In online Appendix A3, we examine a benchmark model showing consumer behavior when observational learning is not available.

Why does the effect of observational learning from a stranger depend on whether the product is quality or taste oriented? In our multidimensional learning model, late consumer i may learn from consumer $j ^ { \prime } \boldsymbol { \mathrm { s } }$ decision in two potential dimensions: (i) the vertical dimension, that is, whether early consumer j receives a signal favoring high quality; and (ii) the horizontal dimension, that is, whether early consumer j receives a signal favoring matched taste. The key is that consumer i does not know consumer j’s type because they are strangers. In other words, there is no additional value for consumer i to have better information on whether consumer $j ^ { \prime } \boldsymbol { \mathrm { s } }$ type matches the product type because consumer $j ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s }$ type is unknown to consumer i. Therefore, the learning process in the horizontal dimension is blocked. On the other hand, for a quality-oriented product, consumer i is able to infer the underlying quality very well through observational learning from a stranger’s decision.

In Propositions 1 and $^ { 2 , }$ we have a threshold condition: If the product is more quality oriented, that is, $\begin{array} { r } { \frac { V _ { H } } { r } > \frac { w - \frac { 1 } { 2 } } { ( 1 - \alpha ) \left( q - \frac { 1 } { 2 } \right) } , } \end{array}$ consumer i will follow consumer $j ^ { \prime } \mathsf { s }$ 5 decision (a stranger’s decision); if the product is more taste oriented, that is, $\begin{array} { r } { \frac { V _ { H } } { r } { < \frac { w - \frac { 1 } { 2 } } { ( 1 - \alpha ) \left( q - \frac { 1 } { 2 } \right) } } , } \end{array}$ consumer i will ignore consumer $j ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s }$ decision (a stranger’s decision) and use the consumer’s own information to make the decision. Next, we examine the impact of the signal precisions (w and $q ) .$ , and the quality difference (α) on this threshold condition.

First, as the precision of the signal on product quality, $q ,$ increases, the condition $\frac { V _ { H } } { r } > \frac { \stackrel { - } { w - \frac { 1 } { 2 } } } { ( 1 - \alpha ) \big ( q - \frac { 1 } { 2 } \big ) }$ is satisfied under a broader range of parameter conditions. This implies that when the product is more quality oriented, consumer i is more likely to follow consumer $j ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s }$ decision (a stranger’s decision). The intuition is that consumer $j ^ { \prime } \boldsymbol { \mathrm { s } }$ decision contains more useful information if the signal on product quality is more precise. Therefore, the learning effect is stronger.

Second, as the precision of the signal on product types, $w ,$ increases, the condition $\overset { \vartriangle } { \frac { V _ { H } } { r } } > \frac { \ v { w } ^ { - } - \frac { 1 } { 2 } } { ( 1 - \alpha ) \left( q - \frac { 1 } { 7 } \right) }$ is satisfied under a smaller range of parameter conditions. This implies that when the product is more taste oriented, consumer i is less likely to follow consumer $j ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s }$ decision (a stranger’s decision). The intuition is that consumer $j ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s }$ decision contains less useful information if the signal on product types is more precise (consumer $j ^ { \prime } \bar { \bf s }$ behavior is more likely to be driven by the consumer’s taste rather than the product quality). Therefore, the learning effect is weaker.

Third, as the quality difference, $1 - \alpha ,$ increases (since $V _ { L } = \alpha V _ { H } ,$ , a larger α implies a smaller quality difference), the condition $\frac { V _ { H } } { r } { > } \frac { w { - } \frac { 1 } { 2 } } { ( 1 { - } \alpha ) ( q { - } \frac { 1 } { 2 } ) }$ is satisfied under a broader range of parameter conditions. This implies that when the product is more quality ori ented, consumer i is less likely to follow consumer $j ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s }$ decision (a stranger’s decision). The intuition is that as the quality difference, $1 - \alpha$ , increases, the uncertainty related to product quality increases. Thus, focal consumer i benefits more from learning when the product quality uncertainty is high, and this results in a more substantial learning effect.

It is useful to compare the learning cases with the case in which consumer i does not observe consumer $j ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s }$ decision (nonlearning case). In the nonlearning case, consumer i uses the prior belief to determine purchase behavior. According to Equations (1) and (2), the consumer should be indifferent between purchasing and not purchasing. However, in the case of learning, consumer i’s decision will be affected by the observation of consumer $j ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s }$ decision, as described in Propositions 1 and 2.

3.3.2. Observing a Friend’s Decision. In this scenario, before making a decision, consumer i can observe a friend, early consumer $j ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s }$ decision. In prior literature, homophily is a typical phenomenon observed in social networks in which there are inherent similarities in friends’ personal characteristics (Aral and Walker 2011, Gu et al. 2014, Qiu et al. 2017). In other words, homophily refers to the tendency of individuals to associate with similar others, as in the proverb “birds of a feather flock together.” Following the idea of homophily, we assume that consumers i and $j$ have the same type (same taste) because they are friends. Other model settings are the same as the case of learning from a stranger.

From a modeling perspective, our assumption of friends (i.e., the late and early consumers are of the same type) is a theoretical abstraction. It simply implies that friends are more likely to have similar tastes and characteristics than strangers. Assuming that friends have the same type allows us to capture the essential idea that friends are more likely to have similar tastes and characteristics than strangers. At the same time, it results in an analytically tractable model.

There is a substantive stream of information systems, economics, and sociology literature on homophily (McPherson et al. 2001, Aral et al. 2009, Currarini et al. 2009, Gu et al. 2014, Lee et al. 2016, Qiu et al. 2017), which motivates our definition of friends as being of the same type. Because of the homophily effect, it has been argued that friends are more likely to have similar tastes and characteristics than strangers. A review of the empirical literature on homophily indicates that patterns of homophily are remarkably robust in different contexts: People’s friend networks are homogeneous with regard to many demographic and social characteristics. For example, in IT settings, Lee et al. (2016) show that in a location-based social network, users with more similar tastes, habits, and other characteristics (captured by their biographic information and places they have visited) are more likely to become friends. Users exhibit a significant pattern of homophily in a global instant messaging network (Aral et al. 2009) and in online investment communities (Gu et al. 2014). In non-IT settings, Shrum et al. (1988) find that schoolmate friendship is more likely to be formed when students belong to the same racial groups. People are more likely to become friends if they have similar education levels (Marsden 1987), similar cultural backgrounds (Mark 2003), or share similar political orientations (Huckfeldt and Sprague 1995). Moreover, friendship ties between nonsimilar individuals also dissolve at a higher rate (McPherson et al. 2001). This is the motivation to assume that friends have the same type in our stylized theoretical model.

In addition, theoretical studies on homophily identify underlying mechanisms of homophily, and these further reinforce the manner in which we define friends. For example, in psychology, the mechanism of homophily is that perceived similarity in various characteristics emotionally attracts people to become friends for ease of communication and shared tastes (Huston and Levinger 1978). In economics, the mechanism of homophily is the process of making new friends through existing friends, which amplifies the mechanism in psychology by making the pattern of homophily much stronger (Currarini et al. 2009).

These mechanisms provide further support for our assumption for why friends are more likely to have similar tastes and characteristics than strangers. In the following proposition, we present the results of observational learning from a friend’s decision.

Proposition 3. In the context of learning from a friend, late consumer i will follow early consumer j’s decision (a friend’s decision) no matter if the product is more quality oriented or more taste oriented: Consumer i will purchase the product when consumer j purchases the product; consumer i will not purchase the product when consumer j does not purchase the product.

Proposition 3 shows that unlike learning from a stranger, the effect of observational learning from a friend does not depend on whether the product is more quality oriented or more taste oriented. Consumer i will completely follow a friend’s decision. The reason is that consumer i knows that consumer j has the same type, and consumer j has more information (private quality and taste signals). Given that consumer j has made a rational decision, consumer i should completely follow the friend’s decision. Recall that in the case of learning from a stranger, the situation is different: Consumer i is not sure if they share the same type.

## 3.4. Hypotheses

Our analytical results are that product type and in formation provider both moderate a potential consumers’ purchase decision. In this section, we propose several hypotheses to examine whether these results hold in an experimental setting. All our hypotheses are derived from our analytical model.

For a known information provider (i.e., a friend or a stranger), our analytics show that consumer decisions differ by product type (Propositions 1, 2, and 3). Hypothesis 1 (information provider is a stranger) and Hypothesis 2 (information provider is friend) examine whether the directionality stated in the analytical results hold in an experimental setting.

Hypothesis 1. The effect of learning from strangers is stronger for vertically differentiated products than for horizontally differentiated product: (i) For vertically differentiated products, a consumer tends to follow the previous stranger’s decision; (ii) for horizontally differentiated products, a consumer tends to ignore the previous stranger’s decision.

Hypothesis 1 is based on our analytical results (Propositions 1 and 2). For the vertically differentiated product, the consumer can interpret the product quality by observing the stranger’s purchase decision.

Hypothesis 2. The effect of learning from friends does not depend on whether the product is vertically or horizontally differentiated, and a consumer tends to follow a friend’s decision.

Hypothesis 2 is proposed based on our analytical results (Proposition 3). For a given product type, we show that differences in consumer purchase decisions depend upon the information provider. This leads to a higher magnitude of learning effect for horizontally differentiated products in the case of a friend as compared with a stranger. However, the magnitude of learning is not moderated by the information provider type in the case of a vertically differentiated product.

## 4. Experimental Study 1

## 4.1. Experimental Design

In Study 1, we conduct a controlled laboratory experiment to test hypotheses derived from our analytical framework. Controlled economic experiments are increasingly useful to strengthen internal validity and establish causal relations (Bapna et al. 2010, Rice 2012, Qiu et al. 2014, Yin et al. 2014, Adomavicius et al. 2018, Gupta et al. 2018). In our context, the laboratory setting allows us to control for many confounding factors. Although laboratory experiments may not promise quantitative external validity, they can provide qualitative external validity if the observed relationship is monotonic and does not change direction when changing the level of variables seen in the field relative to those in the laboratory (Kessler and Vesterlund 2015).

Our economic experiment has a 2x2 design: Corresponding to our analytical framework, we implement four experimental treatments (see Table 3), which involve manipulating the social connectedness of players (i.e., players are strangers or friends), as well as manipulating whether the product is quality or taste oriented. There were four experimental sessions, and each session corresponded to one treatment. We recruited 320 undergraduate students from a large university, and randomly assigned them to one of four experimental sessions (treatments). Each session had 80 participants. The detailed experimental instructions are in online Appendix B. Similar to the setup of the analytical framework, each player in the experiment was considering purchasing a product with both quality and taste uncertainty. The quality of the product can be high or low with probability 1/2, respectively. If the product quality is high, it is worth 100 tokens, and if the quality is low, it is worth 60 tokens. Each participant is a zero-type consumer, and the product can be a zero-type or one-type product with a probability 1/2, respectively. If the product is a zero-type product, then it fits the participant’s preference; otherwise, it does not. At the beginning of the experiment, each participant was given 65 initial tokens (100 tokens = \$10), which could be used to purchase the product. In our experiment, we set $\mathrm { \hat { \cal V } } _ { H } = 1 0 0 , \mathrm { \cal V } _ { L } = \mathrm { \hat { 6 } 0 } ,$ , and α 0.6.

In experimental sessions 1 and 3 (quality-oriented product), the unmatched cost, r, is set to be 30, and in experimental sessions 2 and 4 (taste-oriented product), the unmatched cost, r, is set to be 50.<sup>4</sup> If a participant does not purchase the product, the final payoff in the experiment is 65 tokens. If a participant purchases the product, the net payoff from purchasing the product is given by Equation (1). The price for the product in experimental session 1 is 65 tokens, according to Equation (2). A participant in experimental session 1 can use all the initial tokens to purchase the product. If the product is of high quality and matches the participant’s taste, according to Equation (1), the participant will obtain the utility value, $V - r \cdot I _ { \{ u n m a t c h e d \} } = V _ { H } = 1 0 0 .$ . In this case, the participant’s final payoff in the experiment is 100 tokens (higher than the initial 65 tokens, and the net gain from the transaction is 100 – 65 = 35 tokens). ## 5. Experimental Study 2

5.1. Experimental Design and Summary Statistics A limitation of experimental Study 1 is that in the treatment of learning from friends, a participant has no real social connections with the previous decision maker. It is difficult to envision a laboratory experiment that can fully mirror the circumstances of the external environment of interest. In particular, an exogenous type-matching process in our analytical framework may not capture closeness and intimacy in friendships in the field (Parks and

Floyd 1996). In this study, we conduct an experiment with more field elements to address this issue of artificial friendships. Specifically, we use data from the Facebook Application programming interface (API) to measure social ties that connect our subjects with each other. By looking at socially networked peers, rather than conducting the experiment with artificial friendships, an increase in external validity is the focus.

The focal issue of interest in this experiment is Hypothesis 3(i), that ${ \mathrm { i } } \mathbf { s } ,$ the effect of social ties on the magnitude of observational learning for horizontally differentiated products.<sup>6</sup> Recall that in experimental Study 1, the measure of social ties is an exogenous binary variable: The previous decision maker is either a friend or a stranger. If the previous decision maker is a friend, then the person’s type is known to the participant; otherwise, the type is unknown. This type-matching process could be viewed as overly simplistic since it does not capture many important aspects of social ties. In experimental Study 2, we propose three continuous measures to quantify the strength of social ties. We want to test a continuous version of Hypothesis 3(i), which is: If the social tie between a participant and the previous decision maker is stronger, the effect of observational learning for horizontally differentiated products is more significant.

In Study 2, participants were recruited via an initial email asking that any interested parties sign up for our Facebook application. This initial list of email addresses was obtained from undergraduate and graduate students, as well as from staff and coworkers, at a large university. Our subject pool consisted of 200 people who had Facebook accounts. We made sure that these subjects did not participate in Study 1. When subjects signed up for our experiment and agreed to participate, they granted us access to their Facebook wall. This meant that we were able to use individuals’ data to characterize the social ties between a participant and the previous decision maker using well-validated proxies (Bapna et al. 2017a, b). The following are the tie strength measures:

Gee et al. (2017) proposed two categories of tie strength measures: contact-based measures and structurebased measures. Contact-based tie strength records the number of interactions between an individual and a friend. Our tagged photos and shared wall posts belong to contact-based tie strength measures. In contrast, structure-based measures use the structure of the network to model tie strength and capture the idea of bridging across different groups. Our embeddedness (shared common friends) is a structure-based tie strength measure. According to our estimation results, the role of embeddedness is much smaller than that of tagged photos and shared wall posts, suggesting that contact-based tie strength is more critical than structure-based tie strength in accelerating observational learning. The intuition behind this result is as follows: Contact-based measures (like shared wall posts and tagged photos) indicate stronger ties between friends as compared with structure-based measures (such as common friends). Thus, the effect of learning is more significant between friends for contactbased measures than for structure-based measures.

## 6. Discussion and Conclusions

Brands are using creative methods to encourage consumers to share their purchase information online, such as making their products and packaging aesthetically pleasing or more “instagramable,” and using emotions such as humor to connect to customers. Online retailers have an unprecedented opportunity to increase engagement and nudge consumers by engineering observational learning and increase sales (Cheng et al. 2020). For example, Kohl’s (a department store) has a social gallery, which displays posts on its products from both Twitter and Instagram and allows consumers to purchase via direct links to the product home page.<sup>9</sup> Several other social networking platforms provide direct purchasing links to products shared by the brand’s consumers. These features enable potential consumers to learn about the product by observing the purchase decisions of others. Thus, understanding how product differentiation and social ties moderate observational learning helps retailers target influential consumers.

In this paper, an analytical framework of multidimensional observational learning is proposed. Although previous research has explored the role of learning in a friend or stranger network, this paper presents a unified framework of observational learning with elements of both information provider dynamics and product type. In this framework, the interaction effects of product differentiation and social ties are characterized. Experimental evidence for such in teraction effects is also provided. Not only does this research fill a research gap, but it also addresses an essential aspect of consumer decision making using a multimethodology approach.

Our research sheds light on how retailers should increase observational learning for different types of products. Our first result is that the effect of learning from strangers is stronger for vertically differentiated products than for horizontally differentiated products. Thus, for utilitarian or functional products (e.g., external memory hard disks, small electronics such as headphones, webcams, and kitchen appliances), where quality may be more relevant than taste, retailers can incentivize their customers to share their recent purchases with other customers on social networks. For example, Bose, the American audio equipment manufacturer, displays the purchases made by their customers (strangers) on their website.

On the other hand, for more taste-oriented products (e.g., clothing of different colors, food, and sodas), the effect of learning from friends is more substantial than that from strangers. For such products, retailers will benefit from incentivizing the customer to share their purchases within their own networks (i.e., friends). For example, Dunkin Donuts uses nanoinfluencers (people who have as few as 1,000 followers and are willing to advertise products on socia media) to promote their “coffee first strategy.” Since nano-influencers have a niche following around a particular topic, their followers know them more intimately and have the same tastes.<sup>10</sup>

Another strategy retailers can employ to exploit this asymmetric learning for horizontally and vertically differentiated products is by cooperating with social media platforms in targeting influential customers. Specifically, retailers with marketing budgets offering horizontally differentiated products (such as apparel with different colors) have a substantial incentive to cooperate with social media platforms (e.g., Instagram and Pinterest) in targeting influential customers (Huang et al. 2018). For example, after finishing an online transaction, online retailers can encourage influential customers of such products to share their purchases via social media through a discount scheme (the discount scheme can be a simple percent discount or be based on consumer contests, see Liu et al. 2007). Since purchases by influential customers can lead to significantly higher observational learning effects for horizontally differentiated products, retailers could offer social rewards (e.g., coupons, charity donations, VIP experiences, loyalty points, or sweepstakes prizes) to such customers. Other effective methods employed to encourage consumers to share their purchase pictures are unique and catchy hashtags, responding thoughtfully to customers, and/or showcasing their product purchase pictures on the retailer’s website.

We also show that for horizontally differentiated products, the effect of learning from friends increases with the strength of social ties. By forming alliances with social networks, retailers can target consumers based on the tie strength measures: embeddedness (shared friends), tagged photos, and shared wall posts. The result that contact-based tie strength (i.e., networks connecting users through tagged posts and photos) is more critical than structure-based tie strength (i.e., networks connecting users through shared friends) in accelerating observational learning is also essential in choosing among different social networks.

Future research endeavors could be directed at empirically examining the role of network structures in the context of multidimensional observational learning; and investigating whether our findings are robust for networks across geographical boundaries, whether a more granular definition of the degree of connectivity (e.g., structure based versus contact based) impacts the degree of learning, and whether there are differences between wordof-mouth (learning from others’ comments) effects and observational learning (learning from others actions) in attempting to explain customer purchase decisions (Chen et al. 2011, Susarla et al. 2016, Li and Wu 2018).

Understanding consumers’ product discovery and buying decisions facilitates the development of new and innovative techniques to streamline sales processes and increase customer engagement. Traditionally, retailers engaged with the consumers through brand posts, and the organic reach<sup>11</sup> of such approaches was substantial. In more contemporary settings, a declining organic reach, coupled with adblockers, has increased firms’ reliance on their early consumer’s product purchase posts within social networks to increase overall reach. Considering the ubiquity of social networks in sharing purchases, retailers must craft their marketing strategies to effectively induce and manage observational learning. As consumers become inundated with business and sponsored posts, brands can harness their early and loyal consumers’ power to nudge later consumers by sharing the right product with the right group of people.
