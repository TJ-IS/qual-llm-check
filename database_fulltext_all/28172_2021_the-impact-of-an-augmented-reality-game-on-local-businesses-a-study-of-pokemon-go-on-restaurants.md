Abstract. Pokemon Go, a mobile game that utilizes augmented reality (AR) technology,´ garnered tremendous public interest upon release with an average of 20 million active daily users. The game combines geospatial elements with gami<sup>fi</sup>cation practices to incentivize user movement in the physical world. In this study, we examine the potential externalities that such incentives may have on associated businesses. Particularly, we study the impact of Pokemon Go on local restaurants by using online reviews as a proxy for consumer en- ´ gagement and perception. We treat the release of Pokemon Go as a natural experiment and´ study the postrelease impact on associated restaurants. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that restaurants associated with Pokemon Go do indeed enjoy a higher level of consumer engagement and more posi-´ tive consumer perception. In addition, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the characteristics of a restaurant moderate these effects signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly. To the best of our knowledge, our study is the <sup>fi</sup>rst to examine the economic implications of augmented-reality applications. Therefore, our research lays the foundation for future studies to examine how augmented-reality applications affect consumer economic behavior. Our study also provides insights for business owners and policymakers regarding the potential value of being associated with AR-based applications.

## 1. Introduction

Augmented-reality (AR) technologies have become increasingly prominent and important to businesses (Blau et al. 2017). The convergence of virtual and physical spaces is expected to enable commerce in location-based mixed reality settings to grow to as much as \$6.86 billion by 2024 (Grand View Research 2016). Not surprisingly, researchers have enthusiastically pursued this topic with a number of papers studying how this technology affects human behavior (e.g., Billinghurst 2002). That said, the focus of our paper is different. We study how the externalities caused by activities in the virtual world impact businesses in the physical world. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we study the effect of Pokemon Go, currently one´ of the most popular augmented-reality applications, on local businesses that are associated with the application.

Pokemon Go, a popular mobile game developed´ by Niantic, Inc., incorporates GPS capability and AR technology to locate, capture, battle, and train virtual creatures called Pokemon. Within two months of the´ game’s release on Android and iOS platforms in July 2016, the app generated more than \$440 million in revenue from the App Store and Google Play Store (Nelson 2016). It has become the biggest mobile game in U.S. history (Lovelace 2016). Even after two years, the game remains a worldwide phenomenon, engaging <sup>fi</sup>ve million daily active users and generating a lifetime gross revenue of \$2.2 billion (Dogtiev 2018, Pesce 2018). The popularity of Pokemon Go is such that retail chains, such as Starbucks´ in the United States and McDonald’s in Japan, started to collaborate with Niantic in a paid partnership to have ingame virtual artifacts—PokeStops and gyms´ <sup>1</sup>—at their retail locations (Liptak 2016, Olson 2016). These partnerships raise a number of interesting and unanswered questions. How much does having an in-game artifact at the location help a business retailer? How should Pokemon Go distrib-´ ute the locations of these artifacts in a manner that does not devalue the game for players? How should the gamebased sponsorships for retail chains differ from those of independent businesses? Of these questions, we study the <sup>fi</sup>rst in this paper. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we analyze the economic implications of being associated with Pokemon Go.´

In this study, we leverage a natural experiment that emerges because of the initial placement of in-game artifacts in Pokemon Go. During the´ <sup>fi</sup>rst release, these artifacts were placed at several noncommercial locations. Some restaurants happened to be located inside the radius of the artifacts’ in<sup>fl</sup>uence, and others were not. We use these differences to identify the treatment effects of being associated with Pokemon Go. Meanwhile, we ´ rely on online reviews from these restaurants to measure changes in consumer behavior. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we use review volume as a proxy for consumer engagement with the restaurant and use average star ratings as a proxy for consumer perception toward the restaurant because previous literature has consistently demonstrated that variables derived from user-generated content tend to well capture economic implications (e.g., Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006, Luca 2016, Khern-am-nuai et al. 2017). In addition, we examine the heterogeneity of the effect of Pokemon Go on consumer engagement and perception´ with respect to restaurant characteristics, such as restaurant affordability, popularity, and location. We obtain observational data by collecting locations of the in-game artifacts from the city of Houston, Texas. For restaurant reviews, we use data from one of the major restaurant review platforms in the United States. This data set also provides information about restaurant characteristics (e.g., average menu price, location), which allows us to examine the heterogeneous treatment effect.

Overall, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that Pokemon Go has a positive im-´ pact on consumer engagement toward treated restaurants. These restaurants receive a signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly higher number of reviews compared with their control counterparts. When we further analyze this increased engagement, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that Pokemon Go effectively attracts local´ consumers although its effect on visiting consumers is minimal. Moreover, Pokemon Go appears to engage´ both new and existing consumers similarly. In the meantime, we also <sup>fi</sup>nd that the average consumer perception of restaurants associated with Pokemon Go improves´ signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly as re<sup>fl</sup>ected by an increase in four- and <sup>fi</sup>vestar reviews and a decrease in one-star reviews. In terms of moderating effects, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that several business characteristics (e.g., business affordability, business popularity, and walkability of the area in which the business is located) play a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant moderating role on the effect on consumer engagement but not on the effect on consumer perception. The empirical results align with our theoretical framework, which draws upon underpinning theories from marketing and psychology, and support our hypothesis that the AR technology used in Pokemon´ Go tends to instill additional hedonic utility for users. Such additional utilities are then projected to associated businesses through several underlying mechanisms as explained by indirect reciprocity theory and a parsimonious analytical model that we develop to situate the potential impacts of AR technology in our empirical study, which suggests that an additional hedonic utility instilled by the AR technology may lead to an increase in consumer engagement and perception, independent of an increase in foot traf<sup>fi</sup>c. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings highlight the business value of this emerging technology, which could encourage business managers and policymakers to not only recognize this technology’s value, but also potentially establish partnerships with such an application.

In the next section, we review the literature related to our study. We then present conceptual and theoretical models that support our research hypotheses development in Section 3. In Section 4, we discuss the data used in this study. In Section 5, we describe our econometric model and then present our results in Section 6. Finally, we summarize the implications of our study in Section 7.

## 2. Related Literature

In this section, we review the prior literature related to our work. Particularly, we survey the literature stream that explores how technologies facilitate user engagement and mobility. In addition, there has recently also been some research that studies Pokemon´ Go in particular. Hence, we review those studies in the second section.

## 2.1. Platforms That Facilitate Engagement and Mobility

As location-based technologies have gained more traction in recent years, so too has the related research. Previous literature has studied the implications of location-based technology in several contexts. For instance, Cho et al. (2011) examine the movement of users in a location-based social network and show the dependency of periodicity and social relationships on human movement. In addition, Frith (2013) uses qualitative interviews to establish that users of locationbased social networks tend to change their mobility patterns and enjoy their surroundings more. Also, Frith (2013) explores the gaming elements of a location-based social network and argues that the playful layers of these gaming elements can affect individual behavior with respect to mobility decisions and users’ experiences of the surrounding components. Other works also con<sup>fi</sup>rm the effect of location-based feature applications on users’ mobility choices (Humphreys 2007, 2010; Licoppe and Inada 2010). Noting this strong effect on user mobility, Hjorth (2011) proposes that location-based features can combine with a mobile game to create a novel way for users to experience different physical places. Gazzard (2011) reinforces this concept by arguing that location-based mobile games can be supplemented with augmented reality; this approach has been implemented in Pokemon Go and has´ become the core element of users’ involvement with and understanding of their surroundings. Concurrently, a limited number of studies explores the application of augmented-reality technologies to other industries. For instance, Kaufmann and Schmalstieg (2003) apply augmented reality to enhance student learning experiences in mathematics and geometry. Beyond these subjects, Antonioli et al. (2014) not only suggest a broader use of augmented reality in education, but also highlight the dif<sup>fi</sup>culties of creating such content. Finally, Han et al. (2013) interview tourists in Dublin and conclude that the use of augmented reality in tourism can generate fruitful results. Except for the papers mentioned in the following section, we are not aware of any prior work that studies the business implications of augmented reality in conjunction with other engagement features.

## 2.2. The Impact of Pokemon Go´

This section presents the literature that speci<sup>fi</sup>cally studies Pokemon Go and its impact on user behavior.´ The signi<sup>fi</sup>cant distinction of Pokemon Go from other´ mobile games, particularly in terms of its in<sup>fl</sup>uence on users, has been recognized in the research community (Nacke et al. 2017). As a result, it forms a convenient natural experiment framework and has become a platform for researchers in several contexts. In medical research, the focus has centered on the impact of Pokemon Go on users´ ’ well-being. For example, Howe et al. (2016) use online survey data to show that Pokemon Go players walk 25% more on average after´ they install the game although such an increase gradually weakens and ultimately disappears after <sup>fi</sup>ve weeks. In addition, Althoff et al. (2016) use enhanced user data from Microsoft Band’s physical activity sensors to show empirically that Pokemon Go signi´ <sup>fi</sup>cantly increases physical activity among users of all ages, genders, weights, statuses, and prior activity levels. The app’s ability to reach low-activity populations is particularly unique because they tend not to respond well to apps speci<sup>fi</sup>cally designed to promote a healthy lifestyle. On the other hand, several studies report potential negative consequences of playing Pokemon Go,´ including Serino et al. (2016), who highlight that the app’s location-based gameplay can drive players into inappropriate areas and increase their risk of abduction or trespassing. In a similar vein, Joseph and Armstrong (2016) argue that the augmented reality feature of the game can cause distraction and increase the risk of being injured while playing the game.

In addition, several studies investigate the design aspect of Pokemon Go and its implications at the macro´ level. Colley et al. (2017) examine the geography of Pokemon Go using´ <sup>fi</sup>eld survey and geostatistical analyses and <sup>fi</sup>nd that Pokemon Go not only ampli´ <sup>fi</sup>es existing geographically linked biases (e.g., the game favors urban areas more), but the game also in<sup>fl</sup>uences human mobility patterns on a large scale. Similarly, Sari (2017) uses location data to show the spatial disparities caused by the placement of in-game Pokemon Go artifacts in a´ French metropolis. On a related note, Adlakha et al. (2017) highlight how important it is for urban planners to design people–space interactions carefully.

Additional research focuses on the adoption of Pokemon Go, the user experience, and the sustainabil- ´ ity of the app. Rauschnabel et al. (2017) use a survey of Pokemon Go players to develop a comprehensive ´ framework that explains the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and risks of adopting the app. They <sup>fi</sup>nd the primary factors that drive players to adopt the game are hedonic, emotional, and social, and physical risks are the primary deterrents. Rasche et al. (2017b) show that the augmented reality feature of the game is critical in retaining active users, that a clear majority of players prefer to play the game alone, and that users tend to play the game continuously. Also, active Pokemon Go´ players have been shown to have better daily functions and psychosocial functions, which motivates them to spend money on induced consumption, including restaurants and retail stores (Zach and Tussyadiah 2017). Finally, Lalot et al. (2017) conclude that the sustainability of the game depends on its ability to maintain the agreeableness, perseverance, and premeditation of the players. Meanwhile, Rasche et al. (2017a) show that users quit the game because of the lack of challenge and game quality. Niantic has focused on these aspects in its subsequent version releases.

In summary, prior research con<sup>fi</sup>rms that Pokemon ´ Go in<sup>fl</sup>uences its users signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly. With our paper, we extend the extant literature in this area by investigating the economic impact of Pokemon Go. To the best ´ of our knowledge, our study is the <sup>fi</sup>rst to offer empirical evidence regarding the impact that Pokemon Go has´ on local businesses associated with the application.

## 3. Research Hypotheses

In this section, we formally develop our research hypotheses, which we eventually verify using data. Gazzard (2011) notes that the AR technology leads to changes in user behavior. The rationale for such changes is multifold as several aspects of AR can potentially impact users differently. In this section, we <sup>fi</sup>rst expand upon relevant theories to describe how AR technology impacts the utility or the value that consumers obtain from businesses that are associated with Pokem´on Go. Then, we connect these theories to a parsimonious analytical model to situate the developed theoretical framework in the empirical context our study.

## 3.1. Underpinning Theories

We primarily draw upon the uses and grati<sup>fi</sup>cation model (Katz 1974), which suggests that consumers interacting with surrounding media generate additional utilities. This theory has been used in marketing (e.g.,

Hilken et al. 2017, Rauschnabel et al. 2018) and in experimental works (e.g., Han et al. 2019, Rauschnabel et al. 2019) to explain how users of AR obtain functional (i.e., utilitarian, instrumental, and practical bene<sup>fi</sup>ts) and hedonic (i.e., aesthetic, experiential, and enjoyment bene<sup>fi</sup>ts) utilities. Consistent with that model, we expect that consumers who play Pokemon Go at a business lo- ´ cation obtain additional utility as well. Given the $\mathsf { a p p } ^ { \prime } \mathsf { s }$ entertainment value, we do not expect the utility to be functional in nature, but exclusively hedonic instead.

With the additional hedonic utility anticipated by the uses and grati<sup>fi</sup>cation model, one can connect the indirect reciprocity theory (Nowak and Sigmund 2005) to explain why users may attribute the added utilities from playing Pokemon Go to the business entities at´ which they played. According to this theory, actors may indirectly project the additional utilities or disutilities from an external entity onto a different entity. For example, Khern-am-nuai et al. (2018) empirically show such indirect reciprocal behavior in the context of product reviews. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, consumers indirectly reciprocate their positive feelings from receiving extrinsic rewards from the store by writing more positive product reviews. In a similar vein, we expect the consumers playing Pokemon Go to project their positive´ utilities instilled by the game onto the business locations they visit.

## 3.2. Analytical Model

The underpinning theories presented in the previous section help explain the potential impacts of AR technology at the aggregated level. Drawing upon the aforementioned theories, we anticipate that consumers of the businesses associated with Pokem´on Go would attain additional hedonic utility from the application. We next augment our theoretical framework by developing a parsimonious analytical model to analyze the implications of AR technology at the individual level. In other words, given that rational consumers visit a representative restaurant, how would their engagement and perception of that restaurant change when the restaurant is associated with Pokem´on Go? We situate our model in the speci<sup>fi</sup>c empirical context of our study, in which we use review-writing behavior as a metric for analysis.

To begin, we consider a focal restaurant where its consumers can play Pokemon Go during their visits.´ We <sup>fi</sup>rst characterize the setup with no AR technology. Let consumer $j$ observe a quality of the restaurant drawn from a normal distribution $\mathsf { \bar { Q } } _ { j } \sim N ( \mu , \sigma ^ { 2 } )$ , where $\mu$ is the true quality of the restaurant and $\sigma ^ { 2 }$ is the variance. Without loss of generality, let the probability density function and cumulative distribution function of the normal distribution be $\phi ( Q _ { j } )$ and $\Phi ( Q _ { j } )$ , respectively. Note that the quality being drawn from a normal distribution is a common assumption in the literature (e.g., Qin and Prybutok 2008, Dalvi et al. 2013).

Prior literature demonstrates that the distribution of users writing reviews is not perfectly aligned with observed quality. In the “brag and moan” model, Hu et al. (2009) argue that users experiencing extreme qualities are the ones who write reviews. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, in their model, there exist two thresholds $\underline { \gamma }$ and $\bar { \gamma }$ such that $- \infty < \gamma < \mu < \bar { \gamma } < \infty$ and also that only consumers with $Q _ { j } < \gamma$ and $Q _ { j } > \bar { \gamma }$ engage with the restaurant by providing reviews; in these cases, the former group of users writes negative reviews, and the latter group writes positive ones. The number of reviewers on the platform can be captured with the following speci<sup>fi</sup>cation:

$$
V = \int_ {- \infty} ^ {\frac {\gamma}{}} \phi (x) d x + \int_ {\bar {\gamma}} ^ {+ \infty} \phi (x) d x,
$$

where $V$ represents the volume of engagement.

Note that prior literature empirically demonstrates that online reviews tend to follow the J-shape distribution in which the number of positive reviews tends to be higher than neutral and negative ones (e.g., Hu et al. 2009, Khern-am-nuai et al. 2018). In addition, our own data set, which we describe in detail in the next section, follows a similar pattern. This condition implies that $\mu - \underline { { \gamma } } > \bar { \gamma } - \mu ,$ which we assume for the rest of our discussion. In addition, the average rating, which captures consumer perception of the restaurant, can also be observed as

$$
R = \frac {1}{\Phi (\underline {{\gamma}}) + 1 - \Phi (\bar {\gamma})} \left(\int_ {- \infty} ^ {\underline {{\gamma}}} \phi (x) x d x + \int_ {\bar {\gamma}} ^ {+ \infty} \phi (x) x d x\right).
$$

Suppose that the additional hedonic utility instilled by AR technology shifts users’ perceived quality by a constant $h > 0 \colon Q _ { j } + h ,$ then the volume and ratings of the restaurant among Pokemon Go players that´ receive additional hedonic utility of h are as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} V ^ {\prime} = \int_ {- \infty} ^ {\underline {{\gamma}}} \phi (x - h) d x + \int_ {\bar {\gamma}} ^ {+ \infty} \phi (x - h) d x, \\ R ^ {\prime} = \frac {1}{\Phi (\underline {{\gamma}} - h) + 1 - \Phi (\bar {\gamma} - h)} \bigg (\int_ {- \infty} ^ {\underline {{\gamma}}} \phi (x - h) (x - h) d x \\ \qquad + \int_ {\bar {\gamma}} ^ {+ \infty} \phi (x - h) (x - h) d x \bigg). \end{array}
$$

We next develop theoretical predictions on the differences between $\hat { V }$ and $V ^ { \prime }$ and between R and R. The proofs for the following lemmas are available in Online Appendix B.

Lemma 1. V is strictly higher than V.

Lemma 1 demonstrates that a business tends to receive more reviews when its consumers receive additional hedonic utility from the AR technology used in

Pokemon Go as this added hedonic utility increases´ consumers’ engagement rate. Note that this increase is observed irrespective of an increase in foot traf<sup>fi</sup>c. In other words, Lemma 1 shows that the associated business tends to attain higher user engagement even without accounting for the change in foot traf<sup>fi</sup>c. However, empirical evidence has shown that Pokemon Go ´ is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly effective in promoting users’ mobility, so the associated business should anticipate an increase in foot traf<sup>fi</sup>c as well (Althoff et al. 2016, Howe et al. 2016). Combining both potential effects, the impact on the engagement is only expected to increase further. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 1. Businesses associated with Pokemon Go at-´ tain a higher level of consumer engagement.

Next, we consider the impact of the added hedonic utility on consumer perception, which we measured by the average ratings.

Lemma 2. R is strictly higher than R.

Lemma 2 demonstrates that, when a restaurant is associated with Pokemon Go, it enjoys an increase in its´ average rating because of the added hedonic utility instilled by the AR technology used in Pokemon Go.´ This <sup>fi</sup>nding is also consistent with prior empirical results in the literature that connect the use of AR technology and improved brand attitude (Rauschnabel et al. 2019) with increased intrinsic satisfaction (Jessen et al. 2020). Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 2. Businesses associated with Pokemon Go at-´ tain a more positive level of consumer perception.

It is important to note that our analytical model analyzes the change in user behavior resulting from the additional hedonic utility instilled by Pokem´on Go conditional on users who visit the restaurants. In other words, our analytical model does not take the increased foot traf<sup>fi</sup>c that Pokem´on Go may generate into account. If Pokem´on Go only increases the foot traf<sup>fi</sup>c but does not generate an additional hedonic utility as discussed, then we should only observe the increase in user engagement (i.e., Hypothesis 1) and not the increase in user perception (i.e., Hypothesis 2). Thus, validating Hypothesis 2 is critical to infer the role of the additional hedonic utility in our study.

Finally, we attempt to investigate how hedonic utility may vary with business characteristics with our <sup>fi</sup>nal hypothesis. Note that restaurant characteristics are generally heterogeneous in nature because food is an experience good. As such, previous research demonstrates that restaurant characteristics play an important moderating role in several contexts, including how guest satisfaction in<sup>fl</sup>uences restaurant performance (Gupta et al. 2007) and server motivation (Dermody et al. 2004). Similarly, prior studies also show that restaurant characteristics tend to play a crucial role in moderating consumers’ restaurant choices and dining behavior (Kimes and Robson 2004). Furthermore, Kim and Moon (2009) demonstrate that restaurant characteristics signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly moderate consumers’ pleasure feelings, which may imply similar effects upon the added hedonic utility. For these reasons, AR technology’s impact, particularly that of instilled hedonic utility, may vary based on business-speci<sup>fi</sup>c characteristics. For example, a hedonic utility generated by playing Pokemon´ Go at a restaurant in a highly walkable area could be different than that generated at a restaurant in a less walkable area. ## 4. Research Context and Data

In this section, we describe the research context of our study along with the data we use in our empirical analyses.

## 4.1. Empirical Setting

We begin by discussing empirical settings of this research. First, we explain the details of Pokemon Go´ ’s in-game artifacts and explain their initial placement, which constitutes a natural experiment for our paper. We then establish the scope of our study subsequently.

An in-game artifact is a virtual artifact in Pokemon´ Go that motivates users to interact with the game. Although the artifact itself is virtual (i.e., it only exists in the game), each one corresponds to a physical location (i.e., the latitude and longitude in the real world as chosen by Pokemon Go). Players interacting with the´ artifact must be present within 40 meters of its realworld location. There are two types of in-game artifacts: PokeStops and gyms. Players who visit a Pok´ e-´ Stop can collect in-game items, such as PokeBalls´ (used to capture Pokemons) or potions (used to heal´ Pokemons from battle wounds), that are essential in´ the game. Because players are limited to collecting these items only once every <sup>fi</sup>ve minutes, they spend a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant amount of time at PokeStops and visit them´ often. Meanwhile, players who visit gyms can use their own Pokemons to´ <sup>fi</sup>ght other Pokemons of the´ gym patrons to obtain experience points and in-game currency. The number of gyms is generally much smaller than the number of PokeStops. Further, since´ December 2016, Niantic, Inc., has <sup>fl</sup>ipped the type of in-game artifacts (i.e., a PokeStop became a gym and´ vice versa) back and forth many times in many locations. For these reasons, we do not differentiate between the artifacts in our analysis.

In Ingress, a different augmented-reality game also developed by Niantic, Inc., players nominate locations for the in-game artifacts (called portals). Niantic considered various factors for those locations (e.g., historical foot traf<sup>fi</sup>c, history or educational value of the location) to decide upon portal locations. The Pokemon´ Go artifacts were simply placed in the same locations as the portals when the game was launched. Nevertheless, it is important to note that, when Pokemon Go´ was released initially, in-game artifacts were only placed at noncommercial locations (e.g., monuments, churches, libraries). As a result, some businesses happened to be located inside the radius of an in-game artifact, and others weren’t. We leverage this feature as a natural experiment for identi<sup>fi</sup>cation in our analysis.

Although several types of local businesses (e.g., shopping malls, restaurants, convenience stores) may be impacted by Pokemon Go, we speci´ <sup>fi</sup>cally chose restaurants for our research context for the following reasons. First, in-game artifacts have a limited effective range, which typically cannot cover large business establishments, such as shopping malls or grocery stores. Moreover, the size of most restaurants is suitable for our analysis as it is typically within the range of a single in-game artifact. Furthermore, although our data set contains several types of businesses, the number of restaurants in our data set far exceeds that of other businesses. Finally, consumers are generally more connected to restaurants than other retail establishments, especially in terms of expressing their opinions on product/service quality, thus allowing us to identify the effect of in-game artifacts on our two dependent variables of interest: consumer engagement and perception. Therefore, we hereafter refer to restaurants instead of businesses.

## 4.2. Data

Our study has two primary data sources. The <sup>fi</sup>rst is the application programming interface (API) of Pokemon Go.´ <sup>2</sup> This API allows us to collect locations (i.e., lists of latitude and longitude) of Pokemon Go´ ’s in-game artifacts reliably. The second data source is one of the most popular online restaurant review platforms in the United States. We collect restaurant information and online reviews from this platform. With regards to geographical area, the scope of our data is at a city level (i.e., our data set consists of restaurant reviews and locations of in-game artifacts from one city). We chose the city of Houston, Texas, as our target city for the following reasons. First, Houston is recognized as one of the most diverse cities in the United States (Gates 2012). Therefore, the results we obtain from our data set are less likely to be culturally or ethnically biased. In addition, as our data are from restaurant reviews, multicultural cities typically offer various type of restaurants, which minimizes restaurant-type bias. Second, Houston is one of the most populous cities in the United States. As the initial placement of in-game artifacts was based on historical foot traf<sup>fi</sup>c, we observe an abundance of ingame artifacts throughout Houston. The availability of these virtual artifacts helps us to identify their effect on restaurants.

We started to collect our data in December 2016 by using an automated script. We began by acquiring a list of the locations of in-game artifacts established within Houston. There were 4,832 in-game artifacts spread across the city. At the same time, we recorded a list of restaurants located in 88 neighborhoods across the city. We then collected the details of each restaurant, including the restaurant identi<sup>fi</sup>- cation, name, average menu price, category, and street address. Further, for each restaurant, we also collected online reviews, including the textual content of the review, issued star ratings, date, and reviewer identi<sup>fi</sup>cation. We provide details of the collected restaurant characteristics and review elements in Online Appendix A. In total, our data set consists of 3,424 restaurants, of which 327 have an in-game artifact nearby. In addition, our data set contains all reviews until December 2016 for each restaurant. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we consider reviews between March 2016 and October 2016 (i.e., four months before and after Pokemon Go´ ’s release on July 2016). During this period, there are a total of 67,348 reviews written by 17,417 unique reviewers observed across restaurants.

## 5. Methodology

Using the data described in the previous section, we construct an empirical model. Because our primary objective is to establish the causal inference of an exogenous event (i.e., the introduction of Pokemon Go),´ we face the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation issue of endogeneity. For example, restaurants that are associated with Pokemon´ Go are probably, on average, more active than restaurants that are not. Therefore, we design our research framework as a quasi-experiment by combining propensity score matching (PSM) and difference-indifferences techniques to account for potential endogeneity concerns. These techniques are widely used in the literature for this purpose (e.g., Xu et al. 2016, Bapna et al. 2018, Yu et al. 2021). With this framework, our analysis is analogous to a two-group experiment, in which the treated group is restaurants that are associated with Pokemon Go and the control group is res-´ taurants that are similar to those in the treatment group but that are not associated with the game. We, hereafter, use the terminology of experiments to simplify our presentation, whereas the data are, in fact, observational. By comparing the difference between these two groups and the difference in pretreatment and posttreatment, we are able to identify causally the impact of Pokemon Go on associated restaurants.´

## 5.1. Propensity Score Matching

Our <sup>fi</sup>rst task is to utilize PSM to create a data set that mimics a randomized experiment research design. Particularly, we use PSM to create a pair of similar restaurants, in which one is associated with Pokemon´ Go (“treated”) and the other is not (“control”). Using this PSM technique, we compute the probability of each observation receiving the treatment, conditional on the observable characteristics. Because our intention is to identify a pair of restaurants that are similar in terms of observable characteristics before the release of Pokemon Go and located near one another,´ our matching variables in the PSM process are the total number of reviews, average star ratings, review rate (average number of reviews in a day), price range, and location. We execute this matching process by using all the data before the release of Pokemon ´ Go. After we discard observations that are lying outside of the common support, we obtain 654 restaurants in our matched sample with 327 in each group. To verify that our matching attempt is successful, we then use paired t-tests to verify the differences of matching variables between each group.

Results in Table 2 show that there is no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference between the treated group and the control group in terms of matching variables. We also verify the location of the restaurant pair (i.e., the one in the treatment and the one in the control group) and <sup>fi</sup>nd that restaurants in every pair are in the same neighborhood.

## 5.2. Difference-in-Differences

With the matched data set, we construct a panel data set such that each observation corresponds to a restaurant. Meanwhile, the unit of time is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as semimonthly. Hence, we have a total of 16 time periods, namely eight periods before and eight after the introduction of Pokemon Go. The primary dependent varia-´ bles of our model are the number of reviews each restaurant receives in each period (which we use as a proxy to measure consumer engagement with a restaurant) and the average star rating of the reviews that each restaurant receives in each period (which we use as a proxy to measure consumer perception of a restaurant).

We also verify that the parallel-trends assumption is satis<sup>fi</sup>ed for all of our dependent variables (see details in Online Appendix C.1). Based on this data set, we employ the difference-in-difference regression to analyze the impact of Pokemon Go on these restaurants.´

The difference-in-difference regression speci<sup>fi</sup>cation is as follows:

$$
D V _ {i t} = \gamma (T r e a t m e n t _ {i} \times A f t e r _ {t}) + \beta X _ {i t} + \alpha_ {i} + \delta_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t}.\tag{1}
$$

In Equation (1), subscript i denotes the restaurant and t denotes the time period $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , t = 1$ for the <sup>fi</sup>rst time period, $t = 2$ for the second time period, and so on). $D V _ { i t }$ represents the dependent variable of interest. Meanwhile, $\alpha _ { i }$ captures restaurant <sup>fi</sup>xed effects, which represent the restaurant’s speci<sup>fi</sup>c characteristics that impact our dependent variables and vary across a pair of matched restaurants but remain the same across time. For example, a matched pair of restaurants located downtown could be signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different from the ones located in the suburbs. $\delta _ { t }$ captures time <sup>fi</sup>xed effects, which represent time-speci<sup>fi</sup>c characteristics that affect our dependent variables. Treatment is an indicator variable that represents whether the restaurant is treated. $A f t e r _ { t }$ is an indicator to represent the posttreatment periods $( t > 8 )$ . Finally, $X _ { i t }$ is a vector that represents control variables that vary in both time and location. We collect crime data, which is the number of crimes that occurred within 100 meters of each restaurant in each time period, from SpotCrime API.

This source provides details about the crime type as well as its date, time, and geolocation. In addition, we collect the average temperature for each restaurant location in each period from Wunderground.com, which is a commercial weather service that provides real-time weather information.

Aside from the effect that Pokemon Go may have on´ associated businesses, we are also interested in the heterogeneous treatment effect with respect to characteristics of the businesses. Particularly, we examine how the affordability, popularity, and location of a restaurant moderates the effect that Pokemon Go has on consumer´ engagement and consumer perception. We calculate the average menu price of each restaurant as a proxy for restaurant affordability. Also, we use the total number of reviews for each restaurant before the study period as a proxy for restaurant popularity. Finally, we calculate the total number of restaurants and the total number of in-game artifacts in a given $\mathrm { Z I P }$ code and use them as proxies for the crowdedness of restaurant location. In addition, we also obtain the walkability score for each neighborhood as another characteristic of restaurant location as well. We include these variables, denoted by Moderator, as an interaction term that moderates the effect of Treatment $\times A f t e r _ { t }$ in our speci<sup>fi</sup>cation. Formally, our second model speci<sup>fi</sup>cation is

$$
\begin{array}{r l} D V _ {i t} = & \gamma (T r e a t m e n t _ {i} \times A f t e r _ {t}) + \pmb {\beta} \pmb {X} _ {i t} + \theta M o d e r a t o r _ {i} \\ & + \eta (T r e a t m e n t _ {i} \times A f t e r _ {t} \times M o d e r a t o r _ {i}) \\ & + \alpha_ {i} + \delta_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t}. \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

6.1.1. Business Affordability. The <sup>fi</sup>rst business characteristic that we examine is business affordability. Previous literature demonstrates that this variable plays an important moderating role on multiple outcome variables, including restaurant performance (Mathe-Soulek et al. 2016), customer satisfaction (Han and Ryu 2009), and customer behavioral intention (Kwun and Oh 2004). In our study, we use the average menu price indicated by the review platform as a proxy for business affordability.

Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we include the average menu price of each restaurant as a moderator in our difference-in-differences regression framework of Equation (2). Note that the average menu price is not available for about 1.5% of our restaurants in our data set. Hence, we drop these restaurants from our analysis. As we report in Table 5, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that after adding AverageMenuPrice as an interaction term, the main effect of PokemonGoInfluence remains qualitatively the same. Particularly, the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of PokemonGoInfluence continues to be statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant for both the number of reviews and average star ratings. Meanwhile, the interaction term itself is negative and statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant for the number of reviews but not statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant for average star ratings. These results suggest that the effect of Pokemon Go on consumer engagement´ is particularly strong for inexpensive restaurants but fades away for restaurants with an average menu price of \$45. At the same time, the effect of Pokemon Go on con-´ sumer engagement appears to be homogeneous among restaurants with different average menu prices. To that extent, we <sup>fi</sup>nd a partial support for Hypothesis 3 along this dimension.

## 7. Discussion and Conclusion

Pokemon Go, an augmented reality´ –based mobile game, has been very popular among users. Owing to its popularity, major businesses, such as McDonald’s in Japan and Starbucks in the United States, have partnered with the game developer to associate their businesses with Pokemon Go through an in-game mech-´ anism. Such a partnership program has been recently extended to small and medium-size businesses as well. However, there is limited evidence in the literature that allows us to foresee potential impacts of Pokemon Go on the associated businesses. Noting this´ gap in the literature, we have used this paper to study the economic implications of Pokemon Go.´

We developed our research hypotheses by drawing upon prior literature in marketing and psychology. To hypothesize the potential impacts of AR technology on consumer perception and engagement, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed underpinning theories that are grounded in the context of AR technology. These theories include the uses and grati<sup>fi</sup>cation model and the indirect reciprocity theory. We also supplement our theoretical framework by developing a parsimonious analytical model that situates the potential impacts of the AR technology within the speci<sup>fi</sup>c empirical context of our study. Our theoretical model suggests that, in addition to the increase in foot traf<sup>fi</sup>c induced by Pokemon Go, the associated busi-´ ness also enjoys the increase in consumer engagement rate and in consumer perception as well. Combining underpinning theories and theoretical prediction from our analytical model, we develop research hypotheses. Then, to formally test these hypotheses, we collected a data set that consists of restaurant reviews and the geo-location of Pokemon Go´ ’s in-game artifacts. We then used propensity score matching and difference-indifferences regression analyses to empirically investigate how the introduction of Pokemon Go affects associated´ restaurants. Here, we use restaurant reviews as a proxy to measure consumer economic behavior. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we use the number of reviews to measure consumer engagement and use average star ratings to measure consumer perception of the restaurants.

Our empirical results support our <sup>fi</sup>rst two research hypotheses. Restaurants associated with Pokemon Go in-´ deed observed an increase in consumer engagement. In addition, they also experienced improved consumer perception after Pokemon Go´ ’s release. Furthermore, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the effect of Pokemon Go on consumer engage-´ ment is more salient for local users, and the effect is homogenous to both new and existing reviewers. As for the increase in consumer perception, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that such an increase is driven by the change in review distribution as the number of four- and <sup>fi</sup>ve-star reviews increased and the number of one-star reviews decreased, which is in line with the theoretical prediction from our analytical model. Finally, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the impact of Pokemon Go´ on consumer engagement is moderated by restaurant characteristics, including affordability, popularity, and the walkability of the area in which a restaurant is located. However, we do not <sup>fi</sup>nd evidence that the impact on consumer perception is heterogenous with respect to these restaurant characteristics.

Our study signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly contributes to both research and practice. From a theoretical perspective, our paper is among the <sup>fi</sup>rst to empirically demonstrate the economic implications of augmented-reality technology. We have also used observational data to connect with theoretical foundations and verify existing theories that rely upon laboratory experiments and/or surveys to suggest potential impacts of AR technology on user behavior. In addition, our results also yield signi<sup>fi</sup>- cant managerial implications. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, this study helps business managers develop appropriate policies for governing any partnership between their respective businesses and Pokemon Go (or augmented-´ reality technologies in general) by providing empirical insights into the economic implications of such a partnership. These insights are crucial because Pokemon´ Go is now adding more businesses to its partnership agreement and extending the program to local businesses. Because such a partnership can be costly and the impact on a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s intrinsic value (e.g., consumer perception of the business) can affect business performance in the long run, business managers must understand potential economic implications beforehand. For example, based on the <sup>fi</sup>ndings reported in Luca (2016), the 0.273-star increases observed in this study lead to a 2.46% increase in revenue for an average treated restaurant. In addition, by extrapolating the relationship between review elements and sales in Lu et al. (2013), the 5.7% increase in review volume observed in our study leads to a 1.94% increase in revenue for an average treated restaurant. As a result, the bene<sup>fi</sup>t that an average treated restaurant attains by associating with Pokemon Go is approximately a 4.4%´ increase in its revenue. It is also worth noting that the economic impacts of Pokemon Go uncovered in this´ study offer long-term implications as well because online reviews, which are the outcome variables of our study, are usually available for consumers to read inde<sup>fi</sup>nitely.

Our research is not without limitations, which also represent avenues for future research. As we analyze the data from only one speci<sup>fi</sup>c major city in North America, there could be some cultural differences that explain how consumers react to the Pokemon Go. Also, we only´ analyze the data up to four months after the release date of Pokemon Go; another interesting research avenue ´ would be to investigate the longer-term effects of Pokemon Go. However, it is important to note that´ Pokemon Go introduced multiple changes to the game ´ after December 2016 (e.g., dynamic addition and removal of many in-game artifacts, the introduction of sponsored partnership programs, changes in the gym system). Therefore, a research methodology must be carefully chosen to control for the effect of these policy changes. In addition, in-game artifacts of Pokemon Go in´ our study are homogenous in terms of their value. Hence, future research could examine potential impacts of different types of in-game artifacts that may generate different value to consumers who were introduced to the game after the time period we used in our study. Finally, another future research avenue would be to study directly the impact of Pokemon Go on business revenue.´
