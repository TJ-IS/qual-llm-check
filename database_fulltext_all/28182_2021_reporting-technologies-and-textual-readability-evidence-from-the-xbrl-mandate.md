# Reporting Technologies and Textual Readability: Evidence from the XBRL Mandate

Xitong Li,<sup>a</sup> Hongwei Zhu,<sup>b</sup> Luo Zuo<sup>c</sup>

Abstract. Financial reporting technologies can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affect how <sup>fi</sup>rms construct and disseminate quantitative and qualitative disclosures. Leveraging the opportunity created by the eXtensible Business Reporting Language (XBRL) mandate in the United States, we use a difference-in-differences approach to examine whether and how a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s XBRL adoption affects the readability of its textual disclosures. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that the initial adopters’ HTML-formatted annual reports become harder to read after the XBRL mandate. Further analysis reveals that this effect is concentrated among adopters with more quantitative disclosures, those with smaller <sup>fi</sup>rm size, and those with a higher level of <sup>fi</sup>nancial complexity. Importantly, we show that managers’ reduced attention to preparing HTML-formatted annual reports, rather than increased disclosures, is likely the explanation for this decrease in textual readability. We also <sup>fi</sup>nd that the negative effect on textual readability persists at least in the subsequent year. Taken together, our results suggest that although XBRL can standardize numerical disclosures, its initial adoption can divert managerial attention and result in reduced readability of textual disclosures.

## 1. Introduction

Recognizing “the vital role of the Internet and electronic communications in modernizing the disclosure system under the federal securities laws,”<sup>1</sup> the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) has long required <sup>fi</sup>lers to make their <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports publicly available on the Electronic Data Gathering, Analysis, and Retrieval (EDGAR) system in HTML format. These reports are an important source of public <sup>fi</sup>rms’ <sup>fi</sup>nancial information (Li 2008, Miller 2010, Allee and Deangelis 2015). Accordingly, the SEC has urged public <sup>fi</sup>rms to produce <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports that are more readable. For example, the SEC issued a regulation in October 1998, Rule 421(d), that required public <sup>fi</sup>rms to use plain English in all <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports (U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission 1998). The Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB) has also called for improvements to <sup>fi</sup>nancial disclosures by “clearly communicating the information that is most important to users of each entity’s <sup>fi</sup>nancial statements.”<sup>2</sup>

Although <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports in the HTML format are meant for humans to read manually, they are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for computers to process and compare. To overcome this limitation, the SEC adopted the eXtensible Business Reporting Language (XBRL), an XML-based open standard that uses XML syntax and related XML technologies (e.g., XML schema and XLink) to de<sup>fi</sup>ne and exchange <sup>fi</sup>nancial information digitally. In April 2009, the SEC mandated that public <sup>fi</sup>rms must submit <sup>fi</sup>nancial statements in the XBRL format as part of their <sup>fi</sup>- nancial reports (e.g., 10-Ks), with the initial adopters starting in <sup>fi</sup>scal 2009. Since then, XBRL has been widely adopted worldwide. As of October 2019, it has been used in more than 50 countries, covering more than two-thirds of the global capital markets.<sup>3</sup> Even some governments have adopted XBRL. For example, the governor of Florida signed a bill in 2018 that puts the state on a path to requiring its local governments to submit their <sup>fi</sup>nancial information to the state in the XBRL format by September 2020, and the governor of California has been considering a similar bill. 4

According to the SEC’s XBRL mandate, <sup>fi</sup>rms must tag each numerical value in the <sup>fi</sup>nancial statements, using either standard tags de<sup>fi</sup>ned in the U.S. Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP) XBRL taxonomy (with more than 15,000 unique tags) or customized tags. The XBRL mandate is expected to enhance the use of <sup>fi</sup>nancial disclosures and facilitate data consistency and comparability (Bovee et al. 2002).

With the XBRL mandate, <sup>fi</sup>rms must submit the <sup>fi</sup>- nancial statement portion of their reports in the XBRL format in addition to the conventional HTML-formatted <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports. Reports in both formats must provide the same <sup>fi</sup>nancial information (SEC Final Rule, Release No. 33-9002). Both formats are publicly available via the SEC’s EDGAR system. The existence of the XBRL-formatted <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports does not imply that the HTML-formatted <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports become less important. When qualitative <sup>fi</sup>nancial information is needed, the HTML-formatted <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports are much easier for humans to read. Even when quantitative <sup>fi</sup>nancial information is needed, the HTML-formatted <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports are also useful, because the quality of the XBRL-tagged data is a serious and unresolved concern (Boritz and No 2008, Debreceny et al. 2010, Du et al. 2013). Therefore, the HTMLformatted <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports continue to be a major source of public <sup>fi</sup>rms’ <sup>fi</sup>nancial information (Guay et al. 2016). For example, Amit Varshney, a leading equity research analyst at Credit-Suisse, commented: “I thought the XBRL documents were created to promote the mass consumption of <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting data, but that’s not the case because of the inconsistent tagging. If I have to collect the data for handful of companies, I still <sup>fi</sup>nd it easier to hand collect it from the HTML document” (Harris and Mors<sup>fi</sup>eld 2012).

Recognizing the importance of the HTML-formatted <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports, a number of studies have examined the readability of these disclosures and found that it can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affect market participants (Subramanian et al. 1993, Li 2008, Miller 2010, Lehavy et al. 2011, Lawrence 2013). For example, Lehavy et al. (2011) <sup>fi</sup>nd that less readable annual reports lead to lower analyst forecast accuracy, and Lawrence (2013) shows that individual investors’ investment returns are especially low for <sup>fi</sup>rms with hard-to-read annual reports. Thus, any regulation that affects disclosure readability can have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant economic consequences on the <sup>fi</sup>nancial markets.

Does the XBRL mandate improve or impair the readability of textual disclosures? On the one hand, <sup>fi</sup>rms that have adopted XBRL may leverage the mandate as an opportunity to optimize the data production and <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting process, which can help improve the quality of their reports, including textual readability (Ballou et al. 1998). The production of the XBRL-formatted reports also gives <sup>fi</sup>rms an opportunity to scrutinize and proofread their <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports, including the HTML-formatted reports. Therefore, it is plausible that XBRL adoption helps <sup>fi</sup>rms produce more readable HTML-formatted reports.

On the other hand, the opposite can occur, because managers have limited attention (Simon 1973), and the burden created by XBRL adoption can lead to reduced attention to preparing the conventional HTML-formatted <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports. As Nobel Laureate Herbert A. Simon (1973, p. 270) has noted: “The scarce resource is not information; it is processing capacity to attend to information.” A survey conducted by the Financial Executives Research Foundation (FERF) in 2011 reported that, for large accelerated <sup>fi</sup>lers, more than 40% of the respondents indicated that XBRL was the biggest bottleneck in <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting (Sinnett 2011). Given the nontrivial effort required for XBRL adoption, <sup>fi</sup>rms may have to reduce their attention to their HTML-formatted reports when preparing XBRL-formatted reports.<sup>5</sup> Moreover, the XBRL mandate requires <sup>fi</sup>rms to tag all numerical values in <sup>fi</sup>nancial statements. Accordingly, <sup>fi</sup>rms may add explanatory information to those tagged numerical values, regardless of their relevance. With more explanatory information, relevant and newsworthy disclosures may be buried within an abundance of minutia (Bloom<sup>fi</sup>eld 2012), and the readability of the HTML-formatted <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports may degrade. Therefore, whether XBRL adoption improves or impairs the readability of the HTML-formatted <sup>fi</sup>- nancial reports is an empirical question.

The phase-in approach of the SEC’s XBRL mandate provides an opportunity of a difference-in-differences design to answer this question. With the phase-in, the so-called Tier 1 <sup>fi</sup>rms had to adopt XBRL in 2009, whereas the smaller Tier 2 <sup>fi</sup>rms had to adopt XBRL in 2010. Whether a <sup>fi</sup>rm belonged to Tier 1 or Tier 2 depended on its public <sup>fl</sup>oat but not necessarily on its disclosure readability. Leveraging the XBRL mandate, we can identify the effect of XBRL adoption on the readability of HTML-formatted <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports. In this study, we use two modi<sup>fi</sup>ed Fog indices to enhance the measurement of textual readability. Our results show that the XBRL mandate has made the initial adopters HTML-formatted <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports harder to read. Further analysis reveals that this effect is concentrated among adopters with more quantitative disclosures, those with smaller <sup>fi</sup>rm size, and those with a higher level of <sup>fi</sup>nancial complexity. Notably, the results reveal that the negative effect of XBRL adoption on textual readability is likely to result from reduced attention (as measured by the number of grammatical violations in the <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports), instead of increased disclosures. We also <sup>fi</sup>nd that the effect persists at least into the subsequent year, and its magnitude in the subsequent year is greater than in the <sup>fi</sup>rst year.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that the SEC’s XBRL mandate has degraded the readability of the initial adopters’ <sup>fi</sup>- nancial reports at least in the <sup>fi</sup>rst two years after the mandate. Overall, our results suggest that although XBRL can standardize numerical disclosures, its initial adoption can divert managerial attention and result in reduced readability of textual disclosures. Because less readable <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports reduce price ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and increase information asymmetry between <sup>fi</sup>rms and investors (Lehavy et al. 2011, Loughran and McDonald 2014a), our <sup>fi</sup>ndings imply that the SEC’s XBRL mandate may initially harm the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of the information environment, which can affect <sup>fi</sup>rms, investors, analysts, and regulators.

## 2. Related Literature and Hypothesis Development

## 2.1. Related Literature

2.1.1. Disclosure Readability. The readability of annual reports has profound implications for market participants. Subramanian et al. (1993) report that highly profitable <sup>fi</sup>rms tend to <sup>fi</sup>le more readable annual reports than less pro<sup>fi</sup>table ones. Li (2008) <sup>fi</sup>nds that <sup>fi</sup>rms with more readable annual reports have more persistent positive earnings, and he further shows that the effect of textual readability on earnings persistence is as large as the effect of accruals. Guay et al. (2016) document evidence that <sup>fi</sup>rms with less readable <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports increase voluntary disclosures (e.g., management forecasts or press releases).

Miller (2010) examines the relation between the readability of annual reports and investors’ trading and <sup>fi</sup>nds that longer, less readable <sup>fi</sup>lings are associated with lower overall trading. Lehavy et al. (2011) show that <sup>fi</sup>rms with less readable annual reports have greater analyst following. They also <sup>fi</sup>nd that less readable annual reports lead to increased analysts’ forecast dispersion, lower forecast accuracy, and greater uncertainty in analysts’ earnings forecasts. Lawrence (2013) shows that individual investors are more likely to invest in <sup>fi</sup>rms with more readable <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports and to earn higher returns in these <sup>fi</sup>rms. Therefore, disclosure readability can have profound implications for various market participants, and it is important to understand its economic determinants.

2.1.2. XBRL Adoption. XBRL has drawn much attention from researchers in information systems and accounting (Debreceny et al. 2010, Liu 2013, Blankespoor et al. 2014, Hoitash and Hoitash 2018). Liu (2013) and Perdana et al. (2015) present excellent literature reviews on XBRL. Most studies focus on the association between XBRL adoption and capital market outcomes. For example, Kim et al. (2012) investigate the effect of the XBRL mandate on several aspects of the <sup>fi</sup>nancial information environment and <sup>fi</sup>nd an increase in information ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, a decrease in event return volatility, and a reduction of change in stock return volatility.

Liu et al. (2014) show a positive association between XBRL adoption and analyst following as well as forecast accuracy. Li and Nwaeze (2015) study the association between the use of <sup>fi</sup>rm-speci<sup>fi</sup>c customized tags in XBRL <sup>fi</sup>lings and a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s <sup>fi</sup>nancial information environment.

Only a few studies aim to identify plausibly causal effects of XBRL adoption. Using a differencein-differences design, Blankespoor et al. (2014) examine the market impact of XBRL adoption and <sup>fi</sup>nd that the initial adopters have higher abnormal bid-ask spreads after the XBRL mandate. Their <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest decreased information ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and increased information asymmetry, in contrast to the results of Kim et al. (2012). Blankespoor (2019) <sup>fi</sup>nds that <sup>fi</sup>rms increase their quantitative footnote disclosures after the implementation of detailed XBRL tagging. Using the same difference-in-differences design, our research complements these studies and focuses on a more primitive aspect of the impact of XBRL adoption: the readability of HTML-formatted <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports.

2.1.3. Quality of XBRL Data. Since the SEC’s XBRL mandate, there has been interest in assessing the quality of XBRL data. Boritz and No (2008) <sup>fi</sup>nd that twothirds of the XBRL-tagged <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports in the SEC’s Voluntary Filing Program have inconsistencies and errors. Debreceny et al. (2010) examine the correctness of the mathematical relations implied by the taxonomy and implemented in the instance documents. They <sup>fi</sup>nd that one quarter of the XBRL-tagged reports by the initial 400 large public <sup>fi</sup>rms in the <sup>fi</sup>rstround submissions have errors. Du et al. (2013) <sup>fi</sup>nd that the number of errors per <sup>fi</sup>ling decreases as <sup>fi</sup>rms <sup>fi</sup>le more, suggesting that <sup>fi</sup>lers learn from experience and then improve. The use of a common taxonomy promotes comparability and semantic interoperability of XBRL <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports. However, on average, only 30%–40% of XBRL data between any pair of <sup>fi</sup>rms in the same industry are directly comparable (Zhu and Wu 2011a, b; Zhu and Wu 2014).

Although studies have focused on examining the quality of the XBRL-tagged <sup>fi</sup>nancial data, little research explores whether XBRL adoption affects the quality of HTML-formatted <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports. Given the fact that XBRL-tagged data exhibit various quality issues (Boritz and No 2008, Debreceny et al. 2010, Du et al. 2013) and HTML-formatted <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports continue to be widely used in practice, any possible impact of the XBRL mandate on the quality of HTMLformatted <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports has important implications for the <sup>fi</sup>nancial markets.

## 2.2. Hypothesis Development

The XBRL mandate could allow <sup>fi</sup>rms to improve the readability of their <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports. Although the mandate concerns the production of <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports in the XBRL format, it is part of the SEC’s longstanding effort to enhance the usability of <sup>fi</sup>nancial disclosures. As a result, <sup>fi</sup>rms may respond in several ways. First, the SEC requires consistency between the HTML-formatted and XBRL-tagged reports (Liu 2013, Perdana et al. 2015). This requirement provides <sup>fi</sup>rms with an additional opportunity to scrutinize their reports and bring them into compliance with the SEC’s “plain English” regulation (U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission 1998). Second, <sup>fi</sup>rms may invest in new technologies to support the production of their <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports in both formats. For example, many <sup>fi</sup>rms use supporting information technology (IT) applications, including XBRL instance document builder and XBRL taxonomy builder (Wu and Vasarhelyi 2004), which facilitate the preparation of <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports. Finally, annual reports, as an important information product that <sup>fi</sup>rms release regularly, are expected to be of high quality. It has been long understood that the best practice for information management is to treat information as a product (as opposed to a byproduct), which is manufactured according to a wellde<sup>fi</sup>ned process with quality checks to ensure its quality (Ballou et al. 1998, Wang 1998). Therefore, <sup>fi</sup>rms may take the XBRL mandate as an opportunity to optimize the production of their <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports, including the readability of the HTML-formatted reports.

However, there are two plausible ways that the XBRL mandate may degrade textual readability: reduced attention and increased disclosures. It is well established in the literature that managers have limited attention (Simon 1973). There are three reasons why this might be relevant to the quality of a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s <sup>fi</sup>nancial <sup>fi</sup>lings. First, XBRL is mainly used to tag numerical values (Liu 2013, Perdana et al. 2015), and thus <sup>fi</sup>rms may pay more attention to disclosing quantitative information (Blankespoor 2019) and less to preparing qualitative information. Because textual readability largely depends on the writing of qualitative information (Li 2008, Bonsall et al. 2017), less attention to preparing qualitative information would result in a lower readability. Second, the cost of adopting XBRL is nontrivial. Besides the learning involved in adopting the large U.S. GAAP XBRL taxonomy (with more than 15,000 unique tags), <sup>fi</sup>rms must adapt to new versions of the taxonomy and often extend it by creating their own customized tags when the prede<sup>fi</sup>ned XBRL tags do not apply to their speci<sup>fi</sup>c business context (Li and Nwaeze 2015, Hoitash and Hoitash 2018). With limited cognitive capacity for <sup>fi</sup>ling preparation, managers may have to reduce their attention in preparing HTML-formatted <sup>fi</sup>nancial <sup>fi</sup>lings because of the capacity needed to prepare XBRL-tagged <sup>fi</sup>nancial data. Finally, <sup>fi</sup>rms may have not yet optimized their <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting process. Without a systematic approach, they may have to divide their attention between producing the two forms of <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports separately, which may detract from efforts to maintain and improve the overall quality.

Increased disclosures can also present problems when XBRL is introduced. Firms may increase their disclosures as they adopt XBRL and consequently produce <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports with lower readability. The XBRL mandate requires <sup>fi</sup>rms to tag all numerical values in the <sup>fi</sup>nancial statements, regardless of relevance. Accordingly, <sup>fi</sup>rms may add explanatory information about the tagged numerical values, leading to excessive disclosures. Relevant and newsworthy disclosures may then be buried within an abundance of minutia (Bloom<sup>fi</sup>eld 2012). Another problem is that, with XBRL’s ability to facilitate data exchange and aggregation (Liu 2013, Perdana et al. 2015), market participants’ costs of processing <sup>fi</sup>nancial information in the XBRL-formatted reports may signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly decrease. That, too, could increase the volume of disclosures. The literature on disclosure choice theory (Stocken and Verrecchia 2004, Xu and Zhang 2013) suggests that <sup>fi</sup>rms often respond strategically by increasing the amount of disclosure in their <sup>fi</sup>nancial <sup>fi</sup>lings, because of the considerable bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of disclosure (e.g., increased liquidity and reduced cost of capital) and the costs of nondisclosure (e.g., regulatory <sup>fi</sup>nes). These increased disclosures may result in longer and more complex <sup>fi</sup>nancial <sup>fi</sup>lings, leading to reduced readability.

In sum, these discussions suggest that XBRL adoption can have a positive or negative impact on the readability of <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports. The sign and size of the net effect depend on which mechanisms dominate and are ultimately empirical questions.

## 3. Research Design and Data 3.1. Research Setting

To implement the XBRL mandate, the SEC divided <sup>fi</sup>rms into three tiers, according to their public <sup>fl</sup>oat (market value of publicly tradable shares) in the <sup>fi</sup>scal year of 2009 (between June 15, 2009, and June 14, 2010). Tier 1 <sup>fi</sup>rms included the large accelerated filers with a public <sup>fl</sup>oat of more than \$5 billion; they were required to begin XBRL <sup>fi</sup>lings in <sup>fi</sup>scal 2009. Tier 2 <sup>fi</sup>rms included the remaining large accelerated filers, that is, those with a public <sup>fl</sup>oat between \$700 million and \$5 billion; they were required to begin XBRL <sup>fi</sup>lings in <sup>fi</sup>scal 2010. Tier 3 <sup>fi</sup>rms were the remaining smaller <sup>fi</sup>lers and were required to begin XBRL <sup>fi</sup>lings in <sup>fi</sup>scal 2011. During the <sup>fi</sup>rst year of XBRL adoption, <sup>fi</sup>rms had to tag the numerical values in the four major <sup>fi</sup>nancial statements (e.g., Item 8 of Form 10-K) and tag each note as a text block.<sup>6</sup> After the initial <sup>fi</sup>rstyear adoption, a practice known as detailed tagging had to be implemented from the subsequent year onward. With detailed tagging, adopters must tag all numerical values individually, including those in footnotes to the <sup>fi</sup>nancial statements.

## 3.2. Research Design

3.2.1. Identification. Following prior research (Blankespoor et al. 2014, Blankespoor 2019), we focus on the <sup>fi</sup>rst adoption group (Tier 1 <sup>fi</sup>rms) and the <sup>fi</sup>rst-year effect of the mandatory XBRL adoption for several reasons. First, focusing on the treatment effect of Tier 1 <sup>fi</sup>rms’ <sup>fi</sup>rst-year adoption avoids potential confounds in later years, such as learning. Second, both Tier 1 and Tier 2 <sup>fi</sup>rms are large accelerated <sup>fi</sup>lers, but Tier 3 <sup>fi</sup>rms are much smaller and thus less comparable to Tier 1 and Tier 2 <sup>fi</sup>rms. Blankespoor (2019) provides a more detailed discussion of this rationale. As the literature on readability centers on the analysis of annual reports (Li 2008; Miller 2010; Loughran and McDonald 2014a, b; Bonsall et al. 2017; Kim et al. 2019), this study aims to estimate the impacts of Tier 1 <sup>fi</sup>rms’ <sup>fi</sup>rst-year XBRL adoption on the readability of annual reports.

Therefore, we use a difference-in-differences design to leverage the timing differences of Tier 1 and Tier 2 <sup>fi</sup>rms’ XBRL adoption: Tier 1 <sup>fi</sup>rms adopted XBRL in <sup>fi</sup>scal 2009, whereas Tier 2 <sup>fi</sup>rms did not adopt until <sup>fi</sup>scal 2010. In this setting, Tier 1 <sup>fi</sup>rms are considered as the treatment group and Tier 2 <sup>fi</sup>rms as the control group. This design allows for identifying the effect of XBRL adoption by estimating the difference in the changes of disclosure readability between Tier 1 and Tier 2 <sup>fi</sup>rms before and after the mandate (Bertrand et al. 2004, Angrist and Pischke 2008). Note that, although Tier 2 <sup>fi</sup>rms are smaller than Tier 1 <sup>fi</sup>rms, our design can still identify the effect of XBRL adoption as long as the disclosure readability of <sup>fi</sup>rms in both tiers follows parallel trends in the absence of the XBRL mandate (Bertrand et al. 2004, Angrist and Pischke 2008). Section 4.1 presents supporting evidence for the assumption.

According to the difference-in-differences design, we use Equation (1) as a preliminary model to estimate the effect of Tier 1 <sup>fi</sup>rms’ mandatory XBRL adoption on the readability of their <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports, as follows:

$$
D V _ {\mathrm{i,t}} = \beta P o s t _ {\mathrm{t}} \times T i e r 1 _ {\mathrm{i}} + \theta \mathbf {X} _ {\mathrm{i,t}} + \eta_ {\mathrm{i}} + v _ {\mathrm{t}} + \varepsilon_ {\mathrm{i,t}},\tag{1}
$$

where t indexes a year and i indexes a <sup>fi</sup>rm; $D V _ { \mathrm { i , t } }$ is a measure of textual readability of <sup>fi</sup>rm i’s <sup>fi</sup>nancial report in year $t ,$ as we will discuss in Section 3.3; Tier $\cdot \boldsymbol { { 1 } } _ { \mathrm { { i } } }$ equals one if <sup>fi</sup>rm i is a Tier 1 <sup>fi</sup>rm in the treatment group and zero if it is a Tier 2 <sup>fi</sup>rm in the control group; Post equals one for <sup>fi</sup>scal 2009 and zero for the years before that; $\eta _ { \mathrm { i } }$ is the set of <sup>fi</sup>xed-effect dummy variables for all <sup>fi</sup>rms, controlling for any time-invariant heterogeneity of <sup>fi</sup>rm characteristics (e.g., a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s industry, location, and organizational and IT governance that can be assumed time invariant during the sample period); and $v _ { \mathrm { t } }$ is the set of <sup>fi</sup>xed-effect dummy variables for the <sup>fi</sup>scal years, controlling for any common shocks over the years. We include a set of timevarying <sup>fi</sup>rm characteristics as control variables, ${ \bf { X } } _ { \mathrm { { i , t } } }$ such as <sup>fi</sup>rm size, the market-to-book ratio, and other variables (Section 3.4 describes these control variables). We use contemporaneous controls, because their values are realized before <sup>fi</sup>rms produce the <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports at the end of the <sup>fi</sup>scal year. The main parameter of interest $\beta$ estimates the treatment effect of Tier 1 <sup>fi</sup>rms’ XBRL adoption in <sup>fi</sup>scal 2009. Standard errors are all clustered at the <sup>fi</sup>rm level.

Because Tier 1 and Tier 2 <sup>fi</sup>rms differ in size, other factors (such as the <sup>fi</sup>nancial crisis starting in 2007) might confound the estimation using Equation (1). If the factors affect the disclosure readability of <sup>fi</sup>rms in the two tiers disproportionally and their effects change with <sup>fi</sup>rm size $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , the <sup>fi</sup>nancial crisis may cause larger <sup>fi</sup>rms to produce more complex <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports than smaller ones), the factors could confound the estimation. To address this concern, we use Equation (2) as the primary econometric model in the study, as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{r} D V _ {i, t} = \beta_ {1} P o s t _ {t} \times T i e r 1 _ {i} + \beta_ {2} P o s t _ {t} \times S i z e _ {i} \\ + \theta \mathbf {X} _ {i, t} + \eta_ {i} + v _ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i, t}, \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

where Size is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the equity market value of <sup>fi</sup>rm i in April 2009 minus the cutoff value (\$5 billion, which is used to classify <sup>fi</sup>rms into Tier 1 or Tier 2), divided by the standard deviation of equity market value in April 2009.<sup>7</sup>

3.2.2. Sample Construction. Because there is no of<sup>fi</sup>- cial list of <sup>fi</sup>rms and their af<sup>fi</sup>liated tiers, we selected Tier 1 and Tier 2 <sup>fi</sup>rms using the criteria that comport with the requirements of the XBRL mandate, in a way similar to prior studies (Blankespoor et al. 2014, Blankespoor 2019). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we downloaded the 10-Ks of all public <sup>fi</sup>rms from SEC EDGAR from <sup>fi</sup>scal years 2003 to 2015 and extracted HTML <sup>fi</sup>les and XBRL <sup>fi</sup>les (if any). For readability analysis, we retained plain text of each HTML-formatted 10-K by removing the elements unrelated to textual information, such as page headers and footers, the XBRL portion of the <sup>fi</sup>lings, and the pdf and jpeg <sup>fi</sup>le attachments. We also removed tables that mainly contained numerical values.

For Tier 1 <sup>fi</sup>rms of the treatment group, we found 450 <sup>fi</sup>rms that had <sup>fi</sup>led XBRL-formatted 10-Ks with <sup>fi</sup>scal periods between June 15, 2009, and June 14, 2010. To avoid self-selection bias, we then removed eight <sup>fi</sup>rms that had chosen to submit the XBRL-formatted 10-Ks voluntarily in <sup>fi</sup>scal 2008 (before the mandate). For the remaining 442 <sup>fi</sup>rms, we examined the public <sup>fl</sup>oat in the submitted 10-Ks and found 29 <sup>fi</sup>rms with missing values. For the missing values, we had to use the central index key (CIK) of the <sup>fi</sup>rms to get the public <sup>fl</sup>oat value (market value of publicly tradable shares) from Compustat. We found that 75 <sup>fi</sup>rms with the public <sup>fl</sup>oat value less than \$5 billion and thus removed them from the sample. Ultimately, 367 <sup>fi</sup>rms remained in the treatment group of Tier 1 <sup>fi</sup>rms. Our sample size of Tier 1 <sup>fi</sup>rms resembles that of Blankespoor et al. (2014), who assembled a sample of 369 Tier 1 <sup>fi</sup>rms. For Tier 2 <sup>fi</sup>rms of the control group, we found 1,374 <sup>fi</sup>rms that had <sup>fi</sup>led XBRL-formatted 10-Ks for the period from June 15, 2010, to June 14, 2011. We removed the 450 <sup>fi</sup>rms that submitted the XBRL-formatted 10-Ks in <sup>fi</sup>scal 2009 and removed 116 <sup>fi</sup>rms that had a public <sup>fl</sup>oat value of <sup>fi</sup>scal 2009 not within the range between \$700 million and \$5 billion. As a result, 808 <sup>fi</sup>rms remained in the sample of Tier 2 <sup>fi</sup>rms.<sup>8</sup>

Following a similar procedure, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed Tier 3 <sup>fi</sup>rms by <sup>fi</sup>rst obtaining all <sup>fi</sup>rms that <sup>fi</sup>led XBRL annual reports between June 15, 2011, and June 14, 2012, and then removing those <sup>fi</sup>rms that <sup>fi</sup>led XBRL before or belonged to the large accelerated <sup>fi</sup>ler category. Because many of the smaller <sup>fi</sup>rms did not <sup>fi</sup>le annual reports consecutively, we retained <sup>fi</sup>rms with a public <sup>fl</sup>oat no less than \$50 million. As a result, 1,758 <sup>fi</sup>rms remained in the sample of Tier 3 <sup>fi</sup>rms.

## 3.3. Readability Measures

The Fog index is perhaps the most widely used measure of textual readability in the information systems, accounting, and <sup>fi</sup>nance literature (Li 2008, Miller 2010, Ghose and Ipeirotis 2011, Loughran and McDonald 2014a). By assuming that more syllables per word and more words per sentence make a document harder to read, the index measures textual readability as a function of the two components:

$$
\begin{array}{c} F o g = 0. 4 \times (\text {average number of words per sentence} \\ + \text {percent complex words}), \end{array}
$$

where complex words are those of three or more syllables. The index indicates the number of years of formal education a reader would need to understand the text after reading it once. The range of the index value and the corresponding readability are as follows: 18, unreadable; 14–18, dif<sup>fi</sup>cult; 12–14, ideal; 10–12, acceptable; 8–10, childish. A higher Fog index indicates that the text is harder to read.

Although the Fog index is a widely used textual readability measure, Loughran and McDonald (2014a) criticize the second component of the original de<sup>fi</sup>nition (i.e., complex words) as “a poorly speci<sup>fi</sup>ed measure in business documents,” because many frequently used complex words (such as corporation, company, agreement, management, and operations) in 10-Ks are presumably easy for investors and analysts to understand. A straightforward way to address this limitation is to remove such words from the list of complex ones. Loughran and McDonald (2014a) analyze more than 45,000 different complex words (those of three or more syllables) appearing in 10-Ks and identify 52 words that account for more than a quarter of the total complex word count and thus should be considered common business terms. Accordingly, we treat the 52 words as simple words when computing the modi<sup>fi</sup>ed Fog index and denote it as Fog1.

According to Bonsall et al. (2017), multisyllabic words familiar to an average reader are identi<sup>fi</sup>ed using the commercial software tool StyleWriter. The top 100 complex but familiar words with more than two syllables (e.g., additionally, nanostructure, and establish) account for more than 50% of complex words in all 10-Ks <sup>fi</sup>led between 1994 to 2012 (Bonsall et al. 2017). When assessing readability, these words can be considered simple. Similar to Fog1, we compute an alternative modi<sup>fi</sup>ed Fog index, Fog2, by treating words in the combined word lists of Loughran and McDonald (2014a) and Bonsall et al. (2017) as simple words.

How to de<sup>fi</sup>ne the most valid measure of textual readability is still debated (Loughran and McDonald 2014a, b; Bonsall et al. 2017) and beyond the scope of the paper. In this study, we aim to <sup>fi</sup>nd robust evidence of the effects of the XBRL mandate using the two modi<sup>fi</sup>ed Fog indices: Fog1 and Fog2.

## 3.4. Control Variables

For the control variables ${ \bf { X } } _ { \mathrm { { i , t } } }$ , we refer to the classic study of Li (2008) to identify factors likely to determine the readability of 10-Ks. We collect time-series data for all of the time-varying factors suggested by Li (2008) and make the natural log transformation to those with a highly skewed distribution. These timevarying factors are listed and explained here. (Incorporation state is not included in ${ \bf { X } } _ { \mathrm { { i , t } } } ,$ because it is largely time invariant.)

Firm size: Larger <sup>fi</sup>rms tend to have higher litigation risks (Kasznik and Lev 1995), and <sup>fi</sup>rm size is often used as a proxy for political cost (Watts and Zimmerman 1986). Thus, <sup>fi</sup>rm size can be associated with disclosure behaviors that affect the readability of <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports. We use two variables to capture <sup>fi</sup>rm size: the natural logarithm of the market value of a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s equity at the end of its <sup>fi</sup>scal year (Li 2008) and the natural logarithm of the number of common shares outstanding (adjusted for stock splits) at the end of <sup>fi</sup>scal year (Barth and Clinch 2009).<sup>10</sup>

Market-to-book: This ratio indicates a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s growth potential and investment opportunities. Growth <sup>fi</sup>rms tend to have uncertain business practices that affect their reporting (Li 2008). We de<sup>fi</sup>ne this variable as the sum of the market value of equity and the book value of total liability divided by the book value of total assets at the end of <sup>fi</sup>scal year.

Firm age: We use the number of years since a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s <sup>fi</sup>rst appearance in the Center for Research in Security Prices (CRSP) <sup>fi</sup>les to measure its age.

<sub>•</sub> Special items: A special item is a large expense or income that is not expected to recur. The literature suggests that the magnitude of special items is likely correlated with a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s disclosure behaviors and the readability of <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports (Li 2008). Firms with lower special items (i.e., more negative special items) are likely to have more complex <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports. We de<sup>fi</sup>ne the variable as the value of special items scaled by the book value of total assets at the end of <sup>fi</sup>scal year.

Volatility of business or operation: Firms experiencing high volatility in their business are likely to issue more complex reports. We use two variables to measure volatility. Stock return volatility is the standard deviation of the monthly stock return in the past 12 months. Earnings volatility is the standard deviation of the operating income of the past <sup>fi</sup>ve years (including the current <sup>fi</sup>scal year).

Complexity of operations: More complex <sup>fi</sup>rms are more likely to have complex annual reports. We use two variables to measure the complexity of business and operations. One is the number of business segments, and the other is the number of geographic segments from the Compustat segment <sup>fi</sup>les.

Financial complexity: Firms with more complex <sup>fi</sup>- nancial situations are more likely to disclose more quantitative and qualitative information. We use the number of nonmissing items in Compustat as a proxy for <sup>fi</sup>nancial complexity.

Firm events: Unusual <sup>fi</sup>rm events are likely to require extra and more detailed disclosures. We use two dummy variables to indicate unusual <sup>fi</sup>rm events. One is whether a <sup>fi</sup>rm in a year appears in the SDC Platinum M&A database as an acquirer, and the other is whether a <sup>fi</sup>rm in a year has a common equity offering in the secondary market, according to the SDC Global New Issues database.

## 4.1. Parallel-Trends Assumption

Although Tier 2 <sup>fi</sup>rms are smaller than Tier 1 <sup>fi</sup>rms in terms of their public <sup>fl</sup>oats, the difference-in-differences design allows for identifying the treatment effect under the assumption that the disclosure readability of <sup>fi</sup>rms in both tiers follows parallel trends in the absence of the XBRL mandate. This is known as the parallel-trends assumption (Bertrand et al. 2004, Angrist and Pischke 2008). One common way to assess the plausibility of this parallel-trends assumption is to check whether the treatment and control groups share similar trends prior to the treatment.

We use the six-year pretreatment data (<sup>fi</sup>scal 2003–2008) to provide evidence supporting the parallel-trends assumption. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we conduct a statistical test suggested in the literature (Greenwood and Agarwal 2016, Oh et al. 2016) by estimating the following model in Equation (3).

$$
D V _ {i, t} = \sum_ {t = 2 0 0 4} ^ {2 0 0 8} \beta_ {t} \times D _ {t} + \sum_ {t = 2 0 0 4} ^ {2 0 0 8} \gamma_ {t} \times D _ {t} \times T i e r 1 _ {i} + \eta_ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i, t},\tag{3}
$$

where $D V _ { \mathrm { i , t } }$ is the readability measure of <sup>fi</sup>rm i in year t (that is, Fog1 or Fog2); Tier1<sub>i</sub> equals one if <sup>fi</sup>rm i is a Tier 1 <sup>fi</sup>rm in the treatment group and zero if it is a Tier 2 <sup>fi</sup>rm in the control group; η is the set of <sup>fi</sup>xed-effect dummy variables for all the <sup>fi</sup>rms; and $D _ { 2 0 0 3 } { \sim } D _ { 2 0 0 8 }$ are dummies indicating the pretreatment <sup>fi</sup>scal years (<sup>fi</sup>scal 2003 to 2008), but $D _ { 2 0 0 3 }$ is not included in Equation (3), because <sup>fi</sup>scal 2003 is used as the baseline. The coef<sup>fi</sup>cients $\beta _ { 2 0 0 4 } { \sim } \beta _ { 2 0 0 8 }$ capture the pretreatment common time trends of disclosure readability of <sup>fi</sup>rms in both tiers. Accordingly, the coef<sup>fi</sup>- cients γ \~γ capture the differences in disclosure readability of <sup>fi</sup>rms in the two tiers over the pretreatment years. Parallel pretrends suggest that none of $\gamma _ { 2 0 0 4 } { \sim } \gamma _ { 2 0 0 8 }$ are statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant from zero.

Table 2 reports the results of the test where Fog1 and Fog2 are used as the dependent variable, respectively. Column (1) of Table 2 shows that none of the estimates of γ<sub>2004</sub>\~γ<sub>2008</sub> are statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant $( p ~ > ~ 0 . 3 0 )$ when the dependent variable is Fog1. The joint F test suggests that the set of $\gamma _ { 2 0 0 4 } { \sim } \gamma _ { 2 0 0 8 }$ as a whole is not statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant $( p = 0 . 6 2 )$ . As shown in column (2), when the dependent variable is $F o g 2 ,$ , none of the estimates of $\gamma _ { 2 0 0 4 } { \sim } \gamma _ { 2 0 0 8 }$ are statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, and the joint F test suggests that the set of γ<sub>2004</sub>\~γ<sub>2008</sub> as a whole is not statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant either. Thus, the results in Table 2 support the parallel-trends assumption.

## 4.4. Heterogeneous Treatment Effects

In adopting XBRL, <sup>fi</sup>rms must tag each numerical value in the <sup>fi</sup>nancial statements using either standard tags de<sup>fi</sup>ned in the GAAP XBRL taxonomy or custom tags. Therefore, the cost of XBRL adoption is likely to be higher for <sup>fi</sup>rms with more quantitative disclosures in their reports. Accordingly, if XBRL adoption truly affects textual readability, we expect the adopting <sup>fi</sup>rms with more quantitative disclosures (that is, <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports containing more numbers) to be affected more than those with fewer quantitative disclosures.

## 4.5. Effect of XBRL Adoption in the Subsequent Year

Thus far, we focused on estimating the effect of Tier 1 <sup>fi</sup>rms’ XBRL adoption in the <sup>fi</sup>rst year (<sup>fi</sup>scal 2009). Here, we explore whether the effect of XBRL adoption only exists in the <sup>fi</sup>rst year or persists in the subsequent year of <sup>fi</sup>scal 2010. Because Tier 2 <sup>fi</sup>rms started adopting XBRL in <sup>fi</sup>scal 2010, they cannot be used as the control group. Whereas Tier 3 <sup>fi</sup>rms did not start adopting XBRL until <sup>fi</sup>scal 2011, we use Tier 3 <sup>fi</sup>rms with public <sup>fl</sup>oat greater than \$200 million as the control group. To estimate the effect in <sup>fi</sup>scal 2010, we include the data before the adoption (from <sup>fi</sup>scal 2003 to <sup>fi</sup>scal 2008) and the data of <sup>fi</sup>scal 2010 in the analysis. We use the same model and include the same set of control variables. Table 6 reports the results. ## 4.6. Robustness Checks

4.6.1. Difference-in-Differences in Combination with Matching. The identi<sup>fi</sup>cation assumption of the difference-in-differences design is that the disclosure readability of Tier 1 and Tier 2 <sup>fi</sup>rms follows parallel trends in the absence of the XBRL mandate. Section 4.1 presents the supporting evidence. To enhance the validity of the design, we use the propensity-score matching method (Rosenbaum and Rubin 1983, Dehejia and Wahba 2002) to construct a set of matched treated (Tier 1) and control (Tier 2) <sup>fi</sup>rms that are comparable before the mandate (Blankespoor et al. 2014, Blankespoor 2019). Following the classic study by Li (2008), we aim to match the treated and control <sup>fi</sup>rms based on a set of variables that are plausibly determinants of disclosure readability, including market-tobook, <sup>fi</sup>rm age, special items, stock return volatility, number of business segments, number of geographic segments, <sup>fi</sup>nancial complexity, and <sup>fi</sup>rm events. We construct a set of treated and control <sup>fi</sup>rms that are comparable in terms of the values of those plausible determinants in <sup>fi</sup>scal 2008.

To use propensity-score matching, we <sup>fi</sup>rst run a logistic regression using the matching variables to predict whether a <sup>fi</sup>rm is treated and <sup>fi</sup>nd that the predicted propensity scores of the treated and control <sup>fi</sup>rms differ signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly. Before matching, we put all <sup>fi</sup>rms in a random order to ensure the ordering does not affect the matching. Then we match the treated and control <sup>fi</sup>rms based on their predicted scores. To reduce possible bias, we ensure that the treated and control <sup>fi</sup>rms share the common support of the predicted propensity scores (Caliendo and Kopeinig 2008). We use the nearest-neighbor matching with replacement; for each treated <sup>fi</sup>rm, the control <sup>fi</sup>rm with the nearest predicted propensity score is chosen in the matched pair. Nearest-neighbor matching with replacement allows for minimizing the bias between the treated and control <sup>fi</sup>rms, because bias would occur when multiple (potentially less similar) control <sup>fi</sup>rms are chosen to match with the treated <sup>fi</sup>rm. Matching with replacement guarantees that the most similar control <sup>fi</sup>rm is chosen (Caliendo and Kopeinig 2008). Ultimately, we <sup>fi</sup>nd the corresponding nearest control <sup>fi</sup>rms for 321 (87.5%) of 367 Tier 1 <sup>fi</sup>rms.

To assess the quality of matches, we conduct several tests. First, we repeat the logistic regression using the matched sample and <sup>fi</sup>nd that none of the matching variables can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly predict whether a <sup>fi</sup>rm is treated. Second, we construct the density distributions of the matched treated and control <sup>fi</sup>rms, and they are almost identical. Third, we conduct a balance check on all the matching variables and <sup>fi</sup>nd no statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference in any of the variables after matching. Finally, we repeat the test of pretrends, as we did in Section 4.1, and <sup>fi</sup>nd that none of the estimates of $\gamma _ { 2 0 0 4 } { \sim } \gamma _ { 2 0 0 8 }$ are statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, and the joint F test of the set of $\gamma _ { 2 0 0 4 } { \sim } \gamma$ as a whole is also far from statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant $( p > 0 . 3 6 )$ . The results consistently indicate that <sup>fi</sup>rms in the matched sample are comparable in terms of the determinants of textual readability.

Then we conduct the difference-in-differences estimation based on the matched treated and control <sup>fi</sup>rms using Equation (2). Columns (1) and (2) of Table 7 report the results and show that the effects of the XBRL mandate on both Fog1 and Fog2 are statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, suggesting that our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are robust when we use the matched sample.

When using propensity-score matching, a function of covariates must be speci<sup>fi</sup>ed to estimate the propensity scores (Caliendo and Kopeinig 2008). To alleviate the concern that our <sup>fi</sup>ndings might depend on the function form, we use coarsened exact matching (Iacus et al. 2012) as an alternative matching method. When using this method, the covariates of the treated and control units are <sup>fi</sup>rst coarsened and segmented into different strata. Only the strata that contain at least one treated and one control unit remain in the subsequent analysis. Accordingly, the treated and control units within the same strata are exactly matched based on the coarsened values of the covariates. The subsequent analysis is conducted based on the original (uncoarsened) values of the matched treated and control units. Iacus et al. (2012) present several advantages of this approach. For example, because it is an exact matching method, the balance between the matched treated and control units are reasonably guaranteed. Also, because it is nonparametric, there is no need to specify the function form for matching based on the covariates, and thus the model dependence is largely reduced.

Similarly, we use coarsened exact matching as a preprocessing matching method to assemble the matched treated (Tier 1) and control (Tier 2) <sup>fi</sup>rms. We use the same set of matching variables as we did with propensity-score matching. With coarsened exact matching, we <sup>fi</sup>nd control <sup>fi</sup>rms that match with 254 (69%) of 367 treated <sup>fi</sup>rms. Although balance check is not critical when using this approach (Iacus et al. 2012), we con<sup>fi</sup>rm that the matched treated and control <sup>fi</sup>rms are comparable in terms of all the matching variables. Then, for each treated Tier 1 <sup>fi</sup>rm in the matched strata, we randomly select one control Tier 2 <sup>fi</sup>rm in the same strata, so that the matched treated and control <sup>fi</sup>rms are exactly matched based on the coarsened matching variables. After that, we conduct the difference-in-differences estimation based on the matched treated and control <sup>fi</sup>rms using Equation (2). Columns (3) and (4) of Table 7 report the results and again show that the effects of the XBRL mandate on both Fog1 and Fog2 are statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant.

## 5. Conclusion

## 5.1. Contributions

This study <sup>fi</sup>nds that the SEC’s XBRL mandate degraded the readability of the initial adopters HTML-formatted (human-readable) <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports. This effect is concentrated among adopters with more quantitative information in their reports, those with smaller <sup>fi</sup>rm size, and those with a higher level of <sup>fi</sup>- nancial complexity. Importantly, we show that managers’ reduced attention to preparing HTML-formatted annual reports, rather than increased disclosures, is likely the explanation for this decrease in textual readability. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings here will be of interest to various market participants, including <sup>fi</sup>rm managers, investors, analysts, and regulators.

Our study contributes to several strands of literature. First, we contribute to the literature on disclosure readability. Textual analysis has become popular in the accounting and <sup>fi</sup>nance research (Loughran and McDonald 2016). In particular, the recent review paper by Loughran and McDonald (2020) points out the importance of understanding readability in <sup>fi</sup>nancial disclosures. Although studies have examined the impacts of disclosure readability, the determinants of readability are underexplored. Our study adds to the literature by showing that mandatory (i.e., regulatorrequired) reporting technologies—in this case, the SEC’s XBRL mandate—can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affect disclosure readability.

Second, our study contributes to the literature on <sup>fi</sup>- nancial reporting technologies in general and XBRL in particular. Technological advances in information production and dissemination could reduce investors’ disclosure processing costs. Thus, the investigation on the impacts of mandatory <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting technologies has been a focus of <sup>fi</sup>nancial accounting research (Blankespoor et al. 2020). Particularly, our study contributes to the literature on XBRL, the SEC’s mandatory <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting technology.

Third, our study contributes to the information systems literature on the impacts of data standardization. Standardization can improve data interoperability, consistency, and other quality dimensions (Wang and Strong 1996, Krishnan et al. 2005, Madnick et al. 2009, Bai et al. 2012), which, in turn, can enhance <sup>fi</sup>rm performance (Zhao and Xia 2014). While data standardization can improve the quality of structured and semistructured data, scant research considers whether it has any impact on the quality (e.g., readability) of the accompanying human-readable data. Our study shows that the XBRL mandate, a large-scale standardization effort, led to a reduction in the readability of HTML-formatted <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports.

Finally, our study contributes to the literature on the economic consequences of mandatory disclosures and multitasking. Dranove and Jin (2010) present a literature review on the economics of disclosures in different industries. They point out that mandatory disclosures may be counterproductive. For example, Dranove et al. (2003) <sup>fi</sup>nd that the introduction of healthcare report cards may lead to the loss of social welfare. Lu (2012) shows that multitasking is likely to occur when <sup>fi</sup>rms face mandatory disclosures in service quality. Multitasking theory (Holmstrom and Milgrom 1991) suggests that changes in returns across tasks alter the effort or attention devoted to each. Under the XBRL mandate, adopting <sup>fi</sup>rms must multitask when <sup>fi</sup>ling <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports in both the HTML and XBRL formats. Our study shows that the effect of the XBRL mandate on reduced readability is likely a result of multitasking, which leads to managers’ reduced attention on the <sup>fi</sup>ling of HTML-formatted <sup>fi</sup>- nancial reports.

## 5.2. Implications for Research and Practice

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings yield several implications for research and practice. First, as it is more costly and time consuming to extract market-relevant information from less readable reports (Lehavy et al. 2011, Loughran and McDonald 2014a), the <sup>fi</sup>ndings here imply that the XBRL mandate may initially harm the quality and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of the information environment. Kim et al. (2012) show that the XBRL mandate increases information ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and mitigates information risks in the <sup>fi</sup>nancial market, whereas Blankespoor et al. (2014) <sup>fi</sup>nd that information asymmetry in the market increases after the mandate. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings contribute to this debate and are consistent with and, in fact, can explain the results of Blankespoor et al. (2014), because reduced readability after the XBRL mandate may partially result in the increased information asymmetry in the market.

Second, less readable <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports demand more processing from investors and analysts (Lehavy et al. 2011, Loughran and McDonald 2014a), individual or small investors with less processing ability may struggle and be placed at a disadvantage. Accordingly, information asymmetry between large and small investors is likely to be enlarged. Lawrence (2013) shows that individual investors’ excess returns increase with clearer and more readable <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports. Hence, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings imply that individual investors’ excess returns may decrease as a result of XBRL adoption. With the reduced expected returns, individual or small investors may reduce their investments and trading (Miller 2010, Lawrence 2013). Again, this explains the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of Blankespoor et al. (2014) that the XBRL mandate leads to a decrease in information liquidity and in abnormal trading volume, particularly for small trades.

Third, <sup>fi</sup>rms can mitigate these negative impacts on their <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports. Some have attempted to do this through alternative methods of disclosures, including 8-K <sup>fi</sup>lings, management forecasts, and press releases (Guay et al. 2016). The unique role of social media platforms in the information environment of <sup>fi</sup>nancial market has gained much attention (Chen et al. 2014, Blankespoor et al. 2020). For example, Xu and Zhang (2013) <sup>fi</sup>nd that Wikipedia is used as an information aggregator for <sup>fi</sup>rms’ voluntary disclosure. Therefore, social media platforms may play an increasingly important role in improving the information environment after the XBRL mandate.

Finally, a better approach to mitigating the negative effect of XBRL adoption on the readability of <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports may be through process optimization and technology enablement. First, initial adopters generally view XBRL reporting as a separate step, which occurs after the HTML reports are produced. They thus have missed the opportunity to optimize their <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting. As <sup>fi</sup>rms integrate XBRL into their overall reporting practices, the entire process is likely to become more streamlined. Second, XBRL reports in the early adoption periods are produced semiautomatically, using tools that are separate from <sup>fi</sup>nancial accounting systems. Such tools assist humans in choosing GAAP Taxonomy tags. We anticipate that advanced technologies will arise to enable the generation of XBRL-tagged <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports. These technologies will likely infer and capture the mappings between <sup>fi</sup>nancial concepts and the GAAP Taxonomy. Such mappings will enable <sup>fi</sup>rms to better integrate XBRL into their overall <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting. This will allow them to allocate enough resources to the quality control of all the <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports they produce, regardless of format.

## 5.3. Limitations and Directions for Future Research

We note a few limitations of our study and discuss some opportunities for future research. First, as Leuz and Wysocki (2016) point out, one of the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation challenge for reporting regulation studies is the selection problem. The selection problem arises when <sup>fi</sup>rms can opt out or have ways to avoid the regulations, which is quite common in practice. A detailed analysis on our sample indicates that there are 66 <sup>fi</sup>rms that did not <sup>fi</sup>le XBRL-formatted <sup>fi</sup>nancial statements in <sup>fi</sup>scal 2009, even though they passed the threshold of \$5 billion. Those <sup>fi</sup>rms can be considered noncompliers, whereas the 367 Tier 1 <sup>fi</sup>rms in the treatment group are compliers. We compare the readability measures and several <sup>fi</sup>rm characteristics of the compliers and noncompliers in <sup>fi</sup>scal 2008, one year before the mandate. The results of a balance check show that disclosure readability of compliers and noncompliers is quite similar, but compliers and noncompliers differ statistically on 5 of 12 characteristics. For example, noncompliers are more likely to be smaller and relatively younger <sup>fi</sup>rms, perhaps because they are less visible than more established, accelerated <sup>fi</sup>lers. The signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference suggests that there could be a possible bias because of the self-selection of compliance with <sup>fi</sup>nancial regulations (the XBRL mandate in this case). Nevertheless, our robustness checks likely reduce this possible self-selection bias. Second, all <sup>fi</sup>rms had to adopt XBRL as of <sup>fi</sup>scal 2011, and there are no control (untreated) <sup>fi</sup>rms that we can use to conduct the counterfactual analysis for comparison with treated <sup>fi</sup>rms. Therefore, we cannot rigorously estimate the causal effect of XBRL adoption in the long run, which limits understanding about the long-term implications of adoption. For example, is there any learning and adaptation in the long run? Future research may consider using a longitudinal study to explore the long-term effect of XBRL adoption. Finally, the SEC’s regulations related to XBRL are not static but have evolved over the years. In 2018, the SEC required that <sup>fi</sup>rms <sup>fi</sup>le inline XBRL (iXBRL) reports in which XBRL is directly embedded into HTML <sup>fi</sup>lings. Future research is needed to explore the implications of the recent advances of iXBRL.
