# Role of Social Media in Social Protest Cycles: A Sociomaterial Examination

Monideepa Tarafdar,<sup>a</sup> Deepa Kajal Ray<sup>b</sup>

Abstract. Contemporary social media fueled social protest is self-organized, rapidly dynamic, and decentralized, constitutes vast populations, and is shaped by multiple and concurrent channels of information <sup>fl</sup>ows. Such protest activity is captured in the concept of social protest cycles, which are short periods of intense and contentious protest activity characterized by temporal dynamics, a large repertoire of protest action, confrontation and potential violence, and possible institutional action. Social protest cycles are the microfoundations of long-term social movements. They contain the seeds of potential societal transformation because their intense collective action can be constructively harnessed toward change. This paper examines the role of social media in social protest cycles. Drawing from the theoretical concept of sociomaterial assemblages, we conceptualize the social media enabled social protest cycle as an assemblage having social (e.g., people, elected leaders, police, judges) and technical (e.g., social media applications, online petition applications) components. We analyze how the social protest cycle transforms through performative intra-actions. The empirical context for the study is a social media enabled social protest cycle that emerged after a fatal rape incident in New Delhi, India. Data pertaining to the social protest cycle over the period December 17–25, 2012, were collected from social media activity on Twitter, Facebook, YouTube, online blogs, and newspaper websites. Through mixed methods analysis we identify three intra-actions, consolidation, expansion, and intensi<sup>fi</sup>cation, and theorize how they transform the social protest cycle over time. The paper contributes to the information systems literature that studies social media–enabled social protest action. As theoretical contributions, it develops (1) the notion of intra-actions as organizing mechanisms and (2) a relational ontology for social media–enabled social protest action. Through these contributions, we suggest that the power of social media lies in its socially produced and emergent relationships with other entities in the social protest cycle.

## Introduction

The role of social media in social movements is of strong scholarly interest because contemporary social movements are fueled by social media. Who can forget the powerful images of the many different social medial fueled protests, across the world, in 2020 and early into 2021? Although the Arab Spring and the Egyptian protests are cited as early examples (Ghannam 2011, Tufekci and Wilson 2012), MeToo and Black Lives Matter (Associated Press 2018), among others, pervade current societal consciousness. Such protest action entails extensive use of social media (e.g., hashtags, posts, (re)- tweets, shares, and likes) and leveraging of social networks to articulate and spread protest (Bonilla and Rosa 2015, Valenzuela et al. 2018, Harlow and Benbrook 2019).

Social movements constitute groups of individuals collectively seeking social change on particular issues (Diani 1992). Although social movements often span long periods, within them are recurring and relatively short periods of intense unrest and contentious and often violent protest activity. These periods are referred to as social protest cycles (Tarrow 1998, Porta and Diani 2009). Some key characteristics of social protest cycles are (Tarrow 1988) heightened protest action, possibly violence; large geographical spread; involvement of a large number of societal entities; not led by a single entity; rapid temporal dynamics; and possible institutional action. Social protest cycles are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant because they are the micro-foundations of social movements. Understanding them is necessary (Koopmans 2007) in order to harness their scale and intensity toward institutionalized social change, to engage and dialog with the possible confrontation and violence (made more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult without a leading organization), and to ascertain the potential progress and future direction of the social movement.

Increasingly now, social media are used in social protest cycles. Social media–enabled social protest cycles have characteristics unique from traditional social protest cycles. The protest action is made possible through use of social media simultaneously by a massive and physically dispersed population (e.g., ordinary citizens, celebrities). It also involves government of<sup>fi</sup>cials (e.g., ministers and members of legislative units), law keeping of<sup>fi</sup>cials (e.g., police and riot troops), law makers (judges and lawyers), and media personnel (e.g., reporters, photographers, news anchors/editors). The use of social media is situated within a complex web of political, social, and technological relations (Della Porta and Mattoni 2014) such that there is an even greater potential for geographical expansion, rapid <sup>fl</sup>are up of protest activity, and unpredictability of the scale of violence. Given these properties, the role of social media is theoretically novel and interesting because it is dynamic and entangled with that of the many other diverse entities that are part of the social protest cycle. These include, for example, television, social networks and structures, protestors (individuals and groups) using social media, online content, of<sup>fl</sup>ine events, and government and nongovernment organizations (Fuchs 2012, Juris 2012, Lim 2012, Tucker and Goodings 2014). Such a role focuses on entanglement, emergence, and situatedness, aspects that can shed light on new ways in which large scale and decentralized social protest is organized through social media (Young et al. 2019). Indeed, it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to understand the unfolding of modern day, technology-fueled social movements without an understanding of social protest cycles and social media’s role in them. The objective of our research is thus to theorize the role of social media in social protest cycles.

To engage with the aspects of entanglement, emergence, dynamicity, and diverse entities that characterize social media–enabled social protest cycles, we draw from the concept of sociomaterial assemblages. A sociomaterial assemblage is constituted of social and technical components and has emergent properties (Barad 2003, Introna 2013). We conceptualize the social protest cycle as an assemblage that includes social components such as citizens, protestors, government, law and order–enforcing units such as police, nongovernment organizations, and so on, and technical components such as social media applications, online groups, and so on. The components have material (e.g., social media functionality such as hashtags and online petitions) and expressive (e.g., social media posts, human emotions) aspects. To understand the role of social media, we analyze the assemblage’s dynamic behavior through its intra-actions (Barad 2003, Kautz and Jensen 2013).

The empirical context for the study is a social media–enabled social protest cycle that emerged in response to a brutal and fatal gang rape in New Delhi, India, in December 2012. It has been described as the country’s <sup>fi</sup>rst social media led societal protest (Ahmed and Jaidka 2013). We collected data pertaining to the social protest cycle as it unfolded temporally, using a netnographic methodological approach (Kozinets 2015), and analyzed it using mixed methods analysis. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we gathered social media use data from Twitter, Facebook, YouTube, blogs, and websites.

Our study contributes to the information systems (IS) literature that studies social media–enabled social protest action; it highlights novel aspects regarding the role of social media therein. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that intra-actions are the organizing mechanisms of social media–enabled social protest cycles. We identi<sup>fi</sup>ed three intra-actions, each embodying a particular focus of protest activity in the social protest cycle. The consolidation intra-action brought people together to identify with the social protest cycle, the expansion intra-action mobilized and gathered support for it, and the intensi<sup>fi</sup>cation intra-action strengthened on-the-ground protest and con<sup>fl</sup>ict. Intra-actions are performative; they materialize what they express. Their dynamic rise and fall shape the temporal character of the social protest cycle. Intra-actions bring social media together with the other entities of the social protest cycle. They represent the coconstitutive coming together of the material and social components of the assemblage in situ, suggesting that the social media–enabled social protest cycle has a relational ontology. The structures, resources and collective interests/concerns of social media–enabled social protest cycles emerge endogenously, so that their scope, and the extent of societal and institutional change made possible by them cannot be predicted beforehand. Our study responds to calls in the literature for examining how leaderless social media–fueled protests come about and how decentralized individuals organize themselves through social media without the formal boundaries of established structures and organizations (Young et al. 2019) and for investigating local regularities in complex sociomaterial phenomena (Gaskin et al. 2014).

This paper is structured as follows. In the next two sections, we respectively provide literature background on social protest cycles and the use of social media in social protest action, and describe our theoretical framing of sociomaterial assemblages. In the following two sections, we present the study’s methods, empirical data, and analysis. In the next section, we develop a theoretical explanation of the role of social media in the social protest cycle. We conclude with a discussion of contributions, implications, and limitations.

## Literature Review

In this section, we <sup>fi</sup>rst present background literature on social movements, focusing on social protest cycles. Next, we present the literature on the use of social media in social protest action.

## Social Movements and Social Protest Cycles

Social movements are collective efforts to seek social change with regard to particular issues (Diani 1992). Existing structural and societal conditions, such as political and social systems, the state’s structures for potential repression, suppression and injustice, and support for them by those with political power, often serve as triggers (Tilly 1978, Goodwin and Jasper 1999) for their emergence. Individuals, in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by these conditions, make choices based on existing common causes and marshal available resources, to come together to form social movements (McCarthy and Zald 1977, Jenkins 1983, Morris and Mueller 1992, McAdam et al. 1996, Tarrow 1998). Individuals’ interpretation of the conditions shapes their emotions of discontent and moral outrage (Snow et al. 1986). They frame the prevailing conditions through cultural attributes such as shared social meanings and symbols to identify with a particular cause, which mobilizes people to come together (McCarthy 1996, McAdam 1999, Polletta and Jasper 2001). Diagnostic framing processes deal with understanding the problem in response to which protest action is taken, motivational framing processes legitimize why protest action should be taken, and prognostic framing processes <sup>fi</sup>gure out possible protest actions (Snow and Benford 1988). Formal structures such as social movement organizations and informal structures such as networks of relatives, friends, or like-minded individuals are used for mobilizing the protest. The collective action is pursued through contentious acts such as protests, demonstrations, petitions, and boycotts (Turner and Killian 1987, Snow et al. 2007) and use of mass media for increasing visibility (Gamson 2004).

An important <sup>fi</sup>nding is that, although social movements often span long periods, within them are recurring and relatively short periods of intense collective and contentious activity, such as protests and demonstrations. These periods are known as cycles of protests, social protest cycles/waves, or cycles of contention (Tarrow 1998, Porta and Diani 2009).<sup>1</sup> Such social protest cycles are periods of “contestation in which bodies, symbols, identities, practices, and discourses are used to pursue or prevent changes in institutionalized power relations” (Taylor and Dyke 2004, p. 268). They may be sparked in response to larger societal issues or speci<sup>fi</sup>c events. They represent cyclical periods of heightened unrest and often violence. They may occur multiple times within a social movement. Key characteristics of social protest cycles are (Tarrow 1988) as follows:

(1) intense and heightened protest action, possibly violence; (2) geographical spread; (3) involvement of a large number of diverse societal entities; (4) not led by a single entity, but devolved in coordination and organizing; (5) multiple repertoires of protest actions; (6) rapid temporal dynamics; and (7) possible institutional action. Social protest cycles are the micro-foundations of social movements. Broadly speaking, they begin with a con<sup>fl</sup>ict in response to a triggering event, escalate into a peak, and then decline (Tarrow 1989, 2011). The protest activity, initially limited in scope and reach, engulfs a larger population, often accompanied by violence and clashes with institutional authorities. At its peak, the violence may result in concession or repressive action from the authorities. Subsequent to this, the protest action declines. Although these phases are broadly known, the underlying and speci<sup>fi</sup>c activities constituting them are less understood; there is a high degree of unpredictability because of the spontaneity and rapidity that characterize the protest action (Tarrow 1988, Van de Donk et al. 2004).

It is important to understand how social protest cycles emerge and decline (Koopmans 2007) because such understanding is valuable to those interested in engaging with, fostering, seizing, or opposing their revolutionary potential. Given that social protest cycles are contentious and unstable periods of disruptive change (Porta and Diani 2009), it can enable their scale and intensity to be constructively harnessed toward institutionalized action to address speci<sup>fi</sup>c social issues. Furthermore, given the possibility of violence because of confrontation with institutional authority, such understanding is necessary for engagement and dialog around social problems. Moreover, periodicity and recurrence can provide clues for the larger social movement’s sustenance and future direction.

Social media<sup>2</sup> are increasingly used in social protest cycles. Anecdotal and media accounts of contemporary protest actions point to the use of hashtags such #MeToo, and #BlackLivesMatter (Harlow and Benbrook 2019, Brunker et al.¨ 2020) to marshal protesters and organize protest events. Social media–enabled social protest cycles have distinct social media applications (e.g., Facebook, WhatsApp, and Twitter), traditional media (e.g., television), social networks and structures, individuals and groups of people using social media, online content, of<sup>fl</sup>ine events, and government and nongovernment organizations (Fuchs 2012, Tucker and Goodings 2014). Social media is used for a large and simultaneous digital repertoire of protest actions by a massive and physically dispersed population but is situated within a complex web of political and social relations (Poell 2014). Studies note that social media have “contributed to an emerging logic of aggregation that involves the ‘assembling of masses of individuals from diverse backgrounds within physical spaces” (Juris 2012, p. 259). Accounts of Twitter-based social protest suggest that “social media have been chie<sup>fl</sup>y responsible for the construction of a choreography of ‘assembly’<sup>4</sup> as a process of symbolic construction of public space which facilitates and guides the physical assembling of a highly dispersed and individualized constituency” (Gerbaudo 2012, p. 5). The protest action is organized through social media, with rapid dynamic buildup and dying down of social media activity. These characteristics imply potential theoretical novelty in social media’s role in social protest cycles, namely its temporally dynamic in<sup>fl</sup>uence and how that works in entanglement with that of other entities, within this multifarious milieu.

## Social Media in Social Protest Action

IS studies on the use of social media in social protest action focus on different kinds of protest actions such as social uprisings (e.g., Egyptian revolution and the Arab Spring), social activism against issues such as women’s rights and the environment, and riots, occupy sit-ins, and marches. They do not speci<sup>fi</sup>cally study social protest cycles. However, it is instructive to review them because they do examine use of social media for social protest action. We synthesize the studies along two focal points, giving examples, and providing details in Table 1.

The <sup>fi</sup>rst focus describes use of social media as a tool to support activities for social protest action: for gathering support and connecting people. Social media functionality such as shares, likes, retweets, and blogs (Bimber 2000, Garrett 2006, Van Laer and Van Aelst 2010) is a tool for many-to-many communication and geographically extended connectivity (Skoric and Poor 2013, Poell and Rajagopalan 2015). They can be used to create and share information, often independent of government control (Melucci 1996, Pickerill 2004, Alterman 2011, Choudhary et al. 2012, Tufekci and Wilson 2012). Functionality such as online groups can be used to bring together like-minded people around the particular cause (Boyd and Ellison 2007, Poell and Rajagopalan 2015). Digitally supported social protest action can be conceptualized along a hierarchy, from low (e.g., clicktivism) to high (e.g., hacktivism) effort, impact, and engagement (George and Leidner 2019).

The second focus is on transformed or new processes of social protest action enabled by social media. Social media can transform protest processes of membership through gatekeeping (Segerberg and Bennett 2011), technologically enabled identity framing and personalized targeting (Bennett and Segerberg 2012, Yetgin et al. 2012), trolling, and associated intimidation and polarization (Bharati et al. 2018). They also enable new ways of <sup>fi</sup>ltering and surveillance by those in authority (Ghobadi and Clegg 2015). They can transform participation processes through new ways of solidarity development (Stewart and Schultze 2019). They can alter participation discourse by reducing bias and allowing for a wider spread of authors and content, as well as engender hegemony of opinion depending on the social in<sup>fl</sup>uence of content creators (Miranda et al. 2016). Social media functionality can create new protest components. These include digital spaces through microblogging such as YouTube (Meek 2012), symbols through hashtags for articulating protest (Bonilla and Rosa 2015), and legitimacy establishing networks (Zheng and Yu 2016). Social media also enable new digital action repertoires such as digital petitioning, for social movement organizations such as Amnesty International (Selander and Jarvenpaa 2016) and nongovernmental organizations (Zheng and Yu 2016). Microblogging tools such as Weibo and Twitter can create new spillover effects through spillover of content across different collective actions (Yuce et al. 2015). They can create new modes of engagement through connective affordances through which people come together in particular clusters to accomplish different kinds of roles (Vaast et al. 2017).

Most studies examine the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of one or two social media applications on particular processes of social protest. Recent studies, however (Bennett and Segerberg 2012, Braccini et al. 2019, Young et al. 2019), call for understanding contemporary forms of digitally enabled collective action that are becoming more prominent: particularly, social protest action that is decentralized, selforganized, temporary, constitutes vast populations, and is shaped by complementary and concurrent channels of information <sup>fl</sup>ows through multiple digital technologies. Temporal processes through which such digital organizing and collective protest actions unfold are deemed especially interesting (Young et al. 2019). Social media enabled social protest cycles, which have not been examined, have these characteristics. Their study presents an opportunity to theorize a new kind of role for social media, one that is linked to a vast number of other diverse entities in the social protest cycle and that encompasses entangled and dynamic properties. Thus, the objective of our research is to explain the role of social media in social protest cycles. We next turn to the concept of sociomaterial assemblages, as the theoretical framing to tackle this objective, and in particular, to engage with the aspects of entanglement, emergence, diverse entities, and dynamicity.

## Theoretical Framing

We examine the social protest cycle through the ontological lens of the coconstitution of the technical (i.e., material) and human (i.e., social). This lens emphasizes the inextricability of the two, in processes that constitute human activity (Barad 2003, Orlikowski 2007, Introna 2013, Kautz and Jensen 2013, Cecez-Kecmanovic et al. 2014) and represents a relational ontology that presumes the two are inseparable (Orlikowski and Scott 2008). Described under the broad notion of sociomateriality, it offers a number of concepts in order to understand complex IS phenomena.<sup>5</sup> This view broadly suggests three things. First, the social and material aspects of a sociomaterial collective entangle together as people come into contact with digital and nondigital elements in everyday life. The boundaries between the two act on and constitute each other. This is described variously as, for example, “the recursive intertwining of humans and technology in practice” (Orlikowski 2007, p. 1437), webs (Kling and Scacchi 1982), and ensembles (Bijker 1997). Described thus, it sees people and technology as sociomaterial collectives (Kautz and Jensen 2013, Gaskin et al. 2014) shaped by factors that include, for example, the features of the technology, social structures, and situated actions of individuals. Second, as the material and social components come into con<sup>fl</sup>uence, the relations between them are <sup>fl</sup>uid and iteratively enacted (Kallinikos 2011, p. 12). Third, the sociomaterial collective changes over time (Orlikowski and Scott 2008) through endogenous processes (Kautz and Jensen 2013) that (re)con<sup>fi</sup>gure the relations, whereby the material and the social together constitute and bring into being the very realities that they describe, thus performing themselves into reality (Callon 1998, Gond et al. 2016).

The sociomaterial assemblage<sup>6</sup> is a collective that constitutes technical and social entities coming together in an indivisible whole (Deleuze and Guattari 1987, Holland 2013, Muller¨ 2015). The entities are mutually constitutive in their evolution over time (Orlikowski and Scott 2008, Introna 2013), entailing the assemblage’s ongoing and dynamic (re)con<sup>fi</sup>gurations. Assemblages can be used to analyze large-scale and emergent collectives involving multiple, distributed, and diverse entities (DeLanda 2006). Social media–enabled social protests cycles constitute such a collective. Therefore, we draw on the concept of assemblages to theoretically frame our study. The assemblage’s multiple and heterogeneous human and nonhuman entities are its components. A component can have two qualifying aspects: material and expressive. The material aspect describes what the component is, that is, its properties. These include, for example, the functional features of an application, such as propagation functionality of a social media post button. The expressive aspect describes what the component can communicate, that is, its expressions. These include, for example the linguistic vocabulary of humans. An assemblage embodies the synthesis of these components; the components are local parts, but relationally belonging to a whole. Intra-actions constitute practices within the assemblage that embody the coconstitutive entanglement of the expressive and material aspects of the components (Holland 2013). They enact what is known as agential separability (Barad 2003), which is a condition of endogenous local regularity within the assemblage’s indivisible whole. This separation or cut (Barad 2003) effects a local causal structure that can be analytically and empirically examined. Intra-actions are endogenous and emergent. Therefore, as the assemblage’s components entangle through intra-actions, its properties emerge. That is, an assemblage is dynamic. To understand how it transforms over time, it is necessary to examine its intra-actions (DeLanda 2006, Holland 2013, Jones 2014) because they produce the state of the assemblage.

We draw from the framing of assemblages to analyze the social protest cycle examined in this study. We conceptualize the social protest cycle as an assemblage that has components such as citizens, political leaders, elected government leaders, police, celebrities, judges/law makers, and social media applications such as Twitter, YouTube, and Facebook. Their material aspects include the functionalities of the applications, and the structural relationships between, for example, regular citizens and political leaders. The expressive aspects of the components consist of actions of people using the applications, social media content they generate, as well as the properties of communications between, for instance, individuals, individuals and political leaders, and individuals and police. The social protest cycle undergoes transformation through intra-actions. The objective of this study is to explain how, and in doing so, examine the role of social media in social protest cycles.

## Research Methods: Empirical Data and Analysis

In this section, we <sup>fi</sup>rst describe the empirical setting of the study, followed by a description of data collection and analysis.

## Study Setting

The study analyzed the period of social protest that emerged following a fatal rape<sup>7</sup> incident in the city of New Delhi in India. The incident resulted in widespread anger and outrage among the public, which was expressed in various forums, but most notably on social media. A social protest cycle emerged that lasted from December 17, 2012, the day after the rape occurred, to December 25m 2012.<sup>8</sup> The collective protest activity during this time was characterized by the attributes of a social protest cycle, namely temporal dynamism; wide geographic spread involving a massive number of people and institutions; decentralized with no leader; intense and violent protest activity that left many people injured or dead; multiple repertoires of protest activity through different social media; and government response in the form of two commissions, one a judicial inquiry commission to investigate the rape incident and the safety of women in New Delhi and another as a committee of judges to work on changes in the country’s criminal laws on sexual assault on women.<sup>9</sup> The rapists were charged with murder on January 3, 2013.<sup>10</sup>

We examine this social protest cycle through the trace of digital data generated on social media. Social media activity regarding this incident <sup>fi</sup>rst emerged on December 17, 2012, when numerous pictures, texts, and messages relating to the incident started to be posted on Twitter, Facebook, and YouTube and continued until December 28, 2012. Our empirical analysis reports on the data collected during this period. We next describe the research methods to collect and analyze the data.

## Data Collection

Our objective was to understand how the social protest cycle unfolded. Ethnographic approaches to observe phenomenon that unfold in a digital context are referred to variously, as virtual ethnography, netnography, internet ethnography, and digital ethnography (Kozinets 2015). In these approaches, the researcher collects, analyzes, and interprets data (e.g., text, picture, video, and audio) that are digitally and naturally generated by participants during the unfolding of the phenomenon, in essence following the phenomenon by interpreting human actions through their use of technology (Hine 2000, 2006). We deployed this data collection approach in our study. Such methods are increasingly adopted to study phenomena involving collective human action in various electronically mediated social contexts, such as consumer activism (Kozinets and Handelman 2004), telework practices (Boell et al. 2016), connected engagement and action (Vaast et al. 2017), and online rating (Scott and Orlikowski 2014). Our data collection methods were observational and nonparticipative, in that the authors were not a part of the social protest cycle and did not undertake any activities relating to it. We collected data that was freely available on social media websites and that did not require permission for use. The guiding philosophy was that “the movement of the digital ethnographer involves traversing interrelated digital and co-present (physical) contexts” (Postill and Pink 2012, p. 10).

Given the variety of social media (e.g., Facebook, Twitter, YouTube, blogs), it was necessary to traverse the social movement through them in a systematic way, as we describe next and in Online Appendix A1. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst step, we examined Twitter as the <sup>fi</sup>rst source of social media activity regarding the incident.

Speci<sup>fi</sup>c to our empirical setting, both practitioner and academic writing have pointed out that Twitter was the key social medium used to post information following the incident (Ahmed and Jaidka 2013, Belair-Gagnon et al. 2014). Twitter has the functionality of providing social media postings (tweets) on the predominant trending and current topics that preoccupy the social milieu. To begin with, a search using the key words of “Delhi Rape” and “Delhi gang rape” was performed on Twitter. Top tweets for these key words returned by the Twitter search were downloaded for each day of the period December 17 to 28<sup>,</sup> 2012 (Online Appendix A1, Table A1). Top tweets are relevant tweets returned by the Twitter search algorithm depending on criteria such as keyword match and popularity of tweets.<sup>11</sup> After December 25, 2012, and until December 28, 2012, there was a slowdown of activity on Twitter, indicating the tapering of the social protest cycle. A total of 6,931 tweets were downloaded for this time. In the second step, we examined each tweet for its content and for links to content on other social media. Besides their own content of text, picture, video and hashtags, the tweets contained links to Facebook posts/events/pages, online videos, various websites such as newspaper articles, blogs, and online campaigns/petitions (Online Appendix A1, Table A2). Taken together, all of this formed the total corpus of our data. In the third step, the content and links of each tweet were analyzed. Through these steps, we traversed the collective digital content associated with the social protest cycle in a snowball fashion across different media (e.g., social media such as Twitter, Facebook, blogs, YouTube, and regular news media), starting with Twitter and following through to the respective links. This traversal enabled us to examine the connected-ness among the text, image, and video content across the different social media. We developed a rich picture of the use of various features of different social media such as likes, shares, retweets, and hashtags in sync, to create and propagate this content. In the interests of keeping the paper to a reasonable length, we provided a brief description of the data collection as above, with more detail in Online Appendix A1. We also developed a chronology (Online Appendix A2) of real-world events and associated social media activity to document the activities of the social protest cycle both on social media and in the physical world.

## Mixed Methods Analysis

The analysis of our data rests on a number of foundations. First, we built on emerging studies that analyze digital trace data from social media activity (Vaast and Walsham 2013, McKenna et al. 2017). We engaged in qualitative and quantitative analysis of this data. Qualitatively, we conducted thematic content analysis through open and axial coding of the tweets, Facebook posts, blogs, videos, webpages, and newspaper articles. This helped us to identify different themes in the data and analyze their qualitative nature. We compared the themes with concepts in the literature on social movements to understand how they related. Quantitatively, we developed a numerical measure for each theme based on an understanding of social media characteristics, following similar approaches (Vaast et al. 2017). We used techniques such as tabulation and graphs to understand the numerical qualities of the themes over time and representation techniques based on Tableau to plot their relative intensities. Second, from the point of view of process study, we conducted temporal bracketing, quanti<sup>fi</sup>cation, and visual mapping of the data, by following over time, the relative strengths of the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed themes over the period of the social protest cycle. This enabled us to generate a temporal narrative of the social protest cycle (Langley 1999, Van de Ven and Poole 2005). We further compared the empirical temporal observations with the concepts in the literature on social movements to understand their theoretical nature. Third, guided by the literature on assemblages, we conducted retroductive analysis (Vaast et al. 2017) on the data to explain the concept (Van de Ven and Poole 2005) of intraactions from the empirically observed data. We went back and forth between the data and its retroductive explanation to frame our theoretical understanding of the nature of these intra-actions and the changes in their intensity over time. Fourth, based on metaanalysis (Zachariadis et al. 2013, Lindberg et al. 2016), we integrated the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from the different analyses to develop a theoretical explanation of the transformations of the social protest cycle over time. Figure 1 gives a visual summary of our mixed methods analysis.

## Qualitative Analysis

As the <sup>fi</sup>rst step, we conducted thematic content analysis of the tweets to examine their content. For this we examined each tweet for the following key attributes of microblogging content (Vaast et al. 2017): content, hashtags, and links. The tweets contained feelings and opinions, information about protest events, and pictures and videos of protest events. Many urged people to participate in protest related activities, such as sign online petitions. We coded each tweet based on its content; to do this we examined the information contained in the tweet itself and followed through to its links (i.e., Facebook posts, YouTube videos, blogs, other websites) if any, thus collecting further data from these other social media and websites. Through this analysis, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the key themes. This thematic analysis yielded seven themes and associated categories of tweets. Each theme denoted a particular practice of social media use. We describe them and then summarize them in Table 11.

Shared language described the practice of labeling and qualifying the social media discourse around the incident, as well as around general societal conditions with respect to the safety of women in India. This was done through the use of Twitter hashtags. According to the functionality of Twitter, the # symbol (material aspect) denotes an important/trending topic of conversation (expressive aspect). The name and content of the hashtag, anchored to the crime such as #Delhirape, #stoprapenow, and #reasontorise, denoted the actual topic (expressive aspect). The practice thus constituted entanglement of the material and expressive aspects. The fusion of the tweet’s hashtag’s symbol (#), its particular name, and its content intertwined into a common and easily identi<sup>fi</sup>able lexicon about the event and signaled this topic and event to be important.

Con<sup>fl</sup>ict was the practice of expressing intense anger and confrontation against authorities of the state such as the state of New Delhi’s Chief Minister, the state and central government, police, law courts, elected ministers, and members of leading political parties. The criticism and denigration were directed at what the people perceived to be the authorities’ inadequacy in addressing public anguish at the speci<sup>fi</sup>c event and more generally failing to ensure the safety of women. Examples of tweets are given in Table 10. The expressive aspects included, for example, the language of anger, antagonism, and condemnation, and the act of tweeting; the material aspects included the functionality and ability of tweeting. The practice was a fusion of these two aspects.

We interpreted the themes vis-a-vis the literature on of<sup>fl</sup>ine social movements. Despite some associations, there were important differences between our themes and the literature concepts. Each theme represented a practice of social media use where the material and expressive aspects were entangled and intertwined; thus, their very existence was because of social media.

Shared language was similar to framing (Snow and Benford 1988). However, whereas framing refers to relatively static symbols, shared language represented the dynamic and ongoing creation and use of new and varied lexicon related to the protest using social media (e.g., through Twitter hashtags). Emotions ascribed to of<sup>fl</sup>ine social movements, such as discontent and feelings of injustice (Snow et al. 1986, Gamson 1992) are evident particularly during protest events. Although other members of the social movement may see these events broadcast on mass media, they cannot directly experience the creation of the emotions; they can only indirectly observe the protesters’ emotions. In contrast, collective emotion represented the use of social media to collectively create, express, and further share (liking/ forwarding) a maelstrom of multiple and continually emerging emotions among protestors (e.g., sadness, helplessness, sarcasm, and shame). It includes the ac tive generation, shaping, and furthering of these emo tions via social media. Solidarity had similarities with people coming together for the common cause in offline social movements (McAdam et al. 1996, Tarrow 1998). However, the latter’s focus is on the (external) cause, whereas solidarity represented individuals bonding with one another (e.g., in-group formation through us versus them) through collective articulation on social media of their own experiences of hurtful societal conditions. Recruitment had some similarity with offline structures for mobilizing protest (Jenkins 1983, McCarthy 1996). However, the latter focused on formal and preexisting social movement organizations that enrolled centrally and institutionally or on individuals’ preexisting networks, whereas recruitment did not involve formal organizations. Rather, it was driven by individuals’ situated social media actions involving Facebook events and groups, tweets, online petitions, and campaigns, which leveraged not just per sonal networks, but nonpersonal, weakly connected, and yet widely dispersed networks. Coordination was somewhat similar to the marshaling of resources in offline movements (Jenkins 1983). However, the latter was about summoning existing resources. In contrast, coordination referred to of<sup>fl</sup>ine and digital protest actions organized on-the-<sup>fl</sup>y. It digitally extended the reach of information about of<sup>fl</sup>ine protest actions (e.g., planned protest events) through social media anchors/ hashtags and likes/shares, disseminated across multiple networks, emergently marshaling more people. Propagation bears similarity with the concept of increasing visibility of of<sup>fl</sup>ine protest events through mass media (Gamson 2004). However, the latter denotes (passive) transmission of content created and disseminated formally through channels such as television and radio stations, whose reach and coverage are broadly predictable, whereas propagation entailed dispersed individuals actively creating (i.e., themselves capturing live protest events), posting, and forwarding (i.e., sharing what others had created) permanent and easily accessible digital content of protest images and videos on social media. Fueled by their outrage, such content had enhanced potency. Moreover, the content was unrestricted (e.g., by formal institutions such as media companies) in its reach and was not predictable in spread; this was possible only because social media was used. Con<sup>fl</sup>ict represented the clash emerging from ongoing microlevel actions of politicians and police (i.e., those in power) that the protestors (i.e., those not in power) viewed as repressive. They widely shared and discussed their view through social media. This is in contrast to of<sup>fl</sup>ine social movements where con<sup>fl</sup>ict, mainly because of grievance associated with macro-level societal factors (e.g., repression and unjust labor conditions), is a trigger for beginning the protest (Tilly 1978, Tarrow 1998).

## Quantitative Temporal Analysis

We next measured each theme quantitatively as described in Table 11 and analyzed it temporally. To make comparisons possible, we standardized each measure. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, for each day, we converted the raw count of each measure into a relative percentage based on the total count of that measure for all days. Table 12 shows the standardized values in percentages of each theme from December 17 to 29, 2012.

A visual inspection of the table shows that the themes can be temporally bracketed (Langley 1999) into different clusters, based on the intensity of each theme over time. We see that the three themes of shared language, collective emotion, and solidarity collectively peaked during December 17 to 19, 2012 (Figure 2), those of recruitment and coordination collectively during December 20 to 22, 2012 (Figure 3), and those of propagation and con<sup>fl</sup>ict collectively during December 23 to 25, 2012 (Figure 4). We cluster the respective sets of themes with similar temporal patterns (Langley 1999) and plot. Each of the three groups of themes forms a cluster of peaks in the respective periods. We also note that all themes waned starting from December 26, 2012, and continued to do so.

Thus, the social protest cycle occurred during the period December 17 to 25, 2012. This is in line with the nature of social protest cycles, wherein tapering off is often associated with institutionalized action (Porta and Diani 2009). We note from Online Appendix A2 that two actions by the Government of India happened on December 26, 2012. The <sup>fi</sup>rst one was the setting up of a judicial inquiry commission to investigate the incident and conditions for safety for women in the state of Delhi. The second was the decision to move the victim to Singapore for advanced medical treatment.<sup>12</sup>

## Intra-Actions of the Social Protest Cycle

We conceptualized the social protest cycle as an assemblage. Its components are social/human and technical archetypes. The former included ordinary citizens, celebrities such as prominent actors and sports people, political leaders, police, journalists, news agencies, and social media users. The latter included social media applications (e.g., Twitter, Facebook, YouTube, blogs)

and other media such as television, radio, and newspapers. Our thematic and temporal analysis revealed key <sup>fi</sup>ndings. First, each of the seven themes represented a practice of social media use, wherein components’ material (e.g., functionalities of social media applications, social network connections on Facebook) and expressive aspects (e.g., textual and video content, online petitions, and campaigns) were entangled and intertwined in an emergent and ongoing way. Second, because of this emergent entangling, in contrast to of<sup>fl</sup>ine social movements where protest action takes place largely in a context of existing structures (e.g., formal organizations) and resources (e.g., their partner organizations and other contacts), we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the structures, resources and collective interests in social media enabled protest action emerged as socially produced through these practices in situ. Third, the practices were clustered into three groups. Each group constituted practices of a distinct type that co-occurred in time. The <sup>fi</sup>rst group of practices represented activity that was consolidating in nature in that it brought people together to identify with the protest, through shared language, collective emotion, and feelings of solidarity. The second represented action that expanded the reach of the social protest by bringing in more people and resources into the fold through recruitment and coordination. The third group represented intensified protest action through the expression of con<sup>fl</sup>ict and the propagation of protest action. That is, practices with the same focus of protest action occurred together.

## Expansion Intra-Action: Mobilization of Protest Action

The expansion intra-action included two practices: recruitment and coordination. Recruitment embodied the urging of people to engage in protest action; it aimed to increase the number of people who identi<sup>fi</sup>ed themselves with the social protest cycle. As individuals supported online protest campaigns, for example, by changing their social media display pictures to a black dot or by signing into Facebook protest events, these actions replicated through their social networks through likes and shares. Facebook events were publicized by participating members to their friends on Facebook through tweets and Facebook posts. Individuals marked interest for attending these events, further increasing their popularity and visibility. Facebook’s functionality highlighted popular Facebook events to more networks of users who were in the social networks of group members, further raising their visibility. All of this aimed to recruit members for the online events. The online petitions, circulated on Twitter and Facebook, enabled protesters to participate by signing them. For example, an online petition for better safety measures for women addressed to the President of India was widely endorsed and publicized on Twitter and Facebook. Tweets that asked people to send emails to the judiciary committee helped in getting more people to take such action. Coordination helped those who wanted to attend physical protest events by providing them the necessary information through Facebook events and tweets.

Like consolidation, in expansion also, the material and expressive aspects shaped one another. For example, the use of the (material) display picture on Facebook to show the culturally potent black dot (expressive) signaled intense allegiance to the protest (expressive). Through such effects, the two practices reinforced one another. For example, using the word “protest” and disseminating information on of<sup>fl</sup>ine protest events (coordination) through the Facebook events functionality signaled these events as online protest events; using the feature of signing into such an event was a signal of participation in the protest (recruitment) that others could see and interpret as a sign of support and respond similarly (recruitment). The expansion intra-action increased the opportunities to protest, either online or physically. It enabled protestors to publicize/organize online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine protest activities, increasing the social protest cycle’s support (i.e., membership) and reach (i.e., geographically). It mobilized the social protest cycle, focusing people and resources on protest actions in response to the incident and to the general condition of safety of women.

## Intensification Intra-Action: Escalation of Protest Action

The intensi<sup>fi</sup>cation intra-action included two practices: propagation and con<sup>fl</sup>ict. Propagation had the effect of spreading the violence associated with the physical protest events (e.g., police <sup>fi</sup>ring water cannons on protestors during clashes, police advancing on unarmed women overcome with emotion) to those beyond the actual participants. Watching the violence helped individuals in faraway places connect viscerally with onsite protesters, even though they were not physically there. Social media users watched, shared, and discussed the events through tweets, Facebook posts, and YouTube uploads, often in real time as they happened. Repeated sharing dramatically increased viewership, helping in temporal and spatial propagation of the content and associated passions. Con<sup>fl</sup>ict with those in positions of power and authority indicated outrage and wrath, particularly at the country’s political leaders. Events that enhanced this sense of outrage (such as a television statement by a leading politician questioning why the victim was out late at night) were widely tweeted about. Individuals expressed anger at the difference between safety conditions for women who were ordinary citizens and those with political or government power. One tweet read “In a country like India no one gets Justice except people who have power. RIP Damini (victim of Delhi Rape Case).” Together these two practices worked to bring about a cleft between ordinary citizens and those in power.

Similarly, in intensi<sup>fi</sup>cation intra-action, the material and expressive shaped one another. For example, the posting (material) of graphic content and imagery (expressive) from the clashes in the protest events made the content accessible and persistent to a vast audience (material). Through such effects the two practices reinforced one another. Posting (propagation) fueled outrage and con<sup>fl</sup>ict through repeated viewing (con<sup>fl</sup>ict) and led to further physical events (con<sup>fl</sup>ict). These subsequent events (con<sup>fl</sup>ict) were in turn widely shared (propagation). The intensi<sup>fi</sup>cation intra-action escalated the physical protest action of the social protest cycle against the perceived apathy of the country’s political leaders both with regard to the speci<sup>fi</sup>c incident and to the general societal conditions for women.

From Figure 5, we see that this intra-action was strongest during the period December 23 to 25, 2012. To calculate the daily strength of the intensi<sup>fi</sup>cation intra-action, we took the average of the values of con-<sup>fl</sup>ict and propagation for each day.

To summarize our analysis of intra-actions, we highlight that (1) each intra-action’s material and expressive aspects shaped each other in a situated and emergent manner and its constituent practices reinforced one another; (2) each intra-action was the dominant one for a different set of days (consolidation (T1): December 17 to 19, 2012; expansion (T2): December 20 to 22, 2012; intensi<sup>fi</sup>cation (T3): December 23 to 25, 2012); and (3) none of the intra-actions were completely absent at any time.

## Intra-Actions as Building Blocks for Protest Action

Drawing together the <sup>fi</sup>ndings described in the previous two sections, we theorize an explanation for the intraactions. At the base level, the social movement (conceptualized as an assemblage) consisted of a vast number of components, such as individuals (e.g., citizens, celebrities), social media applications (e.g., Twitter, YouTube, and Facebook), traditional media (e.g., television), law and order forces (e.g., police), and political leaders (e.g., ministers). Each component had material and expressive aspects. The components came together in the seven practices of social media use wherein the components material (e.g., functionalities of social media applications such as online petition features, forwarding, and liking and social network connections such as friends and groups on Facebook) and expressive (e.g., textual/ graphic and video content and content of online petitions and campaigns) aspects entangled. The practices were therefore not just actions, but rather, actions through which the material and expressive aspects of the components intertwined and shaped one another in an ongoing way. Each intra-action constituted a group of practices having a similar focus of activity and cooccurring temporally. In this way each intra-action enacted agential separability (Barad 2003), through the distinctive focus of its constituent practices. In terms of casual structure, the practices within each intra-action endogenously reinforced one another in a situated manner, strengthening their own common focus.

Each intra-action was performative, bringing to the fore its very self, through the practices that constituted it. For consolidation, for example, the more people used common hashtags for shared language, the more they came together through the different conversations about the incident. The more they expressed similar emotions and solidarity, the more closely bonded they became around the common identity. For expansion, the word “protest” was often used as a pre<sup>fi</sup>x for online Facebook events and groups. By showing their support for such Facebook events, people believed that they were protesting, further increasing their own numbers, signaling their support to members of their social network, and reaching more potential supporters. As more people shared information about online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine events on social media, it increased the possibility of further recruitment and expanding their reach for greater support. For intensi<sup>fi</sup>cation, propagation of live videos and pictures of protest events and expression of con<sup>fl</sup>ict, increased the tendency toward further violent action. As more and more people posted, forwarded, and (re)viewed pictures and videos portraying confrontation between police of<sup>fi</sup>cials and protesters, they participated in onthe-ground protest action. For example, tweets and Facebook posts of closed roads and subway services in New Delhi, meant to prevent people from going to protest events, intensi<sup>fi</sup>ed these very protest actions. In this way, every intra-action constituted both the content (expression) and the action (materiality) of the protest, creating (materializing) what it described (expressed). Thus, the intra-actions both constituted and performed the reality they described (Bourdieu 1991, Barad 2003).

Each intra-action represented a distinct character of the protest action, based on the type of activity of its constituent practices: that of consolidation was to bring people together to identify with the social protest cycle, that of expansion was to mobilize resources and people in support of the protest, and that of intensi<sup>fi</sup>cation was forceful and escalated physical protest action directed against authorities in power. The simultaneous presence of all three intra-actions represented the three corresponding characters the social protest cycle could potentially take. Thus, the intra-actions were the building blocks of the social protest action. Their rise and fall led to the temporal progress of the social protest cycle as we explain next.

## Temporal Transformation of the Social Protest Cycle

Each intra-action being performative could very quickly strengthen its own focus and increase in prominence, fueled by its own performativity. The rise and fall of the strength of the different intra-actions during different time periods created the temporal dynamics of the social protest cycle. During T1, the social media activity related more prominently to the practices for consolidation compared with the practices for the other two intra-actions. The performativity of consolidation was thus dominant. However, even though not as prominent, expansion and intensi<sup>fi</sup>cation were also present, as was their performativity, which had the capacity to make those intra-actions prominent at a subsequent time, endogenously giving rise to temporal dynamics in the social protest cycle. This is shown by the three periods of time during which there was the prominence of consolidation, expansion, and intensi<sup>fi</sup>cation, respectively. The prominent intra-action for each period gave the social protest cycle the character of its own distinct focus during that period. During T1, consolidation gave the social protest cycle the character of identifying with the common cause of protest through collective expression of shame, rage, and solidarity around the incident. During T2, expansion gave it the character of mobilizing people and their efforts by increasing the numbers of people who shared information about and urged others to undertake speci<sup>fi</sup>c protest digital and physical actions. During T3, intensi<sup>fi</sup>cation gave the social protest cycle the hue of intense and escalated physical protest action through repeated sharing of images and videos of violent protest events and expression anger and antagonism toward authorities.

The temporal bubbling up and ebbing of each intraaction produced the dynamism of the social protest cycle. The social protest cycle was thus continually becoming (Barad 2007), produced through the endogenous rise and fall of the intra-actions (DeLanda 2006), which performed (Holland 2013) its behavior over time. The intra-actions therefore also enacted agential separability (Barad 2003) through their temporal prominence, the causal structure for which was their performative strengthening and weakening. As a particular intra-action became prominent, the social protest cycle took on the character of that intra-action, changing its character over the three different periods of time. In conceptualizing the social protest cycle as an assemblage and explaining its dynamic behavior through the rise and fall of the different intra-actions, we develop a process theory (Van de Ven and Poole 2005) of the social protest cycle. We explain what (i.e., the intra-actions, each with a distinct focus of protest action) caused the observed temporal narrative to happen and how (i.e., the relative prominence of each intra-action during a particular period, which gave the social protest cycle the associated distinct characters over time).

## Contributions, Implications, and Conclusions

Our theoretical contributions are to the IS literature that examines the role of social media in collective engagement, particularly social protest action (Young et al. 2019). We set out to theorize the role of social media in a new kind of protest action, namely, social protest cycles. Social media enabled protest cycles are decentralized, leaderless, self-organized, and dynamic and constitute vast populations using social media. The use of social media is entangled within a web of a massive number of different entities. Our theoretical challenge was to deconstruct this entanglement. Through our theorizing, we introduce a number of novel concepts into this literature. We next highlight our study’s contributions to the literature and its implications for policy.

## Contributions to the Literature

Our study yields new theoretical insights regarding the role of social media focused on (1) intra-actions as organizing mechanisms for social media enabled social protest action and (2) relational ontology of social media enabled social protest cycles.

Organizing Practices for Social Media–Enabled Protest Action. Intra-actions constitute groups of practices of social media use that have a similar focus of protest activity and that simultaneously occur and endogenously reinforce one another in emergent ways. Each intra-action enacts agential separability and local regularity (Barad 2003) in two ways: through its common focus and its temporal prominence. With regard to the <sup>fi</sup>rst, consolidation brings people together to identify with the social protest cycle; expansion mobilizes it through increased participation; and intensi<sup>fi</sup>cation focuses it toward con<sup>fl</sup>ict. With regard to the second, the performative rise and fall of these intra-actions represent temporal changes that create swift geographical expansion and <sup>fl</sup>are up of violence and give the social protest cycle its rapidly dynamic character. Through the notion of intra-actions, we suggest that what is salient is not, separately and simply, the social media features or actions of their use or the practices but their complex, situated, and performative coming together from within in the form of the intraactions. The endogenous mutual reinforcement of the practices within each intra-action, together with its performativity, constitute the local causal structures (Barad 2003) within the indivisible assemblage. They speak to novel notions of causality as “indwelling change” (Markus and Rowe 2018, p. 1266), wherein causal trajectories are enacted endogenously within undifferentiated social structures. Intra-actions can thus be seen as the organizing practices that produced (Stewart and Schultze 2019) the social protest cycle.

We suggest that the social media–fueled social protest cycle is endogenously organized and socially produced: both through the individual practices and the intra-actions that constitute them. This contrasts to the of<sup>fl</sup>ine social movement literature that suggests that preexisting identities and structures form the bedrock of social movements (Kavada 2016), an assumption now being critically examined by authors who are suggesting that social media–enabled social protest action has its own novel and distinctive logic and dynamics (Bennett and Segerberg 2012). Our study provides a glimpse of such a logic. In doing so, it responds to a recent call for understanding vital changes in the nature of social protest action because of social media (Young et al. 2019).

The concept of intra-actions and their properties of agential separation and local causal structures, which we tackle in this paper, are novel to the IS literature. To our knowledge, this is the <sup>fi</sup>rst study to examine this concept theoretically and empirically to analyze an IS phenomenon. Scholars investigating collective action may <sup>fi</sup>nd it a helpful concept for deconstructing dynamic phenomena relating to collective engagement where large-scale and distributed use of social media brings seemingly unrelated entities spontaneously together in protest. Scholars of sociomateriality can apply this concept to investigate local patterns (Gaskin et al. 2014) in complex phenomena. Our three intra-actions can be applied to study other types of protest activity that uses social media, such as environmental activism and gender and racial equality movements. They can also be applied to nonprotest activity organized through social media such as disaster response operations, where social media is often used for rapid recruiting of multiple entities (e.g., nonpro<sup>fi</sup>ts, volunteer groups, ground civilians), to work together with aid agencies and government departments implying a rapid buildup of recruitment and coordination activities, similar to the expansion intraaction. New types of intra-actions can inform the study of mass-scale social media enabled phenomena that constitute sudden emergence, rapid <sup>fl</sup>are-ups, or temporal transformations, such as social media–enabled <sup>fi</sup>restorms (Pfeffer et al. 2014), cancel movements,<sup>13</sup> and spread of online content after major incidents such as terrorist attacks.<sup>14</sup>

In focusing on intra-actions, we suggest a more nuanced view of actions such as liking, forwarding, and retweeting, often considered as inert actions with low engagement (George and Leidner 2019). We show that when considered as part of the intra-actions, they can be highly salient because they contribute to that intra-action’s collective focus. For example, in the intensi<sup>fi</sup>cation intra-action, forwarding is both an act of escalation (transmitting vivid pictures and videos of protest action to a large number of people) and its active and emergent content (in terms of what is forwarded, who sees it at what time, and how they react vis-a-vis further forwarding). In this way, the actions\` of protest on social media are the very utterances of protest. Social media are simultaneously both the content of the protest and the channel through which the protest takes place; while acting as a conduit for protest, it creates the very protest it spreads.

Recent IS studies on social movements examine what roles people take on and what features they use (Vaast et al. 2017), explain hegemony and ideological control of social discourse (Miranda et al. 2016), and focus on solidarity (Stewart and Schultze 2019) and digital repertoires of action (Selander and Jarvenpaa 2016). Our study adds a new theoretical dimension to this literature by developing a novel role of social media that highlights entanglement, performativity, and emergence, through the concepts of assemblage and intra-action. We do so by examining a new kind of protest action vis-a-vis the IS literature, namely social\` media–enabled social protest cycles, which exhibit the unique characteristics of rapid dynamism, wide geographical dispersion, social media use by a massive number of different entities, decentralized and not led by a single entity, and possibly violent physical protest action. Our traversal and snowballing across multiple social media applications enabled us to investigate this larger scope of social protest activity. We suggest that future research can further build on the assemblage framing developed in this paper and apply a similar representation to study other social collectives that emerge and self-organize through the simultaneous use of multiple IS, such as, for example, response to large-scale societal disruptions such as natural disasters, medical pandemics, and terrorist attacks (Oh et al. 2013).

Relational Ontology of Social Media–Enabled Social Protest Cycles. We suggest that the potency of the social media enabled protest cycle lies in the relationships of social media with the other entities, manifest through the intra-actions; it is not social media acting alone.

What is salient is the coming together of the material and social components of the assemblage through the intra-actions, suggesting a relational ontology that presumes the social and the material are inseparable (Orlikowski and Scott 2008) and a causal ontology that is “constitutive” (Markus and Rowe 2018, p. 1263).

Furthermore, we note that the relationships are enacted and emerge in situ through the evolving intraactions. Seen in this light, the social media–enabled social protest cycle emerges from, and organizes, itself through the intra-actions (Holland 2013), its dynamics generated from the ongoing materializing of different intra-actions. This creates the possibility that the social protest cycle’s structures, resources, and collective interests/concerns are not <sup>fi</sup>xed. Rather, they emerge endogenously and are <sup>fl</sup>uid. Thus, new structures, resources, and interests may be generated and disbanded as the social protest cycle progresses; their scope cannot be predicted. This particular social protest cycle began in response to the speci<sup>fi</sup>c incident but quickly gathered in its fold the strong underlying public grievance about the general lack of safety of women in the larger society. This, in turn means that the scope of institutional change made possible by the social protest cycle addressed not just the incident (i.e., sentencing of the rapists), but also the larger societal issues (i.e., change in laws relating to the safety of women). Of<sup>fl</sup>ine social protest action, without social media use, is examined from the more static and structuralist perspective of organizationally managed and brokered action (Kavada 2016) and thus does not have such generative properties. We respond to calls in the literature to study how decentralized individuals organize themselves into a collective with a distinct identity with the help of social media, without the formal boundaries of established structures and organizations (Kavada 2016, Young et al. 2019). Such leaderless social media–fueled protests are becoming more and more prominent such as those in response to the death of George Floyd in the United States (in 2020), yet they have been largely under-researched till date.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings raise the rather intriguing question of whether social media–enabled social protest cycles always exist (because each intra-action is always there) in a dormant and germinal state and may surface when the intra-actions that shape them grow strong. For example, although the social movement related to carrying of guns in public places is a long-standing one in the United States, shooting incidents have often been followed by short periods of intense and largescale social media–fueled protest action. Our study provides an initial indication of this idea, which future research can further investigate.

## Implications for Policy

Discourses of social activism among citizens, political leaders, and those in government are now performed through social media across the world. Our study provides practical implications for understanding and possibly intervening in short cycles of intense protest activity that form part of this discourse. Different societal stakeholders (e.g., citizens, citizen groups, and governments) can use the <sup>fi</sup>ndings to glean a realistic understanding of the complex nature of social media–enabled social protest cycles.

Because all the social media use practices relating to all three intra-actions are always ongoing, those associated with societal reform, government, and law and order should be attuned to which type of social media activity increases, following disruptive social events. This can lead to more informed and sensitive response. For example, repeated sharing of con<sup>fl</sup>ict-ridden videos and pictures combined with government apathy perceived by the citizens was crucial to escalating protest actions in our study. Thus, it may be that if there is a high level of social media activity that shares images of con<sup>fl</sup>ict and violence from protest events, engaging and dialoguing with protesters may prevent further escalation. Thus, the creation of new organizational roles in relevant government and public-sector departments, whose responsibility includes analysis of social media activity with a view to engage with citizens, is a potential recommendation. Such a position can be oriented toward facilitation and redress of citizens’ grievances in a timely manner based on social media use data and can potentially help preempt and/or address societal unrest.

Those organizing and taking part in social protest cycles can also <sup>fi</sup>nd our study helpful. First, the intra-actions provide helpful clues regarding actions they could take. For example, if they observe a high incidence of use of social media to organize online events and online petitions, it may be an indication of a move toward greater mobilization. They could boost such a move by increasing efforts on social media to strengthen those activities. On the contrary, if social media activity indicates a weak mobilization character, interventions to increase the creation of online protest events or petitions, and their greater sharing, for example, through citizens of prominence, could be undertaken. Similarly, if there is extensive sharing of content from on the ground protest events, activists could point to the possibility of even more intense protest action, as a point of mediation with those in authority, to encourage dialog. Being alert to the different types of social media activity allows for understanding their temporal prominence and thus for informed and constructive interventions that could progress the social protest cycle.

## Limitations and Conclusion

At this point, we caution the reader about the study’s limitations. The single case examination provided a strong contextual and theoretical anchor; at the same time, it has limited empirical scope. Studies of other social protest cycles will, we hope, complement and further develop our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. We also highlight a related methodological point. The challenge to establishing a corpus of data under <sup>fi</sup>eldwork conditions that are highly distributed and rapidly recon<sup>fi</sup>guring have been documented (Law and Urry 2004). Through our traversal of the digital trace from the different social media, we followed the social protest cycle over its natural temporal scope of occurrence. Although this preserved the natural experiment, it did not allow for a <sup>fi</sup>rst-person and lived-experience account of the social protest cycle, as might be expected from interviews conducted with those who actually participated in it. Our approach builds on recent studies that examine social media use data as an empirical basis for theoretical explanations of collective action (Oh et al. 2013, Miranda et al. 2016, Vaast et al. 2017). Research designs based on interviews with actual social protest participants (Lim 2012, Lindsey 2013, Stewart and Schultze 2019) can provide personal accounts of the participants’ protest actions, which our research design did not allow for.

In conclusion, this paper contributes to the IS literature that studies how digital technologies are transforming collective social protest action, suggesting a complex and intricate role of social media in shaping such action. It shows that the power of social media to shape social protest action lives in its entanglements with the other (and vast number of) different components and is manifest through performative intra-actions. In doing so, the paper introduces novel theoretical concepts that future research can draw from and build on.
