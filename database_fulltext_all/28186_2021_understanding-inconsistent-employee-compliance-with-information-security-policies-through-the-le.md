# Understanding Inconsistent Employee Compliance with Information Security Policies Through the Lens of the Extended Parallel Process Model

Abstract. Organizational information security (ISec) threats have exploded with advances in globalization and technology. Thus, organizations are scrambling to <sup>fi</sup>nd both technical and behavioral approaches to shore up security. Whereas security technologies are crucial to these efforts, they are often rendered useless by employees’ misunderstanding, carelessness, or deliberate disregard of ISec polices (ISPs). Accordingly, organizations are increasingly seeking ways to encourage employees to work as security allies. A key approach in many organizations is encouraging employees to better understand and comply with ISPs. Consequently, ISec research has leveraged several theories to identify the underlying reasons for ISP compliance behaviors among employees. However, most of this research focuses unilaterally on compliance without simultaneously considering noncompliance, as if noncompliance were caused by opposite factors. A pressing need thus exists for a theoretical foundation that can consider both common outcomes and whether there is an explainable tipping point that can explain when a normally compliant employee chooses to become noncompliant, and vice versa. In this study, we contextualize the extended parallel process model (EPPM) to ISP compliance by accounting for dual outcomes of compliance/ noncompliance and dual roles of coping—problem-focused coping and emotion-focused coping. We further extend the EPPM to include response costs and maladaptive rewards to predict the two possible outcomes. Additionally, we employ a weighted discriminant value measurement approach to examine the tipping point between compliance and noncompliance. To test our resulting theoretical model and new measure, we conducted two separate empirical studies with 816 employees, using survey and scenario methodologies. The empirical results from these studies indicate that our contextualization and extension of EPPM better explain the gaps than alternative theories in the ISP literature.

## 1. Introduction

Organizational information security (ISec) concerns have increased in tandem with the continued advancement of globalization and information technology (IT). According to a survey of 3,655 IT and IT-security professionals (Ponemon 2019), although organizations are devoting more money and effort than ever to tightening security, breaches continue to increase in frequency and severity. Security technologies such as authentication techniques, antivirus software, and <sup>fi</sup>rewalls offer some level of protection, but these mechanisms are often undercut by employees’ misunderstanding, carelessness, or deliberate disregard of ISec policies (ISPs).<sup>1</sup> Recent ISec studies have emphasized the critical issue of organizational employees posing a potential yet signi<sup>fi</sup>cant internal threat to security management. Thus, it would be especially bene<sup>fi</sup>cial if employees acted as security allies rather than unwitting aides to perpetrators (Posey et al. 2013, Boss et al. 2015, Johnston et al. 2015).

A common approach to avoiding these pitfalls is training employees to better understand ISPs and encouraging compliance with those policies, as noncompliance continues to undermine organizations (Cram et al. 2017). Even if only a small proportion of employees forget a rule, make an error in judgment, or engage in a shortcut, a major breach can occur. Therefore, it is crucial for organizations to continue to <sup>fi</sup>nd ways to increase ISP understanding and compliance. We assert that another key aspect of solving this problem is better understanding and thwarting noncompliance.

A large body of ISP-compliance research exists (see Online Appendix A for a review). However, most of these studies have followed the oft-unstated assumption that noncompliance is merely the opposite of compliance and have thus focused only on explaining and studying ISP compliance. However, a recent study by Karjalainen et al. (2019) argues that the current literature has misled researchers into concluding that if a speci<sup>fi</sup>c mechanism results in compliance, then the lack of that mechanism must therefore explain noncompliance. They warn that “such symmetric assumptions” do not “explain cases where the reasons for noncomplian[ce] . . . are different from those for complian[ce]” (p. 689). They also suggest that an employee may change from compliance to noncompliance or vice versa because of a change in his/her threat and ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy perceptions when dealing with the same threat using the same protective mechanism under different circumstances. This indicates that there might be a tipping point between compliance and noncompliance.

Because most of the ISP literature has focused on the single outcome of compliance, there is a pressing need for theory and empirical research that can account for both compliance and noncompliance—especially given that both are common in all organizations. Likewise, such a theoretical and empirical account should explain how and why these outcomes are different, identify the point at which a normally compliant employee may suddenly choose to become noncompliant, and determine why such choices are made.

One of the ways organizations can help employees reduce their noncompliant behavior is by helping them improve the security coping skills that can enable them to appraise and deal with security threats and thus avoid noncompliant activities more ef<sup>fi</sup>caciously. This idea is already present in multiple ISec theories, in which coping is often represented by ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy constructs (e.g., self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy and response ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy). However, two recent IS security studies (Wang et al. 2017, Liang et al. 2019) emphasize that coping can take two forms: (1) problem-focused coping (PFC), which involves tackling the problem, described in many studies as an important step toward self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy, and (2) emotion-focused coping (EFC), which involves only suppressing the emotions through avoidance or distraction. Unfortunately, EFC has been mostly ignored in the same literature. To this end, the ISP literature requires a theoretical foundation that can account for dual outcomes of compliance/noncompliance and dual roles of coping—PFC and EFC. Until recently, no single accepted theory in the ISP domain could address both outcomes.

This changed when Boss et al. (2015) introduced the extended parallel process model (EPPM) to the ISec literature and Wang et al. (2017) tested the model to address users’ coping processes when they encounter phishing attacks. Nevertheless, although the EPPM is a dual-outcome model, the central focus of both studies was still on adaptive outcomes, and their target populations were consumers, not employees. The EPPM was designed primarily based on EFC in response to fear, which Wang et al. did not examine. Taking inspiration from this foundation, we thus apply the EPPM to address these opportunities in an ISP-compliance/noncompliance context—speci<sup>fi</sup>cally dealing with dualoutcome valences (i.e., adaptive and maladaptive) and dual forms of coping (i.e., PFC and EFC), by including the role of both PFC and EFC in dealing with the fear generated by fear appeals. These compelling issues and opportunities raise the three fundamental questions that drive our research. The <sup>fi</sup>rst question addresses an extension to the EPPM that speci<sup>fi</sup>cally addresses the context of ISP compliance and noncompliance.

Research Question 1. Can a contextualized extension of the EPPM be used to effectively explain ISP compliance and noncompliance in organizations while accounting for both PFC and EFC?

The second and third research questions introduce and formalize the tipping-point concept, which is an important part of the EPPM. The tipping point is engaged “when perceptions of threat begin to outweigh perceptions of ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy, causing people to shift from danger control to fear control” (Witte et al. 1996, p. 321).

Research Question 2. Can the tipping point between adaptive and maladaptive behaviors be effectively measured?

Research Question 3. Can the tipping point explain why an employee is likely to reach the point of discrimination between compliance and noncompliance?

In addressing these questions, this study is the <sup>fi</sup>rst to systematically describe and explain both danger control (i.e., ISP compliance through PFC) and fear control (i.e., ISP noncompliance through EFC) in an organizational ISP context, when individuals process threats and fears related to ISPs. Furthermore, this is the <sup>fi</sup>rst study to explain and create a novel measure of the paramount role of the tipping point vis-a-vis\` protective and defensive motivation in ISec research. We contextualize the EPPM to ISP compliance by applying its underlying assumptions and considering construct extensions congruous with ISP compliance and noncompliance. We thus further extend the EPPM by considering response costs and maladaptive rewards, because these considerations are unique to and common in this context. To test our resulting theoretical model, we conducted two separate empirical studies with a total of 816 employees, using both a self-report survey and a scenario methodology. The empirical results from these studies indicate that our enhanced version of the EPPM is more parsimonious and better explains the gaps than alternative theories in the ISP literature.

## 2. A Review and Typology of the ISP-Compliance/Noncompliance Literature

To lay the foundation for our original contextualized application of the EPPM to ISP outcomes, we conducted a systematic literature review that yielded a typology that further explains, motivates, and supports our theoretical and empirical decisions. The formal procedures of this review, along with the detailed results, are detailed in Online Appendix A. After applying formal inclusion and exclusion criteria, which are performed using both inductive reasoning (e.g., searching for theories) and deductive reasoning (e.g., using measurement methods or including dependent variables or antecedents), our review yielded 116 quantitative and 6 qualitative papers that directly empirically investigate ISP compliance or noncompliance.

The issues described in the previous section form the foundation of our typology. We categorized the literature along three key dimensions: (1) outcome behavior, which indicates whether the behavioral and/or intention-related dependent variable focused on (a) compliance, (b) noncompliance, or (c) both; (2) outcome valence, which indicates whether the nature of the coping and reasons for outcomes were (a) adaptative, (b) maladaptive, or (c) both, and whether (d) the article considered the tipping point; and (3) coping approach, which indicates whether the study focused on (a) PFC, (b) EFC, or (c) both. Table 1 summarizes our categorization of the 122 papers and the issues.

Our typology con<sup>fi</sup>rms several key points of our research motivation but also reveals some other unexpected gaps and issues in the extant ISP literature. Here, we summarize the three most compelling <sup>fi</sup>ndings that motivate our study, as follows:

1. First, a positive outcome bias exists. The literature has a strong tendency to focus on compliance and adaptive outcomes (intentions and behaviors), while downplaying noncompliance and maladaptive outcomes. Nearly twice as many studies focus on compliance vs. noncompliance, and over twice as many studies focus on adaptive vs. maladaptive outcomes.<sup>2</sup> Notably, this is more than a mere research gap; the problem is the explicit or implicit assumption that the motivations and reasons for noncompliance are merely the opposite of compliance, which is not true (e.g., Hedstrom et al.¨ 2011, Kirlappos et al. 2013, Karjalainen et al. 2019).

2. Second, a cognitive coping bias exists. The literature strongly assumes PFC and largely ignores emotions through EFC, such that the inference is that employees behave fully rationally in respect to ISec compliance. The ratio here is $3 . 5 { : } 1 ; { ^ 3 }$ re<sup>fl</sup>ecting the strong bias (with 3.5 times as many studies including PFC over EFC). The key <sup>fl</sup>aw is that substantial research indicates that employees in security settings operate with bounded, not perfect, rationality, and that researchers therefore must consider emotions, not just cognitions, to understand how employees make security decisions (Willison et al. 2018, Burns et al. 2019, D’Arcy and Lowry 2019).

3. Third, a unidirectional bias exists. Most of the literature considers only one path or one outcome possibility toward positive outcomes with cognitive coping. Two key ratios are striking in this category, at 23.4:1 and 29.5:1, demonstrating that studies only considered compliance or noncompliance 23.4 times as much as they considered both potential outcomes. Also, studies only considered adaptive or maladaptive outcomes 29.5 times as much as they considered both potential outcomes.<sup>4</sup> This re<sup>fl</sup>ects a serious ecological validity <sup>fl</sup>aw in the research, because it does not re<sup>fl</sup>ect the actual way in which employees make information systems security decisions in real work settings (e.g., Lowry et al. 2017). That is, employees deal with trade-offs between (a) ISP compliance and noncompliance, (b) adaptive and maladaptive outcome valences, and (c) cognitive and emotional coping. Recent research indicates that such trade-offs can literally <sup>fl</sup>uctuate daily (D’Arcy and Lowry 2019). This bias likely explains why we found only four papers that consider the tipping point at which an employee shifts from an adaptive valence to a maladaptive one, and are only based on anecdotal evidence. Our contextualized EPPM theorization directly addresses all three biases.

## 3. Theoretical Foundation: The EPPM

The EPPM is a natural <sup>fi</sup>t for our context, because it inherently assumes (a) dual coping routes, (b) dual outcome possibilities, and (c) cognitive and emotional coping. Figure 1 depicts the dual-process nature of the EPPM.

The EPPM is naturally suited to the ISP context, because it explains not only why people accept a persuasive message but also why they might reject it. This assumption thus better re<sup>fl</sup>ects employees’ daily cognitive and emotional <sup>fl</sup>uctuations and the decisions they must make with respect to ISP compliance (e.g., D’Arcy and Lowry 2019). We call these messages fear appeals, because they use a combination of appeals to threat and ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy to generate a fear response that motivates people to change their behaviors in a more adaptive manner (Floyd et al. 2000, Witte and Allen 2000, Fry and Prentice-Dunn 2005, Boss et al. 2015). Unlike other persuasion models, the EPPM makes fear a central concern: the “EPPM explains both successes and failures of fear appeals, and fear is reincorporated as a central variable in the model” (Witte and Allen 2000, p. 594).

The EPPM, originally proposed to remedy the de-<sup>fi</sup>ciencies of protection motivation theory (PMT) and competing theories, was used primarily in the healthcommunication context to investigate issues such as smoking cessation (e.g., Chew et al. 2002), weight loss (e.g., Banas 2008), or AIDS prevention (e.g., Witte 1994). As we expand the EPPM to our context, we demonstrate how it can be used to explain the process of persuading employees to engage in ISP compliance, and why this sometimes inadvertently results in ISP noncompliance.

The EPPM has three core predictions: (1) Perceived efficacy, an aggregated variable of perceived self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy<sup>5</sup> and response ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy,<sup>6</sup> is the major force for danger control and a deterring factor for fear control. (2) Perceived threat, an aggregated variable of perceived threat severity<sup>7</sup> and perceived threat susceptibility,<sup>8</sup> elicits fear, which in turn is a principal component of the EPPM, with two outcomes: protection motivation (aka “danger control”) and defensive motivation (i.e., “fear control”). Finally, (3) the interplay between the danger-control and fear-control processes, re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the discriminant value (computed using perceived ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy and fear), reveals whether the person chooses danger control or fear control. Table 2 details these outcomes.

The EPPM subsumes many of PMT’s constructs and those of competing theories used in an ISP context, like rational choice theory (RCT) and the theory of planned behavior (TPB), but makes some important process changes (Popova 2012). One contribution of the EPPM is that it does not assume that compliance and noncompliance are opposite ends of a single continuum, where the opposite of compliance is the lowest score on a compliance scale. The EPPM also shows that compliance and noncompliance are dependent on two different coping responses—PFC and EFC. As suggested by Wang et al. (2017), people engaged in PFC (aka “task-focused coping”) attempt to address the danger rationally and cognitively by taking a planned set of steps to resolve the problem. Conversely, people engaged in EFC are not focused on solving the root problem, but instead focus on mitigating the fear by altering their emotions and associated responses. The choice between PFC and EFC is related to whether fear or ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy takes a dominant role in the coping response. Therefore, the EPPM was developed to explain both success and failure in threat coping by investigating the interplay of fear control and danger control and making fear more central in the model (Witte 1992, Witte et al. 1996). Next, we detail the three possible EPPM outcomes.

## 3.1. Outcome A: “No Response”—Threat Is Not Perceived or Is Not Personally Relevant

The <sup>fi</sup>rst path results in “no response” (Figure 1, Outcome A), when a person perceives a threat to have little to no severity or susceptibility. For instance, a smoker might decide that a fear appeal lacks personal relevance and does not evoke any threat or fear, and the appeal fails to elicit a coping response. Thus, the EPPM predicts that when fear is not elicited (regardless of high or low ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy), the fear appeal fails (Witte et al. 1996).

In the context of ISP compliance, employees who have no awareness of the company’s ISPs might believe that they are solely the responsibility of IT professionals, might have no real awareness of security threats to their organization, or might simply be apathetic (e.g., Boss et al. 2009). Likewise, ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy in dealing with ISPs and security threats is irrelevant in this hypothetical instance, because ISPs have no personal relevance to these employees. In this path, employees show no change in compliance/noncompliance intentions or behaviors.

## 3.2. Outcome B: “Danger Control”—An Employee Responds Adaptively to Threats

Danger control occurs when people perceive a serious and relevant threat, harbor fear about the threat, and experience high enough ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy to address the threat. This outcome thus represents a desirable balance of PFC and EFC in the coping process. EFC is evoked by a persuasive fear-appeal message that catches employees’ attention and acceptance. This personally relevant threat and the associated fear further trigger PFC, causing an internal coping response in which people explore whether they can deal with the magnitude of the threat. Their ability to handle a threat is expressed in terms of ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy. In that case, they practice adaptive behavior, because they believe that they can adopt a countermeasure and mitigate the threat. It thus involves an adaptive response that attempts to cope directly with the danger and lessen its effect (Leventhal 1970, de Hoog et al. 2007).

For ISP compliance, this could be an employee’s recognition that not complying with the ISPs can harm their work performance, their career, and their organization, because doing so potentially unleashes security risks. Assuming a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant level of organizational commitment, if EFC is to be triggered, then these security risks must be perceived by employees as personally relevant, and those employees should receive appropriate training toward that end (e.g., Posey et al. 2015). When such employees encounter a meaningful level of ISP-related threat at work, they then internally weigh their ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy to determine whether they can comply with ISP requirements to thwart the security threat. If they have the requisite ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy, gathered through training and paying attention to ISPs, then they will invoke PFC that overtakes EFC and thus have the con<sup>fi</sup>dence to follow through and comply with various ISP requirements. For instance, they will become more likely to recognize and report suspicious emails to IT, encrypt attachments, recognize attempts to use social engineering to deceive them, use and remember more complex passwords, report suspicious coworker computer behavior, make use of a VPN, log off when leaving one’s workstation, and pay attention and respond to system or browser warning messages.

## 3.3. Outcome C: “Fear Control”—An Employee Responds Maladaptively to Threats

Fear control occurs when individuals perceive a serious and relevant threat and thus fear the threat, but, because they lack suf<sup>fi</sup>cient ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy, they are not convinced they can remove or alleviate it (Leventhal 1970, Witte et al. 1996). High fear is built upon strong feelings of personal relevance and anxiety, whereas low perceived ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy intensi<sup>fi</sup>es the anxiety of being unable to address the threat. This outcome thus represents an undesirable imbalance of coping such that usually much more focus is placed on EFC, via fear, than PFC, causing fear to overwhelm the employee. Fear control involves message rejection and maladaptive behaviors that reduce the unpleasant feelings evoked by the message but not the actual threat.

Although this maladaptive path is just as important to consider as Paths A and B, it is neglected in the ISPcompliance literature. The maladaptive path is triggered when the fear-appeal message does not work as intended and even back<sup>fi</sup>res, which occurs when people register personally relevant threats but do not perceive that they have the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy (i.e., coping skills) to deal with them. Thus, rather than taking an adaptive response and resolving the threat directly, they take a defensive posture, in which they avoid/ignore the threat, or even react negatively—all to cope in a maladaptive way with the fear and not address the root problem or threat.

For ISP compliance, a maladaptive negative reaction would be to lash out in multiple ways when EFC is evoked. A common example is when employees know about the severity and vulnerability of security threats—often through sensational news stories—but in fact have no training or ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy in responding to these threats. Thus, they feel overwhelmed and ignore the threat. For instance, employees may have attended training that raised their awareness, but they came away confused, stressed, or annoyed, because they did not understand the highly technical material and were too embarrassed to ask questions (a common issue for nontechnical employees). A more serious reaction can occur when an employee views noncompliance as an excellent opportunity to “get back” at their boss or company for perceived mistreatment. Such an employee’s reaction could result from feelings of being treated unfairly, micromanaged, overly restricted, overworked, and underappreciated (e.g., Lowry and Moody 2015, Lowry et al. 2015).

## 3.4. Predicting the Tipping Point Between Outcomes B and C Using the Discriminant Value

Levels of perceived threat and perceived ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy work together to determine the fear-control and danger-control processes in the EPPM. Perceived threat can trigger the fear-control/EFC process unless the level of ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy is high enough to trigger the danger-control/PFC process. The extent of the response elicited by perceived threat or by perceived ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy determines the tipping point, which identi<sup>fi</sup>es which control process is dominant, and the success or failure of threat coping (Witte and Allen 2000). Based on these arguments, the tipping point is formally de<sup>fi</sup>ned using a simple, practical formula to compute the discriminant value (see the formula in Equation (1)) (Witte 1992, Witte et al. 1996).

$$
\begin{array}{c} \text {Discriminant value = (z score of perceived efficacy} \\ - z \text {score of perceived threat).} \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

The rule of the discriminant value states that when the computed value is positive, the perceptions of ef<sup>fi</sup>- cacy outweigh those of threat, and danger control becomes dominant; when the value is negative, it indicates that the perceptions of ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy are outweighed by perceptions of threat, and fear control will be dominant. The tipping point occurs when the discriminant value turns from a positive value to a negative value, indicating that an individual is turning from danger control to fear control. But we propose an enhanced formula, as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{c} \text {Discriminant value} = (\beta_ {1} Z \text {score of perceived efficacy} \\ - \beta_ {2} Z \text {score of fear}), \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

where $\beta _ { 1 }$ and $\beta _ { 2 }$ are obtained from two simple regression lines:

Protection motivation <sub>-</sub> β<sub>1 ×</sub> perceived ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy, and Protection motivation $\displaystyle = { \beta _ { 2 } }$ fear.

These weights are empirically determined from the data set. Like Equation (1), Equation (2) emphasizes the role of fear in the threat-appraisal process, but Equation (2) additionally considers the different weights of the two factors in determining which process will dominate. We argue that the enhanced formula improves the discrimination value, because of the EPPM’s underlying assumption that fear can exist only if a relevant threat is perceived and, crucially, because it accounts for the protective motivation itself, the desired outcome.

## 4. Further Contextualizing the EPPM to the ISP-Compliance/ Noncompliance Context

We further recontextualize and expand the EPPM to better <sup>fi</sup>t the ISP-compliance/noncompliance context. In doing so, we draw on the ISP literature to properly propose, de<sup>fi</sup>ne, and operationalize the appropriate corresponding measures, resulting in testable hypotheses. Tables A.4A and A.4B in Online Appendix A summarize all the extant ISP-compliance/noncompliance studies published in journals. Table A.5 cross-references the major constructs found in multiple theories in the ISP context and de<sup>fi</sup>nes them with respect to the EPPM.

Given this foundation, the <sup>fi</sup>rst key conceptual distinction we make for our context is to formally explicate what we mean by “protection motivation” (or an adaptive response) and “defensive motivation” (or a maladaptive response)—the two outcomes we predict. This distinction derives from the intended goal in our context: ISP compliance. Thus, in our context, the equivalent of protection motivation is ISP-compliance intentions, which we de<sup>fi</sup>ne as an employee’s intentions to comply with their organization’s ISPs. Several authors have studied compliance intentions by that approximate name,<sup>9</sup> as shown in Tables A.4A and A.4B.

Maladaptive responses are less commonly studied and are more diversely represented (e.g., avoidance, willful noncompliance, and reactance). However, the primary focus of these constructs is on negative intentions and avoidance. We thus de<sup>fi</sup>ne ISP-noncompliance intentions as an employee’s maladaptive intentions not to comply with their organization’s ISPs, rather than simply as the lack of compliance intentions. This is also known in the ISec literature as “negative behavioral intent” (Bulgurcu et al. 2010, Hu et al. 2011), among other related constructs.<sup>10</sup> Another related maladaptive outcome is ISP threat avoidance, which is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as employees mentally avoiding or disregarding potential threats that could occur if they do not comply with their organization’s ISPs (e.g., Goo et al. 2014). On this basis, every other concept in the EPPM is recontextualized to address ISP compliance/noncompliance. To do so, we <sup>fi</sup>rst recontextualize the fear-control process.

## 4.1. Recontextualizing the Fear-Control Process for ISP Compliance/Noncompliance

In the EPPM, both threat and fear serve to increase ISPcompliance intentions. However, the concepts of threat and fear are not synonymous. Perceived threat is a person’s aggregated (i.e., second-order construct) perceptions about the susceptibility and severity of the threat (Witte 1992, 1994; Witte and Allen 2000). In our context, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne the corresponding construct of ISP-noncompliance threat as an employee’s overall perceived threat to themselves for intentionally violating their organization’s ISPs, as further detailed through the subconstructs of ISP-noncompliance threat severity and threat vulnerability. Correspondingly, ISP-noncompliance threat vulnerability is an employee’s belief that purposeful violations of their organization’s ISPs by any entity increase their vulnerability<sup>11</sup> and their organization’s vulnerability to harm.<sup>12</sup> ISP-noncompliance threat severity is an employee’s belief that their intentional violations of their organization’s ISPs will result in severe negative consequences to themselves and their organization.

The EPPM predicts a direct relationship between personally relevant threat perceptions and fear (Witte 1992, 1994; Witte and Allen 2000).<sup>13</sup> Fear is a negatively valenced emotion representing a response that arises from a threat, which is a perceived danger (Witte 1992, Witte et al. 1996, LaTour and Rotfeld 1997). In our context, we thus de<sup>fi</sup>ne the corresponding fear construct as fear of ISP-noncompliance threat, which is an employee’s emotional fear derived from potential security threats resulting from violating their organization’s ISPs (Posey et al. 2015, Xu et al. 2020). Recent ISec studies suggest that a successful threat-appraisal process helps employees understand that noncompliance with ISPs is a security threat that exposes their organization to potential security attacks. As a result, employees would fear the devastating consequences of noncompliance threats, such as password theft, phishing attacks, and data breaches, and thus would be motivated to comply with ISPs (Posey et al. 2015, Johnston et al. 2019). Thus, if our recontextualizations of the EPPM to ISP compliance/noncompliance hold, then, in terms of a successful threat-appraisal process, the following should occur.

Hypothesis 1 (H1) (Predicting Threat Appraisal). A high level of an employee’s perceived ISP-noncompliance threat is associated with a high level of fear of ISP-noncompliance threat.

In the EPPM, fear is a double-edged sword. An EPPM meta-analysis found that “the stronger the fear aroused by a fear appeal, the more persuasive it is” (Witte and Allen 2000, p. 601). Its direction of in<sup>fl</sup>uence is determined by ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy. The EPPM argues that fear plays a strong role in maladaptive responses when individuals’ ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy is low (Witte 1992, Witte et al. 1996). High fear, along with low ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy, can cause EFC that attempts to deal with threat-induced stress and negative emotions (Wang et al. 2017), and, as a result, a person can adopt maladaptive coping behaviors, such as withdrawal, avoidance, wistfulness, and hopelessness (Leventhal 1970, Lazarus 1993). In our context, ISP-compliance efficacy, like threat, is a second-order construct of employees’ overall ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy to comply with their companies’ ISPs, as further detailed through the subconstructs of ISP-compliance self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy and response ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy. Correspondingly, ISP-compliance self-efficacy is an employee’s belief in their ability to successfully understand, execute, and comply with their organization’s ISPs. ISP-compliance response efficacy is an employee’s belief that if they follow their organization’s ISPs, then they will make a difference in protecting their organization from security threats. ISP-compliance ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy enables employees to overcome fear and reduce its role in maladaptive outcomes (Wang et al. 2017).

We focus on the two most common maladaptive outcomes in the EPPM and coping literature: noncompliance and avoidance (as de<sup>fi</sup>ned earlier in this section). Whereas noncompliance can be intentional or accidental (Guo et al. 2011), avoidance is more nuanced; it occurs when someone makes a simple effort to avoid dealing with a threat or stressor (Zeidner and Endler 1995, p. 514).<sup>14</sup> Both noncompliance and avoidance can occur for several maladaptive reasons and thus are likely maladaptive outcomes in an ISP-compliance context, because a great deal of ISP noncompliance is unintentional and caused by employees being too busy, too stressed, or poorly trained; it can also be an intentionally malicious or microaggressive response performed to “get back” at an organization through moral disengagement (D’Arcy et al. 2014) or procedural justice/fairness (Lowry et al. 2015).

In practice, employees often face multiple rationales, competing goals and roles, and complex security situations in organizations (Hedstrom et al.¨ 2011, 2013; Kolkowska and Dhillon 2013; Kolkowska et al. 2017; Karjalainen et al. 2019). The value-based compliance model proposed by Hedstrom et al. ( ¨ 2011) suggests that, although organizations prescribe rationalities, goals, and values for employees concerning ISP compliance, employees act based on their own rationalities, goals, and values when complying or not complying with ISPs. In this sense, studies have con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that, for ISP compliance, a self-regulatory approach relying on shared security values and beliefs is more effective than a command-and-control approach (Li et al. 2014). When employees struggle with competing goals (sometimes caused by role con<sup>fl</sup>ict introduced by different management systems) and values, they are more likely to appeal to problematic emotions and react with noncompliance (Karjalainen et al. 2019). Consequently, they may downplay the value of the prescribed ISP, resulting in a perception of low response ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy and more noncompliance.

To summarize, if an employee experiences the fearappraisal process with low ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy, then the employee will more likely undertake the maladaptive fear-control process (i.e., EFC). Such an employee is more likely to be overwhelmed by negative loss emotions (e.g., anger, frustration, hopelessness) and avoidance emotions (e.g., anxiety about the negative consequence of security threats) (Burns et al. 2019, Xu et al. 2020). If so, then the following comparisons should hold.

Hypothesis 2 (H2) (Predicting Fear-Control Response). The combination of low employee ISP-compliance efficacy and high employee fear of ISP-noncompliance threat is associated with (a) low levels of ISP-compliance intentions, (b) high levels of ISP threat avoidance, and (c) high levels of noncompliance intentions.

## 4.2. Recontextualizing the Danger-Control

Process for ISP Compliance/Noncompliance In contrast to H2, if ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy is high and threat is strong enough to be personally relevant, generating moderate fear, a person tends to engage in danger control (Witte 1992, Witte et al. 1996). The EPPM suggests that a person’s cognitions about the countermeasure to the threat are the primary processes in danger control and the primary causes for accepting the message and forming the recommended behaviors. When a person’s ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy perception is high, they will respond to the threat and intentionally and cognitively deal with the danger (Witte 1992, Witte et al. 1996). The links between threat and protection motivation (ISP compliance in our context) and between securityresponse ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy (ISP-compliance ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy in our context) and security protection have long been supported in ISec contexts (Gurung et al. 2009, Johnston and Warkentin 2010b, Liang and Xue 2010, Posey et al. 2011b, Jenkins et al. 2014, Johnston et al. 2015). Our literature review further shows that high ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy (either self-ef<sup>fi</sup>- cacy, response ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy, or both) contributes to increasing ISP compliance and decreasing ISP noncompliance. Nearly one-quarter of the studies presented in Tables A.4A and A.4B report such links. Recent ISec fear-appeals research has con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that high-threat and high-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy fear appeals inspire a higher level of fear and protection motivation than would weak or absent fear appeals (Boss et al. 2015). ISec research also documents the <sup>fi</sup>ndings that high perceived ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy resulting from security training programs leads to improved ISP compliance (D’Arcy et al. 2009, Puhakainen and Siponen 2010, Silic and Lowry 2020). In summary, the EPPM argues that high ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy perceptions lead to more deliberative coping behaviors, which control danger, and to fewer emotion-oriented behaviors, which control fear (Witte 1992, Witte et al. 1996). Thus, we have the following.

Hypothesis 3 (H3) (Predicting Efficacy Appraisal). High levels of employee ISP-compliance efficacy are associated with (a) high levels of ISP-compliance intentions, (b) low levels of ISP threat avoidance, and (c) low levels of ISPnoncompliance intentions.

Cognitions in danger control are complex. The EPPM posits that people are willing to intentionally and actively seek and take recommended countermeasures only when fear is evoked and they feel the threat has a relatively high level of relevance and signi<sup>fi</sup>cance. Meta-analysis research in healthcare reveals that high levels of ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy and strong fear appeals are needed to produce a high level of message acceptance and adaptive responses (Witte and Allen 2000). Similarly, recent ISec research has pointed out that employees with properly developed fear via the appeal rhetoric in security messages show high ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy and consequently engage more in danger control (Johnston et al. 2019) and that cognitions and emotions play an interactive role in employees’ ISP-compliance/noncompliance behaviors (Karjalainen et al. 2019). Recent ISec PMT research also recommends a strong fearappeal manipulation in conjunction with high ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy to increase protective motivations and behaviors (Boss et al. 2015, Johnston et al. 2015, Chen and Zahedi 2016). Thus, if this holds in our context, then if an employee experiences the coping-appraisal process with high ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy, the employee will undertake the adaptive danger-control process (i.e., PFC).

Hypothesis 4 (H4) (Predicting Danger-Control Response). The combination of high ISP-compliance efficacy and moderate-to-high fear of ISP-noncompliance threat is associated with an employee’s (a) high intentions to comply with ISP, (b) low ISP threat avoidance, and (c) low noncompliance intentions.

## 4.3. Using the Discriminant Value to Predict the

Tipping Point of Compliance/Noncompliance A fear appeal can trigger both cognitive and emotional thoughts and reactions, depending on whether fear or ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy dominates. The EPPM argues that the tipping point where danger control (i.e., PFC) becomes dominant over fear control (i.e., EFC) occurs when perceptions of coping ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy are greater than perceptions of threat and fear. If the discriminant value between perceived ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy and fear remains positive, then people in general will believe that they can address the threat and will thus be willing to attempt to deter it (Witte 1992, Witte et al. 1996). When the discriminant value turns negative, fear control dominates where people are overwhelmed by fear and attempt to control it via maladaptive responses (EFC) (Witte 1992, Witte et al. 1996).

Although most ISec research has missed the opportunity to investigate a possible tipping point at which employees change from ISP compliance to ISP noncompliance or vice versa, a few key studies offer some support for this concept. Hedstrom et al. ( ¨ 2011) argue that value con<sup>fl</sup>icts can lead to different compliance behaviors; Karjalainen et al. (2019) and Karjalainen et al. (2020) point out that employees change their compliance behaviors in response to changes in security situations and compliance circumstances; likewise, from a situational action perspective, Li et al. (2021) con<sup>fi</sup>rm that ISP compliance is situational per se and that certain contextual factors affect compliance; Kolkowska and Dhillon (2013) discuss the effects of organizational power on compliance and noncompliance behaviors. Both EPPM and ISec research allow us to use theory to predict the tipping point at which an employee is more likely to comply or not comply with their organization’s ISP—the dividing line between adaptive and maladaptive responses.

Hypothesis 5 (H5) (Predicting the Tipping Point). An employee with a high positive discriminant value will more likely engage in danger control, as associated with (a) high levels of ISP-compliance intentions, (b) low levels of ISP threat avoidance, and (c) low levels of ISP-noncompliance intentions; an employee with a low positive discriminant value will more likely engage in fear control, yielding the opposite results.

## 4.4. Enhancing the EPPM with Additional Maladaptive ISP-Compliance Concepts

The EPPM is virtually unused in the ISec literature, with the exception of Wang et al. (2017), whereas several other theories, including TRA, TPB, PMT, RCT, and DT, have been widely used in ISec research (for details, see Online Appendix A). Yet, compared with these theories, the EPPM is more strongly predictive of EFC, PFC, and maladaptive outcomes. Nevertheless, one of the paradoxes exhibited by the literature is that despite the emphasis of previous theories (e.g., PMT and TPB) on adaptive outcomes, several studies (e.g., Herath and Rao 2009, Posey et al. 2015, Johnston et al. 2019) have adopted these theories by integrating constructs involving maladaptive processing—namely, response costs and maladaptive rewards. However, rather than using these two constructs to predict a maladaptive outcome (e.g., avoidance or noncompliance),<sup>15</sup> they have used them as negative predictors of adaptive outcomes (i.e., compliance intentions and behaviors). This points back to our opening motivation: addressing the assumption in previous research that the same mechanism used to ensure compliance can be used dichotomously to thwart noncompliance (Karjalainen et al. 2019).

However, it is perhaps more paradoxical that the creators of the EPPM omitted response costs and maladaptive rewards, even though the EPPM speci<sup>fi</sup>cally includes dual maladaptive outcomes. Early on, Witte (1992) speculated that maladaptive rewards and response costs were partially responsible for some of PMT’s inconsistent empirical results, but this early assumption is generally treated as a “given” by EPPM researchers, rather than tested or challenged. This is also the case in the ISec context. The prior EPPM-based study (Wang et al. 2017) omitted these two factors. We thus posit that the EPPM in an ISec context would be improved in terms of predictive ability—especially for maladaptive outcomes—if these two PMT constructs were used to enhance the EPPM. These enhancements are thus the focus of our <sup>fi</sup>nal two hypotheses.

Maladaptive rewards is a second-order construct of the intrinsic and extrinsic personal motivators for not engaging in a desired adaptive behavior (Rogers 1983, Floyd et al. 2000). In our context, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne the corresponding second-order construct of maladaptive rewards for ISP noncompliance as an employee’s overall perception that their purposeful violations of their organization’s ISPs will provide personal bene<sup>fi</sup>ts/rewards, as further detailed through subconstructs of maladaptive intrinsic and extrinsic rewards for ISP noncompliance (Posey et al. 2014, Posey et al. 2015).

Extrinsic maladaptive rewards are extrinsic rewards for engaging in the maladaptive response of not protecting oneself (Rogers 1983, Floyd et al. 2000), such as saving money or time. Maladaptive extrinsic rewards for ISP noncompliance include similar concepts, especially saving time and/or accomplishing more tasks, and thus getting ahead at work. This is particularly common when ISP compliance is perceived from an employee’s perspective as nothing but a cost or hindrance to what they perceive they are paid to do; for example, employees may have competing rationales or tradeoffs involving being ef<sup>fi</sup>cient and being secure (Hedstrom et al.¨ 2011, Kirlappos et al. 2013). Worse, employees may see security not as their problem but “IT’s problem” (Siponen and Vance 2010, Yazdanmehr and Wang 2016).

Intrinsic maladaptive rewards are intrinsic rewards for engaging in the maladaptive response of not protecting oneself (Rogers 1983, Floyd et al. 2000), such as maintaining pleasure or malicious noncompliance. Maladaptive intrinsic rewards for ISP noncompliance include similar concepts, but the underlying construct is more likely to be about malicious noncompliance, getting back at one’s boss or the company. We posit that maladaptive rewards apply in an ISec setting, because, aside from negative reactance or noncompliance, employees typically have many roles and responsibilities, and thus not adhering to ISPs can often be a means of being more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient in their work (Lowry and Moody 2015, Lowry et al. 2015)—particularly if they are feeling overwhelmed, struggling with value con<sup>fl</sup>icts between security and productivity, suffering role con<sup>fl</sup>ict with multiple bosses, or experiencing low morale (Hedstrom et al.¨ 2011, Karjalainen et al. 2019). Importantly, maladaptive rewards negatively in<sup>fl</sup>uence the fear-control/EFC and danger-control/PFC processes, because people are incentivized to overlook the threat and not take protective actions. Although ISec PMT research has largely omitted maladaptive rewards, two PMT ISec studies show that they can be meaningful in this setting (Boss et al. 2015, Posey et al. 2015). If this holds with our proposed EPPM expansion, then we have the following.

Hypothesis 6a (H6a). An increase in an employee’s perceived maladaptive rewards for ISP noncompliance is associated with a decrease in adaptive behaviors, resulting in a decrease in ISP-compliance intentions.

Hypothesis 6b–c (H6b–c). An increase in an employee’s perceived maladaptive rewards for ISP noncompliance is associated with an increase in maladaptive behaviors, resulting in an increase in (b) ISP threat avoidance and (c) ISPnoncompliance intentions.

Finally, we found no evidence that excluding response costs from the EPPM has been empirically validated as a theoretical improvement, especially for maladaptive outcomes, which are naturally aligned with response costs (see Online Appendix A). Using the response costs construct is a core theoretical feature in several ISP-compliance studies using theories such as RCT (e.g., Bulgurcu et al. 2010, Hu et al. 2011, D’Arcy and Lowry 2019), RT (e.g., Lowry and Moody 2015, Lowry et al. 2015), and FT (e.g., Lowry et al. 2015, Son and Park 2016). Response costs “are any costs (e.g., monetary, personal, time, effort) associated with taking the adaptive coping response” (Floyd et al. 2000, p. 411). These correspond to ISP-compliance costs, which are employees’ beliefs that ISP compliance will be burdensome, time-consuming, or costly.

We assert that these costs are often parallel to maladaptive rewards; for example, if it takes someone a great deal of time to engage in a security action related to their work, then this increases not only their perceived response cost but also the extrinsic maladaptive rewards for skipping the security task so they can complete their work. As response costs increase, employees tend to struggle with competing interests and goals involving task completion, on one hand, and security and productivity, on the other (Hedstrom et al.¨ 2011, Karjalainen et al. 2019).

In an ISec context, studies have shown that response costs are consistently useful in thwarting protection motivation (e.g., Herath and Rao 2009, I<sup>fi</sup>nedo 2012, Vance et al. 2012, Boss et al. 2015, Posey et al. 2015), although most ISec studies have inexplicably ignored response costs as well as maladaptive rewards (see Tables A.4A and A.4B). Separately, other ISec research using reactance and fairness theories has shown that onerous or freedom-threatening security policies can result in negative pushback, such as reactance (Lowry and Moody 2015, Lowry et al. 2015). Thus, there could be a link between response costs and maladaptive responses when response costs are higher for employees. From the cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t perspective, ISP-compliance costs and ISP-compliance ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy beliefs may be two parallel aspects of the danger-control process. We thus believe that the use of ISP-compliance costs is a fruitful expansion of the EPPM in our context, leading us to predict the following.

Hypothesis 7a (H7a). An increase in an employee’s perceived ISP-compliance costs is associated with a decrease in adaptive behaviors, resulting in a decrease in ISP-compliance intentions.

Hypothesis 7b–c (H7b–c). An increase in an employee’s perceived ISP-compliance costs is associated with an increase in maladaptive behaviors, resulting in an increase in (b) ISP threat avoidance, and (c) ISP-noncompliance intentions.

## 5. Methodologies for Two Studies

A central objective of testing a new theory and new measurement (e.g., our measure of discriminant value) is yielding results that are suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently generalizable that researchers can conclude that the theory and measurement are potentially ef<sup>fi</sup>cacious and useful. We thus tested our contextualization of the EPPM to ISPs using our proposed approach to calculating discriminant values and two empirical studies: (1) a selfreport survey and (2) a scenario-based study. It was important to examine these differences, because several behavioral security researchers have claimed that hypothetical vignettes are superior to self-report surveys in organizational security compliance and noncompliance contexts (e.g., Siponen and Vance 2010, Hu et al. 2011). ## 5.1. Measurement for the Two Studies

To test the generalizability of the model, we used a mix of developed measures and scales from the literature, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally to measure fear, threat, ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy, and associated adaptive and maladaptive intentions. Recent ISec research (Karlsson et al. 2017, Karjalainen et al. 2019) has suggested that it is important to measure ISP compliance/noncompliance in different contexts and scenarios. We used standardized data in all the analyses to facilitate our comparison of the studies. All the procedures and measures used for the two studies are documented in Online Appendix B. Both studies were preceded by pilot tests and expert feedback.

## 5.2. Participants in the Two Studies

Participants in Studies 1 and 2 were carefully selected with the help of a market research <sup>fi</sup>rm that targeted the sample frames for each study. In behavioral research <sup>fi</sup>elds, external panels are an effective method for eliciting responses to survey instruments (e.g., Bennett and Robinson 2000, Awad and Ragowsky 2008, Gibney et al. 2009, Posey et al. 2010), as long as they are conducted with the aim of enhancing data quality (Lowry et al. 2016a), on which we strongly focused. This approach also offers three advantages. First, panels guarantee anonymity, a critical element in eliciting honest responses to questions about behaviors potentially in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by social desirability bias (Bennett and Robinson 2000). Second, panels allow a broader spectrum of industries and positions to be included than is feasible through traditional sampling methods. Finally, panels allow a more random selection of participants while enhancing the ability to require certain demographic characteristics.

## 6. Analyses and Results

Before conducting our <sup>fi</sup>nal hypothesis testing, we conducted multiple preanalyses to ensure factorial validity and data quality,<sup>16</sup> lack of common method bias (CMB),<sup>17</sup> and lack of multicollinearity.<sup>18</sup> We then tested our hypotheses using a combination of analysis of variance (ANOVA) and covariance-based structural equation modeling (CB-SEM).

## 6.1. Testing H1–H5 with Latent-Construct-Based ANOVA

We did not use the traditional means approach to AN-OVA, which involves averaging all items equally, whether they load signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly or not. Instead, using STATA’s CB-SEM capabilities, we created a full model of each data set and then created the latent construct scores based on the latent loadings, just as would be done with SEM. We then used these latent scores, based on standardized data, to test our hypotheses using AN-OVA and calculated the discriminant value (Bollen and Lennox 1991, Henseler and Chin 2010). This approach allowed us to correct for error more accurately than would have been possible with traditional ANOVA with unweighted means. ## 6.4. Validation of Our Proposed Critical Tipping-Point Value Formula

We conducted discriminant analyses to validate our classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of respondents into the danger-control and fear-control groups based on whether the discriminant value is greater than zero or less than or equal to zero, so that we could test our enhanced formula (although this has not yet been used in an IS context). We created a dummy variable called group membership for the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation outcome. The analyses used the two dependent variables, ISP compliance and avoidance, as predictors, and group membership as the grouping variable. Signi<sup>fi</sup>cant mean differences were observed for all predictors on the grouping variable. The discriminant function revealed a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant association between groups and the two predictors.

We then estimated the adaptive and maladaptive loadings. All their absolute values were greater than the cutoff of 0.3, indicating an absence of poor predictors (Hair Jr. et al. 2006). We further estimated the prediction accuracy of the two predictors on the grouping variance. ## 7. Discussion

Our study was driven by the opportunity to propose and test an enhanced EPPM model in an ISP context that can account for (1) both adaptive and maladaptive outcomes and (2) both PFC and EFC. Our model also accounts for two maladaptive factors commonly found in the ISP-compliance literature but not considered in the EPPM: response costs and maladaptive rewards. Of the several theories used previously in this context, none are suitable for these purposes. We thus leverage and recontextualize the EPPM, which was partially proposed to address such gaps in the greater persuasion literature (Witte 1992, 1994) and yet has been overlooked in the IS literature until recently (Wang et al. 2017). A key irony here is that the EPPM is also purported to deal better with a key EFC construct already used in the ISec literature—that of fear as generated from fear appeals—but with some degree of controversy (Boss et al. 2015, Warkentin et al. 2016).

We conducted two empirical studies of 816 employees to test our proposed model and the measure. We speci<sup>fi</sup>cally chose the two leading methodologies in this area—surveys and vignettes—to further test the generalizability of the model and the measure. Study 1 involved a survey of participants based on their previous training, experiences, and current knowledge of ISPs in their organizations; Study 2 employed a vignette/scenario approach to examine participants’ self-reported perceptions and protective motivation.

First, we found that, in this ISP context, the following hypotheses are supported: Higher threat is associated with higher fear in employees (H1 supported). We also found broad support for the application of ef-<sup>fi</sup>cacy appraisal (i.e., coping response) in this context. When employees appraised their ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy, higher levels of ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy were associated with higher levels of ISP compliance (H3a supported) and lower levels of ISP noncompliance (H3c supported). The support for higher ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy was mixed, however, in its associated expected relationship with low levels of avoidance (H3b supported in Study 1 but not Study 2). Likewise, we found similar support for the danger-control process in our context. That is, when employees had high levels of both ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy and fear, they had much higher levels of ISP-compliance intentions (H4a supported) and much lower levels of avoidance (H4b supported). Although these same employees also had lower levels of noncompliance in Study 2, the opposite was true in Study 1 (H4c had mixed support).

In terms of our proposed extensions to the EPPM, we found that increased maladaptive rewards were indeed negatively related to ISP compliance and positively related to ISP threat avoidance and ISP-noncompliance intentions (H6a–c supported). Increased response costs were associated with increased ISP threat avoidance and ISP-noncompliance intentions (H7b–c supported). Interestingly, ISP-compliance costs were not associated with decreased ISP-compliance intentions (H7a not supported). Turning from our predicted and expected <sup>fi</sup>ndings, the results were not as expected with the fear-control response, as we further explain in the contributions section. In short, these results were mostly the opposite of what we expected. Again, a fear-control response should be invoked when an employee’s ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy is low and his or her fear is high, causing an overwhelming EFC response. In healthcommunication contexts (e.g., smoking cessation, weight loss), this causes people to focus too much on fear and then negatively react through maladaptive behaviors (Witte 1992, 1994). We did not observe this relationship with ISP compliance; instead, the employees acted more like they were in danger control. Thus, H2 was signi<sup>fi</sup>cant but in the opposite direction. This is a direct indication that the employees were not overwhelmed by the fear involved in the EFC response in our context. This result is particularly compelling, given that response costs and maladaptive rewards did foster maladaptive behaviors.

Given the con<sup>fi</sup>rmed danger-control results and the opposite fear-control results, it is logical that the results of testing our hypotheses on the “discriminant value” (the tipping point between compliance and noncompliance) would follow the same pattern. Consequently, when the discriminant value was high, there were observed higher levels of compliance (H5a supported), but the maladaptive predictions behaved in reverse (tests for H5b–c were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, but in the opposite direction). Given that fear-control works differently in this context, this is also true of the discriminant value.

## 7.2. Contributions to ISec Research, Theory, and Practice

Given our focus on pragmatic organizational security issues with an emphasis on ecological validity, the contributions to research, theory, and practice point to synergistic implications that contribute to all three. Our results support our extension and contextualization of the EPPM in the ISP-compliance/noncompliance context, but with some important exceptions. Overall, we show that, in an ISP-compliance context, it is important to work with theoretical modeling that can account for both adaptive and maladaptive outcomes and for PFC as well as EFC. To date, only the EPPM can account for these four core mechanisms, which we use in our ISP context. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we are the <sup>fi</sup>rst to demonstrate that PFC and EFC have a differential in<sup>fl</sup>uence on compliance/noncompliance outcomes and that these types of outcomes are not just opposites, but different, discernible constructs. We thus echo the opinion that future ISec work should continue to use both EFC and PFC (Willison et al. 2018, Burns et al. 2019, D’Arcy and Lowry 2019) and that our understanding of EFC should be further expanded, elucidated, and elaborated to arrive at more accurate boundary conditions for threat appeals in the ISec context.

We have further demonstrated that embracing the tipping point is a key to unifying the dual processes of EFC and PFC. By explicating precisely how the adaptive forces are combined and compared with the maladaptive forces, the tipping point becomes a powerful tool with which to better model the outcomes of the core elements of a fear appeal and better understand when EFC or PFC will occur, whereas the ISec literature has only hinted at the existence of the tipping point (Hedstrom et al.¨ 2011, Kolkowska and Dhillon 2013, Karjalainen et al. 2019, Karjalainen et al. 2020). This expands our knowledge in this area, because previous work has rarely investigated both types of responses (recall that only 3.3% of reviewed papers hinted at the existence of the tipping point and that merely 22.2% investigated both EFC and PFC, as shown in Table 1), and we can now demonstrate when either response will be engendered. However, our expected <sup>fi</sup>ndings were reversed regarding the effect of a higher discriminant value on maladaptive responses. The results showed greater instead of lower noncompliance intentions, thus highlighting that more research must be devoted to understanding the boundary conditions of the threat appeal and its resultant fear.

Moreover, a key theoretical assumption of the EPPM is that fear acts as a strong mediator for both adaptive responses (i.e., compliance) and any corresponding maladaptive responses (i.e., avoidance and noncompliance). Consequently, measuring fear and testing for mediation are crucial to determining whether the EPPM is appropriate for an ISP-compliance context. We did so, and in virtually all the cases, partial mediation was supported by both studies. We conclude that fear as an EFC response plays a key role in this context and thus that the EPPM is highly appropriate.

This <sup>fi</sup>nding expands extant knowledge on fear and fear appeals (Johnston et al. 2019, Schuetz et al. 2020, Xu et al. 2020). This also means that researchers in this area should measure fear and should not assume that fear is suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently generated when a fear appeal is used, even when a perceived threat is measured in a study (LaTour and Rotfeld 1997). Understanding the level of fear evoked is essential in determining whether a reasonable level of fear is being generated. Thus, several studies have warned that without fear measurement, the effectiveness of fear appeals can be assessed only indirectly (Witte 1992, 1994; LaTour and Rotfeld 1997; Witte and Allen 2000; Boss et al. 2015). If a fear appeal (or an experience that occasions threat and ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy) generates no meaningful fear, then it is an indication of a lack of personal relevance, and the individual will not be motivated to follow the persuasive aims of the message or to achieve the desired outcome (i.e., ISec policy compliance). Yet, extreme levels of fear can have unintended negative outcomes as well, leading individuals to avoid, panic, or even act contrarily to the persuasive element of the message (Witte 1992, 1994).

The roles of fear and fear appeals continue to be overlooked in the broader ISec literature; most papers have not considered either one (Boss et al. 2015). Although Boss et al. (2015) advocate the use of both, they provided empirical evidence only through studies involving personal use. Moreover, Warkentin et al. (2016) suggest that fear has relevance only in a personal setting and not in an organizational context, but their empirical evidence relies on MBA students placed in fMRI machines, not employees in actual organizational settings. We use Witte’s extensive work in this area (e.g., Witte 1992, 1994; Witte and Allen 2000) to assert that using fear appeals and measuring fear are highly appropriate, but only if the task and context are personally relevant, thus coherently explaining the results of both Boss et al. (2015) and Warkentin et al. (2016). This is a compelling example of why ecological validity is more crucial in organizational ISec research than in other settings: contrived and unrealistic security manipulations or settings are simply not personally or organizationally relevant (Lowry et al. 2017).<sup>20</sup> In an ISec context, ecologically invalid manipulations (e.g., MBA students in an fMRI machine completing a task that they do not normally perform with a clumsy and limited interface required for use with fMRI machines, and on someone else’s system) might not generate a meaningful threat or fear that is accurate or valid for making external claims of how threat and fear work in an organizational context, potentially leading to mismatched, unhelpful, or even misleading conclusions. A more ecologically valid approach in our context would involve a longitudinal <sup>fi</sup>eld study of ISP compliance related to fear appeals delivered by an organization.

Moreover, we con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that the danger-control process proposed by the EPPM works similarly in our ISP context. However, despite the importance of fear, this process appears to work differently in an ISP context when it comes to the fear-control portion than it does in a health-communication context, for which the EPPM was originally proposed. Due to the ISP context, no disabling results of fear were evident in our study; instead, fear also served to diminish maladaptive responses. We surmise that fear can simply be more overwhelming in a potentially life-threatening personal health context than in an organizational security setting, even one in which employees care about their work and their organization. In the ISP context, it appears that the fear generated can be coped with through PFC and EFC. Thus, in personal health, there is greater potential for helplessness and personal physical harm, which would generate more fear, which in turn would motivate people to avoid, ignore, or react against the suggested adaptive response. In contrast, security threats do not have a physical-harm component and are in fact decidedly virtual and data-centric. Hence, the nature of fear is starkly different in these two contexts, and the assumptions and outcomes related to the fear-control response need to be adjusted accordingly. Fear is a complex construct, and we explain some compelling research opportunities in the next section.

Considering this important <sup>fi</sup>nding on the fear-control response in the ISP context, our proposed extensions to the EPPM become even more useful, important, and interesting. Practitioners who consider ways in which response costs and maladaptive rewards can be reduced might <sup>fi</sup>nd their security education and training awareness (SETA) campaigns to be more effective. Reducing the effort and time required to comply with ISPs could dramatically improve compliance. In this study, we found that both play a key role in an ISP context: maladaptive rewards diminish ISP compliance while increasing ISP avoidance and ISP noncompliance. Response costs were related not to ISP compliance but instead to increased ISP avoidance and ISP noncompliance. We assert that organizations should focus on threat, ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy, and subsequent fear generation to foster ISP compliance—but, in doing so, they should <sup>fi</sup>nd ways to decrease the maladaptive rewards and response costs that are relevant to employees instead of attempting to directly prevent overwhelming levels of fear. These results are possible only by recontextualizing the EPPM to the realities of ISP compliance/noncompliance. We thus concur with other scholars that “context is king” and foundational to good theorizing in IS (Hong et al. 2013, Davison and Martinsons 2016).

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that it may be straightforward to increase ISP compliance through a SETA program that focuses on the following: making employees aware of threats that they were not previously aware of (to avoid EPPM Outcome A; recall Table 2); increasing coping ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy to deal with threat-generated fear by explaining systematically how to deal with speci<sup>fi</sup>c threats (to foster EPPM Outcome B and avoid Outcome C). Such efforts should subsequently increase positive discriminant values, and these can be easily measured before and after training, as a simple manipulation check.

However, where practitioners and researchers will need extra focus is further efforts in SETA programs that can be used to decrease response costs and maladaptive rewards. To do so, they may have to further account for other related contextual factors such as job roles, organizational commitment, type of security threats, organizational differences, industrial differences, and individual differences, to reduce maladaptive coping behaviors. Again, pre- and postmeasures of the discriminant value from such interventions can point to their utility in fostering ISP compliance. We therefore believe that the EPPM’s discriminant value approach is a meaningful addition to ISec practice, as well as research and theory in general.

## 7.3. Limitations and Future Research

Although our <sup>fi</sup>ndings make a compelling case for using our extended and recontextualized version of the EPPM in an ISP-compliance context, much more research and practice are needed to establish a strong consensus regarding the broad generalizability and applicability of the EPPM. Moreover, ISP compliance is only one element of ISec, and it cannot be assumed that the EPPM is applicable to the many other kinds of security behaviors and security issues—but with further contextualization and extension, it may gain such applicability. Such contexts that need consideration include identity theft, data breaches, and embezzlement.

Another interesting limitation, and the one that points to the most compelling future research agenda, concerns fear conceptualization and measurement. Fear is a complex construct and may include any combination of fright, concern, worry, arousal, discomfort, apprehension, or negative mood, manifested at the cognitive, physical, or emotional level (Osman et al. 1994 Witte et al. 1996, McIntosh et al. 1997).<sup>21</sup> As a core emotion that is a vivid response to a threat, fear is processed cognitively and easily remembered and is therefore readily and appropriately measured in various ways, such as through self-report (Witte 1992, Osman et al. 1994, Scherer 2005, Boss et al. 2015),<sup>22</sup> physiologically, or even through facial expressions (Witte 1992).<sup>23</sup> Again, what is perceived as threatening varies greatly for different individuals, and for a threat stimulus to evoke fear, it must be suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently relevant to a given person (Witte 1992). We thus surmise that a key method for unlocking the differences between fear control in a health context and that in an ISP context is to measure fear more richly on the cognitive, physical, and emotional levels. We suspect that the key difference is that a health-threat context involves physical threat (e.g., the possibility of dying), whereas a security context does not, perhaps indicating why people typically experience much less fear in an ISP context. Future research should determine whether fear appeals could be made stronger and more effective. It would also be interesting to deter mine whether other contexts, such as cyberphysical systems (e.g., drones and autonomous vehicles), elicit stronger fear due to the enhanced possibility of physical harm. Moreover, our fear appeals followed the typica practice in the ISec PMT literature of using self-reports of individuals’ knowledge of threats and ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy in their organizations (Study 1) (e.g., Posey et al. 2015) or of using scenarios (Study 2) (e.g., Johnston and Warkentin 2010a). Although a fear appeal can be as simple as a verbal alert, health research shows that the most effective fear appeals tend to involve a larger SETA campaign, which can include pamphlets, posters, advertisements, training sessions, or other similar interventions (Floyd et al. 2000; Fry and Prentice-Dunn 2005, 2006). In such campaigns, researchers and practitioners should strive to create fear-appeal messages that create a reasonable level of threat (and subsequent fear) and that provide a sense of ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy in addressing the threat.

Future research should determine whether additional messages can counter maladaptive extrinsic and intrinsic rewards and response costs. Here, we might follow the example of dentists who prominently display photos of what can happen when gum disease goes untreated. Rather than warn of thefts or improper use of data, managers who want employees to be more vigilant should explain more clearly why following ISPs will help employees succeed in their work and ensure the future success of the organization. The threat of a lost job or lost promotion might generate stronger fear than abstract security threats.

Finally, another key limitation of our study, and of most ISec studies, is its exclusive focus on participants from Western cultures. Other IS research has shown that individual-level and national-level cultural differences can in<sup>fl</sup>uence decision making regarding technology use, adoption, and associated behaviors (Rao et al. 2007, Zhang et al. 2007, Cyr 2008, Kim 2008, Vance et al. 2008, Posey et al. 2010, Jain et al. 2011, Lowry et al. 2011, Chen and Zahedi 2016). For example, studies have shown that, compared with Western users, Chinese users are much more prone to uncertainty avoidance, perceive greater power distance from their managers, and adhere more strongly to collective norms (e.g., Zhang et al. 2007, Cyr 2008, Lowry et al. 2011). Studies have also revealed signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences in trust based on cultural and contextual differences (e.g., Cyr 2008, Kim 2008, Vance et al. 2008). Such factors could systematically change the calculus involved in the EPPM (and PMT for that matter), and yet, culture has been largely overlooked in this line of research, with one exception (i.e., Chen and Zahedi 2016). Thus, it is imperative to extend this line of research to other cultural contexts. We believe these and similar opportunities will lead to further improvement in studying and enhancing organizational ISec.

## 8. Conclusion

Despite advances in security technologies and the devotion of increasing effort and spending to SETA programs, employees continue to misunderstand, disregard, and even purposefully violate ISPs. Thus, employee ISP noncompliance remains a pressing issue and continues to result in substantial harm to organizations. ISec research has leveraged several theories to identify the underlying reasons for ISP compliance among employees, but it has focused primarily on a single outcome of compliance or noncompliance and unilaterally on PFC (i.e., without simultaneously considering EFC), while overlooking the tipping point between compliance and noncompliance. To further address these issues, we developed a dual-outcome and dual-coping model for ISP compliance/noncompliance. The model recontextualizes the EPPM to an ISec context and extends the EPPM to explain the juxtaposed outcomes of compliance/noncompliance through PFC or EFC. We also devised a weighted discriminant value measurement approach to examine and predict the tipping point between compliance and noncompliance. Both the model and the measurement approach are largely supported by our results; in terms of explaining and predicting both compliance and noncompliance behaviors among employees, they are thus a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant addition to ISec theory and practice.
