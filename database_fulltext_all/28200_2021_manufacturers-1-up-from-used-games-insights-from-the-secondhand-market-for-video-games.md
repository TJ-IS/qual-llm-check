# Manufacturer’s “1-Up” from Used Games: Insights from the Secondhand Market for Video Games

Antino Kim,<sup>a</sup> Rajib L. Saha,<sup>b</sup> Warut Khern-am-nuai<sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Kelley School of Business, Indiana University, Bloomington, Indiana 47405; <sup>b</sup> Indian School of Business, Hyderabad 500111, Telangana, India; <sup>c</sup> Desautels Faculty of Management, McGill University, Montreal, Quebec H3A 1G5, Canada

Abstract. In contrast to industries of other types of information goods, the video game industry still has a sizable secondhand market for games. This is particularly notable because some of the major gaming-console companies (e.g., Sony and Microsoft) actually possess the ability to annihilate the secondhand market altogether; it appears that those companies have given tacit approval to buying and selling used games. Naturally, the question is, what is the special ingredient in the gaming industry that gives a manufacturer incentive to keep a healthy secondhand market even when it has the technological means to shut it down? In this study, leveraging a game-theoretic model, we investigate the effect of gaming console on a manufacturer’s strategy in the presence of a secondhand market for games. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that when the manufacturer offers a valuable console that provides utilities in addition to playing games, the secondhand market increases the manufacturer’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t, and that is not at the cost of consumers; the consumers—as well as the society as a whole— also bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the secondhand market. This is in stark contrast with settings where there are no consoles involved or the consoles do not offer any intrinsic value; in such settings, the manufacturer would opt to shut down the secondhand market. In the case with a valuable console, however, the increasing appeal of the secondhand market to consumers may improve the manufacturer’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t, consumer surplus, and social welfare, all at the same time. We discuss our <sup>fi</sup>ndings along with managerial and welfare implications.

## 1. Introduction

The video game industry is currently one of the largest entertainment industries in the world. With the market size exceeding \$145 billion in 2019 and projected to surpass \$200 billion mark by 2023 (Wijman 2020), the video game industry is now worth more than the music and movie industries combined (Malim 2018). It is also one of the few information goods industries with a healthy secondhand market. For example, GameStop, one of the leading players in the used video game market, made \$2.15 billion U.S. dollars from the market in 2017 even though the sales of new games had slowed down as some of the major gaming consoles (e.g., X-Box 360 and the Playstation) were running an extended product cycle (Tre<sup>fi</sup>s Team 2018). This is in stark contrast with the secondhand markets of other information goods such as movies and music CDs/DVDs, which were once thriving but have signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly declined over time. Although some have speculated that the used game market may disappear once digital games become the only option for consumers (because digital products cannot be resold), recent movements by consumer protection groups in Europe, advocating for rights to resell digital goods (e.g., Orland 2015), have reaf<sup>fi</sup>rmed the relevance of secondhand markets.<sup>1</sup> More recently, a French court ruled that “European Union law allows Steam users to resell their digital games, just like they can any physical product” (Bonifacic 2019). Steam is one of the largest video game digital distribution service platforms, with more than 1 billion accounts and 90 million monthly active users as of 2019 (Scott-Jones 2019). It is also worth noting that the current limitation on reselling digital goods is due to a legislative barrier, not a technical one. For instance, both Amazon and Apple own patents for used goods markets for digital objects (Block and Van Os 2012, Ringewald 2009). In this paper, we consider several unique characteristics of the video game market and investigate the optimal strategy for a game manufacturer in the presence of a secondhand market.

Although secondhand markets may generate some positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence to the society such as promoting informed consumption and increasing product life cycle (Growing Leader Staff 2017), prior literature suggests that manufacturers are typically worse off with such a market (e.g., Rust 1986, Van Cayseele 1993, Nocke and Peitz 2003, and Van Loon et al. 2018). This <sup>fi</sup>nding is consistent with the behavior of manufacturers in several industries such as the textbook industry, where publishers sought to “kill off the existing used book market” (Ferguson 1969, quoted in Miller 1974, p. 612). However, in the video game market, the manufacturers’ reactions toward used games have been somewhat mixed. There were lawsuits over whether selling used games infringed upon copyrights (e.g., Hirayama 2006), and some game developers and publishers, who only get royalties from the sale of new copies, have tried to discourage consumers from purchasing used games (Reisinger 2010). Still, the secondhand market for games remained robust, and one could have argued that the manufacturers’ hands were simply tied in their battle against the secondhand market.

That speculation, however, does not seem to hold. As it turns out, both Sony and Microsoft, two giants in the video game industry, own patents that would allow them to categorically shut down buying and selling used games (Inooka 2012, Yin-Poole 2012, Stuart 2013). Despite having the ability to pull the plug on the secondhand market, both Sony and Microsoft have decided not to implement their patents and continue to allow buying and selling used games (McWhertor 2013). And, as previously mentioned, the used game market is still thriving more than a half-decade later. According to the conventional wisdom—as well as some game manufacturers’ attitudes toward used games—both Sony and Microsoft should have eradicated the secondhand market with their anti-used game technology. Instead, it appears that these major video game manufacturers have given tacit approval to the used game market. Naturally, the question is, why? What is special about the game industry that would hold back a manufacturer from shutting down the secondhand market even when it has the ability to do so?

In comparison with other types of information goods, one unique aspect that sets video games apart is the gaming console. Music, videos, and to a large extent even e-books can be purchased and enjoyed across many different devices and platforms. That is not the case for console-based video games. To play the games, consumers have to purchase the console, and this is true whether the games are purchased new or used. Another interesting observation is the evolution of gaming consoles. Back in the earlier days, gaming consoles could only play games and did not offer any other features. Over time, however, with technology advancements, gaming consoles have become more and more sophisticated, and they now serve as media hubs and platforms for access to online contents and apps. Intrigued by these unique aspects and recent phenomena, in this work, we focus on the implication of valuable gaming consoles (that provide additional values aside from playing games) with respect to the following speci<sup>fi</sup>c questions:

In the presence of a secondhand market, what is the manufacturer’s optimal pricing strategy?

How does the pro<sup>fi</sup>t depend on the appeal of the secondhand market to consumers? Does it always get worse for the manufacturer as the secondhand market becomes more attractive to consumers?

Is it possible for the manufacturer to do better with the secondhand market than without?

What are the implications of the secondhand market on consumer surplus and social welfare?

For our exploration, we develop a game-theoretic model that captures some of the key characteristics of the video game industry and the associated secondhand market. From the consumers’ perspective, for the used game market to exist, it ought to be attractive to both secondhand sellers and buyers. The secondhand sellers derive some value from purchasing a new game and playing it before others. At the same time, they also lose some utility by letting it go in the secondhand market. The secondhand buyers may enjoy less utility from playing the used game compared with the <sup>fi</sup>rsthand buyers, but they also gain some surplus from the lower used game price. In sum, there are aspects that affect the attractiveness of the secondhand market to the buyers and the sellers, and we capture both aspects in our model. Also, in our main analysis, we consider a manufacturer that sells the console (e.g., PlayStation for Sony and Xbox for Microsoft) in addition to games.

We <sup>fi</sup>nd that when the console has nonzero intrinsic value, the used game market can be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to the manufacturer. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we discover that the relationship between the manufacturer’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t and the attractiveness of the secondhand market is nonmonotonic; that is, the pro<sup>fi</sup>t of the manufacturer may actually increase as the secondhand market becomes more attractive to consumers. Interestingly, when the manufacturer bene<sup>fi</sup>ts with increasing appeal of the used game market, the consumer surplus increases also. In other words, the secondhand market can provide a win-win situation for both the manufacturer and consumers. Naturally, the social welfare increases as well. This is in stark contrast with settings where there are no consoles involved or the consoles have no intrinsic value; in such cases, the manufacturer’s optimal strategy is to shut down the secondhand market. We also <sup>fi</sup>nd that the manufacturer enjoys greater bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the used game market, as the console provides more value outside of playing games (e.g., media hub, smart device with apps) to consumers. Our results provide insights into the counterintuitive decision by both Sony and Microsoft to shelve the patents that could have terminated the used game market. Moreover, these results have implications that not only apply to the past and present of the gaming industry but also to its future and to other types of platform-based markets for contents. Hence, this work offers another important angle to consider in evaluating the socioeconomic implications of used goods.

## 2. Literature Review

Our work lies in the intersection of (i) the secondhand market, (ii) the video game market, and (iii) platform pricing. We next highlight relevant prior literature from economics, marketing, and information systems.

## 2.1. Secondhand Markets

Early studies in economics on the topic of secondhand market mostly leverage theoretical modeling approaches. For example, in his seminal work, Swan (1970) demonstrates that there is no implied relationship between the existence of the secondhand market and the manufacturer’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t. This result, which is often referred to as “Swan’s independence result,” attracted signi<sup>fi</sup>cant subsequent interests from researchers in this <sup>fi</sup>eld. In particular, multiple studies have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed conditions where the manufacturer can be better off eliminating the secondhand market, including when the durability of the product is under the manufacturer’s control (Rust 1986), when the quality of the used product can be manipulated by the manufacturer (Anderson and Ginsburgh 1994), and when product valuation among low-valuation consumers is below a certain threshold (Waldman 1996). By contrast, another substream of literature has identi<sup>fi</sup>ed conditions that allow the manufacturer to be better off with the secondhand market, such as when the secondhand market is operated by the manufacturer or by another entity that shares similar interests (e.g., a downstream retailer in the supply chain) (Ghose et al. 2005, Shulman and Coughlan 2007), when the product has high durability or a low rate of technology obsolescence (Arunkundram and Sundararajan 1998), and when there are market frictions and consumer preference changes over time (Johnson 2011).

Our work leverages, as well as contributes to, the prior literature on secondhand markets. Our model includes aspects related to durability and depreciation, which affect the utility consumers obtain from used games. We also incorporate the notion of relinquished utility that consumers would forgo by selling their games in the secondhand market. In line with prior literature, we assume that used games are sold exclusively by those who bought new (e.g., Waldman 1996, Johnson 2011, and Feng et al. 2019). Furthermore, we consider a secondhand market where buyers and sellers interact and the used price is determined through a market clearing mechanism without the manufacturer’s intervention. Even then, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the manufacturer can bene<sup>fi</sup>t when the used games become more appealing to consumers and, ultimately, be better off with the secondhand market than without. The key ingredient is the gaming console that offers utilities beyond just playing games.

In addition to prior analytical works, there exist several studies that empirically examine secondhand markets. For instance, Purohit (1992) examines the relationship between the markets for new and used cars and demonstrates that the depreciation of used cars is highly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the changes included in newer model cars. In a related work, Chen et al. (2013) develop a dynamic model of durable goods in an oligopoly setting and calibrate model parameters by using the data from the U.S. automobile industry. They <sup>fi</sup>nd that the existence of the secondhand market signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly decreases manufacturers’ production and pro<sup>fi</sup>ts. Other empirical works also demonstrate that the existence of the secondhand market tends to decrease manufacturer’s pro<sup>fi</sup>ts both in the context of the used car industry $( \mathrm { e . g . } ,$ , Esteban and Shum 2007 and Engers et al. 2009) and in other contexts such as the textbook industry (e.g., Ghose et al. 2006). Prior works have also consistently reported the same in the context of the video game market, which is the main focus of our study. We next take a closer look at those prior works, along with other studies that examine the video game market in general.

## 2.2. Video Game Market

The market for video games has been a popular venue for academic research, as it has several unique characteristics that match different research agendas. For example, a video game goes perfectly with the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of an experience good, and both the size and the growth of its market is nothing short of impressive. As such, several prior studies have utilized data from the video game industry in their empirical analyses (e.g., Nair 2007, Liu 2010, Zhu and Zhang 2010, Khern-amnuai et al. 2020, and Tanlamai et al. 2021). Another unique characteristic of the video game industry is that it is among the last few remaining information goods industries with a robust secondhand market (Crandall and Sidak 2006). Several studies have examined the implications of the secondhand market using empirical research methodologies. For instance, Shiller (2013) uses the data from the U.S. video game market to demonstrate that the existence of the secondhand market signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly decreases pro<sup>fi</sup>ts of the video games manufacturer. The counterfactual analysis shows that the manufacturer’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t increases by at least 83% when the secondhand market is eliminated. On a similar note, Ishihara and Ching (2019) use the data from the Japanese video game market to estimate a dynamic structural model of consumers’ decisions in the new and used video game markets. Notably, the authors highlight two competing effects of the secondhand market: (i) the substitution effect from consumers choosing used games over new and (ii) the resale effect from forward-looking consumers willing to pay more for a new game because they are able to resell it in the secondhand market. From the analyses, the authors conclude that a pro<sup>fi</sup>t-maximizing <sup>fi</sup>rm would be better off without the secondhand market, indicating that the substitution effect dominates the resale effect. These consistent results, along with conventional wisdom from industry experts (e.g., Hirayama 2006), pique our interest as to why—despite having the necessary technology to completely eliminate the secondhand market (Yin-Poole 2012, Stuart 2013)—both Sony and Microsoft decided to allow used games in the market. As such, we look closely at the unique characteristics of the video game industry, and we develop a new theoretical lens that would help us better understand the effect of a secondhand market on the manufacturer and consumers. In particular, in this work, we focus on the gaming console aspect. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, all consumers—whether they buy new games or used—have to purchase the console (i.e., the platform) in order to enjoy the games. Hence, from the manufacturer’s perspective, there are two pricing decisions to make: one for the games and another for the console. In that regard, we next survey relevant literature on such a pricing scheme.

## 2.3. Durable Goods and Platform Pricing

The platform (e.g., gaming console) is another notable feature of the video game market. Many modern video game companies offer a gaming console (e.g., Sony PlayStation, Microsoft Xbox) together with their video games. In that regard, the pricing of both components is similar to the concept of a two-part tariff (Lewis 1941, Oi 1971) and multiproduct pricing models (e.g., Whinston 1990). Mulhern and Leone (1991) study a market where the demand for multiple products offered by the same <sup>fi</sup>rm is interdependent. They demonstrate that the <sup>fi</sup>rm pro<sup>fi</sup>t can be maximized by exploiting demand interdependencies and by leveraging implicit price bundling. Church and Gandal (1996) examine the scenario where the value of the durable goods in the market is dependent on the variety of complementary goods. They show that the strategic action of a monopolistic <sup>fi</sup>rm may lead to inef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies by forcing the market to accept excessive or insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient standardization. The multiproduct pricing model is also extended to study the implications of intangible products (e.g., Yu et al. 2011 and Hao and Fan 2014) and product bundling (e.g., Chao and Derdenger 2013 and Derdenger and Kumar 2013). Notably, Chao and Derdenger (2013) test (and <sup>fi</sup>nd support for) their model predictions using data from the portable video game console market.

In the online appendix (see Table A1), we summarize prior literature that studies how the existence of the secondhand market affects a manufacturer’s pro<sup>fi</sup>ts in different contexts. However, in the speci<sup>fi</sup>c context of video game market, previous works have consistently demonstrated that the video game manufacturer has an incentive to eliminate the secondhand market. This, obviously, does not explain the decision by Sony and Microsoft. Also, to the best of our knowledge, no prior work has considered the role of gaming consoles in the context of secondhand market for video games. In that regard, we develop a model that includes the platform aspect of the video game industry and demonstrate how the manufacturer can leverage its gaming console to bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the secondhand market. Hence, this work offers not only a new theoretical perspective but also an explanation for a curious real-world phenomena.

## 3. Main Model

In this section, we consider the manufacturer’s pricing decision and consumers’ product choices in a market where the manufacturer sells both the game and the gaming console. The timeline of events is as depicted in Figure 1: First, the manufacturer sets the prices for the game (p > 0) and the console $( c > 0 ) . ^ { 2 }$ In the subsequent stages, the consumer decision journey begins at the console market followed by the game market (Derdenger and Kumar 2013): the consumers decide whether to purchase the console, and in the following stage, they make game purchase decisions to maximize their utilities. In the subgame equilibrium, the secondhand market clears, determining the price for the used version (s > 0). The market clearing mechanism captures an interesting feature of secondhand market; unlike other types of markets where the supply is from the manufacturer and the demand is from consumers, in the secondhand market, both the supply and demand are from consumers. When the secondhand market clears, the supply from consumers (i.e., those who are selling) equals the demand from consumers $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ those who are buying). Following backward induction, we <sup>fi</sup>rst visit the consumer’s game purchase decision, then the console purchase decision, and, subsequently, the manufacturer’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t maximization problem.

## 3.1. Consumers’ Console and Game Purchase Decisions

We consider consumers who—if they purchase the console—enjoy n game titles over the lifetime of the console. Because used games are bought and sold between consumers on a per-title basis, to better understand the market clearing mechanism in the secondhand market, we <sup>fi</sup>rst look at how consumers decide on each game title in Stage 3. For each title, consumers are heterogeneous in their valuations for the new game, $v ,$ which follows a uniform distribution over <sub>[</sub>0, 1<sub>]</sub>. Hence, the net utility a consumer with a valuation v gets from buying a new game and keeping it is given by

$$
U _ {n} = v - p,
$$

where $p > 0$ is the price of the new game. We denote this choice as Buy New & Keep.

We further de<sup>fi</sup>ne the utility that a consumer gets from buying the new game and reselling it in the secondhand market later; we denote this choice as Buy New & Sell. Those who buy a new game but sell after a while would not get the full value of the game. The relinquished utility of the game stems from the loss of ownership itself, as well as the loss of achievements the gamer has accumulated by playing the game. There is utility from keeping a collection of games, and gamers typically become invested in the games as their game characters move up to advanced stages and accumulate skills and experiences (Rigby and Ryan 2011); all such utility would be lost upon selling the game. In order to capture the partial value of the game that consumers get from temporarily owning it, we use $\Delta \in ( 0 , 1 )$ to denote the fraction of the full value that a consumer is able to extract from the game prior to selling it. In other words, $1 - \Delta$ represents the level of relinquished utility from letting go of the game after playing it. Therefore, the net utility a consumer with a valuation v gets from buying a new game and selling it afterward is given by

$$
U _ {n s} = v \Delta - p + s,
$$

where s is the price of the used game determined in the secondhand market. To avoid degenerate cases, we assume that the price of the used game is always lower than that of the new version; that is, $0 < s < p .$

Finally, we consider the utility from purchasing a used version from the secondhand market; we denote this choice as Buy Used. A used version offers lower value to consumers compared with a new one, and this utility loss can originate from various aspects. For example, there is the joy of opening a new game—as well as some packaging materials—which used versions are unable to provide (Ampuero and Vila 2006, Shiller 2013). Even if we ignore any physical depreciation of games, their value depreciates over time as the hype that surrounds the titles during their release tends to dissipate (Ishihara and Ching 2019). In order to capture the partial value of the game for consumers who buy the used version, we assume that they get only a $\delta \in ( 0 , 1 )$ fraction of the full value of the game when bought secondhand. In other words, δ represents the value of the used version relative to that of the new one. A high δ value means that there is little difference between the used and the new versions, whereas a low δ value indicates a large degree of degradation of the used version compared with the new. Therefore, the net utility a consumer with a valuation v gets from buying a used game is given by

$$
U _ {u} = v \delta - s.
$$

In sum. in our model. ∆ represents the fraction of the total value a consumer is able to extract from a new game prior to selling it, and δ represents the fraction of the value consumers get from purchasing the used version. Though we consider these two fractions to be distinct based on prior literature (Ishihara and Ching 2019), together, they simply represent how appealing the secondhand market is to consumers. Therefore, Δ and δ (hereafter collectively referred to as “deltas”) allow us to study how the manufacturer, as well as the consumers, reacts as the secondhand market becomes more or less attractive to consumers. Our comparative statics <sup>fi</sup>ts well with our research objective, and for that, we keep the deltas exogenous; Arunkundram and Sundararajan (1998) discuss some exogenous factors that determine the value of used good with respect to that of the new product. This is in stark contrast with versioning literature where the manufacturer has complete control over different versions. In our study, the manufacturer’s decision regarding used games is binary: it can either allow consumers buy and sell used games or shut down the secondhand market altogether through some anti-used game technology (e.g., the pat ents that Sony and Microsoft hold). We use the latter case as the benchmark to investigate whether the manufacturer has an incentive to keep the secondhand market. Studying the implications behind endogenous deltas is beyond the scope of this work, and we leave that to future research. Although the pricing decisions are made by the manufacturer, the deltas simply represent the appeal of the secondhand market to consumers, and they provide the context for our analyses. Finally, compared with versioning literature—where all transactions involving different versions contribute to the manufacturer’s total revenue—in our model, the manufacturer does not directly bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the transactions that take place in the secondhand market.

Another notable attribute of our model is that the sum of the fractions that represent partial values for consumers who buy and sell in the secondhand market can be greater than the whole (i.e., Δ  δ can be greater than 1). For example, we account for cases where consumers who sell in the secondhand market derive 70% of the total value of the game and those who buy the secondhand version enjoy 80% of the value of a new one. This is indeed the case for durable goods that are also imperfectly rivalrous, and it makes secondhand markets even more threatening for manufacturers of information goods.

Formally, a consumer chooses Buy New & Keep if the individual rationality (IR) constraint $( U _ { n } > 0 )$ and incentive compatibility (IC) constraints $( U _ { n } > U _ { n s }$ and $U _ { n } > U _ { u } )$ are satis<sup>fi</sup>ed. Similarly, a consumer chooses Buy New & Sell if the IR constraint $( U _ { n s } > 0 )$ and the IC constraints $( U _ { n s } > U _ { n }$ and $U _ { n s } > U _ { u } )$ are satis<sup>fi</sup>ed. Finally, a consumer chooses Buy Used if the IR constraint $( U _ { u } > 0 )$ and the IC constraints $( U _ { u } > U _ { n }$ and $U _ { u } > U _ { n s } )$ are satis<sup>fi</sup>ed. For completeness, we also denote the choice of forgoing the use of the game altogether as Not Buy. Here, we only consider the case where the mass of consumers who Buy New & Keep is strictly positive because we do not intend to model a pure rental market. On a similar note, because our focus is on the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the secondhand market, we assume the mass of consumers in the secondhand market (i.e., those opting for Buy New & Sell and Buy Used options) is positive. Depending on the relative value between the deltas, we can have one of three possible scenarios: $\Delta > \delta , \Delta < \delta ,$ or $\Delta = \delta$ . We later show that the equilibrium solution is continuous at $\Delta = \delta ,$ , and we thereby collapse the cases into $\Delta \geq \delta$ and $\Delta < \delta$

First, Figure 2(a) illustrates the choices consumers make when $\Delta > \delta$ . The segment with the highest valuation buys the new game and keeps it, the segment with the second-highest valuation buys the new game and sells it afterward, and the next segment buys the used game. The remaining lowest segment forgoes use altogether. Let $v _ { 1 }$ be the customer type who is indifferent between Buy New & Keep and Buy New & Sell options, let $v _ { 2 }$ be the customer type who is indifferent between Buy New & Sell and Buy Used, and let $v _ { 3 }$ be the customer type who is indifferent between Buy Used and Not Buy. Accordingly, we have the following IC and IR constraints to consider:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathrm{IC} _ {1}: v _ {1} - p = v _ {1} \Delta - (p - s); \\ \mathrm{IC} _ {2}: v _ {2} \Delta - (p - s) = v _ {2} \delta - s; \quad \mathrm{IR} _ {u}: v _ {3} \delta - s = 0. \end{array}
$$

These imply

$$
v _ {1} = \frac {s}{1 - \Delta}, v _ {2} = \frac {p - 2 s}{\Delta - \delta}, \mathrm{and} v _ {3} = \frac {s}{\delta}.
$$

After identifying these indifferent customers, the demands for those who buy new and keep $( q _ { n } )$ and those buy new and sell $( q _ { n s } )$ can be easily speci<sup>fi</sup>ed. For those who buy used $( q _ { u } ) .$ , however, there remains one additional factor to consider: only those who purchase the console in the previous stage (Stage 2) can play the game. We next consider the console purchase decision that consumers make in Stage 2.

Notably, all consumers—regardless of whether they purchase new games or used—need to purchase the console to play the games. The idea of selling both the console and the game is analogous to the two-part tariff or entry fee from prior literature (Oi 1971, Mas-Colell et al. 1995). Essentially, consumers need to pay an “entry fee” to the manufacturer by purchasing the console in order to play the game, new or used. When making the console purchase decision, consumers consider the expected utility they can generate from playing video games as well as the additional value— beyond playing games—they get from the console itself. This intrinsic value of console stems from various features of the console and other entertainment opportunities outside of playing the game. Even for older-generation consoles that do not offer any features other than playing games, there may still be some intangible utility that consumers get from the ownership of the console itself. Furthermore, over time, gaming consoles have come a long way from being simple devices that can only play games, and consumers can now use their consoles as media hubs to store and access music, movies, and photos, both locally and over the internet. On the basis of these observations, we assume that the console has a nonnegative intrinsic value, $m \geq 0$ , that is separate from the value that consumers derive from playing the games.

As our primary focus is the game industry, we assume that the consumers’ main purpose of purchasing the console is to play the game. In other words, if some consumers are only interested in the console’s standalone value (and not the game), they do not purchase the console alone because there are better outside options (e.g., other electronic products) for that purpose. Along the same line, for ease of exploration, we do not consider the secondhand market for consoles in the main model. There are reasons from reality as well. The secondhand consoles are a lot less common compared with games because (i) the console, being a physical product, rapidly depreciates in value (e.g., wear and tear of physical components and lack of warranty for used consoles), (ii) selling the console implies that the consumer is completely leaving the platform, and (iii) whereas buying and selling games occurs during the lifetime of a console—which is what we consider here in the main model—selling used consoles may take place close to the end of the consoles’ life cycle (e.g., when a newer generation console is released).<sup>5</sup>

## 4. Results

We now carefully analyze the equilibrium outcome in the previous section to better understand the impacts of the secondhand market on the incentives of the manufacturer and consumers.

## 4.1. Prices

First, we take a close look at how the secondhand market in<sup>fl</sup>uences the manufacturer’s pricing strategy. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, when the secondhand market becomes more appealing to consumers $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ as the deltas increase), how would the manufacturer’s pricing change when it sells both the game and the console? The following proposition answers this question.

Proposition 1. As $\Delta$ and δ increase, $p ^ { * }$ decreases and $c ^ { * }$ increases.

This proposition demonstrates the shift in the manufacturer’s pricing strategy as the competitive force from the used game market strengthens. When the deltas increase, buying and selling used games become more appealing to consumers, and facing strong competition from the secondhand market, the manufacturer’s pricing power diminishes—at least, that is the case with respect to the game price, p. However, here, the manufacturer has another lever it can turn: the price of its gaming console, c. What we see here is that when the secondhand market becomes more threatening to the manufacturer, it shifts its market focus toward the console and attempts to recoup its lost pro<sup>fi</sup>t by jacking up the console price. This strategy is possible because all consumers, whether they purchase new games or used, must also purchase the console. We also note that, as can be seen from Lemma 1, the manufacturer is able to charge more for the console as the consumers enjoy a greater number of games over the lifetime of the console $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , c ^ { * }$ increases as n increases). Overall, Proposition 1 highlights the importance of the console to the manufacturer in responding to the secondhand market.

## 4.2. Profit

Can the manufacturer bene<sup>fi</sup>t as the used game market becomes more attractive to the buyers and sellers? To answer this question, we present the next proposition.

Proposition 2. When the console has a positive intrinsic value, the manufacturer’s profit can increase even as the secondhand market becomes more attractive to consumers. In particular, when $\Delta \geq \delta ,$ the manufacturer’s profit increases in Δ if $3 \Delta - \delta > 2$ and $m > 0$ . When $\Delta < \delta ,$ the profit increases in $\delta i f 3 \delta - \Delta > 2$ and $m > 0$

This proposition demonstrates that the manufacturer can sometimes be better off even as the appeal of the secondhand market improves. For that to be possible, however, it is important for the console to have a positive intrinsic value $\left( \mathrm { i . e . , } m > 0 \right) . ^ { 9 }$ In fact, with a valueless console $( \mathrm { i . e . , } m = 0 )$ , as can be seen from Lemma 1, the manufacturer’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t (π∗) remains the same at <sup>n</sup> over the entire valid $( \Delta , \delta )$ parameter space.

The general conditions in Proposition 2 indicate that the increasing attractiveness of the secondhand market can be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial when the deltas move far apart from each other. In other words, the manufacturer can expect a positive effect from the secondhand market when the sellers appreciate it more than do the buyers, or vice versa.

Having observed the nonmonotonicity of the effect of deltas on the manufacturer’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t, one question that naturally follows is whether the manufacturer can ever be better off with the secondhand market. Because the equilibrium results in Lemma 1 are only interior ones, though it may be tempting, we cannot simply set the deltas in Lemma 1 to 0 in order to obtain the equilibrium outcome for the no-secondhand market case. Thus, we solve the no-secondhand market case separately as a benchmark to make the comparison. Note that this represents a case where the secondhand market is made impossible by the manufacturer’s technological intervention $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ anti-used game technology) or by the legal environment (e.g., digital downloads cannot be resold). Lemma 4. The consumer surplus CS is given by

$$

$$
\begin{array}{l} S W = C S + \pi^ {*} \\ = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \frac {3 n}{8} + \frac {3 m}{4} + \frac {3 m ^ {2} (4 - \delta - 3 \Delta)}{8 n (\Delta (1 - \Delta) + 2 \delta (1 - \Delta) + \delta (1 - \delta))} & i f \Delta \geq \delta , \\ \frac {3 n}{8} + \frac {3 m}{4} + \frac {3 m ^ {2} (4 - 3 \delta - \Delta)}{8 n (\Delta (1 - \Delta) + 2 \Delta (1 - \delta) + \delta (1 - \delta))} & o t h e r w i s e. \end{array} \right. \end{array}

Interestingly, contrary to common belief, we observe that the consumer surplus may actually decrease as the secondhand market becomes more attractive. By leveraging the extra lever (i.e., the gaming console), the manufacturer is able to effectively extract more surplus from consumers. Then, is the secondhand market simply leading to a zero-sum situation for the manufacturer and consumers? In other words, now that we have found that the manufacturer can bene<sup>fi</sup>t from used games in the market whereas consumers can be worse off, is the gain for the manufacturer at the cost of consumers’ welfare?

We <sup>fi</sup>nd that the message is more nuanced. First, the consumer surplus can increase with the deltas, and when it does, it increases in tandem with the manufacturer’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t. In other words, consumers as well as the manufacturer welcome the secondhand market, and the increasing appeal of it can simultaneously bene<sup>fi</sup>t both parties. As a result, the social welfare can increase as well. Second, compared with the no-secondhand market case $\begin{array} { r } { \big ( C S _ { b } = \int _ { p _ { b } ^ { * } + \frac { c _ { b } ^ { * } - m } { n } } ^ { 1 } ( n ( v - p _ { b } ^ { * } ) + m - c _ { b } ^ { * } ) d v = \frac { ( m + n ) ^ { 2 } } { 8 n } \big ) } \end{array}$ , the consumer surplus is strictly greater with the secondhand market than without, regardless of the values for Δ and $\delta . ^ { 1 0 }$ From Proposition 3, we know that the pro<sup>fi</sup>t is also greater with the secondhand market. As a result, the social welfare follows the same pattern. We highlight these <sup>fi</sup>ndings in the following proposition.

Proposition 5. When $\Delta \ge \delta$ , both the consumer surplus CS and social welfare SW increase in Δ if $3 \Delta - \delta > 2$ and $m > 0$ . When $\Delta < \delta ,$ both CS and SW increase in δ $i f 3 \delta -$ $\Delta > 2$ and $m > 0$ . Moreover, both the consumer surplus and social welfare are strictly greater with the secondhand market than without.

The driving force behind Propositions 2 and 5 can be found in how the secondhand market affects the manufacturer’s strategy, which, in turn, affects the size of the total user base (i.e., $q _ { n } + q _ { n s } + q _ { u } )$ . When the deltas increase, it becomes more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for the manufacturer to pro<sup>fi</sup>t from games, and accordingly, the manufacturer gradually shifts its strategy toward selling the gaming consoles; see Proposition 1. In particular, the manufacturer now pays more attention to consumers who opt to Buy Used because they comprise a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant segment of console sales, though they do not contribute to game sales. Put differently, the manufacturer focuses more on catering to the total user base instead of just to those who would buy the game $( \mathrm { i . e . , } q _ { n } + q _ { n s } )$ . As a result, the total user base expands, and both the pro<sup>fi</sup>t and consumer surplus increase.

Observation 1. When $\Delta \geq \delta ,$ the total user base (i.e., $q _ { n } + q _ { n s } + q _ { u } )$ increases in Δ if $3 \Delta - \delta > 2$ and $m > 0$ When $\Delta < \delta$ , the total user base increases in δ if 3δ $\Delta > 2$ and $m > 0$

In sum, when the gaming console has some intrinsic value, the secondhand market becomes favorable not only to the manufacturer but also to consumers and to the society as a whole. A higher level of appeal of the used game market may improve the manufacturer’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t, consumer surplus, and social welfare, all at the same time.

## 5. Other Considerations

In order to get clear insights, our main model is consciously put together to be as parsimonious as possible. In this section, we consider various different settings to examine whether the results from the main model are dependent on any of our assumptions. As the main model demonstrates, $\Delta > \delta$ and $\Delta < \delta$ results are mirror images of each other, and the results are continuous at $\Delta = \delta .$ . Hence, for brevity, in the extensions that we present in the following subsections, we focus only on $\Delta > \delta ;$ the <sup>fi</sup>ndings naturally extend to other cases as well.

5.1. No Console: Manufacturer Sells Only Games We see from the main model that the valuable console is an important variable that affects the manufacturer’s stance toward secondhand games. To further highlight this point, as a backdrop, we consider a case where no consoles are involved (and the manufacturer sells only games).

Let us consider a manufacturer that sells only games without any control over the platform. This setup describes markets for mobile games and PC games where the game manufacturers do not price or sell the platform on which the games are played. It is also representative of music and movie industries where the manufacturers sell only their contents. The sequence of the game is as follows: First, the manufacturer sets the price for the new game $( p > 0 )$ . Thereafter, consumers select among the same set of options as in Section 3: Buy New & Keep, Buy New & Sell, Buy Used, and Not Buy. Once again using backward induction, we <sup>fi</sup>rst visit the consumer’s decision problem and then the manufacturer’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t maximization problem.

In order to make a proper comparison with the main model, we adopt most of the modeling elements from it. For instance, the utility consumers get from Buy New & Keep, Buy New & Sell, and Buy Used are all exactly the same as before. The most notable difference compared with the main model is that, here, there are no consoles involved. In other words, there are no “entry fees” that consumers need to pay in order to enjoy the games, and all consumers who would derive nonnegative utility from playing games—new or used—would enter the market. As a result, when $\Delta > \delta ,$ , the demand expressions become

$$
q _ {n} = 1 - \frac {s}{1 - \Delta},\tag{17}
$$

$$
q _ {n s} = \frac {s}{1 - \Delta} - \frac {p - 2 s}{\Delta - \delta}, \mathrm{and}\tag{18}
$$

$$
q _ {u} = \frac {p - 2 s}{\Delta - \delta} - \frac {s}{\delta}.\tag{19}
$$

As before, equating $q _ { n s }$ with $q _ { u }$ gives us the market clearing price s in the secondhand market. The manufacturer maximizes its pro<sup>fi</sup>t by choosing an optimal price for the new game. Hence, the problem that the manufacturer faces is

$$
\max _ {p > 0} \pi (p) = \max _ {p > 0} n (q _ {n} + q _ {n s}) p.\tag{20}
$$

We note the absence of the revenue stream that comes from console sales.

For the scenario when the manufacturer sells only the games, the following characterizes the optimal price and pro<sup>fi</sup>t:

$$
p ^ {*} = \frac {1}{2} \biggl (\delta \biggl (3 - \frac {4 \delta}{1 + \delta - \Delta} \biggr) + \Delta \biggr), \quad \mathrm{and}
$$

$$
\pi^ {*} = n \left(\frac {1}{4} - \frac {(\delta + \Delta - 1) ^ {2}}{4 (1 + \delta - \Delta)}\right).
$$

As we saw from Proposition 1, here as well, we can verify that the price of a new game $( p ^ { * } )$ decreases as the secondhand market becomes more appealing to consumers $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ as the deltas increase), and the manufacturer faces a strong competition from the secondhand market. Unlike in the main model, however, here, the manufacturer relies only on the game revenue. As a result, as the secondhand market becomes more attractive to users, the manufacturer’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t predictably decreases.

One question that naturally follows—and is perhaps the most relevant one in our context—is whether the manufacturer can ever be better off with the secondhand market. When the secondhand market is nonexistent, the manufacturer sets the price of each game title at $\begin{array} { r } { p _ { b } ^ { * } = \frac { 1 } { 2 } , } \end{array}$ , the demand for each game is realized as $\scriptstyle { \frac { 1 } { 2 } } ,$ and the manufacturer makes a pro<sup>fi</sup>t of $\begin{array} { r } { \pi _ { b } ^ { * } = \frac { n } { 4 } . } \end{array}$ . With this benchmark, we can now compare the pro<sup>fi</sup>ts with and without the secondhand market. The following remark addresses the desirability of the secondhand market from the manufacturer’s perspective.

Remark 2. When the manufacturer sells only games, its pro<sup>fi</sup>t is always lower with the secondhand market than without. Mathematically speaking, $\pi ^ { * } < \pi _ { b } ^ { * }$

The proof for this remark is included in the online appendix. Remark 2 establishes that when the manufacturer sells only games, the secondhand market is not desirable, as the pro<sup>fi</sup>t will only be hurt by used games. Hence, in this setting, the manufacturer would choose to shut down the secondhand market if it has the technological capability to do so.

We add that there are the two competing effects of used games. On the one hand, the existence of the secondhand market can suppress the sale of new games; this is the competition aspect mentioned previously. On the other hand, the possibility of being able to sell the game in the secondhand market may encourage more consumers to buy the new game and incentivize the manufacturer to add a premium. These two forces have been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in prior literature as the substitution effect (e.g., Waldman 1996 and Ghose et al. 2006) and the resale effect (e.g., Swan 1970 and Miller 1974). What Remark 2 shows is that, in our model, the substitution effect of the secondhand market dominates the resale effect when the manufacturer sells only games. As a backdrop, Remark 2 enables us to clearly see the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of gaming console in its effect on the manufacturer’s attitude toward used games.

## 5.2. Fraction of Consumers Not Purchasing the Console from the Manufacturer

One of the key elements in our main model is that, with the console, the manufacturer is able to charge an entry fee to all consumers who want to play the games, new or used. However, some consumers may be able to avoid paying the entry fee to the manufacturer because they can procure the console through some other means $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , trade-in programs, used consoles). Though we do not consider secondhand consoles in the main model—because we are interested in the total game consumption during the entire lifetime of the console— in reality, consumers can procure used consoles, leading to lost console sales for the manufacturer. We ask, what happens when a fraction of consumers do not purchase the console from the manufacturer?

As it should be clear by now, the console is an important ingredient for the manufacturer’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t to be greater with used games than without. Hence, intuitively, as it becomes more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for the manufacturer to sell the console to consumers, the bene<sup>fi</sup>t of used games for the manufacturer would diminish. We demonstrate this intuition here.

We de<sup>fi</sup>ne $0 \leq \alpha \leq 1$ to be the fraction of all consumers who can play the games without directly purchasing the console from the manufacturer. Hence, α is the fraction of all consumers from whom the manufacturer cannot expect to collect the entry fee. Note that when $\alpha = 0 ,$ , we revert to our main model (where all consumers have to purchase the console from the manufacturer in order the play the games). When $\alpha = 1$ , we obtain our no-console case described in Section 5.1.

We adopt most of the modeling elements from the main model. The one major difference is that, in the main model, there are some consumers who would not purchase the console because the utility they would enjoy from playing the games is not enough to justify purchasing the console; the entry fee imposed by the console deters some consumers from entering, and this is clear from the gap between $v _ { 3 }$ and $v _ { 4 }$ in the main model (see Section 3.1 and Figure 2(a)). However, here, α fraction of consumers are not deterred by the entry fee. Hence, compared with the main model, the additional α fraction of consumers between $v _ { 3 }$ and $v _ { 4 }$ enter to play the games without purchasing the console. At the same time, the manufacturer loses α fraction of its console sales. Accordingly, we can obtain the following demand expressions:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & q _ {n} = 1 - \frac {s}{1 - \Delta}, \\ & q _ {n s} = \frac {s}{1 - \Delta} - \frac {p - 2 s}{\Delta - \delta}, \\ & q _ {u} = \bigg (\frac {p - 2 s}{\Delta - \delta} - \frac {c - m + n s}{n \delta} \bigg) + \alpha \bigg (\frac {c - m + n s}{n \delta} - \frac {s}{\delta} \bigg), \\ & q _ {c} = (1 - \alpha) \bigg (1 - \frac {c - m + n s}{n \delta} \bigg). \end{array}
$$

Equating $q _ { n s }$ with $q _ { u }$ gives us the market clearing price s in the secondhand market. $\mathbf { A } \mathbf { s }$ in the main model, the manufacturer maximizes its pro<sup>fi</sup>t by choosing an optimal price for the new game and also for the gaming console, resulting in the following optimal $p ^ { * }$ , $c ^ { * } ,$ and $\pi ^ { * } { \mathrm { : } }$ :

$$
\begin{array}{l} p ^ {*} = \frac {\alpha (1 - \Delta) (\delta + \Delta) + \delta (2 - \delta - \Delta)}{2 (\alpha (1 - \Delta) + \delta)}, \\ c ^ {*} = \frac {1}{2} \bigg (\frac {n \delta (\delta + \Delta - 1)}{\alpha (1 - \Delta) + \delta} + m \bigg), \quad \text {and} \\ \pi^ {*} = \Bigg (\frac {(1 - \alpha) \Big (\delta (4 - \delta - 3 \Delta) - \alpha \Big (\delta - (1 + \delta) \Delta + \Delta^ {2} \Big) \Big)}{4 n \delta (\Delta (1 - \Delta) + 2 \delta (1 - \Delta) + \delta (1 - \delta))} \Bigg) m ^ {2} \\ \qquad + \frac {1}{2} (1 - \alpha) m + \Bigg (\frac {\delta - \alpha \Big (\delta^ {2} - 2 \delta (1 - \Delta) - \Delta (1 - \Delta) \Big)}{4 (\alpha (1 - \Delta) + \delta)} \Bigg) n. \end{array}
$$

We can verify that setting $\alpha = 0$ yields the same results as in the main model, and setting $\alpha = 1$ leads to the results from the no-console case (except for $c ^ { * } ,$ which is irrelevant because the price of console does not enter anyone’s decision calculus in the no-console case). Basically, what we have here is a situation somewhere between the case described in the main model and the no-console case. From these expressions, we make the following observation.

Remark 3. In a setting where α fraction of the potential consumers do not purchase the console from the manufacturer, as α increases, $p ^ { * }$ increases, $c ^ { * }$ decreases, and $\pi ^ { * }$ decreases.

What the overall pattern illustrates is intuitive; as it becomes more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for the manufacturer to leverage its gaming console, the manufacturer inches toward its pricing strategy in the no-console case. At the same time, the pro<sup>fi</sup>t decreases because the effectiveness of the console as a pricing apparatus diminishes.

We conclude this section with the pro<sup>fi</sup>t comparison against the benchmark case (where the secondhand market is forcibly shut down); see the online appendix for proofs. The following remark highlights the results.

Remark 4. In a setting where α fraction of the potential consumers do not purchase the console from the manufacturer, there exists a threshold $m _ { t h } \ge 0$ such that when $m > m _ { t h } ,$ the manufacturer’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t is higher with the secondhand market than without. The threshold $( \mathrm { m _ { t h } } )$ increases in $\alpha ,$ and it equals 0 when $\alpha = 0$

There are two points worth noting from Remark 4. The <sup>fi</sup>rst—and more obvious—point is that when all consumers have to purchase the console from the manufacturer in order to play the games (i.e., when $\alpha = 0 )$ , we are back to our main model, and as long as the console has any nonzero intrinsic value (i.e., $m > 0 )$ , the manufacturer bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from used games. The second point is that when some consumers procure their consoles through means other than directly purchasing from the manufacturer, the intrinsic value of the console has to be suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently large—above a certain threshold—for the manufacturer to be better off with the secondhand market. This demonstrates the importance of console’s intrinsic value when it becomes more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for the manufacturer to leverage the console in its two-part tariff scheme; as less people pay the entry fee to the manufacturer, the more valuable the console has to be for the manufacturer to consider the secondhand market favorably.

## 5.3. Console Manufacturer and Game Studio

In our main analysis, we assume that the manufacturer prices both the games and the console. As explained earlier, though this conceptualization is simple, it is an appropriate abstraction of many real-world settings. However, in other situations, the console may be produced by one <sup>fi</sup>rm whereas the games are developed by another; we refer to them as the “manufacturer” and the “game studio,” respectively. Our main investigation here is whether both <sup>fi</sup>rms can be better off with used games.

One possible setting is where the manufacturer and the game studio collaborate, but both p and c are still decided by the manufacturer. For such settings, the answer to our main question here is rather obvious: if the $" \mathrm { p i e ^ { \prime \prime } }$ is bigger with the secondhand market than without, then the manufacturer and the game studio can split that extra portion and both be happy. The question reduces to the matter of how they split that extra portion of the pie.

Perhaps the more interesting case is where c is decided by the manufacturer and p by the game studio. For instance, independent game developers often get to price their games and pay royalty—the $\beta \ ( \in ( 0 , \bar { 1 } ) )$ fraction of the game revenue—to the company that owns the gaming brand. In such cases, the game and console prices are determined by different <sup>fi</sup>rms. We borrow many of the elements from the main model; the demand expressions and the market clearing s in the subgame equilibrium are all the same. The point of divergence is in the manufacturer’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t-maximization problem as well as that for the game studio, both of which are formally described as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \max _ {c > 0} \pi_ {m} (p, c) = \max _ {c > 0} n \beta (q _ {n} + q _ {n s}) p + (q _ {c}) c, \text {and} \\ \max _ {p > 0} \pi_ {s} (p, c) = \max _ {p > 0} n (1 - \beta) (q _ {n} + q _ {n s}) p, \end{array}
$$

where $\pi _ { m }$ is the manufacturer’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t and $\pi _ { s }$ is the game studio’s. The optimal $p ^ { * } , c ^ { * } - \mathsf { a s }$ well as the resulting $\pi _ { m } ^ { * }$ and $\pi _ { s } ^ { * } -$ can be obtained by simultaneously solving $\begin{array} { r } { \frac { { \partial { \ddot { \pi } } _ { m } } } { { \partial c } } = 0 } \end{array}$ and $\begin{array} { r } { \frac { \partial \pi _ { s } } { \partial p } = 0 } \end{array}$ . The actual expressions are long and unruly, so we leave them in the online appendix (together with the proof for the next remark).

Now, our main question is, can both the manufacturer and the game studio bene<sup>fi</sup>t from used games even when $p ^ { * }$ and $c ^ { * }$ are separately decided by the two companies (as opposed to the manufacturer deciding on both)? When the secondhand market is shut down, the demands are the same as in the solution behind Lemma 3, and the two <sup>fi</sup>rms simultaneously decide on the optimal prices by solving the pro<sup>fi</sup>t maximization problem as previously speci<sup>fi</sup>ed. We note our main <sup>fi</sup>nding as follows.

Remark 5. In a setting where the manufacturer sets the price of the console (c∗) and the game studio that of the games $( p ^ { * } )$ , and the game studio pays $\beta$ fraction of its revenue to the manufacturer as the royalty fee, both the manufacturer and game studio can be better off with used games than without.

Hence, even in a situation where the console price and the game price are determined by two separate <sup>fi</sup>rms (the manufacturer and the game studio, respectively), we see that both <sup>fi</sup>rms can be better off with the secondhand market. Note that there are some similarities between the no-console manufacturer in Section 5.1 and the game studio here. Both entities decide on the game price, and their only source of revenue is game sales. Despite these similarities, there is one fundamental difference between the two entities: the game studio’s user base consists of only those who purchase the console (i.e., pay the entry fee), whereas the no-console manufacturer’s user base consists of all consumers. In other words, the game studio’s pricing decision is affected by the console price (set by the manufacturer), whereas the no-console manufacturer in Section 5.1 does not have any console price to consider when deciding on its game price. As a result, although the no-console manufacturer always prefers to shut down the secondhand market, the game studio could actually bene<sup>fi</sup>t from used games. This illustrates that the console aspect affects not only the manufacturer’s attitude toward used games but also the game studio’s.

## 5.4. Games with Network Effect

In the main model, we do not consider any network effect. In reality, however, many games exhibit a positive network effect; as more consumers join and play the game, the value of the game increases. This is especially true for online games, where gamers collaborate with, play against, and socialize with others. We would like to see whether our results extend to such a setting as well.

For our analysis, we assume that the utility a consumer expects from each of the options (i.e., Buy New $\&$ Keep, Buy New $\&$ Sell, or Buy Used) is the same as what we have in the main model with the additional utility of $\Gamma > 0$ , which denotes the expected network effect (Raub and Weesie 1990, Etzion and Pang 2014). For instance, the expected utility from Buy New $\&$ Keep option is $v - p + \Gamma$ . Essentially, the network effect adds $\Gamma ^ { \ ' }$ to the net utility a consumer would enjoy from a game title. As in the main model, let $v _ { 4 }$ be the customer type who is indifferent between purchasing the console and not. From the respective IR constraint $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ $\mathrm { I R } _ { c } : n ( v _ { 4 } \delta - s + \Gamma ) + m - c = 0 ) ,$ , we obtain $\begin{array} { r } { v _ { 4 } = \frac { c - m + n s - n \Gamma } { n \delta } . } \end{array}$ Hence, although the $q _ { n }$ and $q _ { n s }$ expressions remain the same as in the main model (because the additional Γ values cancel out in IC constraints), $q _ { u }$ and $q _ { c }$ become

$$
q _ {u} = \frac {p - 2 s}{\Delta - \delta} - \frac {c - m + n s - n \Gamma}{n \delta}, \mathrm{and}\tag{21}
$$

$$
q _ {c} = 1 - \frac {c - m + n s - n \Gamma}{n \delta}.\tag{22}
$$

We consider the network effect to be proportional to the total number of consumers who play the game, new or used. Hence, $\Gamma = \gamma ( 1 - v _ { 4 } )$ , where $\gamma > 0$ represents the strength of the network effect. Therefore, in a ful<sup>fi</sup>lled expectations equilibrium, we have

$$
\Gamma = \gamma (1 - v _ {4}) = \gamma \bigg (1 - \frac {c - m + n s - n \Gamma}{n \delta} \bigg).
$$

Solving this, we obtain the equilibrium Γ as follows:

$$
\Gamma = \frac {\gamma (n (s - \delta) + c - m)}{n (\gamma - \delta)}.\tag{23}
$$

After substituting Γ in (21) and (22) with (23), for the rest of the solution process, we follow the same steps from the main model (i.e., solve $q _ { n s } = q _ { u }$ to obtain the market clearing $s ,$ and we solve the manufacturer’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t maximization problem as speci<sup>fi</sup>ed in (16)).

From the equilibrium outcomes, we make the following observations. First, both $p ^ { * }$ and $c ^ { * }$ are exactly the same as those from our main model: $\begin{array} { r } { p ^ { * } = \frac { 1 } { 2 } ( 2 - \delta - } \end{array}$ $\Delta ) ,$ , and $c ^ { * } = { \textstyle \frac { 1 } { 2 } } ( n ( \delta + \Delta - 1 ) + m )$ : This also implies that the prices do not depend on γ whatsoever; the strength of the network effect has no bearing on the manufacturer’s pricing strategy. The place where we see the bene-<sup>fi</sup>t of the network effect is in the manufacturer’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t:

$$
\pi^ {*} = \frac { \begin{array}{c} m ^ {2} (4 - \delta - 3 \Delta) + n ^ {2} (3 \delta + \Delta - \gamma (2 - \delta - \Delta) ^ {2} \\ - (\delta + \Delta) ^ {2}) - 2 m n \Bigl (\delta^ {2} - (1 - \Delta) \Delta - \delta (3 - 2 \Delta) \Bigr) \\ 4 n \Bigl (3 \delta + \Delta - (\delta + \Delta) ^ {2} - \gamma (4 - \delta - 3 \Delta) \Bigr) \end{array} }.
$$

Here, we see that the manufacturer’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t increases as the strength of the network effect increases $\begin{array} { r } { ( \mathrm { i . e . , ~ } \ \frac { \partial \pi ^ { \ast } } { \partial \gamma } > 0 ) } \end{array}$ . We highlight these <sup>fi</sup>ndings in the following.

Remark 6. The optimal prices for the games and the console remain the same as those from the main model where no network effect was considered. Although the network effect does not in<sup>fl</sup>uence the manufacturer’s pricing strategy, it does increase the pro<sup>fi</sup>t $\begin{array} { r } { ( \mathrm { i . e . , } \frac { \partial \pi ^ { * } } { \partial \gamma } > 0 ) } \end{array}$

Next, to understand the manufacturer’s position regarding used games in this context, we compare the manufacturer’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t with and without the secondhand market (denoted by $\pi ^ { * }$ and $\pi _ { b } ^ { * } ,$ , respectively).

Remark 7. The manufacturer’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t is always greater with the secondhand market than without $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ $\pi ^ { * } > \pi _ { b } ^ { * } )$ . Moreover, as the network effect becomes stronger, the manufacturer’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t gain from having the secondhand market increases.

The proof is in the online appendix. We make two interesting observations from Remark 7. First, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the manufacturer can bene<sup>fi</sup>t from used games, demonstrating that the results from our main model do not critically hinge upon any assumptions around the network effect. In fact, it goes a step further; the second observation we make is that the manufacturer’s appreciation of used games only becomes stronger with the network effect. With a positive network effect, an increase in the user base (i.e., consumers of new games as well as used games) generates more value for consumers, and as a result, the positive effect of the secondhand market for the manufacturer becomes even greater.

## 6. Discussion

Whether used goods affect the manufacturers’ bottom line positively or negatively has been a long ongoing debate. The secondhand market is often welcome by consumers as it gives them opportunities to recoup some cost by reselling the goods and to purchase products at a bargain. However, for most manufacturers, the secondhand market has been an eyesore. It reduces the manufacturers’ monopoly pricing power and negatively affects the pro<sup>fi</sup>t, and manufacturers cannot monetize from all the transactions that take place in the secondhand market. Hence, it is not surprising that the manufacturers have been looking for ways to block buying and selling used goods. Whereas the secondhand markets for many types of information goods have been withering, notably, the market for used games have been going strong—and that is not because the game manufacturers’ hands are tied. For instance, both Sony and Microsoft possess patents to make it impossible for consumers to buy and sell used games. Curiously, however, both companies decided not to implement their patents, giving their tacit approval to the secondhand market. So what is special about the video game industry that could explain the manufacturer’s favorable attitude toward used games? What are the welfare implications of the used game market as re-<sup>fl</sup>ected in consumer surplus and social welfare? We seek answers to these questions in this study.

We <sup>fi</sup>nd from our analysis that the manufacturer in the video game market is able to employ a form of a two-part tariff through its console pricing as well as game pricing. When buying and selling used games becomes more appealing to consumers, though the game price decreases, the manufacturer can increase the price of the console. Interestingly, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the pro<sup>fi</sup>t of the manufacturer can increase even when the appeal of the secondhand market increases. In fact, the manufacturer is better off with the used game market than without when it offers a valuable console—a console that has intrinsic values beyond merely playing games. It signi<sup>fi</sup>es the importance of the gaming console in the strategy for the manufacturer facing competitive pressure from secondhand games. At the same time, however, our analysis shows that with a valueless console, the manufacturer shuts down the secondhand market using its pricing mechanism (rather than any technological interventions). Overall, these <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide explanations as to why Sony and Microsoft decided to leave the used game market intact when they had the necessary <sup>fi</sup>repower to completely shut it down. In addition, perhaps more interestingly, the increase in the manufacturer’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t does not come at the cost of reduced consumer surplus. In fact, consumer surplus is always higher with the used game market than without, and it increases—in tandem with the manufacturer’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t—as the used game market becomes more attractive to buyers and sellers. As a result, the overall social welfare increases as well.

Although we deliberately keep the main model as parsimonious as possible to obtain clear insights, we test our results under various different settings to see how they depend on some of the assumptions we make in the main model. By examining the no-console case, we reaf<sup>fi</sup>rm the importance of the console for the manufacturer to bene<sup>fi</sup>t from keeping the secondhand market; the manufacturer can no longer bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the secondhand market in such a case. In a more complex setting where the console price and the game price are set by two separate <sup>fi</sup>rms (the <sup>fi</sup>rst by the “manufacturer” and the second by the “game studio”), we <sup>fi</sup>nd that it is possible for both <sup>fi</sup>rms to be better off with used games, demonstrating a broader applicability of our results. In our other extensions, we consider a fraction of consumers who procure their consoles through ways other than purchasing directly from the manufacturer (e.g., tradein, used console), as well as the positive network effect of games (where gamers value the game more as more gamers join). All these additional scrutinies help us to understand the main mechanism behind our results better under different settings.

The console (i.e., platform) aspect is not present in many other information goods markets where the manufacturers do not sell both their contents and platforms together. Music and movies have been platform independent for quite some time now, and even ebooks—which were once device (i.e., e-book reader) dependent—can now be read on various devices, thanks to platform virtualization through apps (e.g., Kindle apps for phones, tablets, and computers). This is in stark contrast with the video game industry where consumers must also purchase the console to enjoy the games. This is perhaps why we do not observe a similar attitude of “tacit approval” to the secondhand market from information goods industries other than console-based video games.

In our model, the platform helps the manufacturer bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the secondhand market as all game users—regardless of whether they purchase the game new or used—must also pay for the platform. From this higher level of abstraction, manufacturers of other types of information goods may also bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the insights of this paper. In cases where the manufacturer owns a platform and all consumers must join the platform in order to consume the content—once again, new or used—the manufacturer can bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the secondhand market by charging a fee to join the platform (e.g., membership fee). And this platform need not be physical; it could also be an online platform. If the value of joining the platform to consumers goes beyond just consuming the content (e.g., social value of connecting and interacting with friends), then the manufacturer can expect a positive effect from the secondhand market on its pro<sup>fi</sup>t. As the technology advances and the development cost drops, we can expect the platforms to offer more value to consumers, just as they have in the real world. In that process—according to our Proposition 4—the manufacturer would bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the secondhand market even more.

This work has a few limitations, most of which stem from our effort to keep our model as parsimonious as possible. For instance, although we consider the deltas to be exogenous parameters in our work—and the main results do not depend on any optimal values for the deltas—it is possible for the manufacturer (as well as the game developers) to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the appeal of the secondhand market to consumers. Future research can endogenize the deltas to study the implications of such <sup>fi</sup>rms’ decisions beyond pricing. We also do not consider in-app purchases, which are rising to be another signi<sup>fi</sup>cant revenue source for the manufacturer. However, it is worth noting that considering in-app purchases would only bolster the positive effect of secondhand market, similar to our extension where the network effect is incorporated, as the manufacturer will care less about how the consumers got the game, as long as they are playing the game and paying through in-app purchases.

One <sup>fi</sup>nal note is in order. Even for console-based video games, many game titles are now available for download, and for those digital games, reselling is generally not possible—for now. The current limitation on reselling digital goods is due to a legislative barrier, not a technical one. Both Amazon and Apple now own patents for used goods markets for digital objects, and there have already been several independent movements toward enabling secondhand markets for digital goods. Also, as mentioned in the opening, a recent ruling states that European Union law allows Steam users to resell their digital games. Now, our analysis indicates that for markets where the manufacturer has little control over the platform, the secondhand market for digital goods is unlikely to gain support from the manufacturers. However, for console game markets, it just might, and the secondhand market may deserve a second look from the game manufacturers. The current market observations tell us that, from a technological standpoint, the information goods industry as a whole has the capability to start a secondhand market as well as shut it down altogether. Our study, although largely centered on the context of video game markets, provides general guidance that different manufacturers should consider in their strategy regarding the secondhand market.

## 7. Conclusion

In this paper, we examine the socioeconomic implications of used games by taking a closer look at gaming consoles. In particular, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that a valuable console is an important ingredient for the manufacturer to bene<sup>fi</sup>t from used games, a point we highlight with the backdrop of no-console and valueless console cases. Every additional game player—regardless of whether he or she purchases the games new or used—is a “1- up” for the manufacturer in its console sales. As the intrinsic value of gaming consoles increases, the manufacturer <sup>fi</sup>nds the secondhand market for games more favorable. Our work offers a new theoretical lens through which we can better understand interesting real-world observations from the speci<sup>fi</sup>c context of video game market. At the same time, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings lend insights for other contexts that share the basic structure of a valuable platform and content.
