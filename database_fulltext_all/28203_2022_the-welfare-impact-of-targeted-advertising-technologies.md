# The Welfare Impact of Targeted Advertising Technologies

Veronica Marotta,<sup>a</sup> Yue Wu,<sup>b</sup> Kaifu Zhang,<sup>c</sup> Alessandro Acquisti<sup>d</sup>

Abstract. We analyze the welfare implications of consumer data sharing, and restrictions to that sharing, in the context of online targeted advertising. Targeting technologies offer <sup>fi</sup>rms the ability to reach desired audiences through intermediary platforms. The platforms run auctions in real time to display ads on internet sites, leveraging consumers’ personal information collected online to personalize the ads. The online advertising industry posits that targeted advertising bene<sup>fi</sup>ts advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms (that is, merchants who want to target ads to the desired consumers), consumers who see ads for preferred products, and the intermediary platforms that match consumers with <sup>fi</sup>rms. However, the claims that targeted advertising bene<sup>fi</sup>ts all players involved have not been fully vetted in the literature. We develop an analytical model to analyze the economic and welfare implications of targeting technologies for those three players under alternative consumer information regimes. The regimes differ in the type and amount of consumer data available to the intermediary and to the advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms, and re<sup>fl</sup>ect the presence or absence of technological or regulatory restrictions to personal information <sup>fl</sup>ows. We <sup>fi</sup>nd evidence of incentive misalignment among the players, as the intermediary prefers to share only a subset of consumer information with <sup>fi</sup>rms, whereas advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms prefer having complete information about the consumers. As such, a strategic intermediary with the ability to control which information is shared during the auction can have an incentive to use only the information that maximizes its payoff, overlooking the interests of both advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms and consumers. The information regimes that maximize consumer welfare vastly differ depending on consumers’ heterogeneity along two dimensions: a horizontal dimension, capturing consumer’s heterogeneity in product preferences; and a vertical dimension, capturing consumers’ heterogeneity in purchase power. Consumers prefer none of their personal information to be used for targeting only in limited circumstances. Otherwise, consumers are either indifferent or prefer only speci<sup>fi</sup>c types of information to be used for targeting.

## 1. Introduction

The debate over the economic value of personal data has been lively in recent years. On the one hand, the advertising industry claims that the collection and usage of consumer data for the targeting of ads create economic win-wins bene<sup>fi</sup>ting all parties involved. Targeting, it is said, ensures that ad placements display content that consumers are interested in rather than ads that are irrelevant and uninteresting (AdExchanger 2011); advertisers achieve higher brand awareness and a greater chance of selling the product; and publishers—the entities selling and displaying the ads—win too, as being able to offer behavioral targeting increases the value of the ad placements and therefore their revenues (AudienceScience and

DM2PRO 2010). Accordingly, the industry has posited that overly stringent protection of personal information would hurt internet ad revenues, reducing the availability of free content and free services online (Information Technology and Innovation Foundation 2010). Furthermore, personalized targeting—the practice of tailoring advertising based on increasingly detailed information about consumers—may become much less relevant if less consumer data were available to advertisers. As a result, consumers may be actually worse off when their data are too strictly protected (Information Technology and Innovation Foundation 2010). On the other hand, advocates of privacy protection have argued that allowing <sup>fi</sup>rms to collect consumer personal information without any oversight may put consumers in peril (Federal Trade Commission 2012). Aside from malicious parties engaging in spam or identity theft, even reputable <sup>fi</sup>rms may capitalize on detailed personal information in manners that do not always bene<sup>fi</sup>t their consumers. An early example was provided by Amazon’s well-known 2001 price experiment: shoppers realized that Amazon was charging different users different prices for the same DVD (Dwoskin 2014). A more recent example is offered by Staples, years later, charging users differential prices based on their geographical location (Dwoskin 2014).

Although industry arguments seem, at <sup>fi</sup>rst consideration, plausible, claims that targeted advertising is an economic win-win for multiple stakeholders (publishers, merchants, consumers, and—of course—the advertising intermediaries (AudienceScience and DM2PRO 2010) have not been fully vetted in the literature. In fact, the extent to which the different stakeholders bene<sup>fi</sup>t (or not) from the collection and usage of consumer data in online advertising remains unclear. Furthermore, if and to what extent restrictive policies, technologies, or regulations on targeting will bene<sup>fi</sup>t consumers and/or harm the advertising industry (and, in so doing, ultimately penalize consumers themselves) is still uncertain.

In this paper, we develop a theoretical framework to investigate those trade-offs. Our goal is twofold. First, we analyze the welfare impact of data sharing technologies—speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, how the economic value produced by targeting is allocated to different stakeholders. Second, we analyze the changes in welfare allocation of restrictions to those <sup>fl</sup>ows. We do so in the context of targeted advertising. We focus on programmatic, targeted advertising—the “machine based buying and selling of digital media including auction based methods” such as real-time bidding (RTB) (Interactive Advertising Bureau 2014, p. 7), which, nowadays, constitutes almost 74% of the U.S. digital display ad spending (eMarketer 2017).

Online targeted advertising is one of the most common applications of the market for personal data (Tucker 2012). Targeting relies on the collection of vast amounts of consumer data to allow the delivery of increasingly personalized advertising messages. That collection can be curtailed in several ways (see Section 3). Companies (such as search engine Duck-DuckGo) can voluntarily and strategically limit the amount of information they collect about their users. Users can choose browsers that limit third-party data collection (such as recent versions of Apple’s Safar (Statt 2017)) or adopt variety of privacy-enhancing technologies (PETs) and tools to opt out of tracking. Finally, regulation (such as the California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA) and the European General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)) can limit companies’ access to users’ data by requiring them to obtain users’ explicit consent (opt-in). These various approaches (self-regulatory corporate policies, technological barriers to tracking, and regulatory solution emphasizing explicit consent mechanisms) are expected to affect industries and markets that heavily rely on personal data and pro<sup>fi</sup>ling, such as the online advertising industry (Tucker 2012).

Our model investigates these issues by focusing on how the ability to collect and use certain categories of consumer information, as well as restrictions to that ability, impact the economic welfare of three players: consumers, advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms, and an intermediary ad exchange that facilitates the buying and selling of targeted advertisements. In modeling the interactions among those players, we have two objectives in mind: (i) to analyze what we refer to as alternative consumers’ information regimes—which may be thought of as alternative scenarios that (due to self-regulation, technology, or regulatory intervention) differ in the type and amount of consumer data available on the market to target ads to consumers; and (ii) to compare the economic implications of these alternative consumer information regimes and uncover the regime that maximizes the economic interest of each of the players’ involved.

The consideration of different consumers’ information regimes represents a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant novelty in the data-privacy literature. Usually, restrictions on data are treated as binary in modeling work in this area: either information is available, or no (or very little) information is available. By modeling the existence of different categories of information that can have different effects on welfare, our paper contributes to the literature by providing novel and more nuanced insights on the effects of data restrictions. A second contribution to the literature is the examination of how the economic value of targeting in online advertising is allocated across different stakeholders; when and to what extent different degrees of protection maximizes consumers’ welfare; and to what degree the payoff of industry players—advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms and intermediary platforms—is impacted by restrictions on the collection and use of certain categories of information. Importantly, since we consider advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms separately from intermediary platforms, we can further investigate whether there is any misalignment of interest inside the industry itself.

We <sup>fi</sup>nd that not all personal data affect the economic outcomes of the targeting process in the same fashion. In our model, we distinguish between information that allows the inference of consumers’ product preferences and interests (for example, browsing data, including what articles the individual has recently read online—we call that horizontal information) and information that allows the inference of consumers’ purchasing power (such as information about an individual’s job—we call that vertical information). The distinction between horizontal and vertical information allows us to derive four information regimes (or regulatory scenarios) that differ in terms of the amount and type of consumer data that can be collected and used for the purpose of targeted advertising: no information, only horizontal information, only vertical information, or both.

The overall impact of targeting on consumers can be positive or negative depending on which type of information is used. The horizontal information, for example, can lead to a better matching of consumers with their preferred products (as greater knowledge about consumers’ preferences helps advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms choose their bids in an ad auction). The vertical information, for example, enables the advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms to serve more consumers. However, both types of information can lead to an offer-personalization effect because they allow the advertisers to recognize consumers with different product preferences and different purchasing powers.

The relative strength of the different effects of the two categories of information depends on the degree of consumer heterogeneity. By separating the economic outcomes of possible alternative information regimes, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings reveal—differently from previous works—that the information regime that maximizes consumers welfare can vastly differ depending on consumers’ heterogeneity. Consumers prefer complete privacy—that is, they prefer no information being collected and shared—only in speci<sup>fi</sup>c circumstances, when they are characterized by low degree of horizontal and vertical heterogeneity. Otherwise, consumers are either indifferent or prefer at least some information to be collected and shared. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, when consumers are characterized by high degree of vertical heterogeneity and low degree of horizontal heterogeneity, they prefer companies to know their reservation price for a given product (inferred through the vertical information) rather than their preference about products (inferred through the horizontal information). This result is in contrast with extant views arguing that consumers would always like sellers to know what product they want but not how much they are willing to pay for it (Varian 2009). This result also suggests that regulatory initiatives (such as the aforementioned CCPA in California and GDPR in the European Union), by granting internet users more granular control over what information organizations can collect about them and how they can use it, may allow individuals to select the degree of sharing (and subsequent targeting) that is more bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to them, thus positively affecting their welfare.

Additionally, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that the information regime (or regulatory scenario) that maximizes the intermediary’s economic interest can be the one where only the vertical information about the consumers is collected and shared with the advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms, whereas advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms would like to collect and use all the information available about the consumers to maximize their payoffs. The existence of this misalignment arises from the fact that the intermediary receives revenues from the sales of ad inventory and has incentives to drive up equilibrium payments by intensifying competition among advertisers (through withholding the horizontal information). This result implies that an intermediary platform that acts as a pro<sup>fi</sup>t-maximizing agent may decide to adopt strategies to increase its expected payoff to the detriment of the other agents involved in the process. In turn, this suggests that advertising business models built upon the use of increasingly sophisticated targeting technologies may not always be as effective for advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms—in contrast to the win-win argument af<sup>fi</sup>rmed by the advertising’s industry.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are expanded through four extensions to our model. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst, we allow horizontal and vertical information to be correlated, so that an advertising <sup>fi</sup>rm can infer one piece of information from the other. In the second, we consider the possibility that consumers may act strategically and decide to adopt privacy-enhancing technologies to conceal certain type of information. In the third, we allow consumers to have an outside option; and in the fourth, we allow the two advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms to be asymmetric. The major <sup>fi</sup>ndings under these extensions are consistent with those we present under the main model.

## 2. Related Work

Our work builds upon and expands two streams of research: the literature on online advertising and targeting and the literature on consumers’ data collection, sharing, and privacy.

## 2.1. Online Targeted Advertising

The literature on online advertising (and, more specifically, targeted advertising) has grown considerably in recent years. It spans information systems, marketing, computer science, and economics. Among the <sup>fi</sup>rst to investigate the notion of targeting consumers with personalized ads, Grosman and Shapiro (1984), Soberman (2004), and Iyer et al. (2005) analyzed the impact of targeted advertising (broadly de<sup>fi</sup>ned that is, not necessarily linked to online targeting activities) on <sup>fi</sup>rms’ pricing strategies and market outcomes. With the advent of online advertising and, more recently, programmatic advertising, the advertising landscape has become considerably more intricate, leading authors to focus on specialized areas and speci<sup>fi</sup>c components of the ecosystem. An important distinction has to be made between search advertising and display advertising. In search advertising, advertisers pay a fee to search engines to be displayed alongside nonsponsored or organic web search results (Chen et al. 2009, Ghose and Yang 2009, Liu et al. 2010, Yang and Ghose 2010, Zhang and Feng 2011). In display advertising, advertisements are shown on websites or social media through banners or other ad formats. Our study focuses on display ads. Much empirical work has been conducted in that area (Goldfarb and Tucker 2011, Goldfarb 2014, Hu and Shin 2016). We focus, here, on the theoretical literature.

The display ad market is a two-sided market. On one hand, advertisers buy ads on publishers’ websites to target speci<sup>fi</sup>c segments of consumers; on the other, publishers sell ad spaces on their web pages. In between, intermediaries facilitate the match between publishers and advertisers by managing data and running auctions for the advertisement allocation. Theoretical works in this area have usually focused their attention on a speci<sup>fi</sup>c side of the market (see Choi et al. 2020 for a review): either on the demand side (the advertisers that buy ads online and/or in the interaction between advertisers and consumers); or on the supply side (the publishers that sell ad spaced on their web pages) and intermediary platforms that facilitate the selling of such inventory.

Previous work focusing on the advertisers’ perspective has investigated advertisers’ strategies and bidding behavior during auctions when advertisers face constraints (Ghosh et al. 2009, Balsiero et al. 2015, Balsiero and Gur 2019) or when advertisers can learn their valuations overtime (Iyer et al. 2014). In our model, we borrow the traditional insight that advertisers bid their true valuation (Johnson 2013) and investigate how advertisers’ bidding strategies change when different categories of information about the consumer are available. In addition, previous work focusing on the publisher and intermediary perspective has investigated publishers’ decisions in terms of pricing scheme or information to share. de Corniere (2016) and Hummel and McAfee (2016) show that publishers may bene<sup>fi</sup>t from providing more information as advertisers tend to submit higher bids. Differently, Chen and Stallaert (2014), Levin and Milgrom (2010), and Fu et al. (2012) show that as more information is provided, the number of advertisers interested in a given impression can decrease, therefore lowering bids.

An important contribution of our paper, and one of the factors that distinguish our paper from the aforementioned literature, is to offer a more comprehensive analysis of the targeted advertising ecosystem by analyzing the strategic interaction between (i) competing advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms that want to target consumers and participate in online auctions in order to buy advertisements, (ii) a monopoly intermediary that runs auctions for advertisement allocation and implements the targeting, and (iii) consumers who see advertisements and make purchase decisions. In so doing, we can analyze the nuanced welfare implications of targeted advertising across these three diverse players.

Our paper is also related to the works that have analyzed the role of intermediaries and online platforms that act on behalf of advertisers or publishers (Turner 2012, Aseri et al. 2017). More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, our paper relates to works that study the incentive of online platforms to provide accurate information for targeting, or to act strategically by purposely providing inaccurate information. Hagiu and Jullien (2011) show how intermediaries can use consumers’ information to affect the matching between <sup>fi</sup>rms and consumers by directing consumers to af<sup>fi</sup>liated <sup>fi</sup>rms, even though those may not represent the best match. Relatedly, de Corniere and Nijs (2016), in line with Levin and Milgrom (2010), <sup>fi</sup>nd that though targeted advertising can lead to higher prices, improving the match quality between <sup>fi</sup>rms and consumers by revealing consumers’ information may not be optimal for the intermediary. Similarly, Bergemann and Bonatti (2015) <sup>fi</sup>nd that data providers may choose to restrict advertisers’ access to consumer data to increase their pro<sup>fi</sup>ts. Finally, Zhang and Katona (2012) suggest that the incentive of the intermediary to implement a precise targeting depends on the product market competition—when product market competition is low, the intermediary has an incentive to reduce targeting precision to affect advertisers’ bidding strategies.

Our paper contributes to this stream of literature by considering an intermediary ad exchange who can act strategically by deciding to withhold the use of certain categories of information. This allows us to expand existing results in at least two ways: (i) by investigating whether the intermediary would have incentive to withhold any particular category of information; and (ii) whether the intermediary’s incentive does or does not align with advertisers’ incentives and how this ultimately may affect consumers.

## 2.2. Consumers’ Data Collection, Sharing, and Privacy

Our model takes into consideration how strategic decisions made by advertisers and intermediary platforms can affect consumers who are presented with targeted ads. We are interested in investigating how the availability of different categories of information for targeting purposes ultimately impacts consumers’ (as well as other stakeholders’) welfare. As such, our manuscript also relates to the literature on consumer privacy and data sharing. Although the privacy literature is broad and diverse (see Acquisti et al. (2016) for a comprehensive review of theoretical and empirical research on economic aspects of privacy), our paper relates more closely to theoretical works that analyze the impact on market outcomes and social welfare of the collection and use of consumer data, as well as restrictions on those data <sup>fl</sup>ows.

In one of the earliest contributions to this <sup>fi</sup>eld of research, Varian (2009) suggests that consumers are generally better off when companies know their preferences, but not their willingness to pay for products. On the same line, Taylor (2004) <sup>fi</sup>nds that when companies can infer consumers’ preferences from tracking technologies and engage in price discrimination, consumers are worse off. In such situations, if consumers are not sophisticated, regulations that restrict the use of consumer data can increase consumer surplus. Johnson (2013) shows how the ability to perfectly identify consumers (and show them increasingly targeted ads) may not bene<sup>fi</sup>t consumers, as mainstream consumers may receive ads from niche <sup>fi</sup>rms but would prefer seeing ads from a mainstream <sup>fi</sup>rm. Calzolari and Pavan (2006) show how the transmission of personal data between companies can, in certain situations, reduce information distortions and lead to an increase in social welfare. Additionally, they suggest that even without regulatory intervention, it may be pro<sup>fi</sup>t maximizing for companies to offer privacy protection policies to consumers. In a similar fashion, Kim and Wagman (2015) show that <sup>fi</sup>rms ability to trade in consumer data can lead to lower prices (offered to the consumers) and increased welfare. Finally, Conitzer et al. (2012) suggest that if privacy protection is costly, consumer surplus and social welfare are maximized when privacy protection (restrictions on use of data) is not too strict. These theoretica works show how the disclosure and use of consumer information can have a different impact on social welfare depending on the context. In some cases, information exchanges are bene<sup>fi</sup>cial and lead to an increase in social welfare; in others, the use of information may be harmful and decrease consumers’ surplus.

The previous stream of literature, however, has tended to treat consumer information as an undifferentiated category, usually analyzing the impact of more or less information on the outcome of interest. In this paper, we expand on the literature by showing how different types of information can lead to different effects and can affect the overall economic outcome in different ways. The taxonomy we propose (horizontal versus vertical information) allows us to vary consumer heterogeneity along two dimensions: a horizontal dimension that captures the consumers product preferences, and a vertical dimension that captures differences in consumers’ purchasing power. In addition, we further expand the insights of our basic model by analyzing consumers’ choice to adopt privacy-enhancing technologies and their incentive to withhold certain categories of information.

Our differentiation between different types of consumer data, coupled with the simultaneous consideration of the welfare effects for three different players, leads to a set of wider and more nuanced insights than those previously proposed in the literature.

First, consumers’ preferences for information collection and sharing are heterogeneous: our results suggest that complete privacy—that is, a scenario where no information about the consumers is collected and shared—maximizes consumers’ surplus only in speci<sup>fi</sup>c circumstances. Rather, consumers are either indifferent or prefer only certain types of information to be collected and shared in different scenarios. Second, when consumers have the option to adopt privacy-enhancing technologies, we show that not all the consumers rationally choose to do so and that consumers tend to conceal only certain categories of information. Third, from the intermediary platform’s perspective, our results suggest that the intermediary platform’s payoff can be maximized when only the vertical information about the consumers is made available for targeting purposes. Additionally, we also <sup>fi</sup>nd that depending on the degree of correlation between horizontal and vertical information, there may be situations where the intermediary prefers to disclose no information at all about the consumers (in other words, would prefer a very inaccurate targeting). Furthermore, our results suggest that advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms would always prefer having complete information about the consumers. This leads to the conclusion that players’ interests in targeted advertising can be misaligned, and to a set of important policy and managerial implications, which we discuss later in the manuscript.

## 3. Institutional Details

Online advertising is a complex ecosystem of multiple interacting players. They include advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms (the companies that buy ads), publishers (the websites that sell ads), demand-side platforms (DSPs) and supply-side platforms (SSPs) (technology platforms that, respectively, manage advertising campaigns for advertisers and manage publishers’ ad inventory), and ad exchanges (which constitute a central component of the ecosystem). Ad exchanges are auction-based marketplaces that facilitate the buying and selling of ad inventory (through, for example, real-time bidding) and through which the targeting of advertisements is implemented.

Programmatic auctions such as real-time bidding have been introduced into the online advertising ecosystem in an attempt to bring more liquidity to the online advertising market. RTB allows advertisers to buy online display advertisement spaces in real time through ad exchanges. When a user visits the website of a publisher belonging to an ad exchange network, a request is sent to the ad exchange, which subsequently broadcasts a bid request to bidders (advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms). The request can contain various attributes that describe the impression and the user and from which it may be possible to infer what we have referred to earlier as horizontal information (user’s preferences) and vertical information (user’s purchasing power). The attributes can include (but are not limited to) user’s geographical location, user’s inferred or predicted interests, based on the user’s browsing behavior, as well as features of the publisher (the website that the user is currently visiting and on which the ad will be displayed), features of the ad space (such as size or position), and so on (Interactive Advertising Bureau 2015). Bidders (the advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms that want to buy ads) submit their bids based on the set of available information.

The amount and type of information available about a user may vary due to various reasons. One reason may be the existence of regulatory frameworks limiting the collection and use of certain categories of user’s information. A recent example is the General Data Protection Regulation, introduced in Europe with the purpose of limiting the tracking of users online without their explicit consent. Another reason is the adoption, by users themselves (or by the browsers they adopted), of privacy-enhancing strategies or technologies (such as tools for deleting cookies, opting out of online tracking, or anonymous browsing (Goldberg 2007) to hide browsing histories or protect other personal information).<sup>1</sup> Finally, and perhaps most importantly, some stakeholders may make strategic decisions regarding how much consumer information to collect, and how much of that data to share with other parties, for reasons that may range from marketing and competitive positioning to strategic control of critical assets. For instance, the search engine DuckDuckGo limits the amount of information collected about users;<sup>2</sup> Google and Facebook have limited—but not always stopped— third party access to user data (LeFebvre 2018, Shrivastava and Christopher 2018); and ad exchanges can decide how much consumer data to collect and how much of it to share with other agents, such as advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms (Abraham et al. 2020, Sun et al. 2016).

The model developed in this paper focuses on three types of agents and accounts for the possibility that the tracking of consumer data may be curtailed through any of the methods highlighted earlier. The model considers: (i) advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms—<sup>fi</sup>rms that wish to target their products to speci<sup>fi</sup>c consumers by buying advertisements through online auctions; (ii) an ad exchange—an intermediary platform that implements the RTB process and the targeting of the advertisements (for the rest of the paper, we will refer to this agent as the intermediary); and (iii) consumers— visitors of the website where the ad is displayed and to whom advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms are trying to target their products. Thus, advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms bid for a given consumer, represented by the collection of information that is available during the auction for the advertisement. Our model abstracts away other market agents, such as demand- or supply-side platforms. These agents offer advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms and publishers services and integrated platforms to help them manage the buying of online ads (demand-side platforms, or DSPs) or help them manage the selling of their inventory (supply-side platforms, or SSPs). Since the objective of these agents is to help advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms or publishers maximize their own objective function, and since those agents usually receive a <sup>fi</sup>xed fee for their services, we can abstract them away from the model without losing any key strategic interaction. Additionally, it is common for large advertising platforms (such as Google) to offer integrated services, for example, advertisers using Google AdWords have easy and direct access to the Google Ad Exchange and do not necessarily need to go through further intermediaries.<sup>3</sup> Another class of market agents that do not make a strategic decision in our model are content publishers (e.g., websites) that earn advertising revenues by displaying ads on their pages. We believe that in the context considered in this paper, the decision to focus on <sup>fi</sup>rms, the intermediary platform, and consumers is appropriate. Whereas publishers (the websites selling the ads) usually collect information about their own visitors, it is the intermediary platform that collects the most information about consumers (Bashir et al. 2016). The reason lies in the fact that a large intermediary interacts with a vast network of publishers, each allowing the intermediary to track users on their respective websites. By so doing, the intermediary is able to track the same user across different websites and compile richer pro<sup>fi</sup>les about users. When a user is then targeted on a speci<sup>fi</sup>c website, the entire set of information that the platform has been collecting throughout the network can be leveraged, increasing the targeting capabilities of a publisher. Note that, in order for the publisher to be part of the intermediary network and sell targeted ads through the auctions managed by the intermediary, the publisher has to allow the intermediary to track users on its websites. In our model, we consider the situation of a publisher that is already part of the intermediary’s network.

Our basic model considers alternative information regimes that differ in the type and amount of consumer information available in the market: no information, only vertical information, only horizontal information, and complete information.<sup>4</sup> As mentioned earlier, the amount of consumer data available for targeting—that is, the information regime—can be determined by both exogenous and endogenous factors. Exogenous factors include the existence of privacy regulations, through which policymakers restrict the collection and use of certain categories of data; endogenous factors include users’ choice to adopt privacy-enhancing technologies, and possible strategic choices of the intermediary platform implementing the targeting.

Accordingly, our paper <sup>fi</sup>rst considers and analyzes the case in which the realization of a given information regime is exogenous to the decisions of the players in Section 5 (that is, the intermediary cannot choose how much information to collect about consumers and it shares with the advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms all the information collected). Subsequently, in Section 6, we consider and analyze the case in which the choice of the information regime is endogenous to the ad exchange (that is, the intermediary is able to collect both horizontal and vertical consumer data and is able to strategically choose which to share with the advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms). Finally, in an extension (see Section 7), we further consider the endogenous strategic decision of consumers to adopt privacy-enhancing technologies to hide browsing histories or protect other personal information.

## 4. The Model

We consider two <sup>fi</sup>rms, $j = 1 , 2 ,$ that produce two different products at a constant marginal cost (assumed to be zero without loss of generality). Firms want to advertise their products online to relevant consumers and compete in second-price auctions to buy online advertisements. We focus on the informative role of advertising (Bagwell 2007): an ad makes consumers aware of the existence and the price of a product, an assumption commonly made in the literature (Johnson 2013). Although the two companies compete to buy advertisements, they do not necessarily compete on the product market. In fact, they may be selling, and therefore advertising, different products. For example, <sup>fi</sup>rm 1 may sell (and advertise) digital cameras whereas <sup>fi</sup>rm 2 may sell (and advertise) laptops. As formalized next, this implies that a consumer may like both products, leading advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms to compete for the consumer’s attention.

The market consists of a unit mass of consumers. It is worth noting that a consumer may have demand for both products. The number of consumers can be interpreted as the total number of views of ads. As a result, a consumer can purchase <sup>fi</sup>rm 1’s product after viewing <sup>fi</sup>rm 1’s ad on one occasion and purchase <sup>fi</sup>rm 2’s product after viewing <sup>fi</sup>rm $2 ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ ad on another occasion. Put differently, our model incorporates the possibility that a consumer purchases both goods. We simply assume that in any particular moment the consumer focuses on only one good.

We assume that, at any given point in time, consumers differ along two main dimensions: horizontal and vertical. The horizontal dimension describes whether a product <sup>fi</sup>ts a consumer’s needs. It can be interpreted as a snapshot of the consumer’s preference for a product at a speci<sup>fi</sup>c point in time. These preferences are context dependent and time relevant. For example, if a consumer already owns a laptop, the utility the consumer would receive from buying another may be low, and so we consider a laptop a bad <sup>fi</sup>t for the consumer in that moment. By contrast, if a consumer likes photography and is looking for a new camera, the utility of a camera may be high, and therefore we consider camera a good <sup>fi</sup>t for the consumer in that moment. Although preferences (which affect the utility a consumer expects to receive from a product) are essential to determine the consumer’s choice, so are prices. In the previous example, if the laptop was offered at a very discounted price, the consumer may rationally choose to buy it even though, all else being equal, in that moment, the camera would be a better <sup>fi</sup>t.

Consumer online tracking attempts to infer contextual and timely preferences, which can then be used through RTB to serve ads relevant to visitors to a site. This implies that, at any given time, each <sup>fi</sup>rm’s product may be a better <sup>fi</sup>t for some consumers than for others. Formally, denote <sup>fi</sup>rm j’s product <sup>fi</sup>t for a consumer by $m _ { j } \in \{ 0 , 1 \}$ , where $m _ { j } = 1$ implies a good <sup>fi</sup>t and $m _ { j } = 0$ means a bad <sup>fi</sup>t. We assume that $m _ { j } = 1$ with probability $\alpha ,$ which is assumed to be 0.5 for simplicity. We allow the two products to both be good (or bad) <sup>fi</sup>ts for some consumers—that is, for those consumers, $m _ { 1 }$ and $m _ { 2 }$ can both be 1 $( \mathrm { o r ~ } 0 )$ . Denote the correlation between $m _ { 1 }$ and $m _ { 2 }$ by $\rho \in \left[ - 1 , 1 \right]$ . At one extreme, perfect negative correlation $\rho = - 1$ refers to a case where for any consumer only one of the two products is a good <sup>fi</sup>t. At the other extreme, perfect positive correlation $\rho = 1$ refers to a case where for any consumer both products are good (or bad) <sup>fi</sup>ts. In general, for a proportion $( 1 + \rho ) / 4$ of consumers, both products are good <sup>fi</sup>ts; for another proportion 1 $\rho ) / 4$ of consumers, both products are bad <sup>fi</sup>ts; for a proportion $( 1 - \rho ) / 4$ of consumers, only <sup>fi</sup>rm 1’s product is a good <sup>fi</sup>t; for the rest $( 1 - \rho ) / 4$ of consumers, only <sup>fi</sup>rm 2’s product is a good <sup>fi</sup>t.

The vertical dimension broadly captures consumers differences in purchasing power. A consumer can be a low-valuation consumer or a high-valuation consumer. For a given product, all else equal, a low-valuation consumer has a lower willingness to pay (i.e., can afford to pay less) than a high-valuation consumer. We assume that a proportion ${ \bar { \boldsymbol { \beta } } } \in ( 0 , 1 )$ of consumers are high-valuation consumers and a proportion $1 - \beta$ are low-valuation consumers.

The difference between having a good <sup>fi</sup>t with a product and the actual amount a consumer can afford to pay is crucial in online settings. For instance, a user with a passion for cars may often browse websites about sports cars; therefore, one may infer that sports cars are a good <sup>fi</sup>t for the consumer. Nevertheless, that does not necessarily imply that the consumer can actually afford to pay for a sport car. In other words, a company cannot infer whether the user can afford a sport car or not (and therefore, whether it is a good target)

by simply observing that the user’s is reading an article about sports cars. As a consequence, the separation between horizontal and vertical information is fundamental. In online contexts, examples of horizontal information may include a user’s gender, which can help companies infer the user’s <sup>fi</sup>t with certain products (for example, makeup companies will be mostly interested in targeting female users); the user’s browsing information (for example, knowing that the user read an article about dog care will be relevant for companies selling pet products). Examples of vertical information include the user’s job, which can be used to infer an individual’s purchasing power; or the user’s location, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally at the zip code level (zip codes can be used to distinguish wealthy versus less wealthy neighborhoods). Note that certain pieces of information (for instance, age) may reveal both horizontal and vertical information. For example, a 20-year old consumer may have a lower purchasing power, as well as different preferences, than a 40-year old consumer. In our basic model, we <sup>fi</sup>rst assume that horizontal information and vertical information are independent, to illustrate our main intuitions. We then allow the two types of information to be correlated—that is, companies can infer, to a certain degree, both the purchasing power and the product preferences from the same piece of consumer information—in an extension (see Section 7).

A consumer’s reservation price for a given product—that is, the consumer’s maximum willingness to pay for the product—will depend both on the consumer’s product preferences (that is, whether the consumer is a <sup>fi</sup>t for <sup>fi</sup>rm 1’s product, <sup>fi</sup>rm 2’s product, both, or neither) and the consumer’s purchasing power (that is, whether the consumer is low or high valuation). Formally, the combination of horizontal and vertical dimensions creates eight segments (i.e., types) of consumers, differing in terms of both product <sup>fi</sup>t and purchasing power, as captured by the tuple $\left( m _ { 1 } , m _ { 2 } , t \right)$ , where $t = L , H$ represents whether the consumer has low or high valuation. We de<sup>fi</sup>ne a consumer’s reservation price for <sup>fi</sup>rm $j ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s }$ product as $z _ { t } ( m _ { j } )$ Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we set $z _ { t } ( 1 ) = v _ { t }$ and $z _ { t } ( 0 ) = w _ { t }$ , where $v _ { t } >$ $w _ { t } .$ In other words, $v _ { t }$ is the reservation price of a consumer for a product that is a good <sup>fi</sup>t, whereas $w _ { t }$ is the reservation price for a product that is a bad <sup>fi</sup>t. Thus, there are four possible reservation prices for the products, which are $v _ { H } , w _ { H } , v _ { L } ,$ and $w _ { L }$ , respectively, depending on preference <sup>fi</sup>t and purchasing power. The values of the parameters $v _ { H } ,$ w , $v _ { L } ,$ and $w _ { L }$ are common knowledge.

From our initial assumptions, it follows that $v _ { H } > v _ { L }$ and $w _ { H } > w _ { L }$ . For simplicity, we further assume that $v _ { L } / v _ { H } \equiv w _ { L } / w _ { H }$ . However, we do not make any assumption on the relationship between $v _ { L }$ and $w _ { H } .$ In other words, the price a high-valuation consumer is willing to pay for a product that is a bad <sup>fi</sup>t $( w _ { H } )$ can be higher or lower than the price a low-valuation consumer is willing to pay for a product that is a good <sup>fi</sup>t $( v _ { L } )$ . This makes our model <sup>fl</sup>exible, enabling us to analyze what happens when different consumers’ preferences and market con<sup>fi</sup>gurations are considered.

Note that we have assumed that consumers’ reservation prices, v , w , v , and $w _ { L }$ are the same for the two <sup>fi</sup>rms. In the online appendix, we discuss a model extension that allows the reservation prices to differ across <sup>fi</sup>rms. Whereas the <sup>fi</sup>rm with the higher consumer reservation prices may dominate the other <sup>fi</sup>rm in auctions, our results and intuitions hold in this model extension.

## 4.1. Information Regimes

As previously mentioned, in the basic version of the model, we assume that the type and quantity of pieces of information about the consumer available in the market are exogenously set—that is, they do not depend on consumers, companies, or ad exchanges. As an example, we can think of the case of a policymaker who introduces a regulation that prohibits (or allows) the collection and use of certain consumers’ information; or we can think of the availability (or absence) of technologies that, in fact, allow different degrees of consumer tracking. Since in our basic model we categorize information into two main sets, horizontal and vertical, this leads to four potential information regimes: four different policy scenarios that differ in the type and amount of consumer information available on the market.

The information regimes we consider range from a benchmark case where no information about the consumer is available, to availability of one dimension of consumer information (either horizontal or vertical information), to full information availability.<sup>5</sup> This implies that, when the consumer is online and browsing a web page, the intermediary receives a signal that may reveal certain information about the consumer, depending on the information regime considered.

## 4.2. Real-Time Bidding

Although advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms may or may not compete on the product market, they compete for consumers’ attention on the online advertising market by participating in second-price auctions facilitated by the intermediary. As formalized earlier, when a consumer is online, the intermediary receives a signal and passes this signal to the advertisers.<sup>6</sup> The signal can be informative about the consumer. The advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms, based on the set of information about the consumer contained in the signal, decide how much to bid for the ad and which price to advertise. The highest bidder wins and pays the second-highest bid. As such, the bidding price, and therefore the intermediary’s revenues from the auction, are affected by the information that is available about the consumer on the market. We formalize advertisers decisions and profits and intermediary pro<sup>fi</sup>ts next.

## 5. Analysis

In this section, we solve for and compare the equilibrium solutions for the four information regimes (no information, only horizontal information, only vertical information, and complete information). We assume that the intermediary cannot choose the information regime. In other words, the ad exchange cannot choose what type of consumer information to collect and share with the advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms: more or less information may be available in the market for reasons that are exogenous to the intermediary. For instance, regulation may prohibit the collection of certain types of consumer data, as mentioned earlier. The intermediary collects whatever information is available on the market about consumers and shares it with the advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms. (In Section $^ { 6 , }$ we consider a strategic intermediary that can collect both the horizontal and the vertical information but can decide which to share with the advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms.)

## 5.1. Sequence of Events

The sequence of the events is as follows:

1. Each consumer arrives at a website.

2. For each consumer, the intermediary receives a signal that the consumer is on a speci<sup>fi</sup>c website and forwards the signal to advertisers that may be interested in showing an ad to that consumer. The signal includes all the information about the consumer that is possible to collect on the market. Different types of information about the consumer may be available: no information, only the horizontal information, only the vertical information, or both.

3. For each possible signal, advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms decide how much to bid for the advertisement and select the price offer for the product. Recall that the pricing strategy can be implemented in practice by selectively showing customized, predetermined $\mathrm { o } \dot { \mathrm { f } } -$ fers to consumers.

4. For each consumer, a second-price auction is run to allocate the advertisement space. The advertising <sup>fi</sup>rm that submits the highest bid wins the auction, pays the second-highest bid and shows the ad to the consumer.

5. Upon seeing a product ad, each consumer decides whether to buy the product. The consumer buys as long as the price does not exceed the consumer’s reservation price.<sup>11</sup>

## 5.2. Equilibrium Solutions

To <sup>fi</sup>nd the equilibrium for the ad auction (and therefore the winning bid), we rely on existing equilibrium results in second-price auction theory (Vickrey 1961). Since the object being auctioned is a single, indivisible advertisement space, it is a weakly dominant strategy for <sup>fi</sup>rms to bid their true valuation (see, for example,

Johnson 2013). Each advertising <sup>fi</sup>rm follows this weakly dominant strategy and bids according to its true valuation of each consumer, which is the pro<sup>fi</sup>t the advertiser can get from that consumer if it wins the auction and obtains the right to show the ad for its product. Note that a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s true valuation of each consumer depends on whether the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s product is a good <sup>fi</sup>t for the consumer, regardless of the other <sup>fi</sup>rm’s product <sup>fi</sup>t. This implies that <sup>fi</sup>rms’ bidding strategies are not affected by the correlation, $\rho ,$ between $m _ { 1 }$ and m<sub>2</sub>.

Next, we analyze the equilibrium solutions for the four information scenarios described earlier. Proposition 1. For given values of the parameters, revealing the horizontal information has the following effects:

Proposition 2. Revealing the vertical information has the following effects:

The different effects that the different types of information have on market outcomes lead to a correlation between the underlying consumers’ market structure (the heterogeneity of consumers in terms of purchasing power and product preferences) and outcome in terms of consumer welfare. By contrast, a higher value for the parameter would imply a higher proportion of high-valuation consumers in the market and would correspond to an expansion of regions 3 and 4 (and a consequent reduction in regions 1 and 2).

We next look at the advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms’ preference, summarized in Proposition 4.

Proposition 4 (<sup>Advertisers</sup>’ <sup>Preference</sup>). Each advertiser’s expected profit is the greatest when there is complete information about the consumer.

The result contained in Proposition 4 suggests that <sup>fi</sup>rms’ expected pro<sup>fi</sup>ts are the highest when complete information about consumers is available. Intuitively, when <sup>fi</sup>rms can perfectly target consumers, any uncertainty is removed. When advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms know the consumer preference and also each consumer’s reservation price for a given product, they can make a perfectly informed decision about which consumers to target and which price offer to show. The horizontal heterogeneity among consumers lowers bidding cost and ensures that each <sup>fi</sup>rm is left with a positive surplus after paying the bidding cost.

## 6. Strategic Intermediary

In this section, we consider the case of an intermediary that can strategically choose which information (horizontal and/or vertical) to share with advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms during the auction process. We investigate which information scenario maximizes the intermediary’s expected payoff and, therefore, which choice a strategic intermediary would make in equilibrium. This allows us to answer a series of interesting questions, such as: When would the intermediary choose an information regime that also maximizes consumer welfare? When would a policymaker interested in maximizing consumer welfare want to intervene and correct the information regime preferred by the intermediary?

The sequence of events for this variant of the model is similar to that described in Section 5.1, but with a change to step 2: the intermediary collects all the information available about the consumer and strategically decides which information to disclose to the advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms during the auction process. Although the results discussed in the previous lemmas and propositions do not change, the focus is now on the intermediary’s choice—that is, what is the information regime preferred by the ad exchange if it has control over what information is shared with the advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms.

Proposition 5 (<sup>Intermediary</sup>’<sup>s</sup> <sup>Preference</sup>). An intermediary that can strategically decide which information to disclose has the incentive to disclose only the vertical information when ρ is low; the intermediary prefers disclosing complete information when ρ is high.

Proposition 5 reveals an intriguing <sup>fi</sup>nding. Although consumers may prefer different information regimes under different parameter values, as shown in the previous section, the intermediary prefers revealing the vertical information when ρ is low (and prefers revealing the vertical information along with the horizontal one when ρ is high, discussed later). The intuition for the <sup>fi</sup>rst result relies crucially on the interaction between personal data and the ad auction. Revealing only the horizontal information, as we discussed earlier, improves the matching between the consumers and the products. Although this improves consumer welfare and increases advertisers’ willingness to pay, it reduces the competition in the auction for the advertisement. This is because each advertising <sup>fi</sup>rm would now bid higher for the more relevant segment of users and bid lower for the less relevant segment. This lowers the intermediary’s payoff due to the structure of the second-price auction.

On the other hand, revealing the vertical information facilitates price personalization and increases the advertisers’ willingness to bid. In contrast to the case of horizontal information, the vertical information simultaneously increases the willingness of both advertisers to bid for high-valuation consumers. This intensi<sup>fi</sup>es competition in the second-price auction and increases the intermediary income from the high-valuation segment. It is worth noting that, under certain conditions, revealing the vertical information can also increase the intermediary’s payoff from the low-valuation consumers, who would otherwise be priced out of the market and worth little to the advertisers. These effects, when put together, lead to an unambiguously positive effect on the intermediary’s overall payoff when the vertical information is revealed.

This theoretical <sup>fi</sup>nding seems to be consistent with empirical evidence. There have been various cases in which ad networks or exchanges have been found strategically limiting or even modifying information shared with advertising merchants about key aspects of the auctions (Davies 2018, Welch 2018). More closely related to vertical information is the case of some video platforms (such as iQiYi, a Chinese platform) that were reported by media outlets as showing paid members ads, touted as “VIP-exclusive recommendations.” Since paid members can be considered as H type (high valuation) on the platform, compared with the free users, this supports, at least partially, that those video platforms were trying to target ads focusing on the vertical information.<sup>12</sup>

In addition, Proposition 5 shows that when ρ is suf<sup>fi</sup>- ciently high, the intermediary is willing to provide not only the vertical information, but also the horizontal information. The intuition is that, as ρ increases, there are more consumers to whom both products are good (or bad) <sup>fi</sup>ts, and thus the product <sup>fi</sup>t information (horizontal) plays a more similar role compared with the vertical information. At the extreme ρ 1, the horizontal dimension reduces to another piece of vertical information about consumers. Therefore, the intermediary wants to provide the horizontal information as well to take advantage of the vertical information it contains.

For ease of exposition and to clearly illustrate the intuitions of the model, hereafter in this section we assume that ρ 1, which ensures that the horizontal information is orthogonal to the vertical information. By assuming ρ <sub>-</sub> <sub>−</sub>1, we do not lose much generality, as implied by our aforementioned main results—except for Proposition 5, all previous propositions stay the same for any ρ.

The results reported highlight the existence of a con<sup>fl</sup>ict of interest among the different agents. Firms will be better off if they are able to perfectly target consumers, but the results of our model suggest that, under certain conditions, the intermediary has an incentive to allow this targeting imperfectly by using only the vertical information, rather than both. Consumers may prefer some targeting, but never strictly prefer perfect targeting, depending on the parameters. Compared with the advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms, consumers always prefer less information to be disclosed.

Interestingly, there exist situations where consumers’ interests are aligned with intermediary’s: the results in Proposition 2 suggest that there are situations where consumer surplus is the highest when the vertical information is observed. When this is the case, the intermediary can be trusted to choose the socially optimal outcome for consumers—while being entirely motivated by pro<sup>fi</sup>t concern. In other cases, however, the intermediary’s choice may not coincide with the information regime that is optimal for consumers. This may either mean that the intermediary reveals too much information, or that the intermediary reveals the wrong type of information. In region 4, consumers do not suffer any surplus loss because they are indifferent with respect to which information regime is chosen by the intermediary.

Corollary 1 provides a set of guidelines to policymakers whose goal is to increase consumer welfare by intervening on the information regime. First, the results of the model suggest that there are situations where consumers are indifferent with respect to which information is available and used in the market. This happens in markets where consumers are highly heterogeneous (region 4), both in terms of product preference <sup>fi</sup>t and purchasing power. In such situations, a policymaker may not need to intervene as all of the different information regimes will lead to the same consumer surplus. Second, in markets where consumers’ degree of heterogeneity (both horizontal and vertical) is low (region 1), policy intervention may be needed, as consumers have a strong preference for their information being protected. Indeed, in situations where consumers are more homogeneous, revealing any type of information would lead to an unwanted product and price personalization that is not going to bene<sup>fi</sup>t consumers. These are the markets where consumers may bene<sup>fi</sup>t from a more stringent regulation of data collection and usage. Third, there are situations where consumers only want a certain category of information to be collected and used. This happens (i) when consumers are highly vertically heterogeneous but the degree of horizontal heterogeneity is low, and (ii) when consumers are highly horizontally heterogeneous but the degree of vertical heterogeneity is low. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst situation (region 3), consumers would like advertisers to know their vertical dimension (that is, their purchasing power) because the match improvement effect is not as important. In the second situation (region 2), consumers would like advertisers to know only the horizontal dimension but not the vertical one so to ensure a better product matching and a discounted price.

These <sup>fi</sup>ndings revisit and expand a conventional wisdom of privacy economics, whereby consumers would rationally want <sup>fi</sup>rms to know which product they like (so as to get relevant offers) but not how much they are willing to pay for it (so as to avoid price personalization (Varian 2009)). According to our results, there exist situations where the reverse happens: consumers may want to share their purchasing power, but not their product preferences.

Additionally, recall that advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms’ payoffs are maximized when complete information about consumers is made available to them. This creates a con-<sup>fl</sup>ict with the strategy of the intermediary platform, which prefers targeting only on the vertical information. Furthermore, which regime maximizes consumers’ surplus depends on the consumers’ level of heterogeneity. This, in turn, creates a tension between consumers’ interest and the industry’s interest, as well as a tension inside the industry (between the advertisers and the intermediary). Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that any policy or industry intervention aimed at restricting the use of consumer data should carefully consider the nuances of the situation. If the policy objective is to maximize industry pro<sup>fi</sup>t, any information about the consumers should be allowed to be collected and used; additionally, interventions aimed at increasing the transparency and accountability of the targeting process should be put into place, to avoid having contrasting incentives from the intermediary’s side push the outcome in another direction. On the other hand, if the policy focus is consumer welfare and protection, then the degree of intervention needs to be adequate and targeted to speci<sup>fi</sup>c categories of information, depending on the degree of consumer heterogeneity.

We should note that, in the long term, the strategic decision of the intermediary to only share certain amounts/type of information may lead to responses from advertisers and consumers—for example, advertisers may decide to leave the platform. At the same time, based on our intuition, we believe that most of the insights of our model would still be valid. From the consumers’ perspective, a long-term response could entail blocking ads, turning off cookies, or utilizing other privacy-enhancing technologies—a possibility that we consider in one of our extensions. From the advertisers’ perspective, a long-term response could entail joining another advertising platform. This would lead to competition among different platforms and the intermediary, in turn, to take that into account when strategically deciding which information to share with the advertisers—a direction that would be interesting to explore in detail in future research. That noted, in the long run, the intermediary’s incentives to align its information-sharing strategy with advertisers’ interests depend on the degree of competition it faces. Considering that the advertising platforms market is highly concentrated (according to the Interactive Advertising Bureau, in 2018, about 60% of Internet advertising revenues were concentrated in the top two ad-selling platforms (Graham 2019)), major advertising platforms may not, in fact, face <sup>fi</sup>erce competition.

## 7. Extensions

We extend our basic model in several ways. The complete extended models and associated detailed discussions can be found in the online appendix. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst extension, we allow the two dimensions of information, horizontal and vertical, to be correlated, so that advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms can infer consumers’ horizontal information from their vertical information, and vice versa. Compared with our basic model, consumers preferences are now more complex, because the horizontal information is associated with the bene<sup>fi</sup>t and cost of the vertical information, and vice versa. For instance, in region 2, consumers prefer revealing the horizontal information in the basic model but they may bene<sup>fi</sup>t from withholding all information in this extension. This is because as <sup>fi</sup>rms can infer the vertical information from the horizontal information, the price personalization effect rises, lowering consumer surplus in the horizontal information regime. In this extension, advertisers still prefer having complete information about consumers as in the basic model. However, because of the inferred horizontal information, the intermediary may prefer withholding information as opposed to the vertical information in our basic model.

In the second extension, we allow the possibility that a proportion of consumers strategically choose to adopt privacy-enhancing technologies to conceal information. Compared with our basic model, consumers’ preference are preserved, except in region 4. Instead of being indifferent between the four information regimes, in this extension consumers prefer revealing the vertical information. This is because in the basic model consumers earn zero surplus in region $^ { 4 , }$ whereas in this extension, strategic consumers with high purchasing power can use privacy-enhancing technologies to hide their purchasing power and gain positive surplus. In the presence of privacy-enhancing technologies, advertisers may prefer the horizontal information instead of complete information: the vertical information incentivizes strategic consumers with high purchasing power to protect their privacy, leading to two negative effects on advertisers’ pro<sup>fi</sup>tability. First, the adoption of privacy-enhancing technologies may create a mismatch effect—in that strategic consumers give up the matching bene<sup>fi</sup>t associated with their horizontal information. Second, the adoption of privacy-enhancing technologies may create a downward switching effect—without knowing their identities, advertisers may not be willing to charge those strategic consumers a higher price. Because of these two negative effects, the intermediary’s pro<sup>fi</sup>tability is also limited, and thus it may prefer withholding information.

In the third extension, we allow consumers to have an outside option. The outside option limits the pricing power of the advertisers. As a result, consumers’ willingness to pay is reduced to $v _ { t } - u _ { 0 }$ and $w _ { t } - u _ { 0 } , t = L , H$ . This raises consumers’ vertical and horizontal heterogeneity $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ lowering $\left( w _ { H } - u _ { 0 } \right) ^ { + } / \left( v _ { H } - u _ { 0 } \right)$ and $\left( v _ { L } - u _ { 0 } \right) ^ { + } /$ $\left( v _ { H } - u _ { 0 } \right) )$ ). All the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from the basic model are qualitatively preserved in this extension.

In the fourth extension, we allow the two advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms to be asymmetric. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, there can be more consumers who strictly prefer one <sup>fi</sup>rm than those who strictly prefer the other <sup>fi</sup>rm. Although in this extension, the advantaged <sup>fi</sup>rm can sometimes cover the entire market if the horizontal information is not revealed, all of our results in the basic model hold qualitatively. This is because even though the advantaged <sup>fi</sup>rm can serve all consumers and earn positive surplus in the absence of horizontal information, the disadvantaged <sup>fi</sup>rm’s bid is relatively high. This, in turn, leaves a low pro<sup>fi</sup>t margin for the advantaged <sup>fi</sup>rm.

## 8. Discussion

We developed an analytical framework with the goal of providing a structured approach to understanding the role of personal data in welfare allocation in a market for targeted advertisement. The model focuses on the interaction among three types of agents: advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms (who compete with each other for consumer attention), consumers (the online users, who visit websites, are shown targeted ads and purchase products online), and an intermediary (the ad exchange platform). We represent consumers as heterogeneous along both a horizontal dimension (e.g., product <sup>fi</sup>t) and a vertical dimension (e.g., purchasing power). Advertisers buy advertisements by participating in real-time auctions run by the intermediary. Therefore, rather than considering personal data as binary (that is, existing or nonexisting), our approach takes into consideration the existence of different categories of personal data. This leads to a set of novel, nuanced insights.

First, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the information regime that maximizes consumer welfare differs depending on consumers’ degree of horizontal and vertical heterogeneity: a no information scenario—that is, a scenario where no information about consumers is collected and shared—is optimal for consumers only in speci<sup>fi</sup>c circumstances. For most cases, consumers prefer either the horizontal information or the vertical information being collected and shared. Interestingly, our results point to a direction different from existing views: there are cases where consumers do want companies to know how much they are willing to pay for products.

Additionally, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings reveal a nuanced set of insights regarding the potential misalignment of economic interests among ad exchanges, advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms, and consumers. They highlight that there exist circumstances where the information regimes preferred by the different players coincide, whereas there are others where the economic incentives vastly differ, leading to several policy and managerial implications. A notable implication is that a strategic intermediary able to collect both vertical and horizontal information about consumers, and also able to decide how much to share with the advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms for targeting purposes, may strategically choose an informationsharing policy that maximizes its pro<sup>fi</sup>ts at the cost of advertisers’ or/and consumers’ welfare. Our analysis revealed that there exist scenarios where the intermediary platform’s payoff is maximized when the vertical information about the consumers is collected and shared. For the advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms, revealing only the vertical information is not optimal, as the payoff is instead maximized when all the information (horizontal and vertical) about the consumers is shared. In situations where such an intermediary has an incentive to strategically and selectively reveal certain consumer data but not others, regulation of data exchanges may actually increase consumers’ welfare and/or <sup>fi</sup>rms payoffs. As described earlier, this theoretical <sup>fi</sup>nding seems to <sup>fi</sup>nd some empirical support: there have been cases in which ad networks or exchanges have been found strategically limiting or even modifying information shared with advertising merchants about key aspects of the auctions (Davies 2018, Welch 2018).

From the consumer protection policy perspective, our analysis suggests that not all personal data should be treated the same. It may be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to enforce protection of some personal information and let the market handle other types of data. We should note, however, that our argument focuses on economic considerations, not ethical and security ones.

From the advertising’s <sup>fi</sup>rms perspective, our results have a number of managerial implications. First, the advertising industry has often presented online targeted advertising as an economic win-win. Nevertheless, the existence of incentive misalignment among the players involved in the advertising process, as highlighted by our analysis, seems to contrast the claim. Our results suggest that the industry’s payoff (given by the sum of the advertiser’s payoff and the intermediary’s payoff) would be maximized when complete information about consumers is available. Contrary, intermediary platforms may not have the incentive to completely leverage the targeting technology by sharing and using complete information about consumers (both vertical and horizontal)— information that would also maximize <sup>fi</sup>rm’s payoffs. Stated differently, the intermediary platform may have the incentive to target consumers using only certain features, at the detriment of advertising’s <sup>fi</sup>rms, who should take this under consideration when forming their strategies about online advertising investments. And indeed, a number of large advertisers have started reducing their digital ad spending on the claim that ad exchanges need to provide more transparency in their practices (Vranica 2018).

Second, from a more practical standpoint, our results can inform how advertisers should deal with the increasing adoption of privacy-enhancing technologies by consumers. Our analysis suggests that when individuals adopt PETs, advertisers may increase their pro<sup>fi</sup>ts through imperfect targeting. In other words, advertisers can bene<sup>fi</sup>t from imperfect targeting—that is, targeting consumers based just on a subset of information from which they can infer the consumers’ more recent preference about products (for example, recent browsing behavior). The intuition is that imperfect targeting can discourage consumers from using PETs and may bene<sup>fi</sup>t advertisers.

The proposed model has various limitations. The model takes into consideration the interaction between three types of agents: advertisers, intermediary, and consumers. The online advertising ecosystem is more complex and includes additional subjects. For instance, companies that want to participate in auctions for online advertising usually rely on a demand-side platform that serves advertisers or ad agencies by bidding for their campaigns in multiple ad networks automatically. On the other side, supplyside platforms serve publishers by registering their inventories (ad space) in different ad networks and accepting the most bene<sup>fi</sup>cial one automatically. Publishers themselves play a fundamental role in determining how much of their inventory is sold through programmatic advertising. Similarly, the ad exchange is modeled as a monopolist intermediary that acts undisturbed. This modeling approach captures the empirical reality that the advertising market is highly concentrated, with just a few ad-selling companies (such as Google and Facebook) that account for about 75% of the total revenues generated in the advertising market (Interactive Advertising Bureau 2015). However, a possible extension of the model would allow some degree of competition among intermediaries and consider the possibility that companies decide to enter different ad networks. Additionally, the consumer behavior is simpli<sup>fi</sup>ed to make the analysis tractable. Finally, we focus on the informative role of advertising—an ad makes consumers aware of the existence and price of a product. Future research could focus on the persuasive role of advertising and model the price competition among advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms.

Despite the highlighted limitations, the model provides nuanced insights on the mechanisms through which the collection and disclosure of personal data can affect the welfare of consumers and industry. By illustrating how tracking and sharing different types of consumer data can differentially affect the welfare of data holders and data subjects, these <sup>fi</sup>ndings can contribute to the ongoing industry and regulatory debate over the economic and social implications of the adoption of tracking and advertising systems. Notably, these results do not imply that the collection of consumer information should be completely prohibited—rather, they suggest that there is information that is bene<sup>fi</sup>cial for consumers to share, and other information that instead could be used by others to the consumers’ detriment. For that reason, regulatory interventions that aim at giving individuals more granular control over the collection and usage of their information (such as GDPR) may produce not merely privacy-increasing but also welfare-increasing outcomes for consumers—to the extent that they can allow the latter to selectively manage the degree of sharing and targeting that is most bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to them. Furthermore, what emerges from the model is a scenario alternative to the economic win-win often heralded as the likely and desirable outcome of the increased collection and trade of consumers’ data.<sup>13</sup> We <sup>fi</sup>nd, instead, that different market agents that operate in the ecosystem may have contrasting interests. In turn, such interests may create incentives for practices that are not transparent. For instance, an intermediary that has the power to control which type of information to highlight to other parties (such as advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms) during the auction process, may have the incentive to act strategically, by revealing the information that ensures the intermediary the highest expected return but does not maximize the industry’s payoff.
