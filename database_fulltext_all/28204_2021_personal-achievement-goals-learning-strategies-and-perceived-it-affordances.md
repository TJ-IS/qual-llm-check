# Personal Achievement Goals, Learning Strategies, and Perceived IT Affordances

Saggi Nevo,<sup>a</sup> Dorit Nevo,<sup>b</sup> Alain Pinsonneault<sup>c</sup>

Abstract. Goals are the prism through which actors perceive the affordances of technological artifacts. Yet, personal goals have not been differentiated conceptually, and the learning mechanism through which they shape individuals’ perception of affordances has not been examined. This paper addresses these gaps theoretically and empirically. Drawing on achievement goal theory, we conceptualize information technology (IT) affordances as goal oriented learning outcomes. We then develop a research model that describes several mediated pathways, in which the impact of personal goals on IT affordance perceptions passes through IT-focused learning strategies. The results of the empirical study support the theoretical model and depict three distinct pathways. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, performance-avoidance goals are positively associated with surface processing, which leads to perceptions of common in-role IT affordances. Performance-approach goals are positively associated with surface processing and effort regulation and these learning strategies lead to perceptions of common and specialized in-role IT affordances. Mastery goals are associated with deep processing, effort regulation, and peer learning, which are positively associated with perceptions of specialized in-role and extra-role IT affordances. The paper offers new insights on how and why employees perceive different IT affordances. The affordances we study are conceptualized and operationalized at a level of abstraction that can be used to study affordance per ception across actors, technologies, and contexts. The paper opens new avenues for future research on affordances and on related postimplementation phenomena.

## Introduction

Information technology (IT) affordances are possibilities for behaviors that an actor with a speci<sup>fi</sup>c goal can perform with a particular technology (Volkoff and Strong 2013). Because they emerge out of a relation between an actor’s goal and a technology’s materiality (Leonardi et al. 2019), IT affordances often diverge across organizational members, even when those individuals use equivalent technologies in similar contexts (Cousins and Robey 2015), belong to a particular profession (Strong et al. 2014), or are exposed to a distinct use frame (Leonardi 2013). Because affordances can enable and constrain behavior (Leonardi 2011), it is important to understand the source of this divergence to better foresee and manage the outcomes of IT implementations at the workplace.

Our knowledge of how and why individuals perceive diverse affordances is limited, especially in terms of understanding the role that individual goals play in learning about, and perceiving, affordances. In past literature, the goals of organizational members have not been differentiated conceptually, and their impact on individual affordances has not been theorized. Furthermore, the pathway from goals to IT affordance perception remains largely unexplored and is often depicted as direct and undifferentiated. We trace these issues back to two key assumptions that underlie extant affordance research. First, much of the literature presumes that individuals’ goals re<sup>fl</sup>ect their organization’s or group’s goals. We challenge this assumption because individuals can pursue personal goals that are different from the goals of the group or organization of which they are members (Vancouver and Schmitt 1991, Kristof-Brown and Stevens 2001). Second, existing research provides few details regarding how individuals perceive affordances, implying that affordance perception can be done in a relatively passive manner and without signi<sup>fi</sup>cant learning. We challenge this assumption because perceiving affordances associated with complex artifacts likely involves active and intentional learning (Gaver 1991, Hutchby 2001, Kaptelinin and Nardi 2012).

Motivated by this, this study aims to answer the following question: How do personal goals and learning shape IT affordance perception? To this end, we examine goals and learning in tandem because the manner in which individuals learn—that is, search for, exchange, and process information (Grossberg 2012)—is shaped by the goals they pursue (Poortvliet et al. 2007). We begin answering this question by <sup>fi</sup>rst problematizing prior IS research on affordances; we revisit and challenge the abovementioned key assumptions that shape affordance research. Next, we conceptualize three IT affordances that are differentiated based on the behaviors they enable employees to carry out at the workplace: common in-role, specialized in-role, and extrarole IT affordances. We then draw on a theory that focuses on goals and learning— namely, achievement goal theory—and develop a model that conceptually distinguishes performance-avoidance, performance-approach, and mastery goals, which employees can pursue. The research model traces several pathways from these goals through learning strategies (surface processing, deep processing, effort regulation, peer learning) to the aforementioned IT affordances. We test the model using sample data of working professionals whose organizations had recently implemented new IT.

The paper contributes to the literature on affordances and to information systems (IS) practice by differentiating employees’ personal goals and by paying close attention to the underexplored pathway from goals to IT affordance perception. It also puts forward a new typology of IT affordances that focuses on the potential workplace behaviors available to employees through organizational IT. These IT affordances abstract away from the particulars of any technological artifact and can be used to study how employees perceive affordances of different workplace technologies. The research model describes how employees who pursue different personal goals and use various learning strategies perceive different IT affordances within this typology, and the empirical study depicts three distinct pathways. These insights open up new avenues for research by drawing attention to, and exploring, users’ goals and learning strategies that may in<sup>fl</sup>uence other postimplementation phenomena, such as coping with IT and technology avoidance.

## Background and Literature Review

The theory of affordances (Gibson 1986) was developed to understand animal behavior and perception in the natural environment, and it pays special attention to perception based on available visual cues. The theory proposes that, although objects in the environment consist of physical attributes, what animals and humans perceive when they look at an object is what behaviors it affords them vis-a-vis their needs and\` goals (Gibson 1986, Stoffregen 2003). Hence, affordances are opportunities for goal-directed behavior emerging from a relation between an animal and objects in its environment (Chemero and Turvey 2007).

The framework of Gibson (1986) provides limited insights for the study of possibilities for behaviors afforded by complex, advanced technologies (Bærentsen and Trettvik 2002, Fayard and Weeks 2007). For such technologies, available visual cues are insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient for perceiving affordances without substantial intermediary stages involving exploration, inference, and memory (Gaver 1991). Perceiving the affordances of complex, socially constructed artifacts requires more than simply picking up available visual information; it requires active and intentional learning (Greeno 1994, Hutchby 2001, Kaptelinin and Nardi 2012).

IT affordances are possibilities for behaviors that are available to an actor by a technological artifact, given the actor’s goals (Markus and Silver 2008). That is, people’s goals shape what they perceive they would be able to do with the features of the technology (Leonardi 2013). Because actors have diverse goals they may perceive different affordances when they examine a technological object, and they may perceive the same affordances from different IT artifacts (Treem and Leonardi 2012, Karahanna et al. 2018). Because many IT artifacts are complex, opaque, arti<sup>fi</sup>cial, informational, multipart, and multifunction objects (Burton-Jones and Volkoff 2017), individuals need to engage in a learning process in order to <sup>fi</sup>nd out which behaviors they would be able to carry out (Strong et al. 2014). Accordingly, we may view IT affordance perception as the outcome of a goaldirected learning process.

We organized the literature on IT affordances using the three-step structured approach of Webster and Watson (2002) and created a concept-centric literature review. We searched for the major contributions in the <sup>fi</sup>eld’s leading journals and conferences and leading outlets in reference disciplines (e.g., Organization Science). Next, we went backward and reviewed the citations in the articles identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the previous step to <sup>fi</sup>nd additional relevant articles. Finally, we went forward and searched for papers citing the key articles identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the previous two steps. The result was a “relatively complete consensus of relevant literature” (Webster and Watson 2002, p. xvi). Within this body of research, we focused on empirical research to understand better what affordances we, as a <sup>fi</sup>eld, actually study and how we study them. Table 1 helps to problematize existing research on IT affordances by surfacing the two assumptions that this paper reexamines (Alvesson and Sandberg 2011). First, it demonstrates that individuals’ goals are assumed to echo those of the organization or group to which they belong or the objectives of the focal task (Seidel et al. 2013, Volkoff and Strong 2013). This assumption, which is not always articulated, means that individuals’ goals are frequently not studied by themselves and are approximated in IT affordances research (Leonardi 2011, Goel et al. 2013, Burton-Jones and Volkoff 2017). However, because personal goals often differ from the goals of the social entities to which individuals belong (Vancouver and Schmitt 1991, Kristof-Brown and Stevens 2001), using proxies in lieu of the actual goals of individuals is problematic. For example, we might encounter IT affordances that cannot be traced back to the employees’ proxy goals (Mesgari and Faraj 2012, Leidner et al. 2018, Steffen et al. 2019). Although IS research acknowledges that different actors can have, and be motivated by, different goals, existing models do not explicitly differentiate those goals in relation to affordances. In the few studies that focus on individu als’ goals, these key constructs remain under theorized and are not distinguished conceptually.

A second assumption that is worth revisiting, implicit in much of the literature, is that of a direct and undifferentiated pathway from goals to affordances. That is, that perceiving IT affordances is a direct outcome of goal pursuit and does not involve intermediary steps. However, the pathway from actors’ goals to affordance perception likely involves active and intentional learning (Greeno 1994, Hutchby 2001, Kaptelinin and Nardi 2012). Prior research on IT affordances has alluded to such a mechanism but has not studied it explicitly and separately from perception (Goh et al. 2011, Leonardi 2011, Burton-Jones and Volkoff 2017). Consequently, it is not clear what this learning mechanism involves, how it is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by different personal goals, and how, in turn, it shapes perceptions of IT affordances.

In sum, there is evidence that our understanding of how employees perceive affordances of organizational

IT is held back by prior assumptions, thereby limiting knowledge accumulation in this stream of research and hindering our ability to inform practice. This paper revisits these assumptions and, building on achievement goal theory, conceptualizes different personal goals, and scrutinizes the learning mechanism that informs affordance perception. The ensuing research model describes the relationships among individuals’ personal achievement goals, their learning strategies, and the IT affordances they perceive.

## IT Affordances as Possibilities for Behaviors at the Workplace

A preliminary step that we take before we introduce the theoretical framework is motivated by the observation of Cousins and Robey (2015), and supported by Table 1, that existing classi<sup>fi</sup>cations of IT affordances have little in common. Although important insights can be obtained from studying technology-speci<sup>fi</sup>c affordances, getting too close to the technology and focusing on idiosyncrasies limits generalization and involves the risk of the technology becoming obsolete (Treem and Leonardi 2012). To overcome this limitation, Majchrzak and Markus (2014) propose that affordance research adopts a productive level of abstraction. Volkoff and Strong (2017) echo these sentiments and argue that a useful de<sup>fi</sup>nition should be somewhat abstract and apply across potential actors with that goal.

Affordances can be described at different levels of abstraction (Cheikh-Ammar 2018); from low level operational behaviors, such as collecting and compiling data (Bernardi et al. 2019), to more abstract behaviors, such as delocalization (Seidel et al. 2013). Indeed, Treem and Leonardi (2012) argue that researchers can transcend the particularities of any technology or its features by conceptualizing affordances in terms of “what kinds of behaviors they typically afford across various organizations” (p. 148). Volkoff and Strong (2017, p. 5–7) observe that “unlike Gibson, IS researchers are generally not interested in a single affordance, but in a bundle of affordances” and that affordances can “be aggregated into higher level, and generally more abstract, affordances.”

Building on these papers, we abstract away from the speci<sup>fi</sup>cs of any particular technology and view affordances from a standpoint that focuses on the possibilities for behavior at the workplace through different technologies and across different organizations.

## Possibilities for In-Role and Extra-Role IT Behaviors

To conceptualize IT affordances at a higher level of abstraction that is rooted in workplace behavior, we build on the notions of Katz (1964) of in-role and extra-role behaviors. In-role behaviors are required or expected and are the basis of regular and ongoing job performance, and extra-role behaviors are informal and discretionary (Katz 1964). The distinction between in-role and extra-role behaviors is important and is regularly made by employees, their peers, and their supervisors (Van Dyne and LePine 1998). Distinguishing in-role from extra-role behaviors has helped illuminate various IT-related phenomena (Gaskin and Lyytinen 2012, Hsu et al. 2015).

Abstraction involves information reduction and assists with ef<sup>fi</sup>cient organization, storage, and retrieval of knowledge; it is “a process of identifying a set of invariant central characteristics of a thing” (Burgoon et al. 2013, p. 502). For example, email, chat apps, and videoconferencing can be grouped together based on media characteristics (Nevo et al. 2009). Building on, and extending they typology of Katz (1964), we adopt a productive level of abstraction (Majchrzak and Markus 2014) and conceptualize three individual-level IT affordances that re<sup>fl</sup>ect central characteristics of work place behavior that are largely invariant across space or time: common in-role, specialized in-role, and extra-role IT affordances.

Distinguishing IT affordances in such a manner is also supported by IS research. Beginning with in-role behaviors, prior studies found that employees base their perceptions of affordances on the extent to which the focal IT enables behaviors conducive for jobde<sup>fi</sup>ned task completion and ful<sup>fi</sup>lling administrators expectations (Wagner and Newell 2007, Stein et al. 2015). Such perceptions are grounded in the technology’s capacity to support employees’ in-role behaviors. In-role behaviors are (a) rooted in the employee’s job description; (b) the source of regular and ongoing job performance and compensation assessments; and (c) associated with negative consequences if performed poorly (Katz 1964).

IS research in related streams suggests that employees can perceive two types of affordances related to in-role behaviors. In particular, employees can focus on the potential to use organizational IT to complete essential job duties and meet performance requirements or they can focus on the possibility of using their technologies to beat the standards for satisfactory job performance and to differentiate their work from their colleagues’ work (Orlikowski 1996, Beaudry and Pinsonneault 2005, Stein et al. 2015). We label these as common in-role and specialized in-role IT affordances, respectively.

We de<sup>fi</sup>ne common in-role IT affordances<sup>1</sup> as possibilities for behaviors enabled by organizational IT that allow an employee to complete essential job duties and meet performance requirements. We de<sup>fi</sup>ne specialized in-role IT affordances<sup>2</sup> as possibilities for behaviors enabled by organizational IT that allow an employee to beat the standards for job performance and to differentiate her work from that of coworkers. Because they are uncommon to the workgroup and not available to everyone, the person who perceives these affordances may be able to perform her job at levels that exceeds others’ job performance and draw attention to unique accomplishments (Leonardi 2013).

Prior IS studies also found that employees based their perceptions of affordances on the extent to which organizational IT would allow them to engage in discretionary work behaviors such as teaching and transferring information to newcomers (Goh et al. 2011) and helping colleagues close open cases (Orlikowski 1996). Such perceptions appear to be grounded in the technologies’ potential to support employees’ extrarole behaviors. Extrarole behaviors are (a) not speci<sup>fi</sup>ed by role descriptions and job responsibilities and can involve the development and application of something new for which requisite knowledge is yet to be learned; (b) discretionary and their absence does not lead to reprimands; and (c) often not recognized by formal reward mechanisms and might not provide external rewards (e.g., merit increases), although they can be valuable (Katz 1964). Accordingly, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne extra-role IT affordances as possibilities for generating, developing, promoting, and implementing new ideas at the workplace made possible through organizational IT (Goh et al. 2011).

In sum, common in-role, specialized in-role, and extra-role IT affordances re<sup>fl</sup>ect the possibilities for different types of workplace behaviors available to employees through organizational IT. These affordances are likely to remain central and invariant across contexts, suggesting that studying them would provide generalizable insights.

Given that the IT affordances that individuals perceive are in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the goals they pursue (Markus and Silver 2008) and that employees often strive to achieve individual goals (Volkoff and Strong 2013, Strong et al. 2014), we expect our work-related IT affordances to be associated with different persona goals at the workplace. However, per the observations we made based on Table 1, IS research has not differentiated goals with respect to affordances, and the pathways from goals to affordances have largely been conceptualized as direct and undifferentiated, despite evidence pointing to the existence of an intermediary learning mechanism that shapes affordance perception. Consequently, we now turn to focus our efforts on creating and testing a research model that describes different personal goals, a learning mechanism, perceived IT affordances, and the relationships among them.

Based on our discussion of affordance research in the IS discipline and elsewhere, we conclude that perceiving IT affordances involves learning which potential behaviors are possible given a person’s goal. Therefore, an appropriate theoretical framework for guiding the construction of the research model should allow us to examine personal goals and a learning mechanism in tandem and to treat IT affordance perception as a goaloriented learning outcome.

## Achievement Goal Theory

Achievement goal theory (AGT) was developed to understand learning outcomes in achievement situations, such as students’ performance in the classroom (Dweck 1986, Ames and Archer 1988). The theory has been applied in organizational research for studying the impact of goals on the behaviors and cognition of employees (Button et al. 1996). Prior research found that organizational members’ pursuit of personal achievement goals creates particular response patterns, involving decisions regarding learning (i.e., information processing, commitment and social exchange) that impact important outcomes, such as job performance (Janssen and Van Yperen 2004, Baranik et al. 2010).

AGT provides a conceptual framework connecting personal achievement goals, learning strategies, and goal-oriented learning outcomes. According to this framework, when individuals <sup>fi</sup>nd themselves in an achievement situation, in which they believe their performance will be evaluated, they pursue different personal goals (Elliot et al. 1999). These goals are context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c orientations that represent the individual’s desire to obtain or improve competence, show competence, or hide incompetence (Elliot et al. 2005). The impact of these goals on achievement outcomes is indirect and is mediated by individuals’ selection and use of learning strategies (Grant and Dweck 2003).

In the remainder of this section, we discuss these conceptual layers and the linkages among them.

## Personal Achievement Goals, Learning Strategies, and Goal-Oriented Learning Outcomes

AGT conceptualizes different competence-relevant purposes as drivers of people’s thoughts and behaviors in achievement situations (Fryer and Elliot 2007). These purposes have been modeled as achievement goal constructs, two of which are featured prominently in the literature: a mastery goal and a performance goal (Dweck 1986). Both types of goals focus on the pursuit of competence and its assessment; however, they differ in the point of reference for assessment and in how they de<sup>fi</sup>ne goal attainment (Hulleman and Senko 2010). Individuals who pursue mastery goals have an intrapersonal point of reference and aim to increase their competence relative to past selves and toward aspirational future selves; individuals who pursue performance goals have an interpersonal point of reference and aim to demonstrate high competence relative to other individuals (Barron and Harackiewicz 2001).

Empirical research con<sup>fi</sup>rmed the characterization of mastery goals to a greater extent than that of performance goals. The mixed support for the latter has brought about a conceptual extension in the form of bifurcation of performance goals based on the individual’s valence—that is, approach and avoidance (Elliot 1999). The ensuing three-goal model—the trichotomous approach to achievement goals—offers valid constructs and scales for assessing the goals of adults in the workplace (VandeWalle 1997) and in the context of IT (Zweig and Webster 2004). We identify key aspects and characteristics for each achievement goal and develop them in the context of organizational IT in the next section (Table 2).

According to AGT, when a particular achievement goal is adopted and pursued, different patterns of cognitive and behavioral processes are activated and are used to interpret the achievement situation and to process goal-relevant information (Ames 1992, Elliot 1999).

Cognitive processes refer to how individuals engage with new information in achievement situations (Pintrich and De Groot 1990). Individuals can use a surface processing learning strategy and they can use a deep processing learning strategy (see Table 3 for a description). These cognitive strategies are not mutually exclusive, and a person may engage with new information with a mix of deep and surface processing (Vrugt and Oort 2008). Surface processing is often used by individuals who pursue performanceavoidance goals and to a lesser extent by those pursing performance-approach goals. It can be useful when performance is assessed with well-de<sup>fi</sup>ned measures that cover predetermined knowledge domains. Surface processing is generally associated with poor task performance, unless widely practiced in a particular context (e.g., preparation for college exams). Deep processing is often used by individuals who pursue mastery goals but may also be used by those pursing performance-approach goals in some circumstances. It is not a strategy of choice for individuals pursuing performance-avoidance goals. Deep processing often helps individuals perform well on goaloriented tasks, unless assessment does not reward the type of knowledge gained via this strategy.

Behavioral processes refer to self- and otherfocused learning strategies. Effort regulation focuses on the self and describes how one monitors the effort one invests in learning (Zimmerman and Risemberg 1997). Peer learning concerns exchanging knowledge with others in achievement situations (Topping 2005). Effort regulations is a strategy commonly used by those who pursue mastery goals, who persist in the face of challenges. For individuals pursuing performance goals, withdrawal tends to be more strongly associated with the avoidance valence, and individuals with performance-approach goals may persist or withdraw under different circumstances. Effort maintenance, or persistence, generally contributes to better goal-oriented task performance. Peer learning may be practiced by individuals with mastery goals because these goals are not inconsistent with openness to working with others and sharing knowledge. Social forms of learning have been found to improve goaloriented outcomes in academic contexts as well in or ganizational settings. The defensive stance associated with performance-avoidance goals suggests a corresponding avoidance from peer learning. Individuals with performance-approach goals also tend to work alone and show lower willingness to cooperates with peers.

## An Achievement Goal-Based Model of IT Affordance Perception

In line with the study’s objective, we turn to construct a research model on the foundations of AGT. We begin by developing the trichotomous model of achievement goals in a context in which learning focuses on a new organizational IT. To support our theoretical development, Table 2 describes the different aspects and characteristics associated with mastery, performanceapproach, and performance-avoidance goals that organizational members can pursue in the context of new IT implementation, which locates employees in achievement situations (Sykes and Venkatesh 2017). Table 3 describes and illustrates the four learning strategies when the object to be made sense of, and interpreted, is an organizational IT.

Next, we build on these tables, and with additional arguments and evidence from the achievement goal literature and relevant IS research, develop 12 hypotheses that jointly postulate different pathways, from personal goals, through learning strategies, to IT affordance perception. First, we develop the hypotheses that link personal goals with IT-focused learning strategies and then proceed to develop the hypotheses that link learning strategies with IT affordance perception (see Figure 1).

## Personal Achievement Goals and IT-Focused Learning Strategies

Surface processing, which involves shallow cognitive engagement with new information, is a learning strategy that balances failure avoidance and working too hard (Ainley 1993). In an IS context, users learn about new organizational IT through on-site training, reading manuals, watching colleagues use it, and searching the Web (Orlikowski 2000, Jasperson et al. 2005, Sharma and Yetton 2007). Surface-level cognitive information processing in this situation, may take the form of users only learning to execute commands that were demonstrated by trainers and ignoring additional sources of information (Boudreau and Robey 2005), or repeatedly calling the Help Desk for assistance with basic routines (Beaudry and Pinsonneault 2005). Even online searches can be executed in a shallow manner: a user may reproduce the <sup>fi</sup>rst instructions she <sup>fi</sup>nds without paying much thought to their ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency.

We propose that performance-avoidance goals will lead to surface processing of information about new IT for several reasons. First, AGT literature suggests that surface processing is likely to be favored by individuals with performance-avoidance goals who are concerned over displaying low performance and receiving low normative assessments (Elliot et al. 1999, Harackiewicz et al. 2000). Second, IS researchers suggest that managers prefer that users appropriate organizational IT faithfully (Gupta and Bostrom 2013). By following use instructions, users are less likely to draw negative attention to themselves. Third, limiting engagement with a new IT to a restricted set of functionalities helps users perform those functionalities automatically and with little cognitive effort (Kim et al. 2005). Finally, memorizing basic commands and usage routines can help users make sure that they do not perform tasks at substandard levels (Kim et al.

2005) and thus do not receive negative assessments of low competence.

Hypothesis 1a. There is a positive relationship between performance-avoidance goals pursued in the context of a newly implemented IT and IT-focused surface processing.

As noted in Table 2, individuals who pursue performance-approach goals are motivated by opportunities to display high abilities, but they are also concerned by the possibility of failure. Like their performance-avoidance-pursing counterparts, they too may consider surface processing an appropriate strategy, especially for tasks that reward memorization of facts (Barron and Harackiewicz 2001, Grant and Dweck 2003). Furthermore, using IT can involve habitual behavior (Ortiz de Guinea and Markus 2009), which does not require substantial mental capacity and tends to become more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient with repetition (Verplanken and Orbell 2003). We therefore expect employees with performance-approach goals to use surface processing when learning about new organizational IT.

Hypothesis 1b. There is a positive relationship between performance-approach goals pursued in the context of a newly implemented IT and IT-focused surface processing.

Surface and deep processing are not mutually exclusive learning strategies, and an individual may use both in the same achievement situation (Vrugt and Oort 2008). We propose that employees with performanceapproach goals would also use deep processing when learning about a new IT. The proposed relationship builds on the notion (Table 2) that individuals with performance-approach goals aim to demonstrate that they are more capable than others (Liem et al. 2008). In the context of new organizational IT, a deep processing strategy could involve employees attending advanced training sessions, scrutinizing manuals, joining online user communities, and searching for and installing addons, such as extensions and plug-ins. A new IT can present an employee with opportunities to demonstrate superior relative competence and to outperform her colleagues, but she would likely need to search for special features and functionalities and understand how she can use them advantageously.

## Hypothesis 2. There is a positive relationship between performance-approach goals pursued in the context of a newly implemented IT and IT-focused deep processing.

Researchers generally agree that performanceapproach goals motivate adaptive behavior (Harackiewicz et al. 2002), which involves effort regulation. Per Table 2, performance-approach goals re<sup>fl</sup>ect strong motivation for success and concerns over failure (Elliot et al. 2005), suggesting that individuals can decide to persist (maintain effort) or to withdraw effort when they are facing dif<sup>fi</sup>culties during task engagement. Although some have argued that persisting in a dif<sup>fi</sup>cult task could be interpreted as lack of ability and that effort withdrawal would be a preferred strategy for individuals with performanceapproach goals (Midgley et al. 2001), the bulk of the literature postulates positive association between performance-approach goals and effort persistence (Liem et al. 2008, Martin and Elliot 2016).

Drawing on the overall evidence from the AGT literature, we propose that employees who pursue performance-approach goals will persist in their efforts when they encounter dif<sup>fi</sup>culties using a new organizational IT. This proposition is in line with IS research showing that users who aim for relative advantage at the workplace maintain favorable atti tudes toward new IT even if they perceive it to be complex and dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to use (Moore and Benbasat 1991). We further argue that the potentially offsetting effect of being perceived as lacking in ability can be mitigated in the IS context, where learning, and possibly struggling and experiencing setbacks, can be done in private, using remote access (e.g., from a user’s home), logging in anonymously, or downloading trial versions onto personal computers. In other words, users can obscure the actual effort invested in learning about new organizational IT, thereby helping to lessen concerns over adverse normative comparisons.

Hypothesis 3. There is a positive relationship between performance-approach goals pursued in the context of a newly implemented IT and IT-focused effort regulation (persistence).

Mastery goals direct individuals toward challenges that provide opportunities for skill improvement, acquisition of knowledge, and development of abilities (Table 2) (for a broader discussion, see Dweck 1986 and Ames and Archer 1988). They motivate individuals to understand tasks thoroughly and to commit full attention to completing tasks (Darnon et al. 2007, Poortvliet et al. 2007). Individuals who pursue mastery goals are intrinsically motivated and often use deep, meaningful, and self-regulatory processing of task-related information (Elliot and Church 1997, Elliot et al. 1999).

Accordingly, we propose a positive relationship between mastery goals and deep processing when employees learn about new organizational IT. This relationship is also supported by IS research, where evidence shows that postimplementation challenge seeking can lead users to experience <sup>fl</sup>ow and playfulness, which can promote cognitive absorption and deep exploration (Agarwal and Karahanna 2000, Beaudry and Pinsonneault 2010). Moreover, when technology users are not primarily motivated externally, they may engage in behaviors that are indicative of deep processing; for example, they use features and functionalities that were not covered in training sessions, link new IT with existing technologies, or engage in a trial-and-error form of learning (Grif<sup>fi</sup>th and Northcraft 1996, Beaudry and Pinsonneault 2005).

Hypothesis 4. There is a positive relationship between mastery goals pursued in the context of a newly implemented IT and IT-focused deep processing.

Mastery goals are associated with a belief that success requires maintenance of effort (Table 2), leading to task commitment even in the face of dif<sup>fi</sup>culties, boredom, distractions, and negative feedback (Elliot et al. 1999, Liem et al. 2008). Such effort regulation can be further attributed to mastery goals’ association with intrinsic motivation and the prospects of skill acquisition and development through challenging tasks (Rawsthorne and Elliot 1999). In IS research, intrinsic motivation has been associated with challenging IT-related tasks that foster opportunities for professional growth (Cooper 2000). Intrinsic motivation increases users’ willingness to interact with a new IT because they <sup>fi</sup>nd the process itself rewarding (Venkatesh and Speier 1999).

We propose that employees who pursue mastery goals will maintain effort if they encounter problems when learning about new organizational IT, such as making mistakes and confronting complex procedures.

We expect such a relationship because employees with mastery goals will likely anticipate obstacles, seeing them as a part of learning about new IT, and perceiving them to be surmountable through perseverance (see example in Table 3). Moreover, we can infer from IS research that mastery goals may lead employees to treat challenges as opportunities to learn new computer skills, and to see technical complications not as technological dead-ends but rather as opportunities for IT reinvention (Nevo et al. 2016).

Hypothesis 5. There is a positive relationship between mastery goals pursued in the context of a newly imple mented IT and IT-focused effort regulation (persistence).

Mastery goals are also associated with a belief that success in achievement situations comes from collaboration with peers who can be a valuable source of information (Darnon et al. 2007). In this view, as we noted in Table 2, exchanging information and knowledge does not diminish one’s chances of attaining one’s goals; rather, it increases one’s chances of developing task mastery (Poortvliet et al. 2007).

We propose that employees with mastery goals will engage in peer learning when they learn about organizational IT (see example in Table 3). We anticipate that mastery goals would prompt employees to treat an open exchange of IT-related information as a useful way for gaining access to complementary information, allowing them to augment their own knowledge and to broaden their understanding of a new IT. This notion is consistent with IS research where it was observed that peer learning may be done for reasons other than to enhance one’s centrality and reputation (Sykes et al. 2009). For example, software code contribution may be attributed to beliefs that comments and feedback from others help contributors improve coding skills (Shah 2006). In addition, according to the AGT, collaboration with peers does not interfere with the attainment of mastery goals, suggesting that employees have little reason to keep useful IT-related information to themselves. This also has support in IS research, where, in online communities of practice, community members contributed knowledge partly because helping others with challenging problems was interesting and worth doing in its own right, and partly because it increased the chances for creating additional valuable knowledge (Wasko and Faraj 2005).

Hypothesis 6. There is a positive relationship between mastery goals pursued in the context of a newly imple mented IT and IT-focused peer learning.

## IT-Focused Learning Strategies and IT Affordance Perception

In our model, learning strategies help employees perceive affordances by revealing how they would be able to use the IT to engage in different behaviors in the workplace. Recall that common in-role affordances describe possibilities for behaviors available through organizational IT that enable employees to perform rolebased tasks and to successfully complete their work responsibilities; specialized in-role affordances refer to possibilities for behaviors available through organizational IT that would enable employees complete tasks that others cannot or to complete tasks more ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently than others<sup>3</sup>; and extra-role affordances refer to possibilities for behaviors available through organizational IT that would enable employees to generate, develop, promote, and implement new ideas at the workplace.

We propose that surface processing would lead employees to perceive common in-role affordances. Using this learning strategy helps individuals perform well in situations that involve working with premade lists and menus (Vrugt and Oort 2008), enacting behaviors when work routines are well de<sup>fi</sup>ned (Ainley 1993), or executing prevalidated processes (Entwistle and McCune 2004). In the IS literature, such limited knowledge acquisition is associated with restrictive technology cognizance and low intentions to innovate with IT (Nambisan et al. 1999). Additionally, uncritical acceptance of information, focus on routines, and cognitive rigidity reduce adaptability to new IT (Butler and Gray 2006). Finally, common in-role affordances involve functionalities that employees can learn about during training sessions (Sharma and Yetton 2007). Anecdotal evidence further supports the association between surface processing and perceiving common in-role affordances. For example, Beaudry and Pinsonneault (2005) document how one bank employee, who relied on the basic menu of a recently implemented new banking software and who followed the steps listed in the manual, identi<sup>fi</sup>ed only a handful of the technology’s most elementary routines as relevant for his work. Similarly, Orlikowski (2000) shows that several consultants, who were unwilling to spend time learning a new IT, developed familiarity with only a minimal set of functionalities.

Hypothesis 7. There is a positive relationship between IT-focused surface processing and perceiving common in-role affordances.

As noted in Table 3, deep processing includes using critical thinking and linking concepts with one another. Individuals who use this learning strategy carefully evaluate new information and integrate it with existing knowledge and prior experiences (Elliot et al. 1999). We propose that deep processing will lead employees to perceive both specialized in-role and extrarole affordances. This dual outcome is in line with the notion that re<sup>fl</sup>ective engagement with new IT may result in awareness of the possibility adaptation and reinvention (Nevo et al. 2016).

Beginning with specialized in-role affordances, we note that deep processing of IT-related information can involve employees attending advanced training sessions, experimenting with the IT, making headway through trial-and-error, participating in on-line commu nities and scrutinizing manuals (see Table 3 for an example). Through this form of learning, employees can discover features and functionalities that might be unknown to their colleagues and that can support differentiated job-related behaviors. Deep cognitive processing can help employees apply those features in a manner that would afford them to engage in behaviors that would enhance job performance (Jasperson et al. 2005).

## Hypothesis 8a. There is a positive relationship between IT-focused deep processing and perceiving specialized in-role affordances.

Moving on to extra-role affordances, as we note in Table 3, deep processing can also involve recalling previous experiences with other technologies, possibly outside the present organization and in other contexts, and integrating them with information about the new IT. Employees that challenge and expand upon the information provided regarding an IT’s intended use can create opportunities for affordances that would support discretionary activities. This notion has support in the IS literature, where acquiring in-depth IT-related knowledge, making connections across features, work contexts and business knowledge, and developing technological cognizance are associated with innovative behavior (Nambisan et al. 1999). Furthermore, mindful engagement with IT involves willingness to consider different contexts and perspectives and being open to applying IT differently (Butler and Gray 2006).

## Hypothesis 8b. There is a positive relationship between IT-focused deep processing and perceiving extra-role affordances.

Persistence refers to an effort regulation strategy that involves maintenance of effort in achievement situations or during task execution (Pintrich and De Groot 1990, Elliot et al. 1999, Liem et al. 2008). We propose that maintaining effort when learning about new organizational IT can lead employees to perceive specialized in-role affordances. For example, by repeatedly applying an IT to complete the same tasks with slight modi<sup>fi</sup>cations in each iteration, users may identify possible ways to improve upon a new IT’s built-in procedures that would support performanceenhancing behaviors and position them ahead of other employees (Beaudry and Pinsonneault 2005).

## Hypothesis 9. There is a positive relationship between IT-focused effort regulation (persistence) and perceiving specialized in-role affordances.

We propose that peer learning would lead employees to perceive extra-role affordances. In the context of this paper, peer learning could mean that employees exchange mutually bene<sup>fi</sup>cial information regarding their experiences with a new IT and broadening their understanding of its functions and features. IS research recognizes the positive role that social interaction and collaboration can play in innovation (Nambisan et al. 2017). As peer learning often takes place without the intervention of intermediaries, such as supervisors and trainers, employees tend to feel freer to experiment with novel and unconventional ideas (Boud et al. 1999). Such informal interactions could facilitate serendipitous innovation (Makri et al. 2014). For example, in Orlikowski (2000), we <sup>fi</sup>nd that several support personnel decided to provide proactive help to their peers and, while doing so, realized the potential to train newly hired specialists with the incident tracking system. That is, they perceived a training affordance that made it possible for them to engage in discretionary behaviors. We argue that when employees engage in IT-focused peer learning they may expand and re-evaluate their own understanding of new organizational IT or view existing problems in new light and <sup>fi</sup>nd novel ways to use the technology.

Hypothesis 10. There is a positive relationship between IT-focused peer learning and perceiving extra-role affordances.

To summarize, AGT serves as the foundational framework on which we construct a research model of goal-oriented and learning-based IT affordance perception. We differentiate employees’ personal goals and trace their divergent pathways to perceived IT affordances through learning strategies. We further provide an explanation for the divergence in the IT affordances that organizational members perceive.

## Empirical Assessment

To empirically test our model, we conducted a survey study.<sup>4</sup> The target population was adult working professionals whose employing organizations had implemented an organizational IT within the previous six months, and who were the intended users of that technology (the sampling frame is discussed in the online appendix). To identify respondents, we used the services of a double opt-in panel managed by a reputable data collection company with broad experience in diverse industries. Panel members were screened per the above target population criteria and then invited to participate in the survey. Quality control measures were embedded within the questionnaire to ensure the quality of the responses. Our <sup>fi</sup>nal sample size was 337 (response rate, 12.5%<sup>5</sup>).

## Control Variables

To assess the robustness of the research model in the presence of control variables, we collected demographic data from each panel member. The survey questionnaire also asked them about their job title, work experience, the size of the department to which they belong, the size of the organization, and the industry in which their organization operates. Respondents were further asked to indicate how long it has been since the focal IT was implemented, what type of technology was implemented, and whether they attended training and to what extent. Finally, the questionnaire asked respondents to indicate whether previous IT implementation were generally successful and to indicate their personal experience with the present implementation (positive, neutral, negative). ## Scale Development, Reliability, and Validity

The measurement scales used are presented in Table A4 in the online appendix. All the <sup>fi</sup>rst-order scales were modeled as re<sup>fl</sup>ective. The widely accepted scales of VandeWalle (1997) were used to operational ize users’ personal achievement goals. These scales were developed for assessing individuals’ achievement goals in the context of organizations and thus were not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly modi<sup>fi</sup>ed in this study. The Motivated Strategies for Learning Questionnaire (MLSQ; Pintrich and DeGroot 1990) and the Study Process Questionnaire (SPQ; Biggs et al. 2001) were used to in form the operationalization of the learning strategies scales. Finally, the IT affordances scales were operationalized, in part, using the scales of Janssen and Van Yperen (2004) for in-role and innovative job performance. Common in-role IT affordances were operationalized using the in-role performance scale and extra-role IT affordances were operationalized using the innovative performance scale, we developed new items for the specialized in-role IT affordances scale.

We followed standard procedures for scale development and validation next (Moore and Benbasat 1991). Having developed items for the specialized in-role IT affordances scale we began with a card sorting exercise, in which several judges observed that some of the items we proposed re<sup>fl</sup>ect doing things better than oth er employees, whereas other items re<sup>fl</sup>ect doing things differently than employees. We concurred with this observation and proceeded to create two subdimensions. The doing-things-better-than-others subdimension was labeled specialized in-role IT affordances (extent) and the doing-things-differently-than-others subdimension was labeled specialized in-role IT affordances (nature). Conceptually, however, we did not expect the two new scales to be associated with different antecedents and we therefore did not modify the two hypotheses associated with specialized in-role IT affordances (Hypotheses 8a and 9). Therefore, we opted to treat the two scales, extent and nature, as dimensions of specialized in-role affordances, and to model the latter as a second-order scale. Majchrzak et al. (2013) suggest that constructs should be treated as formative when the items are conceptually similar but are de<sup>fi</sup>ning characteristics of the construct that can occur independently of one another. Because employees may use organizational IT to perform their jobs at higher levels or they may use it to do their job differently from other employees, we modeled specialized in-role affordances as a second-order formative construct with extent and nature as its indicators (MacKenzie et al. 2011). This structure was subsequently con<sup>fi</sup>rmed through factor analysis.

Table A5 (online appendix) reports the results of the reliability checks of the constructs, which were generated using WarpPLS 7.0 (Kock 2020). All the Cronbach’s α values exceeded the cutoff of 0.7, the composite reliability (C.R.) scores were also above the 0.7 threshold, and the average variance extracted (AVE) values were above the 0.5 cutoff (Straub et al. 2004). Convergent and discriminant validity were assessed using factor analysis on all the items of the latent variables used in the study. For each construct, the square root of the AVE is greater than the correlation values with all other constructs (Fornell and Larcker 1981). The results indicate that all items signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly load only on one factor and that cross-loadings differ by more than 0.3 (Table A6).

As noted previously, specialized in-role affordances were modeled as a second-order formative construct composed of two <sup>fi</sup>rst-order re<sup>fl</sup>ective constructs (nature and extent). Validity was assessed by examining the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the weights of the two underlying dimensions. The coef<sup>fi</sup>cients for both dimensions were 0.587 (p < 0.01). In addition, the variance in<sup>fl</sup>ation factor (VIF) was examined to assess multicolinearity between the two dimensions. The resulting VIF value of 1.253 is lower than the recommended 3.3 tolerance level (Diamantopoulos and Siguaw 2006, Petter et al. 2007).

Finally, common methods variance (CMV) was assessed following the marker variable approach. We incorporated indicators from a construct that was not theoretically expected to correlate with any of the other model’s constructs, namely, Perceived Workload. It is assumed that correlations between marker variables and other model indicators are caused by CMV (Lindell and Whitney 2001). We corrected for this CMV and then veri<sup>fi</sup>ed that the correlations between our model variables and the dependent variables remained signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. We further used a recent approach for examining the possible presence of CMV suggested by Kock (2015) in which full collinearity is calculated. Our model’s full collinearity VIFs are below the recommended 3.3 cutoff. These precautions and results suggest that CMV is not a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant risk to our results, and they offer a measure of con<sup>fi</sup>dence in the survey data regarding their ability to assess the theoretical model empirically.

Having established the validity of the measurement model, we then assessed the structural model using WarpPLS 7.0 (Kock 2020).

## Structural Model Evaluation

Structural equation modeling (SEM) using the partial least squares (PLS) method was used to estimate the relationships among the latent variables. PLS SEM is commonly used in a variety of disciplines, including IS (Addas and Pinsonneault 2018), marketing (Shmuel et al. 2019), and management (Rigdon 2016). Under some conditions, PLS SEM techniques are preferred to covariance-based SEM techniques. In particular, when the research model uses formatively measured latent variables and when the data are not normally distributed, PLS SEM’s nonparametric methods are deemed robust to deviations from normality. In our model, the latent variable Specialized In-Role IT Affordances is a second-order formative latent variable, and the latent variables are not distributed normally, suggesting that the structural model should be assessed using a soft ware that implements PLS SEM techniques.

WarpPLS provides traditional PLS algorithms along with factor-based PLS algorithms, which generate estimates of both true composites and factors, thereby accounting for measurement error and addressing the factor indeterminacy problem (Kock 2015). Traditional PLS algorithms generate composites—as linear combinations of indicators—that function as factor approximations, and factor-based PLS algorithms provide estimates of the true factors, as linear combinations of indicators and measurement errors (Kock 2017). Factor-based PLS algorithms bring together the accuracy of covariancebased SEM algorithms under common factor model assumptions with the nonparametric characteristics of classic PLS algorithms (Kock 2017). Similar to covariance-based SEM algorithms, factor-based PLS algorithms are fully compatible with common factor model assumptions, including the assumption of lack of correlation among all indicator errors (Kock 2020).

Robust path analysis was used as the algorithm for the outer model analysis, and Stable3 was used for resampling. The robust path analysis algorithm computes latent variable scores by averaging the scores of their indicators and p values through the nonparametric resampling or stable methods implemented through the software (Kock 2020). The Stable3 resampling method can create scores for latent variables and compute several parameters that match those created by other algorithms, but with better computational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency (Kock 2020).

The quality of the structural model (the explained variance by the independent latent variables, the strength and magnitude of the paths) was assessed through the following measures: average path coef<sup>fi</sup>- cient, average $R ^ { 2 } ,$ , average adjusted $R ^ { 2 } ,$ average variance in<sup>fl</sup>ation factor, and Tenenhaus goodness-of-<sup>fi</sup>t. It is recommended that the values for the <sup>fi</sup>rst three measures be signi<sup>fi</sup>cant $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ , whereas the AVIF should be less than 5 (Kock 2011). ## Discussion

The question that motivated the study described in this paper was as follows: How do personal goals and learning shape IT affordance perception? This question was generated by problematizing the IT affordance literature and revisiting two core assumptions (Alvesson and Sandberg 2011). First, the goals of employees are often assumed to re<sup>fl</sup>ect those of their organizations, the groups to which they belong, or their tasks. This assumption can explain why individuals’ goals have not been differentiated conceptually and why their impact on the perceptions of individual IT affordances has not been theorized and analyzed in prior studies. Indeed, under this assumption, studying individuals’ goals would seem super<sup>fl</sup>uous. Second, the pathway from employees’ goals to IT affordance perception is typically assumed to be direct and without intermediary steps. This assumption is worth revisiting since there is evidence that organizational members engage in signi<sup>fi</sup>cant learning about newly implemented IT in order to perceive their affordances.

We reexamined these assumptions and argued that in order to add knowledge to the literature we ought to pay close attention to employees’ goals and the learning mechanism they use in the context of new organizational IT. Furthermore, we argued that IT implementations at the workplace locate employees in achievement situations and that the personal goals employees pursue determine their learning strategies, in turn in<sup>fl</sup>uencing how they perceive affordances. The conceptual tool that we proposed is a model anchored in achievement goal theory, which conceptualizes different personal achievement goals, describes a complex intermediary learning mechanism, and traces different pathways to IT affordance perception.

## Interpretation of the Findings

The empirical results largely support the model: different personal achievement goals were associated with different learning strategies, and different learning strategies were associated with different perceptions of IT affordances, suggesting the existence of three distinct pathways (Figure 2).

In Pathway performance-avoidance, pursuing othersfocused, avoidance-valenced goals was associated with using surface processing learning strategy and perceiving common in-role affordances. In Pathway performance-approach, pursuing others-focused, approach-valenced goals was associated with using surface processing and effort regulation (persistence) and perceiving both common and specialized in-role affordances. In Pathway mastery, pursuing selffocused, approach-valenced goals was associated with employing deep processing, effort regulation (persistence) and peer learning, and perceiving extra-role and specialized in-role affordances. The three pathways illustrate how employees can perceive that a certain organizational IT affords them different possibilities for workplace behaviors when they pursue different personal achievement goals.

Hypothesis 2, which proposed a positive relationship between performance-approach goals and deep processing, was not supported by the data. This <sup>fi</sup>nding is consistent with some studies within the achievement goals literature (Pintrich 2000) but not with others (Liem et al. 2008). One explanation may be that when the focal artifact is complex and opaque, as is the case with organizational IT, deep processing might demand substantial cognitive resources. Performance-approach goals are undergirded by opposing forces—namely, the desire to outperform others and the fear of failure— raising the possibility that individuals pursing this goal may deem a deep processing approach to IT-related information a risky strategy, preferring to look for alternative ways for displaying superior competence relative to their colleagues.

## Contributions

The study offers contributions in three main areas: differentiating and theorizing individual IT affordances; distinguishing personal goals and studying them in their own right; and explicating the pathways from the latter to the former and revealing the key mediat ing role of learning strategies.

Gibson (1986; p. 134–135) describes an affordance as “an invariant combination of variables,” signifying its nature as an abstraction. Several affordance scholars called on future research to adopt a productive level of abstraction that would allow us to describe affordances that apply across actors, technologies, and contexts (Majchrzak and Markus 2014, Volkoff and Strong 2017). Our study answers this call, and we conceptual ize IT affordances at a level of abstraction that focuses on the potential workplace behaviors available to employees through organizational IT, without tying those behaviors to a particular technology. Common in-role, specialized in-role, and extra-role IT affordances characterize employee behavior in different organizations and are based on a typology (Katz 1964) that has been relevant for research, including IS research (Gaskin and Lyytinen 2012, Hsu et al. 2015, Nov et al. 2016), for more than half a century, denoting our typology’s spatial and temporal stability.

The affordances conceptualized, operationalized, and analyzed in this study offer researcher an apparatus with which to study affordances across technologies and in different organizations. They can help identify commonalities and patterns that might be hard to observe at lower levels of abstraction (Wakslak et al. 2008, Burgoon et al. 2013). By abstracting away from the idiosyncrasies of speci<sup>fi</sup>c artifact types, future research may apply common in-role, specialized in-role, and extra-role IT affordances to consolidate prior disparate <sup>fi</sup>ndings that appear, prima facie, to have little in common. For example, future studies can examine shared catalysts/impediments to affordance actualization that may be relevant across contexts.

The three-affordance apparatus is bene<sup>fi</sup>cial outside the academia as well. It can help employees and managers adjust expectations and attitudes toward new organizational IT by understanding its potential impact on job performance within and beyond the con<sup>fi</sup>nes of formal contracts. These technology-independent affordances can also be used to compare outcomes across implementation projects. Extrarole affordances are particularly important to organizations’ long-term success because the behaviors they enable can support innovation (Janssen and Van Yperen 2004). However, those workplace behaviors might reside outside the boundaries of formal contractual obligations and reward mechanisms, and thus can “<sup>fl</sup>y under the radar” and remain undetected by traditional IT project assessment tools. This study offers a mechanism that can help managers detect and nurture IT-related extra-role behaviors.

Our paper’s second contribution illuminates the important role that employees’ personal goals play in perceiving IT affordances. Past studies (reviewed in Table 1) often discounted employees’ goals because of an assumption that workers’ goals re<sup>fl</sup>ect those of the organization or the group to which employees belong, or the focal task they pursue. This study reevaluates this assumption and shows that employees can pursue different personal achievement goals and, through diverse learning strategies, discover different IT affordances.

To illustrate this contribution, we refer to a case study (Leidner et al. 2018) in which an organization had implemented an enterprise social media (ESM) with the goal of helping new hires acclimate and socialize. Some of the affordances discussed in the case, such as interacting with peers and participating in ESM events, are closely related to the organization’s goal. However, other affordances, such as developing leadership skills, are unrelated to the socializing goal and appear instead to be related to personal goals that several of the new and old employees had pursued.

However, because personal goals were not studied, it is unclear how such affordances were perceived and what goals they served. Our research model can help to identify the personal goals that the new hires pursued. For example, the developing leadership skills affordance, which the authors describe as giving “new hires a chance to make a name for themselves in front of management and peers” (p. 124), might have been the culmination of a pathway that began with performance-approach goals.

Looking ahead, future research can explore factors that may in<sup>fl</sup>uence the personal goals that employees adopt and pursue. The goal-setting literature acknowledges that personal goals may be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by various measures (Shantz and Latham 2011), and future research can explore which measures would locate employees on different pathways. Such <sup>fi</sup>ndings could help managers to steer employees toward certain types of IT affordances and away from others. For example, if management would like to steer employees toward extra-role affordances, then tolerating risk-taking, encouraging professional growth, and recognizing effort (Amabile and Pratt 2016) can motivate employees to pursue mastery goals.

The study’s insights regarding the role of personal achievement goals in perceiving IT affordances can also inform research on other postimplementation phenomena. For example, IS researchers found that individuals use coping mechanisms to deal with the emotions they experience following new IT implementations (Stein et al. 2015), whereas achievement goal researchers found that emotions are linked to individuals’ achievement goals (Pekrun et al. 2009). It may be possible to use emotions as a conceptual glue to link these literatures and explore what role achievement goals play in the strategies that individuals use for coping with IT implementations and the emotions they elicit.

The study’s third key contribution is explicating the pathways from goals to affordance perception through various learning strategies, moving away from the common conceptualization of a direct and undifferentiated path from goals to affordances in past literature. The key role that learning strategies play in shaping affordance perception, and the fact that they are in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the personal achievement goals that employees pursue at the workplace, show that the pathway from goals to affordances is more intricate than suggested by prior research. Our work demonstrates that perceiving IT affordances involves different behavioral and cognitive elements, pointing to a highly personalized and nuanced process that depends on important learning strategies. Taking these elements into account is likely to improve the realism of models of IT affordances and our capacity to inform IT implementations. The mediating role of learning strategies should also sensitize us to the capacity of organizations to make it harder or easier to use certain strategies and to the decisions they make in this regard that can shape employees’ perceptions of IT affordances.

## Additional Future Research Directions

The affordances literature proposes that actors can be motivated by their goals and by their needs (Gibson 1986, Greeno 1994, Hutchby 2001, Stoffregen 2003). Motivation scholars argue that goals and needs are distinct concepts, and they motivate different activities and behaviors in humans (Austin and Vancouver 1996, Pintrich 2003). Goals are adaptable, sociocognitive, and situation-speci<sup>fi</sup>c constructs, and they tend to motivate behaviors in situations where outcomes can be measured and assessed (Pintrich 2003, Shane et al. 2003). Needs specify innate psychological nutrients and are indispensable for continuing psychological progress, integrity, and well-being, and they motivate commensurate behaviors (Yukl and Latham 1978, Deci and Ryan 2000, Pintrich 2003). They operate below the level of consciousness and are assumed to be innate to all humans in all cultures and apply across all situations (Austin and Vancouver 1996, Pintrich 2003). Hence, it is possible that goals and needs may locate individuals on different pathways and culminate in different IT affordances. Future research would shed light on these interesting possibilities and their implications for theory and practice.

Finally, the paper also contributes to the achievement goal literature, which tends to focus on simple measures of goal-oriented learning outcomes, such as exam scores and course grades (Elliot and Murayama 2008). By focusing on interactions between individuals and complex artifacts—that is, organizational IT—the paper broadens the range of goal-oriented learning outcomes that can arise when individuals pursue achievement goals and employ learning strategies. Re searchers who ground their studies in AGT are encouraged to expand their search for more complex and multifaceted goal-oriented learning outcomes. In addition, peer learning has not received much attention in achievement goal literature, and this paper was thus able to add to the body of knowledge evidence regarding its role as an important learning strategy. Moreover, by developing a model anchored in AGT and testing it using data collected from working professionals, this study enhances the applicability and relevance of AGT, which predominantly uses controlled laboratory experiments with students as the main subjects, highlighting its usefulness as a lens for <sup>fi</sup>elds studies in which the participants are adults.

## Limitations

The study’s boundaries can serve as fodder for future research. First, prior research in the achievement goals literature found that individuals interpret personal goals differently in cultures that are less individualistic (Urdan 2004). The participants in this study were working professionals from the United States, a country known for its individualistic culture (Leidner and Kayworth 2006). In other cultures, employees may pursue similar personal goals yet follow different pathways. Future research may extend our work by exploring the impact of culture on the pathways that link personal goals and perceived IT affordances. Second, our study focused on the postimplementation phase. However, it is possible that employees begin to plan how they would learn about new IT before implementation, suggesting that events that take place in earlier phases can impact IT affordance perception. Hence, another avenue for expansion of our work would be to add a layer of temporality to the model. Finally, the peer learning construct focused on helpgiving, and future research could expand it to include help-seeking and explore this learning strategy’s relationship with its antecedents and consequences.

The research methodology’s limitations should be acknowledged. First, the empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings are based on a cross-sectional survey, suited to test the variance model that was constructed. This choice is in line with the study’s aim to capture personal goals and explain differences in IT affordance perceptions across employees.

Future work could investigate further the process of affordance perception using longitudinal approach, beginning with the formation of personal goals by employees and tracing the pathways that emerge. Second, it is possible that some parts of the questionnaire required respondents to recall past events, which might induce some bias. However, we took great care in creating the questionnaire, following best practices to enhance the accuracy of respondents’ recollections. Moreover, it is important to note that most statements in the survey instrument refer to respondents’ current experiences and actions and likely do not necessitate recollection of past events.

## Conclusion

This paper contributes to our understanding of how and why employees perceive different IT affordances while using equivalent organizational technologies under similar contexts. In doing so, the paper answers the research question generated by problematizing the existing literature: How do personal goals and learning shape IT affordance perception? In particular, the paper theorizes that when IT are implemented in organizations, employees pursue different personal achievement goals and use different learning strategies. The paper also proposes and shows that, depending on the learning strategies they use, employees can perceive different individual IT affordances that are distinguished according to the work-related behaviors they would enable— that is, common in-role, specialized in-role, and extrarole. Our theoretical claims and empirical support help to illuminate several pathways that employees can follow when they pursue their personal goals at the workplace. The paper suggests that personal achievement goals can play a key role in other core IS phenomena, thereby opening up new avenues for future research.
