# Reviewing Before Reading? An Empirical Investigation of Book-Consumption Patterns and Their Effects on Reviews and Sales

Abstract. Over the past decades, research on online book reviews has inundated academic circles with numerous theoretical re<sup>fl</sup>ections and empirical manifestations aimed at explaining the effects of such resources on business performance. Yet, these studies succumbed to the conventional pitfall of assuming that consumers write reviews only after they fully read the book that they purchased. A recent industry report revealed that although many individuals initiate book reading, only a few <sup>fi</sup>nish them. With these considerations in mind, we investigated how consumers’ book-consumption patterns affect their review behaviors and how reviews generated from incomplete consumption in<sup>fl</sup>uence subsequent sales. We used expectation con<sup>fi</sup>rmation theory (ECT) as a theoretical foundation to elaborate on the review behaviors of consumers at various stages of their e-book consumption. On the basis of ECT, we argue that customers can submit reviews not necessarily after full consumption, but at any point during this trajectory, and even when consumption has yet to take place. Consumption patterns were traced and captured from records of reading activities on e-book devices and apps. Our results indicate that a considerable number of consumers provide positive reviews even before initiating reading or after progressing up to an extremely early section of a book. In addition, the relationship between review valence and completion rate can be characterized as a U-shaped pattern, given that reviews arising from negative discon<sup>fi</sup>rmations occur more frequently than those emerging out of positive discon<sup>fi</sup>rmations. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings also uncover that customers occupying the extreme ends of the completion continuum provide less extreme review ratings. The effect of completion rate on review length is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly positive. Our textanalysis results suggest that reviews based on suf<sup>fi</sup>cient consumption contain more useful insights than do those grounded in incomplete consumption. Moreover, review comments formed after incomplete consumption adversely affect subsequent sales. Finally, we discuss a number of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings’ implications and provide actionable recommendations that can aid platforms in their efforts to re<sup>fi</sup>ne their online-review systems and policies in pursuit of enhanced credibility in peer evaluation.

## 1. Introduction

Customers are increasingly relying on online reviews to make informed decisions regarding product/service selection (Dellarocas 2003, Godes and Mayzlin 2004). Nevertheless, as suggested in previous studies, online reviews may not always effectively and accurately convey information about the actual quality and characteristics of commodities and other offerings. This de<sup>fi</sup>ciency is attributed to at least four factors. The <sup>fi</sup>rst are fraud and deception: Individuals or <sup>fi</sup>rms possibly manipulate reviews deliberately to create arti<sup>fi</sup>- cially high or low ratings—an act driven largely by the promise of economic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and the desire to cope with competitive pressure (Dellarocas 2003, Mayzlin et al. 2014). The second impediment to ef<sup>fi</sup>cacious and precise information transmission is self-selection bias: Online reviews that are provided at a certain stage of a product lifecycle may not be representative of the opinions of the broader consumer population in later time periods (Li and Hitt 2008). The third is external in<sup>fl</sup>uence: An individual’s review may be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by merchant- or digital-platform-originating external interventions, such as <sup>fi</sup>nancial incentives (Burtch et al. 2017) and performance feedback (Huang et al. 2019). The <sup>fi</sup>nal obstacle is peer effect: Individuals may be affected by one another in the review process and provide biased feedback that does not re<sup>fl</sup>ect their own views (Muchnik et al. 2013, Wang et al. 2018).

The current study unravels a new source of challenges that may strongly constrain the informativeness and accuracy of online reviews. That is, customers may provide these evaluations without the informed judgement enabled by complete product/service experience (i.e., partial or no consumption of goods). We demonstrate that such problems can be especially severe for information goods (e.g., e-books), whose consumption entails a considerable degree of attention.

A New York Times article recently revealed an intriguing pattern with regard to consumers’ bookreading behaviors, indicating that reading declines substantially after the <sup>fi</sup>rst 100 pages of a book and that 90% of readers give up after only <sup>fi</sup>ve chapters of books that contain more than 30 chapters (Alter and Russell 2016). Interestingly, many best-selling books were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as the most frequently initiated, but never completed, readings. Stephen Hawking’s A Brief History of Time, for instance, has sold more than 9 million copies globally since its publication nearly 30 years ago, but consumers who bought this book read, on average, only 6.6% of its content.

The problem with most online-review studies, including those discussed above, is that they were anchored on the premise that a customer shares a review only after complete consumption of a product or service. In reality, however, both industry and academia remain in the dark as to how much of a good a customer has actually consumed when he crafts an assessment. Customers may write a review at any stage of the consumption process before full utilization (Figure 1).

Endeavoring to settle this issue more de<sup>fi</sup>nitively, we conduct an analysis on the basis of a unique individuallevel data set that comprises information on users e-book reading patterns and corresponding review behaviors. The analysis revealed that a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant percentage of customers do not complete e-books, but, nonetheless, provide online evaluations of these products (category 2). It also uncovered that many consumers who purchased e-books assign a rating to these products and critique their content even without reading a single page (category 1).

Why is it imperative to concern ourselves with the tendency of consumers to leave reviews of e-books with incomplete consumption? We posit that reviews grounded in partial experience cannot be immediately distinguished from customer evaluations that are informed by genuine consumption, but can fundamentally differ from the latter in terms of valence, rating distribution, and informativeness (i.e., the provision of details). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, assessments founded on fragmented product engagement are likely to be minimally informative and extreme because of partial information sampling. These reviews are interpreted by consumers in the same way that they regard more credible assessments and may potentially dilute and distort the signals and advice conveyed in trustworthy appraisals. For e-book sellers, therefore, online reviews crafted from incomplete consumption can profoundly affect the subsequent sales of such goods.

In the case of print books that are sold through either online or of<sup>fl</sup>ine establishments, publishers and writers remain unsure as to what actually happens after a consumer purchases a book, given that they cannot trace consumption trajectories. Such challenges can be alleviated through e-books, which are becoming the dominant content medium sold in the book industry. Speci<sup>fi</sup> cally, e-books lend themselves to monitoring in a technically feasible fashion because book readers and apps are electronically connected via a server that can collect data on consumers’ reading patterns. Correspondingly, whenever a consumer opens or closes an e-book, the server can identify the proportion of content read by the customer. To investigate consumption patterns and their effects on reviews and subsequent sales, we develop an innovative scheme that, similar to Amazon’s reading tracker, monitors the segments of a book that have been perused when a person closes an e-book reader. Leveraging a unique and granular consumption data set, we <sup>fi</sup>rst explore how the incomplete consumption of e-books in<sup>fl</sup>uences the quantitative review characteristics (i.e., review valence, length, and extremity) and qualitative text-based evaluations (i.e., informativeness). We then scrutinize the extent to which reviews grounded in partial experience affect subsequent sales.

To explain the review behaviors of consumers at various stages of their e-book consumption, we use expectation con<sup>fi</sup>rmation theory (ECT) (Oliver 1980) as a theoretical foundation. This theory was chosen in accounting for the motives and patterns that underlie the aforementioned behaviors because online peer evaluations explicitly represent the level of satisfaction or dissatisfaction that consumers experience with the goods or services that they buy. ECT suggests that review posting can occur at any point in time, regardless of consumption stage, and even prior to the initiation of consumption. That is, consumers tend to submit their reviews not necessarily on the basis of postconsumption evaluations, but on their preconsumption expectations toward the product that they purchased. Expectations typically trigger positive emotions and wishes regarding product satisfaction of harbored prepurchase assumptions with such ex ante “hopeful thinking” or anticipation often re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the form of evaluative “reviews.” From the perspective of ECT, we also unravel a number of empirical regularities associated with the effects of reading completion rate (i.e., the extent to which a book is read) on review characteristics (i.e., valence, extremity, length, and informativeness). For example, we posit that the relationship between completion rate and review valence can be characterized as a U-shaped pattern. Although negative and positive discon<sup>fi</sup>rmations may encourage consumers to post their reviews while consumption is in progress, the asymmetric loss function of expectations (Anderson and Sullivan 1993) that renders consumers more sensitive to losses than gains may lower the average valence of reviews that were submitted under partial consumption.

The results indicate that the review-entry decision of consumers is positively associated with the discon-<sup>fi</sup>rmation that arises during consumption, pointing to the increased possibility that consumers who experience high discrepancy between initial expectation and perceived quality will tender online assessments before complete consumption. We discovered as well that a similar U-shaped pattern characterizes the relationship between completion rate and review valence, suggesting that evaluations falling within the two extreme ends of the continuum (categories 1 and 3 in Figure 1) exhibit a higher numerical review valence than that re<sup>fl</sup>ected by in-between appraisals (category 2). A noteworthy result is that a considerable number of consumers provide positive reviews even before reading or after completing only an extremely early section (i.e., introductory chapters) of a purchased book. Interestingly, these reviews contain mainly ini tial expectations with respect to future consumption. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings also implied that an inverted U-shaped pattern underlies the association between completion rate and review extremity. Review length measured by word count signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly increases as completion rate rises. Reviews deriving from incomplete consumption are more intemperate than evaluations informed by encounters occurring at the polar opposites of the consumption continuum. In addition to illuminating these quantitative aspects of review characteristics, we used text-mining mechanisms and found that reviews composed on the grounds of de<sup>fi</sup>cient exposure reduce the insightfulness of these assessments and the richness of the information that they contain.

Finally, we broaden our inquiries further by analyzing the manner by which fragmented consumptionbased reviews in<sup>fl</sup>uence book sales at subsequent periods. The overall <sup>fi</sup>ndings revealed that consumers who read reviews that draw from complete consumption are more likely to purchase books than those who read reviews composed on the basis of incomplete consumption. That is, reviews grounded in only partial experience, particularly text-based evaluations, can exert negative consequences on business performance. The validity of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings is reinforced by additional robustness procedures involving econometric models and text-mining analyses.

This study expands the literature on online reviews by scrutinizing consumers’ consumption behaviors— arguably the most relevant, but yet unexplored, factor that determines the nature and magnitude of product reviews. To the best of our knowledge, this research is the <sup>fi</sup>rst to look into the effects of consumption patterns on review behaviors and their subsequent impact on product sales. In academic circles, purchase decisions have long been used as a key metric through which to identify the economic value of online reviews. The lack of consumption data, however, has impeded scholarly initiatives to grasp how actual consumption in<sup>fl</sup>uences consumer preferences and future sales.

On the basis of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, we provide digital platforms with a number of actionable recommendations meant to help them redesign their review structures and policies to enhance the credibility of online reviews and minimize biased peer evaluations. For example, retailers can allow consumers to easily identify reviews generated with full consumption by according such useful evaluations positional advantage (i.e., sorting or searching reviews on the basis of consumption rate) and visual recognition. In addition, marketers can leverage consumption information to devise effective advertising strategies. Furthermore, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings present valuable implications for platforms in regard to effectively redesigning the policies that underlie review management so that consumers are encouraged to post their evaluations after complete or suf<sup>fi</sup>cient consumption. The speci<sup>fi</sup>c implementations we discussed include how nudging, social and <sup>fi</sup>nancial incentives, and promotional campaigns may help consumers to change their customary behaviors and subsequently post high-quality evaluations. These recommendations revolving around the enhancement of structures and policies serve as thoughtful managerial guidance on designing trustworthy, transparent, and economic online-review systems that bene<sup>fi</sup>t both consumers and retailers.

## 2. Literature Review

In this section, we survey previous studies that revolve around the effects of online reviews on product sales and explore diverse sources of inaccuracies in online peer evaluations.

## 2.1. Economic Impact of Online Reviews

The effects of online reviews on sales have been intensively examined for many years since this prepurchase resource became available online, and their economic impact has been dissected in a substantial body of studies (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006, Forman et al. 2008). Some of these explorations revealed that word-ofmouth (WOM) volume measured on the basis of online-review quantity is an important predictor of product sales (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006), while others suggested that the valence and linguistic characteristics of WOM in<sup>fl</sup>uence the purchase decisions of consumers (Archak et al. 2011, Yin et al. 2014, Packard and Berger 2017). Correspondingly, online reviews have gradually become an indispensable component of digital platform ecosystems (Dellarocas 2003, Chen and Xie 2008, Choi et al. 2019), with the “wisdom of the crowd” playing an increasingly pivotal role in driving daily purchase decisions (Luca 2017).

Although extensive research focused on how online reviews bear on sales, these studies presented mixed results. One the one hand, positive relationships form, particularly when reviews are considered suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently informative and comprehensive (Forman et al. 2008). On the other hand, personal experience-based online assessments either fail to inform consumers or increase uncertainty for them (Duan et al. 2008). Chen and Xie (2008) illustrated the limited impact of online reviews, affecting book sales only when they are considerably informative. Contributory to a disparity is the subjective nature of online reviews, which are found on opinions and experiences that are often prejudiced and deceitful. Thus, foregrounding the effects of online reviews without taking account of consumers’ consumption behaviors may give rise to an incomplete understanding of the economic relevance of these evaluations. To the best of our knowledge, no academic works have examined the sales effects of reviews that are grounded in incomplete or no consumption of purchased books. The current study <sup>fi</sup>lls this void by casting light on the association between consumption patterns and review behaviors and by quantifying the economic impact of reviews generated after fragmented product engagement.

## 2.2. Inaccurate Online Reviews

Online reviews generated by customers are often sub ject to failings in communicating detailed and accurate information about the quality of a rated good. Such failure has been attributed to, at least, four factors: fraud and deception (Mayzlin et al. 2014, Luca and Zervis 2016), selection bias (Gao et al. 2015, Ho et al. 2017), external in<sup>fl</sup>uence (Huang et al. 2018), and peer effect (Wang et al. 2018). In more detail, the investigation of Luca and Zervis (2016) into the motives for committing review fraud in the context of food stores demonstrated that restaurants with weak reputations (i.e., those receiving few reviews or critical comments) are more likely to manipulate assessments than are those with established statures. Likewise, the examination of Mayzlin et al. (2014) of how <sup>fi</sup>rm characteristics and market competition are associated with the generation of fake reviews indicated that independent hotels tend more strongly toward deceitful evaluations than do hotels with branded chains. The authors also discovered that hotels surrounded by neighbors (i.e., competitors) exhibit a higher probability of receiving negative counterfeit reviews than do hotels located in isolation. Adding to these insights, Anderson and Simester (2014) reported that approximately 5% of reviews posted on a large retailer’s website are submit ted by consumers who have no record of purchasing the product being evaluated and that these deceptive assessments contain negative and detailed messages unrelated to the commodity. Our study is different from Anderson and Simester (2014) and related literature in that all subjects in our study have already purchased the product, but may still leave a review before completely consuming the product.

In terms of selection bias, Gao et al. (2015) found such an issue in online reviews circulating around the professional healthcare setting, noting that online ratings are subject to such biases, given patients’ per ceptions regarding the quality of hospitals. A similar exploration by Ho et al. (2017) showed that consumers decision on whether to leave reviews is highly associated with the magnitude of discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation that they encounter; the larger the discrepancy between the expected and actual qualities of products, the higher the likelihood of evaluative posts by consumers.

Apart from fake reviews and selection biases, external effects (i.e., economic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts provided to individual consumers) and incentive framing may also induce the production of inaccurate online reviews. Wang et al. (2018) uncovered that the provision of monetary incentives for submitting online reviews can increase product sales, but may diminish the authenticity and accuracy of assessments. Huang et al. (2018) found that cooperatively framed feedback more effectively motivates female consumers to create user-generated content, but that competitively framed messages are more successful in stimulating the same behavior among male counterparts. Finally, with regard to peer or social effects, Burtch et al. (2017) illustrated that the combination of social norms and <sup>fi</sup>nancial incentives can serve as the most effective strategy for eliciting online reviews of high quantities and great lengths. In parallel, Wang et al. (2018) recognized the presence of socialnudging behaviors among friends, which indicated that rating similarity between social acquaintances becomes more extensive after a friendship is formed.

To summarize, previous studies identi<sup>fi</sup>ed various factors that may lead to inaccurate online reviews, yet they all implicitly assumed that such evaluations are written only after customers fully consume or experience a rated product/good. Although this supposition may be inherently valid for goods and service categories (e.g., food or hotels) that require minimal attention during consumption, it may nonetheless fail to precisely represent the review and consumption process for many product classi<sup>fi</sup>cations that require a longer attention span (e.g., movies or books).

## 3. Hypothesis Development: Consumption and Review

Consumers can submit their reviews not necessarily after the full consumption of a product, but at any point in time once acquisition occurs, even when consumption has yet to take place. We drew on expectation con<sup>fi</sup>rmation theory as a conceptual foundation for assessing why many consumers write reviews before they have achieved a suf<sup>fi</sup>cient encounter with a product, particularly when no economic incentives are on offer in exchange for such evaluations. ECT regards consumer satisfaction as a function of expectation, perceived product quality, and discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation of original beliefs (Anderson 1973, Oliver 1980, Ho et al. 2017). Consumer satisfaction or dissatisfaction can therefore be conceptualized and measured on the basis of the discrepancy or disparity between expected and perceived product quality. Expectations are viewed as adapted standards and serve as the initial reference point from which to judge the positively or negatively oriented discon<sup>fi</sup>rmations of anticipated outcomes (Oliver 1980).

From the perspective of ECT, performance-speci<sup>fi</sup>c expectation and expectancy discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation are two core forces that shape the decisions of consumers and how these decisions factor in their satisfaction (Anderson 1973). Correspondingly, a discrepancy between an expectation and an objective or perceived quality gives rise to three possible scenarios: The assumption of a customer regarding a product is negatively discon<sup>fi</sup>rmed if the product’s quality is poorer than expected, con<sup>fi</sup>rmed if the product operates as anticipated, and positively discon<sup>fi</sup>rmed if the product’s quality exceeds expectations (Oliver 1980). The state of con<sup>fi</sup>rmation therefore denotes the midpoint of a discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation continuum that runs across negative and positive discon<sup>fi</sup>rmations, wherein the former may lead to an unfavorable product evaluation, and the latter may produce a favorable prod uct appraisal.

As maintained in ECT, the ex ante expectations and product-quality perceptions of consumers signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affect their ex post affective judgements and product evaluations (Oliver 1980, Anderson and Sullivan 1993). Satisfaction increases as the ratio between (ex post) observed quality and (ex ante) expectation rises (Oliver et al. 1997). Oliver (1980) introduced an integrative framework wherein the expectations, attitudinal changes, and subsequent satisfaction/dissatisfaction of consumers concertedly affect repurchase decisions. In the framework, the expectations of consumers are touted as important attributes for attitude formation prior to purchase, and satisfaction is regarded as a function of initial expectations (adaptation) and perceptions of discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation.

## 3.1. Expectation and Review-Posting Behaviors

ECT suggests that the intention of customers to submit reviews can arise at any point during attitude formation and re<sup>fl</sup>ection because such evaluations are the outcomes of consumer attitudes and satisfaction levels. That is, online reviews re<sup>fl</sup>ect not only the postpurchase assessments (i.e., category 3 in Figure 1) of consumers, but also their expectations before consumption (i.e., category 1) and their ongoing satisfaction and dissatisfaction (i.e., positive and negative discon<sup>fi</sup>rmations) during engagement with a product (i.e., category 2).

Although many consumers post online reviews to share their postconsumption experiences about a product, many others tender numerical evaluation scores and write reviews on the basis of their expectations regarding a product that they purchased, with such anticipations anchored in the hope that the product meets the prepurchase assumptions that they harbored. As maintained in hope theory (Snyder 2002), which derives from ECT, hope or hopeful thinking trigger positive emotions that engender perceptions of successful goal pursuit. For books, consumers who compose and post reviews before poring through content (i.e., category 1) are likely to exhibit strong aspirations that promote favorable feelings concerning striving for a target (e.g., experiencing joy or enjoying fantasy from entertainment books) (Snyder 2002). In this respect, expectations represent a preconsumptive belief toward “anticipated performance” (Westbrook and Reilly 1983), which may cause attitudinal changes and encourage consumers to disclose their preconsumption expectation in the form of ex post “evaluations.” Likewise, during content consumption, customers may encounter a certain positive or negative discrepancy between the expected and actual experience of a product (Ho et al. 2017) and are thus tempted to evaluate product quality prior to completion. In line with these arguments, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1. Given that online reviews reflect not only postconsumption experiences, but also expectations and (dis)confirmations about their anticipated quality, the submission of such evaluations can occur at any consumption stage (i.e., before, during, or after consumption).

## 3.2. The Relationship Between Consumption Rate and Review Valence

Although consumers post reviews at any point in time, regardless of consumption stages that range from expectation formation to complete product consumption, review valence may vary depending on consumption rate. Expectations provide a frame of reference for evaluative consumer judgement (Oliver 1977) and are often theorized as a function of assimilation, which refers to “the movement of perceived quality in the direction of expectations” (Anderson and Sullivan 1993, p. 129). That is, acquisition utility (Thaler 1985)—a form of utility (i.e., pleasure or excitement) derived from the purchase of a product—positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences consumers’ perceived quality, which increases directly with degrees of expectation. ECT postulates that preconsumptive expectations can in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumers’ affective states that arise out of approving quality perceptions, which, in turn, stimulates affective actions. Given that information on actual product quality is unavailable prior to consumption, consumers rely on their initial expectations in forming and implementing affective actions. In the case of reviewing, the acquisition utility gained from the customers’ purchase of a product is bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to expectation formation during the preconsumption stage, after which favorable expectations are assimilated with perceived quality that subsequently results in positive affective actions (e.g., submitting a review). In line with this reasoning, consumers who purchase a new book obtain acquisition utility that advances auspicious ex pectations about the product, thereby motivating high quality perceptions—all of these occurrences lead to a positive valence of online reviews at preconsumption.

While consumption is in progress, consumers are possibly inclined to post reviews when they are either strongly satis<sup>fi</sup>ed or dissatis<sup>fi</sup>ed and accordingly exhibit a bimodal distribution of review valence (Ho et al. 2017, Hu et al. 2017). Once they initiate the consumption of a product (i.e., books), acquisition utility diminishes accordingly (Thaler 1985). As consumption continues, they obtain more information about the “true” quality of a product, which may deviate from the originally perceived quality. Con<sup>fi</sup>rmation or discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation possibly emerges, potentially driving consumers to abandon a book before reaching its <sup>fi</sup>nal chapters. This decision is rooted in the belief that the disutility (costs) perceived from the consumption of chapters in the past or present eclipses the utility (bene<sup>fi</sup>ts) gained from reading the rest of the chapters in the future. These consumers are therefore likely to relinquish reading midsession and post reviews online to express their dissatisfaction. The behavioral pattern described above can be explained by ECT (Oliver and Swan 1989), which submits that the negation stemming from product consumption signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affects consumers’ postpurchase activities and behaviors, such as repurchase, loyalty, and online review or rating (Ho et al. 2017). In this regard, reviews grounded in fragmented product engagement may not be as favorable, considering that such assessments potentially evince a mixture of discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation and satisfaction.

Alternatively, consumers post reviews before <sup>fi</sup>nishing a book because the utility that they acquire from the consumption of early or middle chapters exceeds the possible disutility ensuing from reading the remaining chapters of the book. These readers post assessments to convey ful<sup>fi</sup>llment and then carry on reading afterward. However, such positive discon<sup>fi</sup>rmations might not be as frequent and prominent as negative discon<sup>fi</sup>rmations because consumers are more sensitive to losses than gains (Tversky and Kahneman 1991). This aligns with the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of Anderson and Sullivan (1993), who revealed that discon<sup>fi</sup>rmations can be characterized as an asymmetric loss function (i.e., losses loom larger than gains.). Review ratings informed by partial experience are therefore more negatively oriented than those provided at pre- or full-experience stages, owing to asymmetric discon<sup>fi</sup>rmations. Given that consumers are more responsive to losses than gains, negative discon<sup>fi</sup>rmations are more likely than positive ones to motivate consumers to submit reviews.

Finally, consumers who are in the full-consumption stage tend to enjoy the consumption of the product. When considering opportunity costs, greater consumption may be indicative of either con<sup>fi</sup>rmation or positive discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation, both of which engender satisfaction with and high evaluation of a product (i.e., books). Grati<sup>fi</sup>cation from consumption drives readers to consume more content, giving rise to higher content-completion rates. To sum up, review valence can be associated with consumption rates and follows a U-shaped pattern with respect to consumption stages. In accordance with the discussions above, we put forward the hypothesis below:

Hypothesis 2. Review valence is associated with consumption stages, with the relationship typified by a U-shaped pattern.

## 3.3. The Relationship Between Consumption Rate and Review Extremity

Previous studies (e.g., Forman et al. 2008, Mudambi and Schuff 2010, and Cao et al. 2011) demonstrated that the review extremity of numerical star-based ratings (i.e., one star or <sup>fi</sup>ve stars that deviate substantially from a middle point (three stars)) is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly associated with its helpfulness, although the directions of association are inconsistent. For books, extreme and two-sided numerical peer evaluations are often thought to be more helpful than moderate scores, as the former are more persuasive than the latter (Forman et al. 2008, Mudambi and Schuff 2010). However, such extreme reviews are considered less credible than their moderate counterparts for other product categories, such as movies (Schlosser 2011).

We suggest that review extremity varies contingent on consumption stages, each of which is underlain by different expectations—or what are referred to as con-<sup>fi</sup>rmation trajectories. Because review extremity can be conceptualized as the absolute deviation between a consumer’s evaluation and the true quality of a product, reviews submitted during preconsumption stages are likely to exhibit low extremity. Prior to consumption, consumers tend to have positive attitudes toward the perceived quality of a product because of their predispositions as regards assimilation (i.e., the movement of perceived quality in the same direction as expectations; Anderson 1973). An optimistic preconsumptive belief and the expectation toward “anticipated performance” (Westbrook and Reilly 1983) mean that extremely positive reviews are dominant at preconsumption, particularly for experienced goods, such as books (Ghose and Ipeirotis 2006); contrastingly, extremely negative ones rarely occur prior to consumption. Expectations can spring from products themselves, as well as from the context in which they are made available (e.g., social reference) (Helson 1959, Oliver 1980). Consumers may form their expectation on the basis of the information or reference (e.g., existing review) that they attain at purchase stage. Because consumers today can easily access online channels through which to obtain information on products of interest, they develop presuppositions and optimistic thinking that bring about attitudinal changes before they com mence with consumption. Correspondingly, review extremity at preconsumption stages is expected to be lower than that observed at midsession or completion phases.

Conversely, a high degree of review extremity can occur under partial experience of a product, as extremely positive and negative discon<sup>fi</sup>rmations are more liable to emerge while consumption is in progress than at preconsumption or full completion. As similations are unlikely to occur because differences between expectations and true quality go beyond consumers’ “latitude of acceptance” (Sherif and Hovland 1961). Consumers may aspire to spontaneously re<sup>fl</sup>ect their fondness or dislike for particular storylines or their progression and impulsively communicate their sentiments upon the encounter of positive or negative discon<sup>fi</sup>rmations. These spontaneous, or “unplanned,” ratings are more likely to be extremes than “planned” evaluations, and they appear more often during midsession than at pre- or full-consumption stages. Discon<sup>fi</sup>rmations that occur midsession may therefore enhance the extent of review extremity.

Finally, consumers who complete their consumption may maintain more balanced views about a product than those who abandon it, seeing as they are more likely to holistically and rationally evaluate the disparities between their expectations and con<sup>fi</sup>rmations. Excessively negative discon<sup>fi</sup>rmations that are the main source of review extremity are not as severe and frequent at the completion stage, as consumers who exhibit such dissat isfactions would have discontinued their consumption. Moreover, consumers who <sup>fi</sup>nish a product may feel a sense of accomplishment upon the completion of valued tasks (Elliot and Harackiewicz 1996), which, in turn, increases satisfaction and positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences rating. Against this backdrop, diminished negative discon<sup>fi</sup>rmations and the sense of pleasure derived from task completion lower review extremity at the completion stage, rather than during the consumption phase. In essence, the extremity of reviews that are informed by partial experience is higher than that of evaluations based on preconsumption or full completion. This results in an inverted U-shaped pattern in respect of the effects of consumption completion on review extremity. On this basis, we posit the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 3. Review extremity is associated with consumption stages, with such a relationship characterized by an inverted U-shaped pattern.

## 3.4. The Relationships Among Consumption Rate, Review Length, and Informativeness Rate. Review Length, and Informativeness

Review length and informativeness affect product sales and perceived usefulness (Ghose and Ipeirotis

2010). Text-driven reviews that contain contentspeci<sup>fi</sup>c information facilitate consumers’ reduction of uncertainties associated with a product and enhance con<sup>fi</sup>dence in their purchase decisions (Schlosser 2011). Review length, which measures the amount of information in a written review, is positively associated with review helpfulness (Kuan et al. 2015); when all else is equal, long reviews contain more comprehensive information and are more diagnostic and useful than short assessments (Mudambi and Schuff 2010). Likewise, review informativeness represents the extent to which peer evaluations speci<sup>fi</sup>cally inform consumers to make a successful purchase decision (Ghose and Ipeirotis 2010). When all else is equal, long reviews that provide details about speci<sup>fi</sup>c product characteristics and features therefore help consumers <sup>fi</sup>nd products that match their preferences (Goh et al. 2013). For books, long text reviews embedded with rich content about characters, stories, and styles are more useful than those that are short and general in nature (Ghose and Ipeirotis 2010).

On the subject of review length and informativeness, reviews that are crafted on the basis of full consumption are expected to be longer in length and more informative than those that are grounded in no or partial consumption. Either incomplete or no consumption is correlated with minimal facts and inaccurate assessments of book content. Although consumers can numerically rate a book on the basis of expectations or indirect experiences (i.e., others’ review comments) at preconsumption stages, they are unable to provide their own content-speci<sup>fi</sup>c evaluations through text commentaries. Given that reviews by others usually present insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient details about content (Choi et al. 2019), preconsumption structurally prevents users from writing comprehensive evaluations. Correspondingly, text reviews that are guided by preconsumptive expectations are likely to be shorter and less informative compared with those provided at either partial or full consumption stages.

Text reviews derived from positive or negative discon<sup>fi</sup>rmations at midsession may contain spontaneous reactions, with assessments lacking in detail about characters and stories. For experienced goods, such as books (e.g., <sup>fi</sup>ction and novels), customers consume noninstrumental information and derive entertainment value from these products in the form of suspense and surprise (Ely et al. 2015) or fantasy, emotional stimulation, and enjoyment (Holbrook and Hirschman 1982). Incomplete consumption precludes consumers from undergoing an entire course of emotional stimulation and intensi<sup>fi</sup>ed excitement. Moreover, as consumers only partially engage with a book, they are unable to unfold speci<sup>fi</sup>c details regarding characters and narratives. Their text-based reviews basically re<sup>fl</sup>ect their discon<sup>fi</sup>rmations about a book itself up to the point of consumption rather than unraveling content features. Consequently, reviews that are submitted upon incomplete consumption are longer and more informative than those composed at preconsumption stages, but they are relatively shorter and less informative than those that are written after full consumption. Consumers who <sup>fi</sup>nish a book, in all likelihood, wholly experi ence an array of features from which suspense and surprise ensure at the beginning to the end of the book. Thus, they are more solidly assimilated with the book and are more willing to express their feelings and emotions about its characters and narratives. The after consumption satisfactions or dissatisfactions of these customers are likewise conveyed in longer and more comprehensive detail than those that composed on the basis of partial experience. From this perspective, we espouse the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 4. Review length and review informativeness are positively associated with consumption rate.

## 5.1. Effects of Consumption Rates on Review Patterns

5.1.1. Relationships Between Review Posting and Consumption Rate. To scrutinize the relationship between review intention and consumption patterns, we considered how information (i.e., reviews from other individuals) attained at the purchase stage affects the decision of consumers on when to generate their own reviews. Discon<sup>fi</sup>rmation may arise when the qualityrelated beliefs formed on the basis of product information deviate from consumption experiences at the postpurchase stage (Ho et al. 2017). Thus, depending on the information that consumers derive at the purchase stage, they may form different expectations about product quality, which, in return, may motivate them to write reviews at various consumption stages. Given that review posting can occur at any point during the consumption process, we conducted survival analyses to cast light on how preconsumption information (e.g., exposure to reviews) affects consumers review intention.

In comparison with a binary choice model (i.e., logit), which uses dichotomous values (zero or one) as outcome variables, a hazard model allows consideration of not only whether an event happened, but also the period of time leading up to an event. Because our interest lies in when (i.e., before, during, or after consumption) a consumer writes an evaluation as he is in the process of consuming an e-book, survival analysis is more appropriate than the choice approach. We therefore utilized an extended Cox hazard model that is characterized by shared frailty (Kleinbaum and Klein 2011) to take account of the unobserved heterogeneity shared by groups of consumers. Shared frailty refers to the random effects or unobserved heterogeneity common to users whose consumption of an e-book falls within the same level. Shared frailty thus multiplies random effects in a hazard model, thereby explaining the within-group correlations in our analyses. This approach allows indi viduals who purchase the same e-book to distribute vul nerability among themselves; contrastingly, individuals across different e-book groups may have different frailty-related characteristics.

The proportional hazard model is one of the most widely used survival models and permits us to leave the underlying survival function unspeci<sup>fi</sup>ed. This leeway prevents a misspeci<sup>fi</sup>cation of the model and enables accurate results, as it ensures the estimation of the correct parametric form (Kleinbaum and Klein 2011). We matched the click-stream data on online reviews with the consumers’ book-description views and transactions based on consumer and product identi<sup>fi</sup>cation codes.

We expected the presence of unobserved and observed heterogeneity in purchase decisions across individuals. We therefore implemented a <sup>fi</sup>xed-effect logistic regression by including individual and product characteristics <sup>fi</sup>xed-effects terms in the analysis. A consumer i makes a purchase decision on the basis of the utility that he gains from this choice; this process depends on observed product information that is disclosed when the consumer arrives at a book-description page. Upon landing on that page, he looks over reviews and other book features, such as <sup>fi</sup>xed price, publication date, and content category (i.e., whether a book belongs to the adult category). Depending on the details acquired, the consumer may decide to buy the e-book in question. Thus, the latent utility of purchasing an e-book can be speci<sup>fi</sup>ed as follows:

The use of Equation (15) centered on the estimation of the effects that reviews crafted under partial consumption $( I n c o m p l e t e R e v i e w _ { i j } )$ exert on the purchase decisions of potential consumers. We computed the ratio of reviews that were written after incomplete consumption by dividing the number of such evaluations by the total number of reviews of a given e-book. As previously described, the empirical context of interest in this work is characterized by review exposure that transpires only upon clicking of the “review” tab on a book-description page. Correspondingly, examining the outcomes of inadequate assessments as regards consumers’ purchase decisions requires incorporating the aforementioned clickingdriven exposure as an additional condition in analysis.

Given our empirical setting, reviews composed under de<sup>fi</sup>cient consumption affect a consumer’s purchase decision only when he gains access to the concannot in<sup>fl</sup>uence a consumer’s decision to buy an e-book when he does not activate the “review” tab. We therefore interacted a variable that indicates whether a consumer is exposed to reviews (Review ) with the ratio of reviews crafted under partial product engagement (IncompleteReview ) to delineate the effects of reviews after incomplete consumption on consumers who are exposed to review content.

Our model speci<sup>fi</sup>cation can give rise to the assertion that exposure to reviews may be correlated with unobserved attributes, which potentially in<sup>fl</sup>uence the purchase decisions of consumers. For instance, customers who read review content by clicking on the “review” tab on a book-description page may be highly interested in corresponding books—a possibility that increases the likelihood of purchase. This selfselection issue may create bias and inconsistent estimation results. These endogenous problems are most frequently addressed through the use of an instrumental variable (Wooldridge 2010), but this approach may produce inaccurate estimates, given that the dependent and endogenous explanatory variables in our model are both binary (Wooldridge 2010). Alternatively, we employed the approach adopted by Danaher et al. (2015) in carrying out two-stage residual inclusion. A detailed explanation of the model speci<sup>fi</sup>cation can be found in Online Appendix J.

To conduct the two-stage residual inclusion for the purpose of alleviating endogeneity concerns, we <sup>fi</sup>rst carried out a logistic regression to calculate the predicted probabilities that an individual will access review content (Online Appendix J). Using the residuals (Res) obtained from estimation results in the <sup>fi</sup>rst-stage regression, we approximated the effect of reviews grounded in incomplete consumption on a consumer’s purchase decision. The estimates derived through the two-way <sup>fi</sup>xed-effect logistic regression are reported in the <sup>fi</sup>rst column of Table 10. The estimated coef<sup>fi</sup>cient Review is both positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at the 0.01 level, indicating that consumers are more likely to purchase an e-book when they are exposed to review content. These results are consistent with those of Choi et al. (2019), who found that the source of product information enhances e-book demand. Our main interest in this estimation was the exploration of the effects that incomplete-consumption reviews exert on the decision to buy an e-book. The estimated coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of the interaction between Review<sub>ij</sub> and IncompleteReview is negative and statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at the 0.01 level, demonstrating that consumers who are exposed to review content are less likely to purchase an e-book as the percentage of reviews based on incomplete consumption increases. However, the estimated coef<sup>fi</sup>- cient of IncompleteReview is statistically insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant, suggesting that reviews formed after incomplete consumption do not affect the purchase decisions of consumers if they are unexposed to such resources. These results corroborate our <sup>fi</sup>ndings in the main results. Note that the estimated coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of the probability residual is positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, suggesting that the naïve estimation would have overstated the impact of exposure to review content, even without consideration of the endogeneity issue.

In addition, we conducted a robustness check with a subsample of reviews to corroborate the economic impact of reviews derived from incomplete consumption. In our empirical setting, consumers must click on the review tab to access book reviews, which are structurally segmented by pages (10 reviews per page). Given this structure, consumers are required to click on the “next” button to proceed to another set of 10 reviews in the succeeding review page. Because consumers may not read the entire reviews available on the pages, we chose the 10 most recent reviews displayed on the <sup>fi</sup>rst review page to further validate the economic effects of reviews generated after partial consumption. The results are consistent with those obtained using full samples, thus reinforcing the validity of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings (column (2), Table 10).

## 7. Implications

Given that data on consumers’ actual consumption are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to collect, online-review studies have largely overlooked this behavior with respect to the evaluation of products. Researchers have thus far construed the act of purchase to be identical to consumption, but the reality is that many of the goods bought by customers are often only partially consumed, or not at all. Understanding the distinction between buying and consuming motivated the current work, as such difference in<sup>fl</sup>uences the evaluative behaviors of consumers and the sub sequent sales generated by vendors.

This research found that consumers submit their e-book reviews at various consumption categories— before, during, and after actual consumption—and that a considerable number of consumers in our sample did so with either partial or no consumption. Using ECT as theoretical grounding, we articulated the motives behind consumers’ posting of reviews under fragmentary or no consumption. We uncovered that consumers provide evaluations not only to re<sup>fl</sup>ect their ex post feelings or satisfaction, but also to convey their ex ante expectations and initial con<sup>fi</sup>rmations. Expectations can trigger the performance of behaviors as a means of re<sup>fl</sup>ecting positive emotions or hopeful thinking toward goal pursuit. This may theoretically expound on why many of the customers in our sample submitted their numerical ratings on books that they purchased even before they initiated reading.

On the basis of consumption data and text-mining analytics, we empirically demonstrated that consumers assign numerical review ratings and provide textbased commentary with guidance from their anticipated encounters with products. These expectation-based ratings do not count as fake reviews, given the absence of intention behind the act, but they can be as inaccurate as deceptive reviews. Consumers also proffer assessments in the middle of the reading trajectory or when reading is in progress. A U-shaped pattern was observed for review valence, indicating that the reviews generated either at the initial consumption stage (i.e., extremely low completion rate) or the full consumption phase (i.e., extremely high completion rate) exhibit a higher review valence relative to those that fall between extreme ends of the consumption continuum. Contrastingly, however, an inverted U-shaped pattern characterizes the effects of completion rates on review extremity. That is, reviews informed by partial product experience exhibit higher extremity than do evaluations grounded in full or no consumption. As illuminated through ECT, consumers are predisposed toward instantly expressing negative or positive sentiments that stem from negative or positive discon<sup>fi</sup>rma tions, respectively. This mixture of emotions that occur during consumption therefore causes high extremity for reviews grounded in incomplete consumption.

These results add meaningful nuances to previous works that delved into review characteristics and thei importance as a sales driver. For example, the presence of reviews underlain by incomplete and no consump tion may partially account for the grade-in<sup>fl</sup>ation prob lem on a star-rating scheme. A Wall Street Journal article has recently reported that the average rating of Net<sup>fl</sup>ix <sup>fi</sup>lms is above 4.3 stars (Fowler 2017). Similarly, Zervas et al. (2015) found that 95% of Airbnb properties received either 4.5 or 5 stars. The reviews derived from either insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient or no consumption may, to a certain degree, explain in<sup>fl</sup>ated numerical averages. Our results re<sup>fl</sup>ected that the incomplete experience of content is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly associated with high variances in review ratings. Although review extremity has been extensively adopted as a sound measure that represents diversity in reviews, no underlying mechanisms have been proposed as to why such extreme patterns occur among reviewers. This void can be <sup>fi</sup>lled by the use of consumption level as a source of review extremity. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings can also extend the works of Mudambi and Schuff (2010), who focused on product type and review depth in identifying the characteristics of helpful reviews, as the current research re<sup>fi</sup>ned the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of bene<sup>fi</sup>cial reviews as those anchored in suf<sup>fi</sup>cient content consumption. Our text-mining analysis clari<sup>fi</sup>ed that assessments founded in full consumption are more informative and comprehensive (e.g., details) than those ground ed in either incomplete or no consumption.

This paper likewise sheds light on the value of consumption data, which contain <sup>fi</sup>ne-grained information on when and how consumers read e-books. Whereas conventional marketing relies on either download or purchase information to devise effective strategies, consumption-based analytics capitalizes on granular information regarding consumer behaviors. Our study demonstrated that consumption information unravels essential aspects of the evaluative mode that consumers deploy when assessing products— aspectsthat can precisely capture the value perceptions and preferences of customers.

A number of key practical perspectives for digital platforms and content marketers emerged from our results. We ascertained that completion rates are directly associated with consumers’ online-review-entry behaviors. E-book consumers with either extremely high or low completion rates tend to provide higher review ratings than do consumers with moderate completion rates. These customers can therefore be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to e-book sellers with respect to the quantitative aspects of their online assessments, which can increase sales. Marketers can target these users when necessary to solicit reviews for newly published books that have received limited evaluations. In terms of review quality, however, consumers who show incomplete consumption write shorter and less informative text reviews than do consumers located at a suf<sup>fi</sup>cient consumption stage. Consequently, online reviews produced by these consumers may provide insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient and inaccurate information. Supporting this assumption, our additional analyses showed that review comments based on incomplete consumption adversely affect subsequent sales. Because overrated numerical star scores are frequently misleading and meaningless, many consumers depend heavily on text-based commentaries, in which case, reviews derived from incomplete consumption may do more harm than good for retailer performance.

Digital platforms may leverage our <sup>fi</sup>ndings and insights to improve their design of review systems and policies in ways that enhance the trustworthiness of peer evaluations and correct potential inaccuracies from fragmentary assessments. For example, managers can revise their review “sorting” structure so that consumers can <sup>fl</sup>exibly reposition or reorder text-based reviews, thereby enabling comments informed by full product completion to be displayed <sup>fi</sup>rst before those that are predicated on incomplete consumption. Similarly, platforms can provide visible review signals from which future consumers can infer the quality of assessments based on the consumption rates of previous consumers. Scheme managers can af<sup>fi</sup>x a visual mark denoting a full-consumption-based review next to assessments that are carried out after suf<sup>fi</sup>cient consumption. Managers can also consider providing aggregated review statistics, such as average consumption rate or variance. These implementations enable consumers to accurately identify and adopt evaluations that rely on ample experience. Finally, content retailers can establish marketing schemes meant to advertise books that are most extensively consumed, similar to the measures implemented for best-selling publications. For example, sellers can promote the top 50 best-read books to augment sales and boost their online-review structures and performance. Likewise, marketers can design promotional strategies wherein consumers who exhibit high average completion rates are regularly targeted with the promise of lucrative discount coupons. Platforms must decide on whether they provide these additional services and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts for free or at minimum fees, as well as determine whether these are to be geared toward certain consumer segments (e.g., highly valuable consumers) or entire customer populations. We advise retailers not to exploit the offering of these services for additiona pro<sup>fi</sup>ts, but to use them as a means of enhancing the trust and con<sup>fi</sup>dence of current and future consumers toward their review structures.

Managers can also take advantage of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings to effectively re-establish their review-posting policies to encourage consumers to submit reviews after full consumption. For instance, review systems can send nudge messages to customers who attempt to post evaluations grounded in insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient consumption to stimulate action only after complete consumption. Alternatively, managers can award badges as social incentives to consumers who frequently perform evaluations after fully or suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently enjoying a product. Managers who can gain substantial business bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from accurate and trustworthy reviews may provide monetary incentives or reward points to consumers who generate adequately informed evaluations. When such <sup>fi</sup>nancial inducements are a burden or cannot be sustained for long periods, they can be offered in the short term as part of promotional campaigns. Even campaigns of short durations can effectively change consumers’ habitual posting behaviors and sensitize them so that they offer quality reviews that help other consumers. All of these initiatives, whether in the form of social or <sup>fi</sup>nancial incentives, can increase the trustworthiness and credibility of online reviews, thus bene<sup>fi</sup>tting consumers as well as retailers.

## 8. Conclusion

Extensive research has presented evidence on the salient aspects of online reviews as information sources, but such scholarly works relied on the supposition that consumers proffer online valuations after thoroughly experiencing the products on which they pass judgment. Our work presented an alternative perspective to this traditional view, unveiling various aspects of online reviews that are generated on the basis of incomplete consumption. Customers provide online reviews before, during, and after consumption, and many of them do so without the informed judgment enabled by product/service experience. Consequently, assessments founded in fragmentary encounters are a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant new source of challenge to efforts at achieving informativeness and accuracy in critiques. More importantly, such “incomplete reviews” exert negative effects on the subsequent sales of books.

A number of directions are worth further investigation. For instance, although we attempted to explore the motives that drive incomplete reviews, future studies can use qualitative approaches to probe more extensively into why consumers write online assessments without the bene<sup>fi</sup>t of suf<sup>fi</sup>cient consumption. Our study focused exclusively on entertainment products—hedonic books (e.g., <sup>fi</sup>ction and fantasy)— we made no attempt to generalize our <sup>fi</sup>ndings to other content categories. Given that review characteristics and helpfulness may vary depending on product type (Mudambi and Schuff 2010), the effects of content completion rates on ratings and written comments can also differ when other book categories (i.e., utility and educational books) are the objects of analyses. Finally, this study devoted its attention to only completion rates, but other types of consumption patterns (e.g., reading duration or concentrated reading) can also in<sup>fl</sup>uence review characteristics. Consumption patterns can serve as a valuable predictor of review rating. Other researchers would do well to pay attention to more dynamic and comprehensive aspects of consumption patterns and incorporate such regularities when assessing their effects on reviews and subsequent sales.

In conclusion, the consumption behavior of consumers is mostly an uninvestigated issue, particularly as regards online reviews. The majority of customers are unaware of whether a particular review was crafted by someone who actually consumed a product or by a biased customer who has never experienced it. To the best of our knowledge, this study is among the <sup>fi</sup>rst to identify the practice of review provision under the incomplete consumption of content and untangle the economic effects of such evaluations. Consumptionbased analytics is still an emerging technology, but it is a powerful means of deriving renewed insight into consumer behaviors and preferences beyond those that traditional purchase records and other transactional data have provided.
