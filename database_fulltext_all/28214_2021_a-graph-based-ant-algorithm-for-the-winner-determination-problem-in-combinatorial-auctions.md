# A Graph-Based Ant Algorithm for the Winner Determination Problem in Combinatorial Auctions

Abstract. Combinatorial auctions (CAs) are used to allocate bundles of items among interested bidders. However, to resolve bidder preference elicitation problems, CAs are conducted iteratively. Winner determination is a key bottleneck that restricted the widespread adoption of such iterative CAs. Time bounded winner determination is further complicated by the increased variety and velocity of bids each round, and so regular solvers such as IBM CPLEX and A Mathematical Programming Language have been demonstrably ineffective in determining winners within a short duration. In response, heuristic-based meth ods have enjoyed an increase in applicability. We add to the growing body of work in heuristic-based winner determination by proposing an ant colony–based anytime algorithm (<sup>tr</sup>aveling <sup>a</sup>nts for <sup>c</sup>ombinatorial <sup>a</sup>uctions, or TrACA) that produces optimal or nearoptimal winner determination results within speci<sup>fi</sup>ed time. Experiments are performed on 94 open test instances that display a variety in bid-bundle composition. We show and compare the performance of TrACA to 20 most recent state-of-the-art heuristics and two of the best exact algorithms (CPLEX and Max W Clique). Compared with 12 of 20 heuristics that employ classic search (e.g., stochastic local search, tabu search) or genetic or memetic algorithms, TrACA does signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly better on all instances. On the other eight heuristics that employ advanced hybrid search methodologies (e.g., hyperheuristics, biased random keys), TrACA performs signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly better on the harder test instances. Additionally, we show that TrACA resolves the trade-off between time and accuracy by achieving optimal solutions on 76 out of 94 instances, in at most one-sixth the time taken by exact algorithms. Finally, we analyze the TrACA search process and highlight factors that make TrACA suitable for solving hard auction instances within a prespeci<sup>fi</sup>ed (short) duration.

## 1. Introduction

Combinatorial auctions (CAs) are increasingly used to improve allocative ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency in diverse industries such as telecommunications and transportation logistics (Triki et al. 2020). However, computational limitations are quite severe for CAs. A major one is regarding bidder preference elicitation. Because CAs allow bidders to express complex preferences (e.g., substitutability or complementarity) over bundles of items, the preference elicitation complexity increases exponentially with the number of items (Sandholm and Suri 2003). In order to address the preference elicitation problem, combinatorial auctions are conducted iteratively, with multiple rounds of bidding (Lahaie and Lubin 2019). Major iterative combinatorial auction (ICA) formats are, for example, continuous or discrete, based on when a bidder can submit bids (Bichler et al. 2009; refer to Figure 1 for an overview of the ICA mechanism). A continuous ICA allows bids to be submitted at any time with continuous updates to the current provisional winner determination. Discrete ICAs are round based and revise provisional winner determination periodically, with bidders given an opportunity to revise bids between rounds. Essentially, in either auction format, provisional winner determi nation entails identifying the tentative winners based on bids submitted thus far. This problem is known as the winner determination problem (WDP) and is known to be NP-complete (Sandholm 2002a). As the ICA is becoming widely used in cloud computing and electricity and energy markets, WDP intractability is a bottleneck for businesses looking to conduct such auctions quickly, for example, in 15–20 minutes.<sup>1</sup> The key trade-off is between speed and accuracy—solving the WDP fast and also close to optimality (Bichler 2010, Adomavicius et al. 2020).

In this paper, we design a search methodology that addresses this speed versus accuracy trade-off. Speci<sup>fi</sup>- cally, our search algorithm incorporates an improved metaheuristic and dynamically adapts to the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty of a given WDP problem, while searching for the globally optimal solution within limited time. To achieve this, we convert the integer programming formulation of the (WDP-IP) into a directed graphical formulation. Unlike previous work in exact algorithms that use a search tree (Sandholm et al. 2005) or heuristics (refer to Table 1) that use a binary search space representation, we use a directed cyclic graph to represent WDP solutions. Our formulation is especially useful for intractable WDP problems (Lau and Goh 2002), is extendable to other graph search algorithms or heuristics, and can be adapted to multiunit multi-item auctions (e.g., virtual machine instance auctions in cloud computing; Bichler et al. 2009). We develop an ant colony–based anytime algorithm (TrACA—traveling ants for combinatorial auctions) that searches the directed cyclic graph for the optimal solution. The anytime property allows <sup>fl</sup>exibility for auctioneers to stop algorithm execution before completion, if required, but still obtain optimal or near-optimal solutions (Jesus et al. 2020). For example, energy market auctions typically conduct short bidding rounds within an hour that allow bidders time to address any communication issues, resort to back-up systems, or contact the auctioneer to extend the round if needed (Ausubel and Baranov 2017). This <sup>fl</sup>exibility is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant given iterative auction formats that stress adaptable and bidder-centric round timing. The convergence property allows auctioneers to use more computational capacity for improved convergence to optimal solutions, over the same predetermined time. For example, competitive auction software<sup>2</sup> are offered as “auction-as-a-service” for businesses to customize auctions as per computational capacity, time window, and bid volumes. Timeindependent convergence is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant given everincreasing computational resources available to businesses (Adomavicius and Gupta 2005). Additionally, it also helps in tackling problem of auction data velocity (speed of bid-data generation), given such auctions are being conducted iteratively (Adomavicius et al. 2013). Finally, we introduce two methodological improvements to the ant colony heuristic: randomized phero mone updating (RPU) and randomized graph pruning (RGP). RGP and RPU together improve the exploration– exploitation trade-off for TrACA. RPU improves on recent pheromone updating methods such as MMAS (min-max ant system; He et al. 2017) and path-guided pheromone updating (Ning et al. 2018, Zhang and Zhang 2018) by reducing complexity of pheromone update mechanism. Better pheromone updating implies better exploration of the search space for the global opti mal solution. RGP improves on recent pruning-based ant colony algorithms (Mehrabi et al. 2009, Goodwin and Yazidi 2016) by introducing random pruning of unattractive edges. Pruning randomization prevents loss of valuable but unexplored edges while improving the speed of exploration of the graph.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we provide a basic overview of the WDP, our ant colony formulation, and the mathematical basis for fast convergence. Sections 3 and 4 explain the test beds used for testing the metaheuristic and the experimental results.<sup>3</sup> Section 5 delves into the search effectiveness of TrACA, compared to the past work. In Section 6, we extend our approach to multiunit multi-item CAs. Finally, we conclude in Section 7 by explaining the relevance of our approach in the context of recent applications of heuristics for solving combinatorial auctions in the industry.

## 2. WDP Formulation 2.1. Preliminaries

Consider the simplest version of the WDP as described in Lehmann et al. (2006). Let there be k items represented by the set $S = \{ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 . . . , k \}$ that are being auctioned. Interested bidders submit bids on bundles (combinations/subsets) of items from S. The auctioneer conducts this auction in multiple rounds, wherein at the end of each round, winning bids are provisionally allocated before bidders are invited to bid again (Adomavicius et al. 2019). Two key constraints are maintained throughout all the auction rounds: (1) an item can be allocated to at most one bidder, and (2) not all items need to be sold to bidders in each round. Suppose for the given round, a set of l bids, $B = \{ b _ { 1 } , b _ { 2 } , . . . , b _ { l } \}$ is received. A bid $b _ { j } \in B$ for al $j \in l$ is a tuple de<sup>fi</sup>ned as $( S _ { j } , q _ { j } )$ , where $S _ { j } \subseteq { \dot { S } }$ is a unique subset of items (or bundles), and $q _ { j } > 0$ is the maximum bid price associated with $S _ { j }$ . Furthermore, consider a matrix $A _ { k \times l }$ such that $A _ { i j } = 1$ for all $i \in k , j \in l$ if item $i \in S _ { j }$ . Finally, the decision variables are $x _ { j } = 1$ if bid $b _ { j }$ is accepted as winning and $x _ { j } = 0$ otherwise. The formulation for maximizing auctioneer’s revenue is equivalent to the weighted set-packing problem:

$$
\mathrm{WDP-IP:} \quad \max _ {x _ {j}} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {l} q _ {j} x _ {j}\tag{1}
$$

$$
\text { subject   to } \quad \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {l} A _ {i j} x _ {j} \leq 1 \quad \forall i \in \{1, 2, 3, 4...., k \},\tag{2}
$$

$$
x _ {j} \in \{0, 1 \} \quad \forall j \in \{1, 2, 3, 4 \dots , l \}.\tag{3}
$$

Equation (2) ensures that each item is allocated at most once. This formulation also relaxes the assumption of selling all items every round. This formulation is NP-complete (Sandholm et al. 2005). Previous attempts at solving the WDP-IP have used exact approaches $( \mathrm { e . g . } ,$ CPLEX solver). However, as the volume and variety of bundles increase, the exact approaches either fail or take too long to solve for winners (Takalloo et al. 2021).

## 2.2. Graphical Formulation of the WDP

Transforming the WDP-IP into a graph consists of two steps: preprocessing and construction. For preprocessing, the auction data set is analyzed to extract unique bundles and their associated maximum prices. This follows Sandholm (2002a), where it is shown that when maximizing revenue, an auctioneer is better off accepting the highest bid for a bundle. Using preprocessed data, a directed graph is constructed in four steps:

1. Let $G ( V , E )$ have vertices as the unique bundles. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, if m is the number of unique bundles, for which an associated maximum bid price exists, then, $V = \{ v _ { i } \mid v _ { i } = S _ { i } \subseteq S \forall j = 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 . . . m \}$

2. A directed edge exists between any two vertices $( v _ { i } , v _ { j } ) \in V$ if and only if the subsets represented by the vertices are disjoint. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally,

$$
E = \left\{\left(v _ {i}, v _ {j}\right) \mid v _ {i} \cap v _ {j} = \emptyset \forall v _ {i}, v _ {j} \in V \right\}.\tag{4}
$$

3. Add source s and target t to the vertex set $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ $V \equiv V \cup \{ s , t \} )$ ). They respectively represent a starting and an ending point for a path traversed by each ant. Create a directed edge from s to every vertex in $V \backslash \{ t \}$ Similarly, create a directed edge from every vertex in $V \backslash \{ s \}$ to t. No direct edge exists between s and t.

4. Add edge weights. The weight of the edge $( v _ { i } , v _ { j } )$ is the maximum bid value associated with bundle $v _ { j }$ and vice versa for edge $( v _ { j } , v _ { i } )$ ; weight for any edge incoming to vertex t is zero:

2.2.1. Example Construction. Consider the set of bun dles $( b _ { 1 } = \{ A \} , b _ { 2 } = \{ B \} , b _ { 3 } = \{ A , B \} )$ . Let the associated maximum bid amounts for these bundles be $\{ 3 , 4 , 9 \}$ respectively. Denote the vertices as $V = \ ( s , \{ A \} , \{ B \}$ $\{ A , \bar { B } \} , t )$ . Construct edges following Equation (4). An example path on the graph is $s  b _ { 1 }  b _ { 2 }  t .$ The auctioneer gets a revenue of seven. However as obvious, the optimal revenue is on the path $s - b _ { 3 } - t ,$ which is nine. Note also that $s - b _ { 1 } - b _ { 2 } - t$ and $s - b _ { 2 } -$ $b _ { 1 } - t$ are equivalent as expected (Figure 2).

Definition 1. A path on G(V, E) is a sequence of vertices starting at s and ending at t.

Definition 2. A feasible path of the graph $G ( V , E )$ is a path such that for bid bundles $b _ { j }$ associated with each vertex $v _ { j } , b _ { i } \cap b _ { j } = \emptyset \forall i , j = 1 , 2 , . . , n$ and $i \neq j$

Definition 3. An optimal path is a maximum weight feasible path starting at s, ending at t, and containing each node of $V = \{ v _ { 1 } , v _ { 2 } , v _ { 3 } , . . . . . , v _ { m } \}$ at most once.

By construction, edges exist between nodes (bundles) only when bundles are disjoint (refer to Equation (4)). Infeasible paths are those that exist between nodes without any connecting edges or those that have more than one visit on any node. Hence, our search algorithm rejects such infeasible paths as solutions. In the following proposition, we show, given feasibility, how our graphical formulation is equivalent to the WDP-IP.

Proposition 1. Directed graphs G(V,E) from the preprocessing step have the following properties:

1. A feasible path from s to t represents a feasible solution.

2. A feasible path of maximum weight on the graph represents an optimal solution.

The proofs are in Appendix A. Proposition 1 serves as the proof of correctness for the solution representation of the WDP-IP as a graph-based path search problem. Such correctness proofs are essential in demonstrating ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of reductions of the stated problem between different representations (Tao et al. 2019, Paliwal et al. 2020). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, because conversion from one form of representation to another should not alter problem complexity, Proposition 1 demonstrates that even though the linear WDP-IP is reduced to a graph-based one, the complexity of searching for the maximum weighted simple path on a cyclic graph retains the complexity (NP-Hard) nature of the WDP-IP. However, because combinatorial auction bids imply substitutability and complementarity between items, graphs allow improved solution representation and means of ef<sup>fi</sup>cient search for global optimum. This is because graph-based problem representations are one of the best ways of formally taking into account all the correlations between the entities that may or may not display obvious connections, for example, items in combinatorial auctions.

2.3.4. Randomized Graph Pruning. Randomized graph pruning is used for ef<sup>fi</sup>cient and fast approximations of solutions for very large problem instances. Our approach differs in two ways from deterministic graph pruning that is used to remove infeasible edges (Mehrabi et al. 2009, Goodwin and Yazidi 2016). First, we do not enforce feasibility using pruning (refer to Algorithm 1, Line 9). Second, using randomized pruning, we remove a subset of those edges that are likely part of local optima. Edge removal randomization reduces the likelihood of convergence to different local optima and speeds up search. The pseudocode is shown in Algorithm 2. Pruning edges is accomplished using a count matrix C. Essentially, C keeps count of number of times an edge is visited by ants in each iteration. Those edges on the globally optimal path would have the highest count. Those edges on local optima would have less than the global optimum but greater than one. We identify such edges using the count matrix and randomly prune a subset of those edges. Random pruning accomplishes two objectives: <sup>fi</sup>rst, it reduces computational burden on the solver, on determining which edge(s) are the best to prune, and second, it prevents introduction of bias in pruning that might lead to removing edges that might get added to the globally optimal path in future iterations.

## 2.4. Theoretical Convergence to Optimal

Graph-based ant system (GBAS) formulations can be theoretically shown to converge to the global optimum, under certain parameter values (Gutjahr 2000). Typically, such proofs of such convergence have been time based and have been shown for Traveling Salesman Problem formulations (Stutzle and Dorigo 2002). In this section, we prove the time-independent convergence to optimal for TrACA. Time-independent convergence is different from previous approaches in the following ways: <sup>fi</sup>rst, unlike Stutzle and Dorigo (2002), we consider the ant system de<sup>fi</sup>ned by Algorithm 1 for a speci<sup>fi</sup>c period $t \in ( 1 , 2 , \ldots , T )$ , where T ; second, whereas pheromone bounds in time-dependent convergence might deter fast convergence (Stutzle and Dorigo 2002), in our case, pheromone bounds facilitate convergence, within a given iteration $t ;$ <sup>fi</sup>nally, whereas both Gutjahr (2000) and Stutzle and Dorigo (2002) emphasize that convergence can only be guaranteed given a minimum number of iterations $t \geq t _ { 0 }$ are reached, in our proof, convergence is guaranteed for any number of iterations $t \in \{ 1 , { \overset { \cdot } { 2 } } , \dots , T \}$

Consider the ant system (GBAS) as described in Algorithm 1. First, recall the following: $p _ { j h }$ from Algorithm 1 (Line 8) and Equation (5) for edge weights. Additionally, recall that only a vertex $v _ { h } \notin T L$ can be selected to construct a feasible path. Last, we assume the following: <sup>fi</sup>rst, we assume that in any given iteration, pheromone levels on edges are bounded, $\tau _ { j h } ( t ) \in [ \tau _ { m i n } , \tau _ { m a x } ] ;$ second, at any node $v _ { h } \neq \{ s , t \}$ during any iteration, an ant will have at least one edge to choose from. Let the set of paths constructed by the ants during iteration t be $M \bar { ( t ) }$ , and let the path representing the global optimal path be $M _ { o p t } .$ . Furthermore, let L denote the set of edges on the globally optimal path $M _ { o p t } ,$ and so the cardinality L is the number of edges in the path $M _ { o p t } .$ Let there be N ants $( a _ { 1 } , a _ { 2 } , a _ { 3 } . . . , a _ { N } )$ traversing the graph in each iteration $t = \left\{ 1 , 2 , 3 . . . \right\}$ . The following proposition characterizes the convergence property of our algorithm.

Proposition 2. The probability $P _ { o p t }$ that at least one ant traverses the optimal path during iteration t can be made arbitrarily close to one for values o $\cdot \tau _ { m i n } , \alpha , \beta , N > 0$

This proposition highlights the usefulness of TrA-CA as a search algorithm for the WDP. Proposition 2 indicates that using our approach, convergence can be achieved within a speci<sup>fi</sup>ed amount of time, rather than an increasing length of time. Time-independent convergence is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant from a practical standpoint. Auctioneers want faster clearing of rounds that allows giving bidders feedback and facilitating price discovery (Ausubel and Baranov 2017). Major auction software platforms<sup>5</sup> promote customizable computational capacity as a value proposition for auctioneers (Bichler et al. 2020). TrACA’s design framework shows how faster market clearing with variable computational capacity can be achieved. The pheromone values on edges can be used to adaptively learn and improvise the construction of optimal solution. With higher intensity of search, there is improved learning of the search space, which helps to converge onto the optimal solution. Convergence proofs for other heuristics (e.g., genetic algorithms (GAs) or memetic algorithms (MAs)) rely on <sup>fi</sup>nding better solutions with increased numbers of iterations. However, we show TrACA has convergence close to optimal for speci<sup>fi</sup>ed iterations, but driven by the number of ants.

Basically, the procedure for dynamic parameter update is as follows: at the start of each experimental trial, the parameters are instantiated to a range $\alpha \in ( 1 , 5 ) , \beta \in ( 1 , 5 ) ;$ ; during run time, at prespeci<sup>fi</sup>ed iterations, for each node (column in pheromone matrix) entropy is calculated using the standard Shannon entropy formula $\begin{array} { r } { ( X ) = - \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { n } \breve { P } ( x _ { i } ) \mathrm { l o g } _ { 2 } ( x _ { i } ) } \end{array}$ . The resulting vector of entropy for each node is tested for skewness. Based on skewness, α and $\beta$ are updated to improve search. This means there are more nodes with low entropy values, which implies the search is getting concentrated on fewer regions of the graph. Hence, an increase in α and decrease in $\beta$ is done to improve search.

Generally, winner determination is computationally complex in those cases where more items are common among different bundles up for auction (Sandholm 2002a, Sandholm et al. 2005). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, exact algorithms (e.g., CPLEX) face search complexity because they typically use a con<sup>fl</sup>ict graph (edges between bundles as nodes, if those bundles have items in common) when searching for the optimal solution. In practice, when item variety is high and bundles are fewer in comparison, increased common items among bundles implies denser con<sup>fl</sup>ict graphs, which implies more time-consuming search (Sandholm et al. 2005). However, TrACA search using our directed cyclic graph achieves optimal or near optimal solutions in much less time. The directed cyclic graph has edges only between bundles that do not have items in common, in contrast to con<sup>fl</sup>ict graphs. Furthermore, RGP and RPU work together to reduce the exploration-versus-exploitation trade-off effectively. Finally, entropy-based search intensity modi-<sup>fi</sup>cations helps TrACA adapt to instance dif<sup>fi</sup>culty faster than other approaches. ## 5. TrACA Search Effectiveness Analysis

We use the standard empirical hardness approach for analyzing TrACA search effectiveness (Leyton-Brown et al. 2002, Hanselle et al. 2020). We regress TrACA run times on search space features. Results from the regression help analyze what makes TrACA search effective in solving the WDP optimally. Furthermore, regression coef<sup>fi</sup>cients help characterize the impact of bid-bundle features of dif<sup>fi</sup>cult WDP instances on TrACA run times. Because the normality assumption on residuals is relaxed, estimation of predictor variable coef<sup>fi</sup>cients is done using iterated reweighted least squares (IRLS) regression. Because the sample size is small, coef<sup>fi</sup>cients generated from the IRLS step are bootstrapped. Con<sup>fi</sup>- dence intervals are constructed for each bootstrapped predictor variable coef<sup>fi</sup>cient. Clearly for CC , (transitivity in directed graph), run times can be signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly decreased if more bids tend to cluster together. In other words, if the bid-bundle distribution is such that sets of bundles have few items in common (hence, form clusters), then run times are shortened. With increased data velocity, clustering helps solve the problem of larger variety and volume of auction data. For $A E V C _ { i } ,$ de<sup>fi</sup>ned as average eigenvector centrality, run times can increase if there are more “important” bundles (vertices) connected to each other; that is, if the variety of bundles received is such that there are a few bundles that have fewer items in common with other bundles, then run times are increased. In contrast to the clustering effect, here individual bundles (vertices) gain prominence (more edges) in the directed graph. In sum, when $C \check { C _ { i } }$ and $A E V C _ { i }$ are considered together, our approach demonstrates that increase in variety of bundles should not necessarily imply increased run times for computing winners. In fact, the structure (e.g., itembundle composition) of bundles in each round has a major impact on winner determination run times. If bidders bid on rarely bid-on bundles, the resulting bid-bundle data set has a certain number of nondominated bids of high bid price. These bids need to be considered during every WDP computation, which impacts the run time of determining winners each round adversely. Average eigenvector centrality of the directed graph captures this effect. In contrast, if more bidders are bidding for mutually exclusive bundles (not necessarily unique altogether), thereby producing clusters in our graphical formulation, run times are shortened. Transitivity of the directed graph captures this effect. TrACA incorporates search improvements without compromising on run time or solution quality. Both $\operatorname { R G P }$ and RPU reduce search complexity by incentivizing search on promising areas of the graph (RPU) while reducing overall search space size (RGP). Search space size reduction allows faster exploration, whereas randomized pheromone updates incentivize more effective but unbiased exploitation. 6. Extension for Multiunit, Multi-Item CAs In this section, we extend the TrACA approach from the traditional single-unit CAs to multiunit CAs. Mul tiunit CAs are increasingly becoming the mechanism of choice in two major domains: cloud computing auctions and electricity markets (Sandholm 2002b, Kwasnica et al. 2005). With dynamic pricing of resources getting more common in these domains (Brena et al. 2015), winner determination under time constraints and increased participation from interested bidders are poised to become important problems to solve.

The setting for a multiunit multi-item auction is as follows. Let $N = \{ g _ { 1 } , g _ { 2 } , g _ { 3 } , . . . , g _ { m } \}$ be a set of items. Each item $g _ { i }$ for all $i \in N$ has $q ( g _ { i } )$ units of it available for auction. Consider a set of bids $B = \{ b _ { 1 } , b _ { 2 } , b _ { 3 } , . . . , b _ { n } \}$ Each bid $b _ { j }$ for all $j \in B$ is a pair $( p ( b _ { j } ) , e ( b _ { j } ) )$ , where $p ( b _ { j } )$ is the price offer for bundle $e ( b _ { j } ) =$ $( e ( b _ { j } ) _ { 1 } , e ( b _ { j } ) _ { 2 } , . . . , e ( b _ { j } ) _ { m } )$ that consists of $e ( b _ { j } ) _ { i } \leq q ( g _ { i } )$ units of item $g _ { i }$ for all $i \in N .$ . An allocation is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a subset $S \subseteq B$ where $\begin{array} { r } { \sum _ { b _ { i } \in S } e ( b _ { j } ) _ { i } \le q ( g _ { i } ) } \end{array}$ for all $i \in N .$ . If S<sup>ˆ</sup> denotes the set of all such allocations, then the multiunit WDP is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as computing an allocation $S \in \hat { S }$ that maximizes auctioneer’s revenue $\textstyle \sum _ { b \in S } p ( b )$ . Using this formulation, TrACA is extended to search for optimal winner determination in multiunit CA as described in the following sections.

## 6.2. TrACA for Multiunit WDP Search

The approach is similar to single-unit mutli-item TrACA as described in Algorithm 1 with the only difference being the additional use of capacity constraints in determining node choice. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, when choosing vertex v<sub>j</sub> from $v _ { i }$ (where $v _ { i } , v _ { j } \in V \backslash ( s , t ) )$ , if the sum of units of each item requested by $v _ { j }$ and those in all vertices chosen in the constructed path up to $v _ { i }$ exceed the capacity limit for any item, then $v _ { j }$ is not chosen.

Because real data sets pertaining to multiunit CAs are unavailable, we test our approach on 20 arti<sup>fi</sup>cially prepared data sets that follow Leyton-Brown et al. (2000). Each test bed consists of 50 bids on a set of 50 items. Each bid can request anywhere between zero and six copies of each item. Each item capacity varies between 100 (minimum) and 300 (maximum) units. Results from the simulation are in Appendix E. There are two key takeaways from the results. First, as indicated in Sandholm (2002b), our results show that capacity constraints on units of each item tend to limit the number of winning bids to less than half of total bids submitted. For instance, the ratio of the average number of winning bids to total bids across 20 instances tested is 17:6%. Second, the convergence pattern for our approach shows the inherent dif<sup>fi</sup>culty of solving such problems. Typically, hard multiunit, multiitem auctions have bids that tend to request a small number of units per item, independent of the total number of items (Gonen and Lehmann 2000, Leyton Brown et al. 2000). In our example instances, because units per item requested are zero to six (compared with overall capacity of minimum 100 units/item to a maximum 300 units/item), the search for the optima solution is harder, as now more bid combinations are explored. The convergence pattern therefore shows spurts in search values that are more frequent than the single-unit multi-item cases. As future work, we plan to develop more realistic instances of larger sizes and add to the increasing body of work in this regard (Leyton-Brown et al. 2000).

## 7. Conclusion, Applications, and Future Directions

We use ant colony optimization to solve hard instances of the winner determination problem by devising our own algorithm—TrACA. Randomized pheromone updating and randomized graph pruning were implemented to increase the speed of search. We test our approach on hard instances of the problem and compare the results with best in class search heuristics for WDP, the memetic algorithm and ACLS, and the best in class exact algorithm, Max W Clique. Although results are encouraging, we analyze TrACA for empirical hardness results and at tempt to understand the aspects of the WDP that make it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult. We <sup>fi</sup>nd evidence of “tightness” as demonstrated in the multidimensional knapsack problem to be an important factor in determining complexity of the WDP. Furthermore, we establish how our graphical formulation can help in demonstrating how the problem of variety and velocity can be solved in auction data. Last, we analyze the relationship between TrACA run times and instance complexity to characterize the factors that make search for global optimum dif<sup>fi</sup>cult in the Lau and Goh (2002) WDP instances.

Three factors contribute to the rise in use of heuristics for WDP in iterative scenarios: (1) the increase in size of market participants, (2) increase in number of items (e.g., shipping route, procurement units) up for auction, and (3) increase in usage of iterative combinatorial auctions for deciding on allocations. All three factors have become more prominent in the age of big data. Additionally, solving the WDP when conducting ICAs involves a trade-off between optimality and speed. Auctioneers need to <sup>fi</sup>gure out round duration and duration between rounds to maximize revenue as well as keep bidders interested in future participation. This is where cleverly designed heuristics can provide optimal or close-to-optimal solutions within prespeci<sup>fi</sup>ed time limits (Karaenke et al. 2019). Fundamentally, heuristics facilitate opening up the market to a higher number of bidders and items and holding more rounds iteratively to rule out inef<sup>fi</sup>cient allocations, thereby increasing ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. Our analysis shows that an increase in the variety of bids impacts winner determination times negatively, which may imply the auctioneer needs to predetermine bundle composition, number of items, etc., for faster winner approximations. On the other hand, we also show that if variety is limited, then even if velocity is high, our algorithm approximates winners fast, that helps with conducting more rounds.

In the future, we would like to direct our research in two primary directions. First, we would like to extend TrACA to solve multiunit multi-item auctions. We are in process of building a test instances for such auctions, because these are unavailable currently for researchers (Leyton-Brown et al. 2000). Second, we would like to extend TrACA for other NP-complete problem estimations such as multiunit multi-item CAs, vehicle routing problems, and modern variants of the capacity planning problem that have important applications in industry. In extending our work, our aim would be to not only improve TrACA estimations but also help improve the trade-off between ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and speed as much as possible.
