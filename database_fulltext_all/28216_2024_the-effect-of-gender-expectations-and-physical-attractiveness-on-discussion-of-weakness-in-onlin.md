# The Effect of Gender Expectations and Physical Attractiveness on Discussion of Weakness in Online Professional Recommendations

Abstract. In the current professional environment, recruiters are using online professional networks at an increasing rate to find qualified candidates for job openings. Online professional recommendations on these sites can provide valuable information; however, because of the medium by which they are provided, their effectiveness may suffer from lower levels of trust. We theoretically conjecture why including a discussion of a candidate’s weakness (termed, scope of improvement) can lead to a higher likelihood of a recruiter being willing to interview a candidate. We theorize that the effect of scope of improvement will depend on the nature of the weakness discussed and the physical attractiveness of the candidate, which is only relevant in an online context where that information is known. Analysis of the data we collected from industry professionals indicates that for average candidates, scope of improvement has a positive effect when the scope discussed does not counter expectations derived from common gender stereotypes (referred to as gender-expected scope), and a negative effect when it does. For attractive candidates, any discussion of scope has a negative effect. A theoretical contribution is our explaining the process of how different types of scope effect the likelihood of interview across different levels of attractiveness. Another theoretical contribution was the counterfactual performed in a second study to show how reinforcement of gender-expected strengths can attenuate the penalty of gender-expected scope for attractive candidates. Findings indicate that the penalty observed for attractive candidates can be avoided by reinforcing traits commonly associated with their gender. Data for both studies were collected from industry professionals involved in the hiring process, allowing us to offer practical guidelines to users of online professional networks.

## 1. Introduction

At an increasing rate, recruiters and businesses are using information found on online professional networking (OPNs) websites to aid in their recruitment efforts. As of 2016, as many as 87% of recruiters are using social networking websites to find potential job candidates for open positions (Jobvite 2016). Likewise, users are using social networking sites to search for employment opportunities (Garg and Telang 2017). Professional networking websites allow users to create profiles that showcase their qualifications, credentials, and accomplishments.

A common feature of OPNs that is meant to add credibility to individual users’ profiles is the ability for a user to show recommendations received from other users (Butow and Taylor 2008, Van Dijck 2013). We refer to these recommendations as online recommendations. Much of the information shared in an online recommendation may be similar to the information provided in a more traditional letter of recommendation, but we argue that the medium by which it is provided may lessen its perceived credibility. Because OPNs afford users full control over which recommendations are displayed on their profile, if an online recommendation contains negative information, the user can simply hide it from their profile (LinkedIn 2017). Although a user may not be hiding any recommendations, the fact that they have the ability to may give recruiters cause to view online recommendations with a lower level of trust (Cvetkovich 1999, Walther and Parks 2002, DeAndrea 2014). Furthermore, OPNs lower the cost of disseminating professional recommendations, which may lead to lower levels of perceived source credibility (Metzger and Flanagin 2013). When “more information from more sources is available and more easily accessible-… authority is no longer a prerequisite for content provision on the Internet. This obviously raises issues of credibility” (Metzger 2007, p. 2078). Given this environment, issues of trust may be lowering the effectiveness of online recommendations. Thus, we address the broad research question of how can job candidates (users) make up for this loss of trust in online recommendations to positively affect recruiters’ decision making in their favor?

In this study, we focus on the content of online recommendations and how different content affects recruiters decisions differently. If a concern is that online recommendations are not providing an accurate representation of the candidate, because any recommendation containing negative information would be hidden, a natural solution to explore would be to display recommendations that provide more balanced information. Specifically, we consider the effect of including information regarding a weakness of the candidate in the form of an opportunity for improvement (referred to as scope of improvement). Including scope of improvement in a recommendation should increase the perceived trustworthiness of the recommender (Eisend 2006) as inclusion of such information would lead recruiters to consider the recommendation as an accurate representation of the candidate (Crowley and Hoyer 1994). This increase in perceived trustworthiness, however, does not necessarily mean the recommendation will positively affect a recruiter’s likelihood of interviewing a candidate. Scope may have a direct negative effect, depending on the nature of the scope discussed (Pechmann 1992). This creates a tradeoff that is worthy of exploration as the direct negative effect need not always be the same and may vary across the nature of the scope and across candidates. Recruiters may expect to see certain scope more than others among certain candidates, and seeing a scope that they expect to see may not surprise them. However, if the scope discussed deviates far from recruiter’s expectations, then scope will likely do more harm than good (Berlyne 1971, Crowley and Hoyer 1994). For instance, gender stereotypes have been shown to create expectations of which traits an individual should possess (Eagly and Mladinic 1989), and when someone fails to adhere to such expectations, they often suffer negative consequences (Heilman 2012). Therefore, we theorize and test the effects of scope that is expected and scope that is unexpected based on expectations derived from common gender stereotypes. We refer to these as gender-expected and gender-unexpected scope.

The online setting is further mired by a factor, the candidates’ physical attractiveness, that generally does not play a role in an offline setting at this stage in the employment process. Profile pictures in an online setting allow recruiters to see the physical appearance of the candidate under consideration (Edwards et al. 2015) prior to interview. A long literature on attractiveness suggests that attractive people are treated differently than the rest (Hickling et al. 1979, Eagly et al. 1991, Hart et al. 2011). People often evaluate attractive individuals more favorably, not just for job positions (Marlowe et al. 1996), but in nearly all aspects of life (Hamermesh 2011). Such evaluations include expectations that attractive individuals possess more desirable traits than those who are not attractive (Dion et al. 1972). Therefore, if the scope included in a recommendation reveals that an attractive person lacks such a trait, people may react more strongly and penalize them more than other candidates (Heilman and Saruwatari 1979). Hence, it is important to study how this factor that exists only in an online setting may moderate the effect of scope and to study how attractive candidates can mitigate such penalties in the presence of scope.

Prior work on professional recommendations have primarily focused on traditional letters of recommendation (Ceci and Peters 1984, Madera et al. 2009, Leising et al. 2010) with online professional recommendations receiving little attention despite the prominent role OPN profiles play in the hiring process (Jobvite 2016, Garg and Telang 2017). By addressing online recommenda tions specifically, we are able to theoretically motivate why online recommendations may suffer from lower levels of trust, thereby lessening their effectiveness. We are then able to theorize and empirically test how includ ing scope of improvement can help or hurt a candidate’s chances of interview while considering aspects unique to the online environment. Notably, we show how the attractiveness of a candidate, as displayed in their profile picture, can change the effect of scope drastically. Results indicate that gender-expected scope positively affects the likelihood of interview for average candidates, whereas gender-unexpected scope negatively affects the likelihood of interview. However, attractive candidates are negatively affected by both kinds of scope. Furthermore, we show how reinforcing gender-expected strengths in a recommendation can help attractive candidates avoid the penalty caused by gender-expected scope. We also contribute to the body of work on online trust by con ducting a mediation analysis to confirm perceived trust worthiness as an underlying mechanism explaining why discussing gender-expected scope can have positive outcomes for candidates.

Further contributions are made by testing hypotheses using data collected from actual hiring managers and recruiters for industry jobs. The difficulty of collecting such data has led to nearly all previous research on letters of recommendation being done in the context of college admissions or the medical field (Aamodt et al. 1993, Trix and Psenka 2003, Madera et al. 2009). Thus, our work has numerous managerial implications. Recommenders and candidates alike should be aware that recommendations including gender-expected scope can be beneficial and improve the perceived trustworthiness of the recommender. Likewise, they need to consider the effect a candidate’s attractiveness can have on such methods as OPNs reveal the physical appearance of the candidate. With the level of control afforded to users by OPNs to determine what information is shared with others, optimizing their self-presentation on these networks may be an effective way to increase their chances of improving future employment opportunities (Chiang and Suen 2015). These results allow us to provide users of OPNs with valuable insights that can help guide them in developing a decision policy regarding which recommendations to share with the network, and which to hide.

The paper will proceed as follows. First, we will discuss the literature and theory development for our hypotheses, followed by an explanation of the procedures followed to test the hypotheses, the measures used, and an analysis of the results. Then we will provide a discussion of the collective findings, the implications of the results, and end with a brief conclusion.

## 2. Theory Development

This section discusses the related literature in the development of our hypothesis. First, we discuss why including scope of improvement will increase the effectiveness of an online professional recommendation. Then we discuss how gender stereotypes create expectations, and violating these expectations will negatively impact the effectiveness of the recommendation. Next, we discuss why physically attractive candidates may be penalized for any version of scope and may not benefit from the same recommendations that average candidates do. This leads to the development of our first set of hypotheses. Last, we develop a counterfactual in which we develop a second hypothesis regarding when physically attractive candidates will not be penalized by gender-expected scope.

## 2.1. Scope of Improvement

Professional recommendations, by their very nature, positively endorse an individual. If a recommender did not have positive experiences to share, they likely would not offer a recommendation. This results in most recommendations being highly favorable and positive for the recommendee (Cascio and Aguinis 2010). In some cases, recommendations can come across as so positive that they appear inflated and risk becoming uninformative (Range et al. 1991). Similar to other forms of promotional messages, this creates a situation where readers must determine which recommendations are accurate representations of reality, and which are onesided promotional messages (Crowley and Hoyer 1994, Rui 2018). This concern is especially salient with online recommendations as the recommendee has the option of hiding any recommendation that contains any form of unfavorable information from their profile (LinkedIn 2017). This control afforded to the recommendee may lessen the impact of online recommendations (Walther et al. 2009) because it lessens the “extent to which information is immune to manipulation by the source it describes” (DeAndrea 2014, p. 186). The impression that information cannot be manipulated (including hid ing it altogether) positively influences the receiver’s assessment of whether the information accurately reflects reality (Walther and Parks 2002).

Further issues of credibility may arise with online recommendations as the cost of producing and sharing the recommendation with a large audience is so low. A common heuristic individuals use in assessing the credibility of information is the cost to produce and disseminate it (Rieh and Danielson 2007, Metzger and Flanagin 2013). Such heuristics often play a vital role in users’ evaluation of information found online (Sundar 2008). Therefore, OPN users must find ways to overcome such credibility issues to make online recommendations as effective as possible.

Extant literature has found that promotional messages, in general, are more effective when they are not composed entirely of positive information (Crowley and Hoyer 1994, Rucker et al. 2008, Ein-Gar et al. 2011, Brauer et al. 2012, Hamilton et al. 2014). By including negative information, the message becomes more novel, interesting, credible, and influential in leading to a desired out come (Crowley and Hoyer 1994). For this reason, we propose that an online professional recommendation could benefit from including scope of improvement, a discussion about a trait that is not a candidate’s strength. Including scope in a recommendation should positively affect the perceived trustworthiness of the recommender as it demonstrates their ability, and willingness, to provide a more balanced representation of the candidate (Golden and Alpert 1987). If a recommender is perceived as more trustworthy, then their recommendation is likely to be more influential (Crowley and Hoyer 1994), leading to positive results for the candidate. However, we do not expect this to be a universal effect. In the following sections, we will discuss other factors that may influence the effectiveness of this approach.

## 2.2. Gender-Expected and Gender-Unexpected Scope

Although including scope of improvement in a recommendation should increase the perceived trustworthiness of the recommender (Settle and Golden 1974; Smith and Hunt 1978, Golden and Alpert 1987), this does not necessarily mean the recommendation will be more effective in increasing a recruiter’s likelihood of interviewing a candidate (Pechmann 1992). The effectiveness will depend on the nature of the scope discussed. If the scope discussed is unexpected, then any benefits of increased trust may be outweighed by the harm of revealing such information (Crowley and Hoyer 1994).

Many expectations individuals have for others are derived from gender stereotypes, which are formed as people “observe male and female behavior and infer that the sexes possess corresponding dispositions” (Eagly and Wood 2012, p. 458). Gender stereotypes in the workplace have been an important topic of study, revealing that individuals are expected to possess certain traits based on their gender (Grams and Schwab 1985, Basford et al. 2014). A majority of these expectations relate to communal and agentic traits (Rudman and Glick 2001, Rosette and Tost 2010, Eagly 2013). Communal traits are those that promote a concern for maintaining relationships with others, such as empathy, likability, and kindness (Uchronski et al. 2013, Abele and Wojciszke 2014, Kramer and Harris 2016). Agentic traits are those that relate to goal-achievement and task functioning, such as confidence, competence, and ambition (Rudman and Glick 2001, Kehn 2012, Abele and Wojciszke 2014). Considerable research has shown how communal traits are more strongly associated with women, whereas agentic traits are more strongly associated with men (Heilman 1983, Cejka and Eagly 1999, Eagly and Karau 2002, Elsesser and Lever 2011, Dimitriadis et al. 2017).

When individuals of either gender fail to adhere to the stereotypes that have been placed on them, the result is often harmful to the individual. When females lack communal traits, they are often looked down upon by others as they can come across as being less psychologically healthy (Costrich et al. 1975), cold (Porter and Geis 1981), hostile (Heilman 1995, 2001), and in general, they are disliked (Heilman and Okimoto 2007). Males also may suffer negative consequences from failing to adhere to gender stereotypes, such as lacking agentic traits (Rudman 1998, Mahalik 1999, Chen 2008, Moss-Racusin et al. 2010). Therefore, an online recommendation that includes scope of improvement could cause harm to the candidate if the information provided illustrates how the candidate does not conform to a gender stereotype. The negative effects of violating gender norms could result in recruiters being less willing to interview the potential candidate.

It is reasonable to believe that every candidate will have strengths and weaknesses, and when information is lacking, gender stereotypes may lead one to assume what some of those strengths and weaknesses may be. When a recommendation discusses scope of improvement that is consistent with a gender stereotype (referred to as gender-expected scope), the negative impact of exposing scope may be minimal because the reader may have already assumed that this trait was not a strength. However, if the scope is not consistent with a gender stereotype (referred to as gender-unexpected scope), then this would be showing that an assumed strength is actually a weakness of the candidate, and the negative impact of revealing scope may be more substantial.

When including scope of improvement, the question becomes, which effect on the likelihood of interview will have a greater magnitude, the positive benefit of providing a more balanced message, or the negative impact of revealing scope. We believe that the answer will depend on whether a gender-expected or genderunexpected scope is used.

## 2.3. Physical Attractiveness

The use of OPNs introduces a new consideration at the earliest stages of the employment process that is generally not applicable in an offline setting, namely, the physical attractiveness of the candidate. When recruiters review online recommendations, they do so knowing the physical attractiveness of the candidate. In an offline context, decisions made based on resumes and letters of recommendation are not necessarily done with any knowledge of the candidate’s physical appearance. This is significant as many studies have documen ted the effect attractiveness has on people’s perception of individuals (Heilman and Stopeck 1985b, Mobius and Rosenblat 2006, Ruffle and Shtudiner 2014) and have been shown to affect hiring decisions (Mobius and Rosenblat 2006, Rooth 2009, Baert 2018). These effects can be positive, but in some instances, they can be negative (Heilman and Stopeck 1985a, Eagly et al. 1991). Attractive individuals are often assumed to possess certain personality traits that are considered socially desirable; most notably, they are perceived as more competent and likable (Dion et al. 1972, Heilman and Saruwatari 1979, Sigelman et al. 1987, Eagly et al. 1991). Because they are associated with desirable attributes, they tend to elicit more favorable responses from others (Dion et al. 1972). This favorable response has been shown with voters having preferences for attractive political candidates (Efran and Patterson 1974) and juries favoring attractive defendants (Efran 1974). It would thus be expected that, in general, recruiters may favor attractive candidates and be more willing to interview them than candidates not considered attractive.

However, the positive perceptions of attractive people lessen and disappear when possible contradictory information is present (Eagly et al. 1991). When a weakness of an attractive person is discussed, people are much more likely to judge the extent of that weakness to be more severe and attribute their success to their attractiveness rather than their abilities and personal qualities (Fo¨ rsterling et al. 2007). As such, attractiveness has been shown to have potentially adverse effects in different workplace environments (Heilman and Saruwatari 1979), putting attractive people at a disadvantage because of their attractiveness (Heilman and Saruwatari 1979; Heilman and Stopeck 1985a, b).

With online recommendations, we expect that scope of improvement may cause reviewers to reconsider the perceived positive traits they may typically associate with attractive individuals and instead, judge them more severely for their shortcomings. This judgment would lead to scope having a negative effect on a recruiter’s likelihood of interviewing an attractive candidate regardless of whether a gender-expected or gender-unexpected scope is used. On the other hand, for average users, we expect to see the nature of the improvement to affect whether it has a positive or negative effect on interview likelihood.

Therefore, we formally hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 1a. For average looking candidates, including gender-expected (gender-unexpected) scope of improvement in an OPN recommendation should positively (negatively) affect the recruiter’s willingness to interview compared with a recommendation with no scope of improvement present.

Hypothesis 1b. For attractive candidates, including either type of scope of improvement in an OPN recommendation should negatively affect the recruiter’s willingness to interview compared with a recommendation with no scope of improvement present.

## 2.4. Counterfactual for the Penalty of Attractiveness

Our focus now turns to creating a set of conditions where the hypothesized negative effect of genderexpected scope is not observed for attractive candidates. We focus on gender-expected scope as this is where we expect to see scope of improvement to have a different effect for average and attractive candidates. To do this requires an understanding of what may be causing this penalty to occur. As discussed, attractiveness has been shown to generate assumptions of positive personal attributes in multiple environments including law (Desir 2010), business management (Cavico et al. 2012), science (Hart et al. 2011), and psychology (Braun et al. 2012). These positive evaluations of attractive individuals can be based on nothing more than a photograph (Hickling et al. 1979, Sigelman et al. 1987, Chung and Leung 1988, Todorov et al. 2005, Etcoff et al. 2011). Extant work suggests that attractive candidates are more strongly associated with gender-expected strengths than average looking candidates (Heilman and Saruwatari 1979). However, when additional information is provided, these assumptions are far less impactful, and in the face of criticism, attractive people can be judged more critically (Heilman and Stopeck 1985b, Eagly et al. 1991, Wigboldus et al. 2003). Past work seems to suggest that when people see any sign of weakness, they adjust for their bias more than they should and end up penalizing attractive people more. Nevertheless, if we were to reinforce common favorable assumptions made about attractive people, then we should be able to offset the penalty caused by gender-expected scope for attractive candidates. Because attractiveness enhances the perception of gender traits (Heilman and Saruwatari 1979), rein forcing a gender-expected trait should be most effective. Hence, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 2. For attractive candidates, including a genderexpected strength in an OPN recommendation should decrease the negative effects of gender-expected scope of improvement.

## 3. Methodology

We conducted two studies to test the two hypotheses. The second study, to test Hypothesis 2, was very similar to the first study. We will begin by describing the setup of study 1 and then describe the modifications made in study 2. Data for both studies were collected by surveying hiring managers and recruiters. Participants were presented with manipulated OPN profiles of job candidates and were asked to review the profiles and rate the likelihood of interviewing the candidate for a job and the perceived trustworthiness of the recommender. Data were collected from 180 participants for study 1 and 90 participants for study 2. Each participant completed two data points resulting in 360 and 180 observations for study 1 and study 2, respectively.

## 3.1. Study 1 Design

For study 1, profiles were manipulated with the presence or absence of scope of improvement within a recommendation and the physical attractiveness of the candidate to test our hypotheses. Furthermore, to ensure the robustness of our findings, as well as to improve our data collection quality, we conducted the experiment using two different jobs: a marketing analyst position and a software developer position.

Profiles were created with possible combinations of presence (or absence) of gender-expected and genderunexpected scope (that is, no scope, gender-expected scope, and gender-unexpected scope), and attractiveness level of the candidate (that is, average or attractive). Accompanying each recommendation was information that typically appears on a user’s OPN profile: their name, education, past work experience, a short biography, and a photograph of the candidate (see Online Appendix II for an example profile). A short questionnaire followed each profile to measure the reviewer’s likelihood of interviewing the candidate and the perceived trustworthiness of the recommender (see Online Appendix III).

To collect the data, 720 industry professionals, who are involved in recruitment processes at their place of work, were approached for participating in this study. Each subject was presented with a set of two profiles. The first profile presented to each subject was completely randomized. That is, the job version they received (marketing analyst or software developer), whether scope of improvement was present (gender-expected scope, gender-unexpected scope, or none), the gender of the candidate (male or female), and the attractiveness of the candidate (average or attractive) were randomly assigned. The random assignment of the second profile, on the other hand, was ensured to represent a different profile from the one already shown. At the end of data collection, we received two data points each from 180 subjects (360 total data points).

## 3.2. Study 2 Design

Study 2 was designed to test Hypothesis 2. Because Hypothesis 2 was specific to attractive candidates, only photos of attractive candidates were used (more on the development of this measure is provided in the next section), so attractiveness was no longer a source of variability. In study 2, we also focused on genderexpected scope of improvement. This focus was because the expected penalty for attractive candidates would be unique in these conditions as it is anticipated that all candidates will be negatively affected by gender-unexpected scope. Recommendations were manipulated to include (or not include) statements reinforcing a gender-expected/unexpected strength. Profiles were developed with possible combinations of presence or absence of gender-expected scope, and presence or absence of gender-expected strength or gender-unexpected strength. Data were collected in a similar manner from industry professionals involved in the hiring process. Similar randomization techniques were used as in study one to ensure each participant was randomly assigned to a treatment condition. From 252 subjects approached for study two, we received two data points each from 90 subjects (180 data points total).

## 3.3. Measures

To aid with the development of many of the measures in the studies, 11 hiring managers were consulted to provide feedback and preliminary ratings as will be discussed later. The decision to use a software developer and marketing analyst job was reached after discussing with these hiring managers two common job positions that, although different from each other, could be screened by the same recruiter.

3.3.1. Development of Recommendations. A rigorous process was followed to prepare the recommendations to read like a typical recommendation that a recruiter may come across on a professional networking website for marketing analyst and software developer jobs. First, we obtained and examined more than 75 LinkedIn recommendations for similar jobs to determine common themes used. Next, we crafted a base recommendation for each job. To ensure that the recommendations read like a typical recommendation, they were shown to 11 hiring managers and 28 graduate students. The managers and students gave feedback on whether the recommendations read as they would expect, and they were also asked to rate the likelihood of interviewing a candidate based on each of the recommendations using a seven-point Likert scale. To ensure the two base recommendations (one for the marketing analyst job the other for software developer job) had comparable effects on the likelihood of interview, a t test was used, which showed the differences were insignificant $( t = 0 . 5 4 ,$ , standard error $( \mathrm { S E } ) = 0 . 1 4 , p = 0 . 5 9 )$ . An example recommendation can be seen in the sample profile provided in Online Appendix II. Next, we discuss how the scope of improvement was operationalized.

3.3.2. Scope of Improvement Operationalization. Scope of improvement in a recommendation is a discussion about a trait that a candidate is not good at or is not a candidate’s strength. It suggests a trait that the candidate needs to work on for self-development and for becoming a better professional. Furthermore, we define gender-expected scope as a discussion about the need to improve on a trait that is not expected to be a strength of the candidate’s gender. It discusses an attribute that is generally not associated with or considered a characteristic of the candidate’s gender. The gender-unexpected scope is defined as a discussion about a trait that is commonly believed to be a strength of the candidate’s gender but is noted as an area in need of improvement in the recommendation. Next, we discuss how we determine gender-expected and gender unexpected scope for male and female candidates.

The scope of improvement portion of the recommendations was prepared after interviewing the 11 experienced hiring managers and recruiters. The focus of these interviews was to identify traits that they typ ically associate with a specific gender. The list of traits generated from these interviews was then shown to the same hiring managers and recruiters, and they were asked to separate the list into two lists based on whether the trait was more strongly considered a male or female trait. Cohen’s kappa (Cohen 1960) for the interrater reliability showed significant agreement beyond chance $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ among the two lists created. The traits in each list were then ranked according to the number of times they were mentioned in the interviews. The most frequent trait from each list was then chosen. Empathy was found to be commonly considered a strength of females and confidence a strength of males.

The appropriateness of selecting these traits was validated by having 41 students follow the card-sorting methodology of Moore and Benbasat (1991), where the participants were given the list of traits identified in the interviews and were asked to sort the traits into malespecific and female-specific traits. A too ambiguous/does not fit category was also included to ensure that the participants were not forced to fit any trait into one of the two gender categories. The list included affectionate, agreeable, ambitious, assertive, caring, confident, competent, daring, empathetic, forceful, kind, and likeable. The item placement ratio was used to measure construct validity (Moore and Benbasat 1991). We computed the item placement ratio by dividing the frequency with which all participants placed traits within the target category divided by the total number of placements. The placement ratio of traits within the target gender categories was 86.99% (428 correctly placed out of 492 total placements), indicating that the gender-specific traits identified by recruiters in our study were well representative of the general view about gender-specific traits.

The selection of these traits is also consistent with extant literature, which has consistently shown empathy to be a trait more strongly associated with females than males (Eagly and Karau 2002, Elsesser and Lever 2011, Dimitriadis et al. 2017, Yaghoubi Jami et al. 2019). Likewise, a long stream of literature has shown that confidence is a trait more strongly associated with males than females (Lundeberg et al. 1994, Rudman and Glick 2001, Abele 2003, Niederle and Vesterlund 2007, Charness et al. 2018). With the traits identified, wording for the scope of improvement was developed to communicate that the candidate would benefit from being more empathetic or confident, depending on the scenario used. These statements were then appended to the baseline recommendations to create versions of the recommendation that contained scope.

To sum up, empathy and confidence are typically considered as a female strength and a male strength respectively. Hence, their discussions as scope of improvement are unexpected for a female candidate and male candidate respectively. On the contrary, their discussions as scope are not surprising for a male candidate and female candidate, respectively. For empirical testing, consequently, the Gender-Unexpected Scope dummy is one when empathy is discussed as scope for a female candidate or when confidence is discussed as scope for a male candidate, and this dummy is zero otherwise. Similarly, the Gender-Expected Scope dummy is one when empathy is discussed as scope for a male candidate or when confidence is discussed as scope for a female candidate, and this dummy is zero otherwise.

3.3.3. Strength Operationalization. For Hypothesis 2, we needed to identify a gender-expected strength that can reduce the negative effects of gender-expected scope of improvement faced by attractive candidates. We selected likeability as a gender-expected strength for female candidates, and competence as a gender-expected strength for male candidates as per our discussion with the 11 experienced hiring managers and recruiters. The details for how we got their inputs are discussed in the scope of improvement operationalization section. The selection of likeability and competence as strengths associated with female and male candidates, respectively, is consistent with the literature, as competence is consistently considered as an agentic trait (Rudman and Glick 2001, Abele and Wojciszke 2014) and likability a communal trait (Abele and Wojciszke 2007, Kehn 2012). For empirical testing, consequently, the Gender-Expected Strength dummy is one when likeability is discussed as a strength for a female candidate or when competence is discussed as a strength for a male candidate, and this dummy is zero otherwise. Similarly, the Gender-Unexpected Strength dummy is one when likeability is discussed as a strength for a male candidate or when competence is discussed as a strength for a male candidate, and this dummy is zero otherwise.

3.3.4. Attractiveness. To select the four photographs to be used for the attractiveness manipulation, we collected 50 photographs of males and 50 photographs of females representing different levels of attractiveness from a professional photographer. Each photograph was of an individual of the same approximate age, build, and race. All the photographs were headshots and standardized to the same size and resolution. These photographs were then shown to a convenience sample of 120 students (60 males and 60 females), and each student was asked to rate the physical attractiveness of 25 photographs for each gender depicted on a scale of one to five (one, attractive; four, above average; three, average; two, below average; one, unattractive). The photographs selected to represent average candidates had an average score of 2.9 for the male candidate and a score of 2.98 for the female candidate. The photographs used to represent attractive candidates had an average score of 4.75 for the male candi date and 4.72 for the female candidate. The selected attractive candidate and average candidate photographs represented the 90th and 40th percentiles, respectively. The results of t tests comparing the scores for the four selected photographs, reported in Table AI.3 of Online Appendix I, confirm that, although the score of the photo graph representing an average male was statistically similar to the score of the photograph representing an average female, it was statistically different from both the attrac tive male and the attractive female (as expected).

## 3.4. Test of Constructs

Because our questionnaire used multiple questions to capture the reviewer’s likelihood of interviewing a candidate and the perceived trustworthiness of the recommender, it is important to determine if the measures used are valid. The appropriateness of using factor analysis was determined by using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. For the collected data, the KMO is 0.80, and the chi-square statistic is 1494.40 (p < 0.001), thus indicating that factor analysis is appropriate (Hair et al. 1998).

The next step is to perform principal components analysis (PCA). PCA examines the factor loading of each indicator, extracting items that do not significantly load on the construct of interest. An item is extracted if it does not load above 0.60. Results show that all the factor loadings are greater than 0.6, the accepted standard (reported in Table AI.4 of Online Appendix I).

Reliability analysis used Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability to assess the internal consistency (Chin 1998) of each question intended to measure the same construct. Composite reliability (similar to Cronbach’s alpha) of 0.70 or higher is considered adequate (Fornell and Larcker 1981) and is considered more robust than Cronbach’s alpha because it weighs items differently depending on factor loading considerations (Agarwal and Karahanna 2000). Both composite reliability and Cronbach’s alpha are greater than 0.70, indicating high reliability and stability for the measurement items of each construct.

Bagozzi and Yi (1988) provide three standards used to assess convergent validity: (1) all indicator facto loadings should exceed 0.5 (Hair et al. 1998); (2) composite reliability should be above 0.7; and (3) the average variance extracted (AVE) of every construct should exceed 0.5 (Fornell and Larcker 1981). The indicator factor loadings of every item in the measuring model of this study exceeded 0.7. The composite reliability of the two constructs are 0.908 and 0.873. The average variances for the two constructs are 0.767 and 0.562; therefore, all conditions for convergent validity were met.

## 4.3. Trustworthiness as a Mediator

Results indicate that gender-expected scope of improvement has a positive effect on the likelihood of interview for average candidates. The proposed explanation for this is that the inclusion of scope increases the perceived trustworthiness of the recommender to a degree that the overall effect is positive (Eisend 2006). This is consistent with past research, which has found that when information comes from a source that is perceived as trustworthy, it is more useful and influential in affecting decisions and behavior (Sussman and Siegal 2003, Ko et al. 2005). Although gender-unexpected scope should also increase the perceived trustworthiness of the recommender (Eisend 2006), the benefits of this may not be strong enough to outweigh the direct negative effect of revealing gender-unexpected scope (Settle and Golden 1974, Crowley and Hoyer 1994). To test these presumptions, and to confirm that perceived trustworthiness is acting as a mediator, thereby explaining the underlying mechanism by which gender-expected scope increases the likelihood of interview, we conducted a formal medi ation analysis.

We used the mediator analysis advocated by Preacher and Hayes (2008) to test for mediation effects. The primary advantage of this technique is its robustness. Because indirect effect estimation involves the product of terms, they tend to have a skewed sampling distribution.

To confirm the adequacy of our sample size, we performed statistical power analysis. The power of a test is the probability of detecting the phenomenon under test (Cohen 2013). That is, the probability of rejecting the null hypothesis, when the alternate hypothesis is true (Larsen and Marx 2013). Therefore, when statistical power is low, there is a high likelihood of not finding significant results even when the relationship actually exists. To compute the power of the analysis for a sample size (n), the values of alpha (α), and effect size (f) have to be predetermined. Alpha is the risk of mistakenly rejecting the null hypothesis, thus committing a type I error. Setting the alpha at 0.05 is considered the most conventional level of significance. Assigning a less stringent alpha would increase the risk of false rejection, casting doubts on the validity of the results. Whereas, setting alpha to a too conservative value might result in failing to reject the null hypothesis in the presence of substantial population effect. Effect size is the strength of the theoretical relationship found in an analysis and provides an estimation of the degree to which a phenomenon exists in a population (Chin et al. 2003). Cohen proposed that the effect size of 0.15 is desirable as it would be able to approximate the average size of observed effects in various fields, and it would represent an effect that would likely be “visible to the naked eye of a careful observer” (Cohen 1992, p. 156). This recommendation has been supported by a metaanalysis of 302 meta-analyses of more than 10,000 studies (Lipsey and Wilson 1993). Accordingly, we used the effect size of 0.15 to perform the power analysis (Faul et al. 2009).

The analysis suggests that samples of 360 and 180 in our first and second study would have guaranteed the power to be more than 0.9, which is well above the conventional threshold of 0.8 (Baroudi and Orlikowski 1989, Cohen 2013). Thus, our sample sizes should have adequate power to fail the null hypotheses. This can also be confirmed by the fact that the theorized effects are statistically significant, and the null hypotheses got rejected, refuting any existence of type 1 error. The actual observed power achieved for different analyses in our sample are listed in Tables 2–4. The F statistic was computed using (R<sup>2</sup> with new variable � R<sup>2</sup> without new variable) $\times \left( n - k - 1 \right) / ( 1 - R ^ { 2 }$ with new variable) (where n is sample size, and k is the number of interdependent variables) (Mathieson et al. 2001, Chin et al. 2003).

An additional robustness check was performed to test the generalizability of results using a different gendered trait. Using data collected from a convenience sample of 41 participants, we repeated the primary analysis using a male candidate profile where the trait used for gender-expected scope was caring, another trait more strongly associated with females than males (Abele 2003, Rosette and Tost 2010). Results were qualitatively similar and indicated that genderexpected scope continued to have a positive effect on the likelihood of interview even when a different gendered trait was used.

## 5. Conclusion and Discussion

In this paper, we examine the effect of gender-expected and gender-unexpected scope of improvement on the effectiveness of online professional recommendations. In the first study, we show that gender-expected scope can positively affect a recruiter’s likelihood of interviewing an average candidate, whereas gender-unexpected scope can have the opposite effect. Furthermore, we show that for physically attractive candidates, any type of scope has a negative effect. The same recommendation that helps an average male’s prospects of interview hurts an attractive male. The same is true for average and attractive females. In study two, counterfactual was performed to create a situation where attractive candidates were not hurt by gender-expected scope. This situation was accomplished by reinforcing the gender-expected attributes of competence for males, and likability for females. A follow-up study showed that reinforcing other gender-expected strengths can be an effective way to attenuate the negative effect of gender-expected scope for attractive candidates. Furthermore, mediation analysis confirmed that the perceived trustworthiness of the recommender mediated the effect of scope on the recruiter’s likelihood of interviewing a candidate. Scope consistently had a positive effect on the perceived trustworthiness of the recommender, and increased trustworthiness consistently had a positive effect on likelihood of interview.

These two studies offer unique insight into how the discussion of scope of improvement affects the likelihood of interview. Likewise, the interplay among attractiveness and scope of improvement was explored. By empirically testing and theorizing on these issues, the following managerial implications can be provided for those asked to write online recommendations, those who are receiving online recommendations, as well as companies using online recommendations in their hiring process.

First and foremost, the notion that one should avoid saying anything that could come across as negative because it would disqualify the candidate (Larkin and Marco 2001) is not necessarily true. There are opportunities for recommenders to improve the candidates’ interview chances by discussing scope of improvement. Specifically, by addressing a trait that does not counter gender stereotypes, a recommender can improve the candidate’s likelihood of receiving an interview. Therefore, if recommenders have observed such a need for improvement, they need not shy away from sharing such information. Likewise, recommenders should be conscious that the discussion of gender-unexpected scope has a significantly negative effect on the likelihood of interview. Therefore, such remarks should not be made lightly. If providing such information is warranted, recommenders should understand the potential impact they may have.

Results indicate that including scope of improvement in a recommendation has a different effect on attractive candidates than it does on those of average physical attractiveness. Specifically, for attractive can didates, including gender-expected scope has a negative effect on the likelihood of interview. However, if a recommender feels the discussion is necessary, but they do not wish to damage the candidate’s chances of receiving an interview, they can mitigate the adverse effects by including (if applicable) reinforcing statements of gender-expected strengths possessed by the candidate.

Regarding those seeking recommendations on online professional networks, they are in a position to request and receive a recommendation and then choose whethe to display the recommendation on their profile publicly (Rui 2018). Although it may seem counterproductive to willingly present a recommendation that contains a discussion of their need for improvement, given the proper circumstances, it can be beneficial to do so. Using the findings of these two studies, candidates should be able to identify potential elements of the recommendations they receive that will ultimately help or harm them. In doing so, they can make better decisions on which recommendations to post publicly and which to hide. If elements of a recommendation are identified that may cause harm, the candidate can always discuss the issue with the recommender and see if a different technique could be used to communicate similar information, or if the recommendation could be supplemented with relevant strengths.

Although the focus of this study has been to provide guidance to job candidates and recommenders using OPNs, the findings have implications for companies that use OPNs in their hiring process as well. Companies can encourage candidates to seek more objective recommendations through the use of scope of improve ment. This way, recruiters and hiring managers can trust the recommendations more and hire accordingly. However, this can leave attractive candidates at a serious risk. They may get penalized unduly in the screen ing process if scope is present for them. One way to avoid this penalty is for companies to block images from OPNs on their network so that their hiring team does not get biased. Our results add further support to previous findings suggesting that the physical appearance of candidates can distort the hiring process (Ruffle and Shtudiner 2014), thus, blocking images on OPNs may improve hiring decisions.

The findings and limitations of these two studies open the door for interesting extensions of this work. In study two, we focus on reducing the negative effects of genderexpected scope of improvement for attractive candidates. We focused on gender-expected scope because our review of the literature, as well as discussions with recruiters, lead us to believe that effectively compensating for the direct negative effects of gender-unexpected scope may not be possible (Settle and Golden 1974, Stayman et al. 1987). Therefore, to better use the data collection process, we focused on gender-expected scope. Likewise, because attractive candidates, unlike average candidates, were the ones who saw a net-negative effect of genderexpected scope, we focused our efforts on them. This does, however, leave the possibility of future research to explore what it would take to overcome the direct negative effect of gender-unexpected scope, as well as the effect of including gender-expected/gender-unexpected strengths with average candidates.

As discussed, in each study we use specific traits that are traditionally associated with males and females to form our gender-expected/unexpected scope and strengths. Although we chose empirically validated traits that are consistently used in prior literature as gendered traits (Eagly and Karau 2002, Dimitriadis et al. 2017), we acknowledge that these associations may not be universal nor perfectly dichotomous. Traits likely fall on a spectrum with traits having varying levels of overlapping associations between males and females. Further research could explore how a more nuanced view of different traits not being perfectly characterized as gender-expected/unexpected affect likelihood of interview when used in scope of improvement.

The scenarios used in this study involved one of two jobs that represented low-experience positions. In such positions, it may be more common for job candidates to need improvements in one area or another. This may not be the case for candidates seeking senior-level positions. Discussing scope of improvement in recommendations for these candidates may have different results. Conducting a similar study where the experience-level of the candidates is manipulated could reveal potential moderating effects of experience on the effects of scope on the likelihood of being interviewed.

Another potential extension of this work would be in the area of professional image construction (Little et al. 2015). This task of professional image management is no longer limited to personal encounters with peers and coworkers but has extended to the image created and maintained on social networking websites (Ollier-Malaterre et al. 2013, Van Dijck 2013, Gandini 2016). As professionals continue to develop strategies for creating the most effective professional image possible, understanding how online professional recommendations fit into their efforts will be an important insight to gain. We have shown in this paper how the same information has different effects on the likelihood of interview for different groups of people. Future work can be done to investigate specifically how different information affects different group’s professional image and how to incorporate professional recommendations into an individual’s personal image management strategies.

As online professional networks continue to be used by companies to find qualified candidates to fill job positions, it is essential to understand how the information contained within online profiles affect candidates’ opportunities. Increasing understanding of not just what types of information can be beneficial, but how other factors such as gender, stereotypes, and physical attractiveness interact with these effects will help users and companies make better decisions, and better use professional recommendations.
