# Business Models in the Sharing Economy: Manufacturing Durable Goods in the Presence of Peer-to-Peer Rental Markets

Vibhanshu Abhishek,<sup>a</sup> Jose A. Guajardo,<sup>b</sup> Zhe Zhang<sup>c</sup>

Abstract. Business models that provide access to assets rather than transfer ownership of goods have become an important industry trend, representing a challenge for incumbent <sup>fi</sup>rms. This paper analyzes the interaction of a peer-to-peer (P2P) rental market and a manufacturer of durable goods, and highlights the important role of consumer heterogeneity in usage rates in determining which business model would be preferred by the manufacturer. The introduction of a P2P rental market creates an equalizing effect, which leads to purchases from low-usage consumers. P2P rentals act as a discrimination device, allowing the manufacturer to segment consumers and extract a larger fraction of surplus, which might hurt consumers. The manufacturer is better off with P2P rentals when the heterogeneity in usage rates is intermediate, whereas the consumers are better off with P2P rentals when the heterogeneity is suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently high. This paper examines different business models such as the manufacturer with only sales, with rentals in addition to sales (the “dual” <sup>fi</sup>rm), with its own P2P rentals platform alongside sales (the “P2P-sponsoring” <sup>fi</sup>rm), and with a mixed structure in which the manufacturer competes against P2P rentals by introducing it own direct rentals (the “dual-plus-P2P” <sup>fi</sup>rm). Consumer heterogeneity in usage rates plays a fundamental role in business model outcomes. When usage rates and heterogeneity in usage rates are suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently large, the manufacturer is better off offering sales and facilitating a P2P rental market. In contrast, if heterogeneity in usage rates is too low, the manufacturer prefers to offer only sales. If heterogeneity is too high but usage rates are below a threshold, the manufacturer prefers to operate as a dual <sup>fi</sup>rm that offers both sales and rentals directly to consumers. If P2P rentals are unavoidable, introducing its own rentals to compete against P2P rentals might not be the best strategy for the manufacturer under certain conditions. Overall, contrary to what could be expected, the manufacturer has an incentive to facilitate P2P rentals in a large variety of cases.

## 1. Introduction

Business models that provide access to assets rather than transfer ownership of goods have become one of the most fundamental recent industry trends (Bardhi and Eckhardt 2012). The trend is re<sup>fl</sup>ected, for example, in what has been called the sharing economy (Sundararajan 2016). Industry reports have indicated that the <sup>fi</sup>ve key sectors of the sharing economy— travel, product sharing, <sup>fi</sup>nance, staf<sup>fi</sup>ng, and music and video streaming—have the potential to increase their global revenues from \$15 billion in 2015 to approximately \$335 billion by 2025 (PricewaterhouseCoopers 2015, p. 17). The same survey established that 81% of consumers consider it less expensive to share goods than to own them individually, whereas 43% agree that “owning today feels like a burden,” re<sup>fl</sup>ecting the relevance of this consumer trend.

The proliferation of sharing-economy companies can represent a challenge for traditional <sup>fi</sup>rms. It is sensible to postulate that traditional <sup>fi</sup>rms should adapt to compete in this new scenario (Cusumano 2015). However, it is unclear whether the effect of the sharing economy on incumbent <sup>fi</sup>rms is necessarily harmful, and it also is uncertain how the latter should react. Peer-to-peer (P2P) rental markets are part of the sharing-economy phenomenon, hereafter referred to in short as “P2P rentals.” In such markets, consumers who own assets (such as cars, bikes, and tools) not only can use them whenever needed, but also can monetize ownership when they are not in use by renting them to other consumers willing to pay for temporary access to the assets. Examples of such markets include Turo and Getaround (P2P car rentals), SpinLister (P2P bike rentals), Boatsetter (P2P boat rentals), Airbnb (P2P home rentals), and Zilok (P2P rentals for products and tools). The entry of P2P rentals could affect consumer ownership preferences and hence incumbent manufacturers. From the perspective of manufacturers, it is possible that P2P rentals cannibalize sales, but it also is possible that P2P rentals make ownership more attractive. For consumers with low usage rates, ownership may not be a possibility without the option of earning revenue from renting out their products to P2P rental markets. This suggests that usage rates can play an important role in explaining market outcomes in the sharing economy.

Uncertainty in usage has been consistently highlighted as a main driver of the sharing economy in practice. A boat manufacturer, Brunswick, partnered with the P2P boat rental platform Boatbound (now Boatsetter), based on the belief that “the P2P boat rental model encourages boating participation and trial, while allowing boat owners to offset some of their ownership costs.” Boatbound offers Brunswick “access to an emerging segment of boating consumers” (Brunswick 2014). The automobile industry also has considered how P2P rentals expand ownership to low-usage consumers. One example is Tesla’s master plan, which states that P2P rentals can “dramatically lower the true cost of ownership to the point where almost anyone could own a Tesla” (Musk 2016). On this Tesla Network, Tesla chief executive of<sup>fi</sup>cer Elon Musk envisions that people sharing cars when they are not using them “is the obvious thing that’s going to happen” (Dickey 2018). In the hospitality sector, real estate developers such as Natiivo and Niido are partnering with Airbnb to sell Airbnbready condos to owners who otherwise would not consider the ownership option. In the words of a developer: “ ‘What we're creating is the <sup>fi</sup>rst-ever building designed and built for home sharing’ … Many condo owners … ‘come to the city on a regular basis’ but are not year-round residents. ‘They cannot justify the fact that [the unit] is empty … it is costing them money. … ‘We are creating a product for that kind of buyer. We call it <sup>fl</sup>exible ownership.’ ” (Lyons 2019). These examples of P2P rentals increasing interest in ownership among low-usage-rate consumers illustrate the importance of usage-rate heterogeneity in different industries.

In practice, some manufacturers have embraced the idea of P2P rentals. One early example is General Motors (GM) partnering with RelayRides in 2013 (rebranded as Turo in 2015) to use its OnStar technology to facilitate participation of GM customers in the P2P rentals marketplace. Similarly, Porsche partnered with Turo to improve the Porsche P2P rental experience. More recently, some manufacturers have even considered launching their own P2P rental platforms, arguing that they could make ownership more attractive. Perhaps the best illustration of this is the aforementioned master plan for Tesla, which states that a Tesla owner “will also be able to add your car to the

Tesla shared <sup>fl</sup>eet just by tapping a button on the Tesla phone app and have it generate income for you while you’re at work or on vacation, signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly offsetting and at times potentially exceeding the monthly loan or lease cost” (Musk 2016). Other manufacturers have explored a different strategy of offering direct rentals (non-P2P) to consumers. For example, in line with Toyota’s plan to switch from being a car maker to a mobility company that includes services (Toyota 2018), it allows direct rentals in the U.S. market through its Rent a Toyota program, available only through Toyota dealers. This illustrates that although incumbent <sup>fi</sup>rms have been actively reacting to the access-based consumption trend, how to better adjust to it is far from obvious for manufacturers. It remains largely unclear which business model is the best and under what conditions.

Motivated by this trend, this paper analyzes the interaction of P2P rentals with a durable goods manufacturer under alternative market structures. The <sup>fi</sup>rst line of inquiry is to understand the impact of the entry of a P2P rentals market. The paper investigates the conditions under which a monopolistic manufacturer and its consumers are better/worse off with the avail ability of P2P rentals. In line with industry insights, the analysis reveals the important role played by usage-rate heterogeneity on the observed market outcomes. As an illustrative example, if consumers are homogeneous in their usage rates, both the <sup>fi</sup>rm and the consumers are generally worse off with the entry of P2P rentals. This surprising result arises from the rational expectations equilibrium (REE). Although consumers who prefer to rent in the presence of P2P rentals are better off, those who continue to prefer to own are worse off, because the manufacturer is able to extract all the rental revenues via a higher sales price. Furthermore, P2P rentals allow the manufacturer to segment consumers into buyers and renters while offering only one product in the market.

In contrast, when consumers are heterogeneous in their usage rates, both the <sup>fi</sup>rm and consumers can be better off with the entry of P2P rentals. We introduce one key mechanism that explains this result, the equal izing effect, which refers to the ability of P2P rentals to reduce the disparity in the willingness to pay (WTP; for the good) among consumers with heterogeneous usage rates. This paper documents the impact of this equalizing effect in the context of P2P rentals, showing how it enables the manufacturer to extract a larger fraction of total surplus with a P2P rental market than it could otherwise.

As a second line of inquiry, motivated by the question of whether and how manufacturers should adjus their business models, the paper investigates the equilibrium outcomes under different market structures. This paper investigates the sales-only manufacturer (“monopoly” operating in the absence of P2P rentals) and the “P2P-facing” manufacturer (the monopolist manufacturer operating in the presence of P2P rentals) to analyze the impact of P2P rentals. Then, it investigates three alternative scenarios. One, it analyzes the setting where the manufacturer directly offers sales and (non-P2P, direct) rentals to consumers (the “dual” <sup>fi</sup>rm). Two, this paper considers a manufacturer that itself introduces a P2P platform to the market (the “P2P-sponsoring” <sup>fi</sup>rm). Three, it analyzes a mixed structure in which the manufacturer competes against a P2P by introducing its own direct rentals (the “dualplus-P2P” <sup>fi</sup>rm).

Consumer heterogeneity in usage rates continues to play a fundamental role in market outcomes. When usage rates and heterogeneity in such rates are suf<sup>fi</sup>- ciently large, the manufacturer <sup>fi</sup>rm is better off both offering direct sales and facilitating a P2P rentals market. In contrast, if heterogeneity in usage rates is too low, the manufacturer prefers to operate as a salesonly monopoly. If heterogeneity is too high and usage rates are below a threshold, the manufacturer prefers to operate as a dual <sup>fi</sup>rm that offers both sales and rentals directly to consumers. If the presence of P2P rentals is unavoidable, the manufacturer would not always be better off by introducing its own rentals to compete against P2P rentals. The manufacturer would prefer to not offer direct rentals when consumers are more homogeneous, and such rentals would substantially cannibalize sales.

Contrary to what might be expected, this paper shows that manufacturers have an incentive to facilitate transactions of P2P rental markets in a large variety of cases. Although heterogeneity in usage frequency is a factor commonly acknowledged in the popular press as one of the most important elements for sharingeconomy companies (e.g., Badger 2014), the academic literature on P2P rentals has not suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently emphasized its role in determining market outcomes. This paper <sup>fi</sup>lls that gap and shows that usage-rate heterogeneity leads to the equalizing effect of P2P rentals. Overall, this paper makes several contributions to the literature. First, it introduces a new mechanism characteristic of the sharing economy—the equalizing effect— which occurs when there is heterogeneity in consumer usage rates, and which explains how manufacturers can bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the P2P rentals. Second, it counterintuitively shows that the manufacturer can use P2P rentals as a segmentation device. The presence of P2P rentals allows the <sup>fi</sup>rm to discriminate between the higher- and lowervalue consumers by selling the good to the higher-value users but letting the lower-value users rent from P2P rentals, allowing it to extract a higher portion of consumers’ surplus. This aspect of P2P rentals is not only interesting but unique to sharing-economy markets and is fundamentally new in the broader literature. Third, to the best of our knowledge, this is the <sup>fi</sup>rst paper in the sharing-economy literature to analyze the business model problem for the manufacturer, which is perhaps the most fundamental question for incumbent <sup>fi</sup>rms in practice. In particular, we show how our proposed mechanism (the equalizing effect) drives the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s optimal strategy toward acting as a sales-only manufacturer, sales-plus-rentals manufacturer, or P2P-sponsoring manufacturer. Finally, it provides a contrast between P2P rentals and traditional (non-P2P) rental markets in the context of their interaction with product sales.

## 2. Related Literature

Rental markets have been the focus of considerable at tention in information systems, operations management, and marketing. Several studies on the topic have focused on aspects such as optimal capacity (Savin et al. 2005), pricing (Gans and Savin 2007), and new product introductions (Bassamboo et al. 2009). Some literature has contrasted leasing versus selling (Desai and Purohit 1999, Bhaskaran and Gilbert 2005, Agrawal et al. 2012) and the interplay between sales and rentals (Purohit 1997, Gilbert et al. 2014).

Although the roots of the literature on rental markets may go back to early developments in the literature on durable goods and secondary markets (e.g., Coase 1972, Bulow 1982), there has been a renewed interest in rental markets for at least two reasons. First, manufacturingas-a-service business models (servicizing) have become popular in industry, receiving increasing attention in the literature both in terms of analytical models (Agrawal and Bellos 2017, Bellos et al. 2017) and empirical work (Guajardo et al. 2016, Guajardo 2018). Second, rentals are at the core of the sharing economy (Einav et al. 2016). Analytical models formulating new theories include the analysis by Cachon et al. (2017) of the role of surge pricing for matching supply and demand in Uber-type services, Taylor’s (2018) analysis of operational trade-offs in on-demand service platforms, and the work of Benjaafar et al. (2019) and their characterization of ownership, usage, and matching frictions in P2P sharing platforms. Our paper is different from the latter in that it focuses on the interaction between P2P and the manufacturer, whereas Benjaafar et al. (2019) focus on characterizing different aspects of P2P rental markets but ignores their interaction with manufacturer’s decisions. Thus, whereas selling prices and quantities are completely exogenous in their model, in our analysis, we endogenize these decisions by fully considering the manufacturer’s problem

The information systems literature has had a longstanding interest in markets where consumers can share goods, primarily from an intellectual property perspective. For example, Leibowitz (1985) shows that sharing can have a positive impact on sellers, as revenues can be appropriated from consumers who are not original purchasers. Similarly, Bakos et al. (1999) show that sellers of information goods can bene<sup>fi</sup>t from sharing in several scenarios. More recently, sharing in the domain of transportation has received particular attention in the information systems community. For example, Fraiberger and Sundararajan (2017) developed a dynamic equilibrium model of a sharing market that distinguishes which segments of the market are more prone to switching to rentals; Zheng et al. (2020) analyzed the impact of sales promotions on two-sided platforms in the context of a transportation network company; Filippas et al. (2019) empirically examined the impact of different pricing mechanisms in a transportation sharing economy platform. There also has been interest in how and when sharing markets themselves develop, and the behavior of market-making intermediaries, as analyzed by Razeghian and Weber (2016) and Zimmermann et al. (2018). These papers differ from ours in their focus on the intermediary market itself rather than on the interaction of these P2P rentals and incumbent manufacturer. Finally, our study has connections with the secondary markets literature. We note, however, that the temporary nature of transactions in P2P rental markets is distinct from the traditional secondary markets literature, which has explored conditions under which secondary markets can hurt or bene<sup>fi</sup>t primary markets $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , Hendel and Lizzeri 1999, Chen et al. 2013). In P2P rental markets, assets are shared between consumers even for a short period of time and without the need of a change in ownership; this is different from traditional secondary markets, where consumers typically need to become owners of goods in order to use them.

Despite the growing stream of literature attempting to characterize key aspects of the sharing economy, much less is known about how the introduction of P2P rental markets affects incumbent <sup>fi</sup>rms. One exception is the work of Weber (2016), who analyzed consumer purchase decisions with and without the presence of secondary sharing markets, with mixed results depending on the product type. In addition, Jiang and Tian (2018) investigated how the entrance of a P2P rental market affects the incumbent’s pricing and quality decisions. Their results show that a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s pro<sup>fi</sup>ts and quantity sold are not monotonically related to the marginal cost of production and the transaction costs in the sharing market, showing that P2P rentals can be good news for the manufacturer when marginal costs of production are large. Our current paper extends this <sup>fi</sup>nding by showing that even in the absence of production costs, the manufacturer (and sometime even consumers) can be better off with P2P rentals when consumers are heterogeneous in their usage rates. Moreover, the equalizing effect that our paper identi<sup>fi</sup>es is still valid in the presence of production costs. Finally, Filippas et al. (2020) also examined the economic properties of P2P rental mar kets. Note that although Filippas et al. (2020) allow for heterogeneity in usage patterns, their results do not refer to customer heterogeneity as a driver of market outcomes. Although most of the papers have focused on the P2P market, none of them has explicitly consid ered how it changes the manufacturer’s decisions. Hence, we extend the literature by analyzing the pertinent but hitherto unaddressed business model problem for manufacturers. We believe that the new insights, emerging from consideration of the business model for the manufacturer and the incorporation of heterogeneity in usage rates as a driver of market outcomes, signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly advance the understanding of P2P rental markets.

## 3.2. Equalizing Effect of P2P Rentals

Before discussing the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s choices, we describe the four distinct effects of the availability of P2P rentals on consumers. There is a cannibalization effect because some consumers may switch from purchasing to rent ing. On the other hand, the availability of P2P rentals leads to a participation effect because P2P rentals allow participation from consumers who were unable to consume when sales were the only option $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , lowusage, high per-use valuation customers). P2P rentals also generate a value effect, increasing the value of ownership as a result of P2P rentals providing revenue streams for owners. These <sup>fi</sup>rst three effects are similar to those present in the literature on secondary markets. The value effect has also been documented in the P2P literature (e.g., by Benjaafar et al. 2019).

There is a fourth effect, unique to the sharing economy, which we call the equalizing effect. When consumers are heterogeneous in their usage rates, the equalizing effect is the reduction in the disparity of WTP for owning the good among the heterogeneous consumers. The intuition is that lower-usage owners bene<sup>fi</sup>t more from the availability of P2P rentals than higher-usage owners do, as they have more periods when they could rent out their goods.

The equalizing effect is an important characteristic of the sharing economy. It occurs only when consumers are heterogeneous in their usage rates. To be clear, the equalizing effect is not capturing the increase in consumers’ WTP—that is the aforementioned, wellknown value effect—but rather re<sup>fl</sup>ects the reduction in the disparity of WTP among consumers due to asymmetric value effects. Both high- and low-usage consumers experience value effects individually; however, the value effect for low-usage consumers is larger, resulting in this equalizing effect and reduced disparity in WTP. Incorporation of the equalizing effect leads to key insights, which, to our knowledge, has not been established in the existing P2P literature. Note that the equalizing effect is generalizable under any form of heterogeneity in usage rates in the market.

## 4. Impact of P2P Rentals

This section presents results for the manufacturer operating in the presence of P2P rentals, compared with a benchmark scenario in which the manufacturer operates in the absence of P2P rentals. In this section, we refer to the manufacturer in these cases as the P2Pfacing <sup>fi</sup>rm and the monopoly <sup>fi</sup>rm, respectively. The section <sup>fi</sup>rst discusses the case in which consumers are homogeneous in usage rates $( \phi = 1 )$ to develop the intuition for how $\mathrm { P } 2 \mathrm { P }$ rentals affect consumer decisions and to highlight the importance of usage-rate heterogeneity. Afterward, the section develops the case of heterogeneous usage rates. The proofs for all lemmas and propositions are in the online appendix.

## 4.1. Homogeneous Usage Rates

Consider the simpli<sup>fi</sup>ed case of a homogeneous usage rate λ common to all consumers. Proposition 1 describes the equilibrium outcomes.

Proposition 1. The presence of P2P rentals results in lower firm profits and consumer surplus (relative to the monopoly case). A fraction $q ^ { P } = 1 - \hat { v }$ of consumers purchases the product and a fraction $\hat { v } - \tilde { v }$ rents the product in equilibrium, where

$$
\hat {v} = \frac {1}{2} + \frac {(1 - \lambda) \mu}{2 (\lambda^ {2} (1 - \mu) + \mu)} a n d \tilde {v} = \frac {1}{2} - \frac {\lambda (1 - \lambda) (1 - \mu)}{2 (\lambda^ {2} (1 - \mu) + \mu)}.
$$

The presence of P2P rentals is expected to bene<sup>fi</sup>t consumers, as it allows owners to rent out their products when they are not using them (value effect) and nonowners to rent when they need to (participation effect). However, consumers actually are worse off in the sharing economy. To understand this counterintuitive result, <sup>fi</sup>rst note that the lifetime utility of ownership can be decomposed into value from ownership use plus value from rental revenue. Each buyer expects to receive $( 1 - \lambda ) p _ { r }$ in rental revenue each period, with a lifetime expected rental revenue $( 1 - \lambda ) \bar { p } _ { r } / ( 1 - \delta )$ .This rental revenue is identical across all owners. When consumers are homogeneous, the <sup>fi</sup>rm is able to raise the durable good price to extract all the value effect. Because of this optimal response by the P2P-facing <sup>fi</sup>rm, the buyers with valuations $v _ { i } \in \left( \hat { v } , 1 \right]$ are worse off with the sharing economy. Although the consumers who switch from purchasing to renting face a decrease in their per-period consumption utility by a factor of $\mu ,$ they face a relatively lower price from rentals. As a consequence, consumers whose valuations $v _ { i } \in [ \tilde { v } , \hat { v } ]$ are better off with the sharing economy. This points to an interesting feature of the sharing economy, in which some consumer surplus from higher-valuation consumers is redistributed to lower-valuation consumers, as illustrated in Figure 4. Because $q ^ { P } \leq 1 / 2$ , the quantity purchased is lower than the quantity purchased under monopoly $( q ^ { M } = 1 / 2 ) ,$ but $\tilde { v } < 1 / 2$ , so a larger fraction of the consumers is able to use a product when they need to. The latter re<sup>fl</sup>ects the aforementioned participation effect. However, because the negative impact on buyers is larger than the bene<sup>fi</sup>t to those who shift to renting, consumers are overall worse with the sharing economy.

Lemma 2. Now that we have examined the consumption behavior by different types of consumers, we focus on how surplus generated in the presence of P2P rentals is shared between the manufacturer and the consumers. Introduction of P2P rentals allows consumers to choose between renting and purchasing, and hence creates competition for the durable good. However, the <sup>fi</sup>rm can capture some of the rental revenue by charging a higher sales price and internalize a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant portion of the bene<sup>fi</sup>t generated by P2P rentals. This intuition is formalized in the following proposition.

Proposition 3. The manufacturer is able to use P2P rentals as a discrimination device and extract a larger fraction of the social welfare compared with the benchmark monopoly firm when $\phi \ge 1 / 3 .$

The monopoly <sup>fi</sup>rm can charge only exactly one price to both the high- and low-usage consumers when $\phi \ge 1 / 3 ,$ and is unable to extract a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant fraction of the social welfare—the sum of <sup>fi</sup>rm pro<sup>fi</sup>ts and consumer surplus. Interestingly, $\mathrm { P } 2 \mathrm { P }$ rentals allow the <sup>fi</sup>rm to implicitly segment consumers into buyers (with relatively high $v _ { i }$ and $\lambda _ { i } )$ and renters (with either relatively high $v _ { i }$ and low $\lambda _ { i } ,$ or relatively low $v _ { i } )$ despite having just one product in the market. Furthermore, the equalizing effect reduces the disparity of WTP among consumers, allowing the <sup>fi</sup>rm to set its price facing a more homogeneous market. Even when low-usage consumers do not buy and only use P2P rentals, the <sup>fi</sup>rm still bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from facing a more homogeneous market of just high-usage buyers, while still bene<sup>fi</sup>ting indirectly from low-usage participation in P2P rentals. These factors enable the manufacture to extract a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant portion of the social welfare, which would not have been possible without P2P rentals. Hence, contrary to common intuition, P2P rentals can act as a discrimination device and might be more advantageous for the manufacturer than for consumers. Thus, the <sup>fi</sup>nding of this proposition is an important factor driving the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of Propositions 4 (on equilibrium <sup>fi</sup>rm pro<sup>fi</sup>t outcomes) and $5$ (on consumer welfare outcomes).

This proposition highlights an interesting win–lose scenario that arises with the entry of P2P rentals. Although a <sup>fi</sup>rm bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from increased pro<sup>fi</sup>ts in many cases, the effect on consumers depends on the level of heterogeneity and usage rate. When the benchmark monopoly <sup>fi</sup>rm will be selling only to the high-usage segment $( \phi < 1 / 3 )$ , consumers bene<sup>fi</sup>t overall from the entry of P2P rentals. The rental participation among consumers who were previously priced out of the market results in a net increase in total consumer surplus as long as the participation effect is suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently high $( \phi _ { 0 } < \phi < 1 / 3 )$ . If $\phi < \phi _ { 0 }$ though, the usage demand of low-usage consumers is too rare, and the market devolves to an approximately homogeneous market with only high-usage consumers, leading to a reduction in consumer surplus as already shown in Proposition 1. When both usage rate and heterogeneity are high (low φ, high λ), both types of consumers can bene<sup>fi</sup>t from P2P rentals. The high-usage consumers bene<sup>fi</sup>t because the large equalizing effect in this parameter region leads to substantial purchasing demand from lowusage consumers, which puts downward pressure on the durable good price. Overall, this shows that both consumers and the manufacturer can be better off even in the absence of marginal production costs. Furthermore, social welfare also increases with P2P rentals when $\phi _ { 0 } < \phi < 1 / 3 ; \phi _ { 0 }$ is decreasing in λ.

When $\phi \ge 1 / 3 ,$ the benchmark monopoly <sup>fi</sup>rm sells to both consumer types, so the large majority of consumer surplus goes to high-usage consumers who bene<sup>fi</sup>t greatly from the relatively low purchase price. With the entrance of P2P rentals, the <sup>fi</sup>rm extracts more rents, as shown in Proposition 3. This consumer segmentation substantially reduces high-usage consumer surplus, and as a consequence, the overall consumer surplus and social welfare decrease when $\phi \ge 1 / 3$

Interestingly, the entry of P2P rentals always reduces disparities in surplus between low-usage and high-usage consumers. Low-usage consumers bene<sup>fi</sup>t from both the participation and equalizing effects, and are therefore better off under most conditions, as shown in Figure 7 (unless the market is suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently homogeneous; see Proposition 1). Because the presence of P2P rentals allows the manufacturer to extract a larger surplus from high-usage consumers due to its discrimination ability, the surplus of high-usage consumers reduces and becomes closer to the surplus of low-usage consumers.

## 5. The Manufacturer’s Business Model Problem

The analysis so far has focused on comparing outcomes for a monopolistic manufacturer operating in the absence or presence of P2P rentals. This section analyzes three alternative business model strategies for the manufacturer. First is a P2P-sponsoring <sup>fi</sup>rm, where the manufacturer directly operates a P2P platform. This is in the spirit of the aforementioned Tesla network; it is also consistent with the example of Airbnb’s direct involvement with real estate developments mentioned in the introduction. A P2P-sponsoring <sup>fi</sup>rm derives revenue both from the sales of the durable good and from any transaction fees it charges in the P2P platform. Note that the rental price continues to be the market clearing price and is not set by the <sup>fi</sup>rm, which instead has the ability to capture a fraction of the rental revenues by setting a transaction fee for each P2P rental. The second strategy is a dual <sup>fi</sup>rm offering both sales and direct rentals (non-P2P), in which case the manufacturer sets the price for both. As mentioned earlier, the Rent a Toyota program by Toyota can serve as an illustration of this model. Discussed further in Section 5.2, if the dual <sup>fi</sup>rm chooses to offer only sales, this is the same as the monopoly <sup>fi</sup>rm of Section 4; for clarity of exposition, offering only sales is referred to going forward as the “sales-only monopoly” <sup>fi</sup>rm strategy. Finally, we consider the scenario, in which the manufacturer offers direct rentals to compete against P2P rentals (the dual-plus-P2P <sup>fi</sup>rm).

This section investigates the conditions under which these alternative strategies (P2P-sponsoring, dual, dual-plus-P2P) are most pro<sup>fi</sup>table for the manu facturer. The intention is to identify the best business model for the manufacturer. This analysis is useful to identify the key forces that a <sup>fi</sup>rm should keep in mind while deciding the market structure. For example, a <sup>fi</sup>rm can choose whether to use a dual strategy or a P2P-sponsoring strategy to segment consumers. As the results illustrate, it should choose the latter when the equalizing effect is stronger.

## 5.1. The P2P-Sponsoring Firm

The manufacturer can proactively introduce its own P2P rental platform. The P2P-sponsoring <sup>fi</sup>rm obtains pro<sup>fi</sup>ts from both sales and P2P rental operations, charging a percentage fee α per P2P rental $\bar { ( \alpha } \in [ 0 , 1 ) )$ Using the superscript N to denote the P2P-sponsoring strategy, the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t function is thus

$$
\pi^ {N} = p _ {s} ^ {N} q _ {s} ^ {N} + \frac {\alpha p _ {r} ^ {N} q _ {r} ^ {N}}{1 - \delta},
$$

where $\{ p _ { s } ^ { N } , q _ { s } ^ { N } \}$ are the durable good price and quantity sold, and $\{ p _ { r } ^ { N } , q _ { r } ^ { N } \}$ are the equilibrium rental price and quantity of rentals in each period, respectively. We assume that the fee α is incurred by the owners of the good. Thus, the utility of buying is now

$$
U _ {i} ^ {B, N} = \frac {\lambda_ {i} \mathrm{max} \{v _ {i} , p _ {r} ^ {N} \} + (1 - \lambda_ {i}) p _ {r} ^ {N} (1 - \alpha)}{1 - \delta} - p _ {s} ^ {N}.
$$

The utility of renting remains the same for consumers. When there is no platform fee $( \alpha = 0 )$ , this model is identical to the P2P-facing scenario described in previous sections. The α represents a mechanism for the P2P-sponsoring <sup>fi</sup>rm to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the market rental price.

Surprisingly, under our modeling assumptions, it is optimal for the <sup>fi</sup>rm to not charge any platform fee for P2P rentals $( \mathrm { i . e . , } \alpha = 0 ;$ see the proof in the online appendix). To understand why, note that the <sup>fi</sup>rm is already able to capture a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant part of the rental revenues indirectly, by raising the durable good price. When the <sup>fi</sup>rm charges a fee for P2P rentals, the low-usage owners are hurt disproportionately, as there is a larger reduction in their value effect size of reduction $( 1 - \phi \lambda ) p _ { r } ^ { N } ~ ( 1 - \alpha ) / ( 1 - \delta ) )$ . This is important because these fees also reduce the size of equalizing effect, due to the P2P rental market being less ef<sup>fi</sup>cient. Because the equalizing effect is important for pro<sup>fi</sup>tability in the presence of P2P rentals, it is optimal for the <sup>fi</sup>rm to provide the platform for free. An increase in the platform fee also would hurt consumer surplus, reducing sales to low-usage consumers and reducing the rental supply.

## 5.2. The Dual Firm

The dual manufacturer offers sales and rentals directly to consumers. Under this business model (using superscript D for this scenario), the <sup>fi</sup>rm sets the prices $\bar { p } _ { s } ^ { D }$ for the durable good and $p _ { R } ^ { D }$ for the per-use rental. The capitalized R subscript (instead of lowercase r) denotes that the dual <sup>fi</sup>rm directly sets the rental price. The consumer problem is similar to the one described for a P2P-facing <sup>fi</sup>rm. However, in this case, durable good owners derive value only from their own use and do not gain additional utility from rental revenue. The dual <sup>fi</sup>rm’s direct rentals also have peruse rental frictions, represented as $\nu ,$ where consumers receive $\nu v _ { i }$ utility per rental, with no additional transaction costs associated with the supply of rentals. In the comparison of strategies in Section 5.3, we assume that $\nu = \mu$ to keep the comparison parsimonious between the dual <sup>fi</sup>rm and the P2P-sponsoring strategy; we relax this assumption in Section 5.4. The consumer utility for the different choices is thus given by

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & U _ {i} ^ {B, D} = \frac {\lambda_ {i} v _ {i}}{1 - \delta} - p _ {s} ^ {D}, \\ & U _ {i} ^ {R, D} = \frac {\lambda_ {i} [ \nu v _ {i} - p _ {R} ^ {D} ] ^ {+}}{1 - \delta}, U _ {i} ^ {0} = 0. \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

Let $q _ { R } ^ { D }$ denote the equilibrium number of rental units in the market for the dual <sup>fi</sup>rm. The dual <sup>fi</sup>rm’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t is given by $\pi ^ { D } = { \textstyle \frac { 1 } { 2 } } p _ { s } ^ { D } \big [ ( 1 - v _ { L } ^ { D } ) + ( 1 - v _ { H } ^ { D } ) \big ] + \frac { p _ { R } ^ { D } q _ { R } ^ { D } } { 1 - \delta }$ : Here, $\{ v _ { L } ^ { D } , v _ { H } ^ { D } \}$ are the valuations of the marginal low-usage and high-usage purchasers under the dual <sup>fi</sup>rm, such that all low-type (high-type) consumers with valuation $v _ { i } \geq v _ { L } ^ { D } \Big ( v _ { H } ^ { D } \Big )$ purchase the good. The <sup>fi</sup>rm simultaneously chooses $\{ p _ { s } ^ { D } , p _ { R } ^ { D } \}$ . As indicated in Lemma 3 below, the <sup>fi</sup>rm offers both sales and rentals only when consumer usage rates are suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently heterogeneous, and if so, has optimal values of $\begin{array} { r } { p _ { s } ^ { D } = \frac { \lambda } { 2 ( 1 - \delta ) } , \bar { p } _ { R } ^ { D } = \mu / 2 , \left\{ v _ { L } ^ { D } , v _ { H } ^ { D } \right\} = } \end{array}$ $\{ 1 , 1 / 2 \}$ , and $\begin{array} { r } { \pi ^ { D } = \frac { \lambda \nu \phi + \lambda } { 8 - 8 \delta } } \end{array}$

Lemma 3. The dual firm chooses to offer both sales and rentals when $\begin{array} { r } { \phi \le \tilde { \phi } ^ { D } = \frac { 3 - \nu - \sqrt { ( 9 - \nu ) ( 1 - \nu ) } } { 2 \nu } , } \end{array}$ . Under this condi tion, it segments high-usage consumers into sales and lowusage consumers into rentals. Otherwise, the dual firm chooses a sales-only strategy and sells to both consumer groups.

The introduction of direct rentals allows a dual <sup>fi</sup>rm to segment the market by targeting sales of the durable good to high-usage consumers and per-use rentals to consumers who have low usage rates but a high valuation from consumption. However, rentals can cannibalize the sales of durable goods. When the heterogeneity between the high-usage consumers and low-usage consumers is small $( \boldsymbol { \phi } \geq \tilde { \boldsymbol { \phi } } ^ { D } )$ , the optimal rental price will still cannibalize some sales to highusage consumers and is less pro<sup>fi</sup>table than offering no direct rentals. Hence, the <sup>fi</sup>rm decides to act as the sales-only monopoly <sup>fi</sup>rm. On the other hand, with signi<sup>fi</sup>cant heterogeneity in usage rates $( \boldsymbol { \phi } < \tilde { \boldsymbol { \phi } } ^ { D } )$ ), the <sup>fi</sup>rm is able to realize the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of segmentation, offering both sales (to high-usage consumers) and rentals (to low-usage consumers).<sup>12</sup>

5.3. Comparison Between the Different Strategies Having outlined the alternative strategies that a <sup>fi</sup>rm can adopt, we now compare the conditions under which a <sup>fi</sup>rm would prefer these different business models. Note that, because it is optimal to charge no platform fees (under our model), the analysis of the P2P-sponsoring <sup>fi</sup>rm is equivalent to the analysis of the P2P-facing <sup>fi</sup>rm in Section 4 (which was simpli<sup>fi</sup>ed by assuming a feeless P2P platform); hence, the subsequent discussion will refer to only the P2P-sponsoring <sup>fi</sup>rm.

This section highlights the comparative advantages of each strategy. The unique bene<sup>fi</sup>t of adopting the dual strategy is segmenting high- and low-usage consumers into separate markets, by directly controlling both the sales and rental prices (direct pricing effect). The unique bene<sup>fi</sup>t of adopting the P2P-sponsoring strategy is the equalizing effect of P2P rentals, which creates a more homogeneous set of consumer WTP for the P2P-sponsoring strategy <sup>fi</sup>rm. The sales-only monopoly strategy does not suffer from the cannibalization effect of P2P rentals. The choice between these three strategies depends on which of these comparative advantages is stronger, which in turn depends on both the level of and heterogeneity in usage rates of the market (as determined by λ and φ). The comparison between the different strategies is formalized in the following proposition.

Proposition 6. The manufacturer never prefers a P2Psponsoring strategy when the usage rate λ is sufficiently small $( \lambda \bar { < } \lambda _ { m i n } )$ . Under this condition, the manufacturer prefers the sales-only monopoly when $\phi > \tilde { \phi } ^ { D }$ and the dual strategy when $\phi < \stackrel { \sim } { \phi } ^ { D }$ . For sufficiently large usage rates $( \lambda \geq \lambda _ { m i n } )$ , as φ decreases from $1  0 ,$ , the manufacturer first moves from a sales-only monopoly to a P2Psponsoring strategy when $\phi = \dot { \phi } _ { 2 } ^ { \pi } .$ , and then subsequently adopts the dual strategy when the direct pricing effect dominates the equalizing effect.

Figure 8 illustrates the optimal <sup>fi</sup>rm strategy. Consider the case when $\lambda < { \lambda _ { m i n } } .$ A P2P-sponsoring strategy is dominated, regardless of usage heterogeneity, because the low usage rates lead to a large amount of rental supply that competes with durable sales. Instead, following Lemma $^ { 3 , }$ the manufacturer prefers either to act as a sales-only monopoly when consumers are suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently similar to avoid the cannibalization effect of direct rentals $( \phi > \tilde { \phi } ^ { D } )$ or the dual strategy when consumers are suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently different such that they can be segmented into sales and rentals $( \phi < \tilde { \phi } ^ { D } )$

Consider the case when $\lambda \ge \lambda _ { m i n }$ . When the heterogeneity is suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently low $( \phi > \phi _ { 2 } ^ { \pi } )$ , the manufacturer prefers the sales-only monopoly strategy over the dual strategy (as shown in Lemma 3) and the P2Psponsoring strategy (as shown in Proposition 4). As φ decreases, the heterogeneity increases and the P2Pstrategy becomes more pro<sup>fi</sup>table compared with sales-only monopoly strategy. However, when $\phi$ decreases beyond $\tilde { \phi } ^ { D }$ , the direct pricing effect might start dominating the equalizing effect, and the manufacturer will adopt the dual strategy. To understand why the transition from a P2P-sponsoring strategy to a dual strategy occurs, note that the pro<sup>fi</sup>tability of the P2P-sponsoring <sup>fi</sup>rm here is due to two factors: <sup>fi</sup>rst, the equalizing effect leading to additional purchases from low-usage consumers, and second, the lowusage purchasers avoiding rental frictions by owning. If the level of usage heterogeneity is high $( \phi < \tilde { \phi } ^ { D } )$ and the usage rates are not suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently high $( \lambda < \tilde { \lambda }$ , as described in the online appendix), then the equalizing effect will lead to few or no purchases. In this situation, the direct pricing of sales and rentals dominates the equalizing effect; hence, the dual strategy is preferred. ## 5.4. Dual Firm in the Presence of P2P

This section considers this additional scenario and refers to it as the dual-plus-P2P strategy because the <sup>fi</sup>rm is offering both sales and direct rentals in the presence of P2P rentals. To understand the intuition behind this observation, consider the choice between adopting a P2P-sponsoring strategy versus a dual-plus-P2P strategy. Introducing a second form of rentals would further cannibalize those sales. However, as the level of heterogeneity increases, the number of purchases from low-usage consumers decreases and a larger fraction of low-usage consumers rent. Once the number of low-usage buyers is low, the manufacturer prefers to forgo some of its sales to low-usage consumers in exchange for introducing its own direct rentals. This is not an unlikely scenario, as several manufacturers and insurance companies have discussed technologies and contractual restrictions that can prevent P2P rentals from occurring. They also can push for regulations that prevent the sharing of goods for pro<sup>fi</sup>t, similar to the media industry.

This occurs as long as the difference between ν and μ is not too large.

In short, regardless of whether the manufacturer fully controls the market structure or not, it would not necessarily be better off by introducing its own rentals to compete against P2P rentals and might forgo that option in a meaningful number of cases.

## 6. Extensions

In this section, we consider a variety of extensions to examine market outcomes under alternative modeling assumptions.

## 6.1. P2P Market Size and Participation

In the base model, all consumers potentially participate in the P2P rental market. However, because of factors like risk aversion, lack of physical proximity to sharing markets, psychological factors, etc., it is possible that, in reality, only a fraction of consumers might be willing to participate in P2P rentals. To examine this, we extend our base model to allow only a γ fraction of the consumers to participate in P2P rentals, that is, a fraction $( 1 - \gamma )$ of consumers are not affected by the entrance of P2P rentals. (Consumers in the latter group would not participate in P2P rentals either as supply or demand, regardless of the economics of that market.) The case of $\gamma = 1$ analyzed so far is thus a special case when the whole population is part of the sharing economy. The <sup>fi</sup>rm’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t is given by

$$
\pi = p _ {s} \bigg \{(1 - \gamma) \frac {\left[ (1 - v _ {H} ^ {m}) + (1 - v _ {L} ^ {m}) \right]}{2} + \gamma \frac {\left[ (1 - v _ {H} ^ {p}) + (1 - v _ {L} ^ {p}) \right]}{2} \bigg \},\tag{7}
$$

where $\{ v _ { H } ^ { p } , v _ { L } ^ { p } \}$ denote the valuations of the participating consumers who are indifferent between purchasing and renting/not consuming. Similarly, $\left\{ v _ { H } ^ { m } , v _ { L } ^ { m } \right\}$ represent the indifferent consumers among those who are not participating in P2P rentals. As a special case, one can consider the homogeneous usage rate case (as in Section 4.1). The next proposition shows how in this case, unlike the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of Proposition 1, the sharing economy can bene<sup>fi</sup>t consumers even if there is no usage-rate heterogeneity in the market.

Proposition 7. When consumers have a homogeneous usage rate $( \phi = 1 )$ and the fraction of consumers not participating in P2P rentals is sufficiently large, $\gamma < \tilde { \gamma } =$ $\frac { 4 \lambda ^ { 2 } \mu ^ { 2 } - 5 \lambda ^ { 2 } \mu + \lambda ^ { 2 } - 4 \mu ^ { 2 } + \mu } { 4 \lambda ^ { 2 } \mu ^ { 2 } - 4 \lambda ^ { 2 } \mu - 4 \mu ^ { 2 } + \mu } ,$ then consumer surplus increases under the sharing economy compared with the sales-only monopoly case.

Intuitively, this occurs because a larger nonsharing market mediates the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s increase in the durable good price caused by P2P rentals. It is easy to see that consumers in the sharing segment are better off when $\gamma < 1$ because the <sup>fi</sup>rm is not able to increase the durable price as much as it can if $\gamma = 1 . \operatorname { I f } \gamma < 1$ , consumers participating in the sharing market bene<sup>fi</sup>t in two ways. Consumers purchasing the product retain more surplus because their rental revenues are no longer extracted completely by the manufacturer. There also is an increase in the number of consumers who rent, which also increases consumer surplus. Consumers in the nonparticipating segment are slightly hurt by a price increase, but we show that the consumer surplus gained by the participating segment outweighs the lost surplus in the nonparticipating segment when $\gamma < \tilde { \gamma }$ . This same intuition of partial participation mediating the manufacturer’s welfare extraction continues when usage rates are heterogeneous. We show numerically that if $\gamma < 1$ , the parameter region of λ, φ where consumer surplus is larger under a P2Psponsoring <sup>fi</sup>rm than a dual <sup>fi</sup>rm is expanded. If a frac tion of consumers do not participate in P2P rentals and only choose whether to buy or not, this mediates the P2P-sponsoring <sup>fi</sup>rm’s durable price increase, also mediating the rent extraction from consumers. Correspondingly, if $\gamma < 1$ , the parameter region where the P2P-sponsoring <sup>fi</sup>rm is the most pro<sup>fi</sup>table business model shrinks.

## 6.2. Correlation Between λ and v

In the main model, we consider that the usage rate and the per-use valuations are independent. However, consumers who have a higher valuation might also have higher usage rate. In this extension, we examine how our results change when there is a correlation between λ and v. Let $c o r r ( \lambda , v ) = \rho$ . We continue to assume that low-usage consumers have a valuation $v _ { i } \sim U [ 0 , 1 ]$ , whereas high-usage consumers have a valuation $v _ { i } \sim U [ b , b + 1 ]$ , where b is a constant. As shown in the online appendix, the correlation can be expressed as a function of b, as follows:

$$
\rho = \frac {\sqrt {3} b}{\sqrt {1 + 3 b ^ {2}}}.
$$

As long as there are overlapping segments of highand low-usage consumers, that is, $b < 1$ (or $\rho < \sqrt { 3 } / 2 )$ our results remain qualitatively similar to the main results. The region when P2P rentals are pro<sup>fi</sup>table for the manufacturer adjusts when usage rates and peruse valuations are correlated. At moderate correlations, the size of region is of a similar magnitude to the base results, but shifted to the right. For large correlations between $\lambda$ and v, the parameter region begins to shrink for lower values of $\lambda ,$ and for given $\lambda ,$ the range of the intermediate values of $\phi$ shrinks. When $\rho \ge \sqrt { 3 } / 2$ (or b 1), the situation devolves to a case with only one dimension of heterogeneity because there is either a very high-value group or a very low-value group. When the values of the two groups of consumers are extremely different, where the highvaluation consumers are also very likely to be high usage too, then the <sup>fi</sup>rm will sell only to this high-value group. This is similar to the homogeneous usage rate case presented in Section 4.1.

## 6.3. P2P Matching Frictions

In the main model, we assume there are no frictions in the matching process: if there is suf<sup>fi</sup>cient demand for rentals at the market rental price, an owner of a good is able to <sup>fi</sup>nd a renter. In this extension, we consider the case in which owners are not guaranteed to <sup>fi</sup>nd a renter for their good, but instead only have a $\psi$ chance of matching with a P2P renter in each period $( 0 < \psi \leq 1 )$ . In the main model, $\psi = 1$ , but in this extension, we consider how the results differ if $0 < \psi < 1$ . If $\psi < 1 ,$ , this affects the utility of ownership and the rental supply available. The utility of ownership is now

$$
U _ {i} ^ {B, P} = \frac {\lambda_ {i} \mathrm{max} \{v _ {i} , p _ {r} \} + (1 - \lambda_ {i}) \psi p _ {r}}{1 - \delta} - p _ {s} ^ {P},
$$

where the expected rental revenue is affected by $\psi .$ Because of matching frictions, the expected rental supply in each period is now given by

$$
S _ {r} (p _ {r}, p _ {s} ^ {P}) = \frac {1}{2} \psi \big [ (1 - \lambda) (1 - v _ {H}) + (1 - \phi \lambda) (1 - v _ {L}) \big ],
$$

where the rental supply is similar to earlier (the expected number of owners who will not be using the good in a period) except multiplied by ψ.

The results do not change qualitatively as a result of considering matching frictions. There are still three substantial parameter regions where sales-only monopoly, P2P-sponsoring, and dual <sup>fi</sup>rm strategies are each preferred; however, because of the inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency created by rental frictions, the region where P2P sponsoring is preferred reduces. When P2P matching frictions are higher, this results in a higher rental price and a larger amount of durable good purchases.

## 6.4. Other Extensions

We also considered several alternative modi<sup>fi</sup>cations to the model or minor extensions to the strategies. We considered a case with different proportions of highand low-usage consumers (i.e., markets with a split different than 50% of each type) and the results hold qualitatively. We considered a model without multi-

plicative rental frictions by setting $\mu { \stackrel { l i m } { \to } } 1$ for P2Psponsoring and dual <sup>fi</sup>rms, but including positive <sup>fl</sup>at transaction costs instead. In that simpli<sup>fi</sup>ed case, the qualitative insights of Sections 4 and 5 continue to hold; the parameter region where P2P sponsoring is optimal is larger but sensitive to transaction costs. This is in addition to extensions that considered additive transaction costs or production costs. Transaction costs shrink the parameter regions where sharing is preferred because it adds additional frictions to the rental market, and production costs increase the parameter regions because sharing also allows a costly good to be used more ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently. In another extension, we considered the impact of an additional rental friction that depends on the total number of durable goods sold, re-<sup>fl</sup>ecting network effects among consumer decisions. In this case, because a friction was added, the parameter region shrank where sharing was preferred, but, qualitatively, the main results remained robust to this change. Refer to the online appendix for additional discussion on these extensions. Last, we also considered a model in which high-usage consumers have a usage rate $\lambda + \phi$ and low-usage consumers have a usage rate of $\lambda - \phi$ , and a model in which high-usage consumers have a usage rate of $\lambda / \phi$ and low-usage consumers have a usage rate of φλ. Our results continue to be robust to these alternative formulations.

## 7. Conclusion

The sharing economy represents a substantial change in the structure of a market because consumers also be come the providers of goods and services. Our analysis showed that a manufacturer’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t-maximizing responses depend a great deal on the heterogeneity in consumer usage rates. When consumers are homogeneous in usage rates, surprisingly, both the <sup>fi</sup>rm and consumers can be worse off with a $\mathrm { P } 2 \mathrm { P }$ rental market. However, when there is heterogeneity in usage rates, P2P rentals make high- and low-usage consumers WTP more similar. This leads to a novel effect that we call the equalizing effect, which is a unique characteristic of the sharing economy. This effect is maximized when usage rates and level of usage-rate heterogeneity are above a threshold, and it explains why a <sup>fi</sup>rm bene-<sup>fi</sup>ts most from P2P rentals in such cases. The bene<sup>fi</sup>t of P2P rentals is realized by the consumers when the heterogeneity in usage rates is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly high and they can participate in the market. We also consider the business model problem for the manufacturer and characterized the conditions under which the manufacturer would prefer to operate offering sales only, sales plus direct rentals, and sales only plus P2P rentals.

The analysis leads to new insights with important implications for manufacturers—for example, deciding when and why to adopt different business models. To illustrate, if a manufacturer is evaluating the introduction of its own P2P platform and can choose across multiple markets with varying degrees of heterogeneity, it may introduce the P2P option (or equivalently, facilitate the operation of an external P2P platform) in markets where the level of consumer heterogeneity is such that a P2P platform would be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial. Alternatively, it can choose to operate a sales-plus-rental operation in markets with very large degree of heterogeneity. Overall, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that manufacturers can bene<sup>fi</sup>t from P2P rentals in a variety of situations, consistent with many industry examples. For instance, several manufacturers have promoted car sharing as a means of increasing sales. Beyond cars, VanMoof (a bicycle manufacturer) partnered with SpinLister (a P2P rental market for bicycles) to sell smart bikes that are easily entered and accessible via SpinLister, and the company marketed this as an added incentive for purchases. In essence, these examples are consistent with the motivation we used to analyze the P2Psponsoring strategy in this paper.

More broadly, this paper provides insights for which business models might be most appropriate across different industries. When the usage rates are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly high and there is little heterogeneity in the market, for example, in the case of home appliances, the manufacturer will continue to adopt the sales-only business model. Even though the evidence remains anecdotal, we see that P2P rentals under these conditions have not taken off, and start-ups like Peerby.com have not gained much traction (Morrissey 2015). However, the dual strategy has worked extremely well for retailers like Home Depot (in the presence of signi<sup>fi</sup>cant heterogeneity), which sells equipment to contractors and heavy-duty professionals while operating a very successful rental service for do-it-yourself consumers. Industries with an intermediate amount of heterogeneity and suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently high usage rate are favorable for the manufacturer to support the P2P rentals, for example, in automobiles and heavy equipment. As pointed out earlier, most automobile manufacturers are directly or indirectly supporting P2P rentals. Unicorn start-ups like EquipmentShare are growing very rapidly, supported by manufacturers such as Caterpillar (Konrad and Carson 2019). Hence, empirical evidence suggests that our results are a good representation of real-world outcomes.

The analysis suggested several avenues for future research. For example, two natural extensions to consider are the impact of competition between manufacturers and the analysis of the interactions between incumbent <sup>fi</sup>rms and sharing-economy companies in settings different from P2P rentals (e.g., on-demand services). Similarly, although we analyzed and discussed results under multiple ex tensions, future research can further expand the analysis to consider, among others, the impact of alternative costs structures, the evolution of the mar ket dynamics at different stages of the diffusion process (e.g., see Razeghian and Weber 2016), and markets where goods are subject to network effects. Finally, empirical work can be useful to test some of the implications from this research. For example, it could be examined empirically whether the entrance of a P2P rental market affects prices, quantities, and consumer segments in a way consistent with our <sup>fi</sup>ndings (e.g., whether sale prices indeed increase when a manufacturer introduces a sponsored P2P platform). The entry of sharing-economy companies is challenging traditional incumbent <sup>fi</sup>rms in several industries. This paper contributes to a better understanding of the potentially bene<sup>fi</sup>cial interactions that traditional <sup>fi</sup>rms can experience in this new scenario where consumers are not only always right, but also competing to supply. We hope that this paper can generate interest in understanding how incumbents respond to these new technology-enabled business models.
