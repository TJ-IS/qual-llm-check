# Mobile Consumer Scanning Technology: A Replacement for Interorganizational Information Systems for Demand Information Learning in Supply Chains?

Abstract. Upstream <sup>fi</sup>rms in supply chains have shown a growing interest in deploying consumer scanning technology (CST), which relies on consumers scanning the purchased products with their mobile devices, as a novel alternative to conventional interorganizational information technology (IOIT) to learn downstream consumer demand information. However, there is limited understanding in the literature on (i) how CST helps improve upstream <sup>fi</sup>rms’ performances and (ii) whether CST will replace IOIT. This paper develops a theoretical model to examine the value of CST to a supplier that bypasses a retailer and employs CST to directly collect scan information from consumers who are incentivized with a reward for their scanning effort. Our theoretical analysis demonstrates that CST offers both operational and strategic value to the supplier. On the operational level, recognizing that the scan information gathered by CST is a censored version of the realized demand, we de velop a simple and effective approach for the supplier to learn the realized demand from the censored scan information. We then investigate the learning ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of our approach, the optimal reward decisions, and the savings in the supplier’s inventory overage and underage costs arising from CST. On the strategic level, we examine the choice of IOIT and CST within supply chains in equilibrium. Contrary to the conventional view, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the availability of CST may expand (instead of suppress) the use of IOIT within supply chains. Using an extensive simulation analysis based on real-world data from a manufacturer that has implemented a CST program, we show that the value of CST to a manufacturer can be substantial and provide insights into how market conditions affect the value.

## 1. Introduction

Information technology (IT) has been transforming supply chains for several decades. The initial IT implementations were primarily at the individual <sup>fi</sup>rm level, such as point-of-sale systems and barcode scanners at the retail level and manufacturing resource planning (MRP) at the manufacturer level. Advances in data communication technologies led to the integration of IT systems across supply chain <sup>fi</sup>rms; a wellknown example was electronic data interchange (EDI). The advent of internet-enabled critical data sharing subsequently drove tighter coordination of processes across supply chain <sup>fi</sup>rms. Collaborative planning, forecasting and replenishment (CPFR), and vendor-managed inventory (VMI) fall in this category.

More recently, the advances in sensor-based technologies such as radio-frequency identi<sup>fi</sup>cation (RFID) and Internet-of-Things (IoT) have enabled the tracking of material <sup>fl</sup>ow within and beyond a supply chain at the individual unit level. Much of the recent IT-enabled supply chain innovations have focused on supply chain integration and coordination by improving data and process visibility across a supply chain to achieve the goal of providing the right product to the right place at the right time at the right cost.

One key issue addressed by IT-led initiatives is the costs associated with inventory overage and underage risks arising from the well-known “bullwhip effect”, which refers to the demand variability ampli<sup>fi</sup>cation as one moves upstream within a supply chain, which results in wasteful costs for upstream <sup>fi</sup>rms (Lee et al. 2000, Rai et al. 2006). Several information systems (IS) and operations management (OM) researchers have demonstrated the value of downstream <sup>fi</sup>rms’ sharing their demand information with upstream <sup>fi</sup>rms in reducing these costs (Lee et al. 2000, Raghunathan and Yeh 2001, Aviv 2007, Ganesh et al. 2014). However, the conventional supply chain collaboration is based on interorganizational IT (IOIT), a business-oriented or business-to-business technology, to establish electronic linkages between partner <sup>fi</sup>rms and integrate their IT systems. Although collaboration through IOIT could be technically feasible, organizational issues including misaligned incentives to share information, strategic gaming by players, con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality, and a lack of trust between <sup>fi</sup>rms often impede collaboration (Bock et al. 2005, Mishra et al. 2007). Some <sup>fi</sup>rms (particularly small- and medium-sized) also face additional obstacles (e.g., costs of investment, training, and maintenance) in implementing traditional IOIT initiatives.

In recent years, mobile-app based initiatives have emerged as a potential alternative to IOIT for an upstream <sup>fi</sup>rm, such as a manufacturer, to learn end consumer demand. In the mobile-app-based approach, the manufacturer learns the demand information by directly interacting with the end consumers using technologies such as mobile apps and quick response (QR) codes. This approach generally relies on consumers scanning the products after their purchase. The ubiquity and convenience of these technologies not only makes a mobile-app-based initiative easier to implement than IOIT but also enables the manufacturer to bypass the retailer to obtain the demand information. This consumer scanning technology (hereafter referred to as CST) differs from IOIT, as it is a consumer-oriented or business-toconsumer technology. This paper examines both the operational and strategic implications of the novel CST in the supply chain context by demonstrating that (a) CSTsupported demand information learning could also alleviate the bullwhip effect faced by the manufacturer, just as in IOIT, and (b) the CST also has subtle strategic impact on current supply chain IT.

1.1. A Real-Life Example of CST in Supply Chains This work is partially motivated by a CST program implemented by a (snack) food manufacturer in China that relies on retailers to sell its products. The manufacturer faces high demand uncertainty and operating cost, partly because of the bullwhip effect. Moreover, the retailers have been unwilling to collaborate and share their demand information. A few years ago, the manufacturer launched a CST program based on We-Chat, a highly popular social mobile app, to learn consumer demand information. The program works as follows. The manufacturer associates a unique QR code with an individual product unit and tracks its sale when its QR code is scanned by a mobile phone. When a consumer buys the unit and scans the QR code in WeChat, the manufacturer observes a scan record that contains the time, location, and product information for that sales transaction. Figure 1(a) depicts a spatial distribution based on the location data of scan records observed by the manufacturer in one hour, in which the dots mark the locations of WeChat scans. The scan records help the manufacturer learn about consumer behavior (e.g., consumer preferences) and, more importantly, enable the manufacturer to learn consumer demand information at different retail locations. The manufacturer offers consumers a monetary reward for each scan (hereafter referred to as the reward rate) and distributes the reward using WeChat pay (mobile wallet). Figure 1(b) shows the total reward distributed by the manufacturer during a one-week period, in which the manufacturer spent approximately ¥140,000 ( \$21,000) on a CST reward to consumers.

The CST program of the above-mentioned manufacturer is built on the WeChat app, which supports QR code scanning and mobile wallet. However, the CST program is not restricted to either WeChat or the region of China. Indeed, one of the most popular worldwide mobile apps with capabilities similar to those of WeChat is WhatsApp, which has more than 1.5 billion monthly active users and is used extensively in India, Brazil, Mexico, Russia, and many countries in South America, Europe, and Africa. Moreover, although the CST program is relatively recent, the idea of upstream <sup>fi</sup>rms interacting directly with their end consumers after purchase is not new. For instance, many manufacturers have long used product registrations and mail-in rebates for such consumer interactions. Those programs were often inconvenient for consumers and were not explicitly designed to learn demand information; they were primarily intended to provide after-sales support for durable products or to serve as a price discount mechanism. On the other hand, the CST program is convenient for end consumers as it requires little effort and is designed explicitly to learn demand information. Although convenient and a potential alternative to IOITenabled collaboration efforts, the CST program has its unique disadvantages. First, unlike IOIT that can track information <sup>fl</sup>ow (e.g., by accessing downstream transaction data using EDI) and provide complete demand information, CST tracks material <sup>fl</sup>ow and provides censored demand information, especially when downstream <sup>fi</sup>rms encounter inventory shortages (“retailer’s limited inventory”). Second, not all consumers will scan their purchased products (“consumer partial scan”), which also limits demand visibility. Finally, although consumer partial scan can be remedied by increasing the reward rate, the CST program can get expensive by doing so.

## 1.2. Research Questions

The unique features of the CST program, compared with IOIT, raise several questions for upstream <sup>fi</sup>rms that are evaluating CST as a strategy to obtain downstream consumer demand information. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we focus on the following fundamental questions.

a. Can upstream <sup>fi</sup>rms learn (or restore) the actual demand information from the censored scan data provided by CST, and if it can, how?

b. What is the optimal reward rate for the CST?

c. What is the value of CST to upstream and downstream <sup>fi</sup>rms?

d. If both CST and IOIT are potential alternatives for demand information learning, what would be the equilibrium choice, and would CST replace IOIT in supply chains?

To answer these questions, we develop a theoretical model consisting of an upstream supplier (he) and a downstream retailer (she). Following the vast body of literature that analytically quanti<sup>fi</sup>es the value of IT in supply chains (e.g., Lee et al. 2000, Raghunathan and Yeh 2001, Aviv 2007, and Ganesh et al. 2014), we let the consumer demand follow an autocorrelated process— namely, the autoregressive AR(1) process—which is widely observed in practice. In this model, the supplier observes the retailer’s historical orders but does not ob serve the consumer demand that the retailer knows, without any IT. The supplier can use either IOIT or CST to acquire more information about consumer demand. In the case where IOIT is used, the retaile would collaborate and share the demand information with the supplier (Lee et al. 2000, Ha et al. 2011). However, there is evidence to suggest that the retailer may not collaborate because of the potential risk of losing market power and data con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality if the supplier knows the demand information (Cheng and Nault 2012, Ha and Tang 2017). The supplier compensates the retailer’s information loss (that represents the cost of losing market power and data con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality) by providing the retailer a side payment to induce collab oration (Li 2002). In the case of using CST, the supplie observes the scan information generated by consumers who scan the products purchased from the retailer. As mentioned previously, the scan information is es sentially censored demand data because of the re tailer’s limited inventory and consumer partial scan. The literature (e.g., Lee et al. 2000 and Shi et al. 2019) has shown that the retailer holds more inventory in hand if her inventory critical ratio (formally de<sup>fi</sup>ned unit underage cost as more consumers will scan as the supplier’s reward rate increases. As such, the retailer’s inventory critical ratio and the supplier’s reward rate are two additional important factors in our model that shape the value of CST. ## 1.3. Findings and Contributions

Similar to the prior literature (Mukhopadhyay et al. 1995, Lee et al. 2000, Yao and Zhu 2012), we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the value of IOIT, in the absence of CST, is shaped by the trade-off between (a) the retailer’s information loss and (b) the supplier’s cost saving. Speci<sup>fi</sup> cally, the supplier is able to access complete demand information using IOIT and use it to improve his forecast accuracy and reduce his inventory-related (both overage and underage) costs. Meanwhile, the retailer suffers the information loss that has to be compensated by the supplier’s side payment under collaboration. As a result, the value of IOIT is nonnegative (hence, IOIT is adopted) only when the supplier’s cost saving covers the retailer’s information loss (see Proposition 1).

Even though CST provides only censored demand information, we demonstrate that a simple and easyto-understand approach (see Algorithm 1) that we have developed can be used to learn demand information from the censored information over time. Therefore, the CST program can also help improve the supplier’s forecast accuracy and reduce his inventory-related cost, just like IOIT. However, unlike IOIT, the supplier has to pay the reward cost (for acquiring censored demand information) and learn the demand information over several periods. Consequently, the value of CST is shaped by the trade-offs among (b) supplier’s cost savings, (c) reward cost, and (d) demand learning ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency (de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the probability that the demand information can be learned within a given number of periods). In particular, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the demand learning ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of our approach is increasing in the retailer’s inventory critical ratio and the supplier’s reward rate (see Theorem 3). An increase in the inventory critical ratio mitigates demand censoring arising from product shortages at the retailer’s end, whereas an increase in the reward rate mitigates demand censoring arising from partial scanning at the consumer’s end. Interestingly, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the inventory critical ratio and the reward rate can complement or substitute each other in enhancing the demand learning ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and improving the bene<sup>fi</sup>t of CST. When the demand information improves the supplier’s forecast accuracy by a high (low) degree, the optimal reward rate is decreasing (increasing) in the inventory critical ratio (see Lemma 2). Moreover, we also <sup>fi</sup>nd that the value of CST to the supplier increases with the inventory critical ratio (see Proposition 2) because the demand learning ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency increases with this ratio. This result implies the CST program offers a nonnegative value to the supplier (hence, CST is adopted) only when the inventory critical ratio exceeds a threshold (see Lemma 3).

We also reveal a strategic interaction between IOIT and CST: the mere presence of the CST option diminishes the retailer’s outside option value in her negotia tion with the supplier to collaborate through IOIT— the retailer gets zero surplus when the negotiation fails if there is no CST versus a negative surplus in the presence of CST. This means that the supplier can use CST as an effective threat to reduce his side payment to the retailer, and thus the value of IOIT to the two players is altered by the presence of CST. We refer to the impact from the strategic interaction as the CST spillover effect on IOIT, which includes reducing the side payment and altering the value of IOIT (see Lemma 4). Because of the CST spillover effect, the retailer is more willing to collaborate in the presence of CST than in its ab sence. Therefore, contrary to the conventional view, the availability of CST expands (instead of shrinks or replaces) the use of IOIT within the supply chain. The supplier is also more willing to collaborate through IOIT because the bene<sup>fi</sup>t from IOIT is greater than that from CST. Moreover, the supplier can extract more of the surplus from the retailer because of the retailer’s diminished outside option value when there is CST. As a result, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that CST is a viable alternative to IOIT only when the retailer’s information loss is so high that the retailer is unwilling to collaborate and the retailer’s inventory critical ratio is suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently high such that the CST program offers a nonnegative value to the supplier; when the retailer’s information loss is moderate, the presence of CST would force the retailer to collaborate through IOIT (i.e., CST acts as a threat). On the other hand, when the retailer’s inventory critical ratio is too low to make CST pro<sup>fi</sup>table, then neither CST nor IOIT would be implemented within the supply chain (see Proposition 3).

Finally, we supplement the theoretical analysis by conducting an extensive simulation of the CST model using real-world data from the aforementioned manufacturer. After <sup>fi</sup>tting our model to a data set provided by the manufacturer, we apply our model to evaluate the value of the CST program under current market conditions and explore the value at other potential market conditions by changing the autocorrelation coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of the AR(1) demand model, which is an important indicator of market conditions (Lee et al. 2000, Shi et al. 2019). The simulation results not only con<sup>fi</sup>rm our theoretical results but also provide the following additional observations. First, we observe that the learning ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency increases with the absolute value of autocorrelation coef<sup>fi</sup>cient; in our data set, when the retailer’s inventory critical ratio is high (e.g., 0.99) and the consumer demand is strongly positively autocorrelated (e.g., the autocorrelation coef<sup>fi</sup>cient is 0.8), the learning ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency exceeds 0.9, which means the manufacturer can recover more than 90% of cost saving that can be achieved by IOIT by using CST. Second, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the optimal reward rate also increases with the absolute value of the autocorrelation coef<sup>fi</sup>cient, and the autocorrelation coef<sup>fi</sup>cient could play a major role in the equilibrium IT choice: IOIT would be the likely choice when the absolute value of autocorrelation coef<sup>fi</sup>cient is large, whereras CST is likely when the absolute value is small. Overall, the numerical results suggest that CST, indeed, can be a viable strategy for manufacturers in a variety of market conditions.

This paper makes several contributions to the literature. First, a vast majority of the literature on supply chain IT has focused on business-oriented IOIT within supply chains to share information, coordinate business processes, and improve the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and effectiveness of operations. We demonstrate that some of the key bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of IOIT, such as demand information sharing and bullwhip mitigation, can also be realized by deploying technologies outside the supply chain. To our knowledge, this is the <sup>fi</sup>rst paper that examines one such technology—consumer-oriented CST—and shows how the CST can be effectively used to manage costs within a supply chain. Second, the extant literature largely focuses on a single technology (such as, e.g., EDI or CPFR) when analyzing the value of supply chain IT. Unlike these papers, we consider two potentially substitutable technologies, IOIT and CST, and examine the strategic interactions between them. Consequently, we identify the CST spillover effect on IOIT, which reveals that failure to account for such strategic effects among substitutable technologies may lead to incorrect conclusions on the value of one speci<sup>fi</sup>c IT and equilibrium technology choice. Third, prior studies in the supply chain IT literature (e.g., Dong et al. 2009 and Cheng and Nault 2012) do not consider the demand censoring phenomenon (perhaps because these papers focus on IOIT that provides complete demand information). Moreover, the current approaches used in IS literature (e.g., Tobit regression and the hazard model; Kauffman et al. 2000, Ghose and Yao 2011) can be inappropriate for our data censoring because these approaches do not capture consumer partial scan and demand autocorrelation. This paper contributes to the literature by devising a new approach for autocorrelated demand censoring subject to consumer partial scan and explaining how to alleviate the censoring and improve demand learning ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. In particular, we identify the retailer’s inventory critical ratio as a key factor that shapes the demand learning ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and value of supply chain IT, which has not been examined by the extant literature.

## 2. Related Literature

This paper is related to two broad research areas in the IS discipline: mobile apps and supply chain IT. A comprehensive review of the vast research on these topics can be found in Jeyaraj and Zadeh (2020). We brie<sup>fl</sup>y review closely related papers in each area and clarify the key distinctions between those and our paper. A summary of key features of this paper is given in Table 1.

## 2.1. Mobile Apps

Mobiles apps have become ubiquitous, and they enable and support a variety of services such as communication, entertainment, healthcare, and <sup>fi</sup>nance, among others. Recognizing the potential of mobile apps as a new consumer-driven technology and the rapid growth of the app market, IS researchers have recently started to study the mobile app market. Research on the demand side of mobile app market includes empirical examination of consumer behaviors related to mobile apps (Kwon et al. 2016, Xu et al. 2019) and mobile app demand and choice (Han et al. 2016, Dong et al. 2020). Research on the supply side has investigated economic is sues such as pricing in-app purchases and detecting app copycats (Wang et al. 2018, Shulman and Geng 2019). Moreover, inspired by the industrial application of mobile apps, researchers have also explored the business value of mobile apps, especially in marketing and consumer targeting (Sun et al. 2017, Ghose et al. 2019). Our paper also addresses the business value of mobile apps but complements the existing stream of work by examining the value of a novel use of a mobile app by an upstream supplier in a supply chain context. To the best of our knowledge, this work is the <sup>fi</sup>rst attempt to study the application of mobile apps in supply chain management as an alternative or a competitor to existing IOIT.

## 2.2. Value of Supply Chain IT

This study is related to the literature on supply chain IT, especially IOIT-enabled collaboration, which has been extensively studied by IS and OM researchers (Lee et al. 2000, Raghunathan 2001). IS researchers have focused on empirical studies of speci<sup>fi</sup>c IOIT (e.g., EDI) to explore their potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and costs based on industrial examples of IOIT (Mukhopadhyay et al. 1995, Rai et al. 2006). A typical bene<sup>fi</sup>t generated by IOIT is upstream suppliers’ inventory-related cost savings because IOIT helps improve their forecast accuracy and reduce the bullwhip effect across supply chains (Dong et al. 2009, 2020; Yao and Zhu 2012). Unfortunately, downstream partners can reject IOITenabled supply chain collaboration because of classical costs including losing market power and data con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality, which can offset bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of information transparency (Cheng and Nault 2012, Ha and Tang 2017). Related to this issue, a few IS researchers have also studied supply chain <sup>fi</sup>rms’ incentives to adopt IOIT and equitable sharing of value appropriated from IOIT (Wang and Seidmann 1995, Ganesh et al. 2014). The literature provides limited insights about alternatives to JOIT when it is not feasible.

Similar to the literature, we also study the value of IOIT and CST based on the trade-off between the bene<sup>fi</sup>t of cost saving and the cost of losing market power and data con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality. Moreover, our paper differs from the literature in several directions. First, our paper demonstrates that CST can be an effective alternative when IOIT-enabled supply chain collaboration does not occur. Second, unlike the majority of prior studies that focuses on a single IT, we jointly consider two technologies, CST (new IT) and IOIT (traditional IT), with CST as a possible replacement for IOIT. In a broader sense, this paper is similar to the work of Steelman et al. (2019) that empirically examines <sup>fi</sup>rms problem about allocating resources between new IT and traditional IT from the perspective of organizational commitment, and it does not consider the potential interactions between the technologies. Distinct from their work, this paper considers <sup>fi</sup>rms’ strategic choices between new IT and traditional IT from the perspective of business value; more important, our analysis complements theirs by identifying the strategic interactions between the technologies (i.e., CST spillover effect on IOIT). Third, the existing papers often focus on IOIT that provides complete demand information, and our paper complements them by considering CST that provides censored demand data. Data censoring is a common issue happening in other IS areas (e.g., IT-enabled <sup>fi</sup>rm survival and healthcare), and several approaches (e.g., Tobit regression and hazard model; Kauffman et al. 2000, Ghose and Yao 2011) have been proposed for data censoring. However, these approaches, which are often designed for right (or left) data censoring under several assumptions (e.g., independence assumption), cannot be applied to our problem because consumer partial scan makes our data censoring beyond right censoring, and the autocorrelated demand data can violate the assumptions. This paper also complements the IS research on data censoring by developing a simple and interpretable approach that can effectively handle autocorrelated demand censoring subject to consumer partial scan and ensure successful demand information learning. Our model and analysis also provide insights into the effects of demand censoring on the learning process and factors that can alleviate the negative effects of demand censoring. To our knowledge, these results are new to the literature.

The AR(1) process is an intuitive and insightful demand model that is widely observed and discussed in practice and literature $( \mathrm { e . g . , }$ Lee et al. 2000, Raghunathan and Yeh 2001, and Aviv 2007), whose long-run distribution has mean $\frac { \tau } { 1 - \lambda }$ and variance $\scriptstyle { \frac { \sigma ^ { 2 } } { 1 - \lambda _ { - } ^ { 2 } } } .$ As shown in the prior literature on AR(1) models (Lee et al. 2000), the retailer can easily and accurately estimate the parameters of (1) using historical demand data; meanwhile, the supplier can know the demand process through periodic communication with the retailer or using the retailer’s historical order data. Hence, we assume that the AR(1) process with the exact values of τ and λ is common knowledge to the two players.

At the beginning of period $t ( = 1 , 2 , \dots )$ , the retailer’s inventory level (that equals inventory on hand minus backorders) is $I _ { t } .$ During period $t ,$ consumer demand $D _ { t }$ is satis<sup>fi</sup>ed using retailer’s inventory, and the unsatis<sup>fi</sup>ed part is backlogged. At the end of period $t ,$ the retailer places an order $Q _ { t }$ to replenish inventory and receives the ordered products at the beginning of period $t + l + 1 \ ( \mathrm { i . e . }$ , the retailer’s order lead time is $l + 1 )$ ). The retailer’s inventory overage (holding) and underage (backorder) costs are h and $p$ per unit per period, and $p > h .$ . When receiving the retailer’s order at the end of period $t ,$ the supplier <sup>fi</sup>lls the order using inventory on hand. When the inventory is not enough for <sup>fi</sup>lling the retailer’s order, the supplier meets the shortfall by procuring from another source and resupply this source later (Lee et al. 2000), where the supplier’s overage and underage costs (of resupplying) are H and P per unit per period, and $P > H$ . Then the supplier manufactures to replenish his inventory. It is worth mentioning that the retailer in our model with backorders observes true demand information (Lee et al. 1997, 2000; Aviv 2007); however, in certain industries, the retailer may face lost sales when the consumer demands exceed her inventory on hand and thus not observe true demand information. Although our model with backorders is not perfect to capture the lost-sale scenario, it can be a good approximation when the inventory stockouts do not often happen, which is the case in many retail contexts. The literature $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , Lee et al. 2000) and Shi et al. 2019) has shown that the inventory stockouts depend on the retailer’s inventory critical ratio $\begin{array} { r } { \eta : = \frac { p } { p + h } \dot { \cdot } \qquad } \end{array}$ as η increases, the retailer increases her safety stock and reduces inventory stockouts accordingly. Because η is a key parameter in our analysis, we follow the literature (e.g., Bernstein et al. 2015) and let $\eta$ be an exogenous parameter in the remainder of this paper by reexpressing the retailer’s underage cost as a function $p ( \eta ) : =$ $\textstyle { \frac { \eta } { 1 - \eta } } h$ of $\eta$ given the overage cost h.

## 3.1. Interorganizational Information Technology 3.1. Interorganizational Information Technology

When the two players collaborate using IOIT, the retailer shares her demand information with the supplier. Consistent with the literature (e.g., Lee et al. 2000 and Ha and Tang 2017), we let demand information sharing refer to a scenario in which the supplier is able to access the retailer’s (uncensored) historical demands over time. However, it has been observed that retailers are often unwilling to share demand information because they can suffer a loss (called information loss) by doing so. Two typical reasons cited for retailers’ information loss are (a) suppliers’ response of altering wholesale prices based on the shared information (L 2002, Ha et al. 2011) and (b) the risk of direct/indirect information leakage of retailer’s demand data to their competitors (Cheng and Nault 2012, Ha and Tang 2017). In particular, the information leakage and breaches to data con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality could cause retailers to lose market power, which could even offset the bene-<sup>fi</sup>ts of information transparency (Zhu 2004). Because retailers’ information loss can play a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in the adoption of IOIT, we incorporate this factor into our model. Prior literature has modeled different information loss mechanisms in different ways. Instead of modeling a speci<sup>fi</sup>c mechanism, we model the information loss by a constant $\gamma$ which captures a possible disincentive for the retailer to share information. In some sense, the constant γ represents the retailer’s perceived cost for losing marketing power and data con<sup>fi</sup>- dentiality over time. We also assume the supplier knows the value of $\gamma$ through possible communications or negotiations for IOIT adoption with the retailer. As such, the supplier has to compensate the retailer by offering a side payment $S P$ to induce the use of IOIT, where $S P$ depends on the relative bargaining powers of the two players. Following game theory, we employ a Nash bargaining framework to characterize $S P ,$ , and the retailer’s and supplier’s bargaining powers are denoted by α and $1 - \alpha \ ( 0 \leq \alpha \leq 1 )$ , respectively.

## 3.2. Consumer Scanning Technology

By using CST, the supplier observes the number of scanned products $S _ { t }$ in period $t ,$ which is expressed by

$$

$$

where $\left( D _ { t } - I _ { t } ^ { - } \right) \land \left( I _ { t } ^ { + } + Q _ { t - l - 1 } \right)$ is the number of sold items, and $\psi _ { t }$ is the scan rate (i.e., the fraction of scanned items), which depends on consumer scanning behavior. However, consumers may lack the incentive to scan because scanning is not effort-free. To incentivize consumers to scan, the supplier provides a reward θ (called the reward rate) for every consumer scan. We model consumer scanning behavior and derive the expression for the scan rate $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ the fraction of consumer scan) as follows. Let w denote a consum-$\mathrm { e r ^ { \prime } s }$ cost of scanning effort. Note that the supplier does not observe the exact value of w for every consumer but can obtain the distribution of w among consumers through market surveys. Here, we assume w is uniformly distributed over 0, 1 for tractability. $\mathrm { A }$ consumer’s valuation of scanning is $\theta + \zeta _ { t } ,$ , where θ is set by the supplier, and $\zeta _ { t }$ is white noise capturing valuation interference by other uncertain factors (such as, $\mathrm { e . g . , }$ emotion). Moreover, $\psi _ { t } ( \theta )$ is stochastically increasing (dominant) in $\theta ,$ , which means that consumers are more likely to scan as the reward rate increases. The boundaries of $\psi _ { t } ( \theta )$ are as follows: when $\theta = 1$ (maximum reward rate), almost every consumer scans, and the probability that every consumer scans is 1 $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ $\mathbb { P } \{ \hat { \psi _ { t } } ( 1 ) = 1 \} = \stackrel { \cdot } { 1 } ) ;$ when $\dot { \theta _ { } } = 0$ (no reward rate), almost no consumer scans, and the probability that no consumer scans is 1 $( \mathrm { i . e . , ~ } \mathbb { P } \{ \psi _ { t } ( \bar { 0 } ) = 0 \} = \bar { 1 } )$ . A concrete example of such $\zeta _ { t }$ is a Bernoulli variable with the following probabilities:

$$

$$

Accordingly, $\psi _ { t } ( \theta )$ is also a Bernoulli variable with $\mathbb { P } \{ \psi _ { t } ( \theta ) = 1 \} = \dot { \theta }$ and $\mathbb { P } \{ \psi _ { t } ( \boldsymbol { \theta } ) = 0 \} = 1 - \boldsymbol { \theta }$ . $$

## 4.4. Equilibrium Technology Choice

From the previous results, we can characterize the equilibrium technology choice, which is obtained by a backward induction. Because CST is a supplierinitiated consumer-oriented technology that does not require retailer’s cooperation, the adoption of CST is only determined by the supplier. On the other hand,

IOIT is business-oriented technology that requires retailer’s collaboration, the adoption of IOIT is also determined by the retailer.

## 5.2. Simulation Results

A central question for the manufacturer is whether CST is an effective technology alternative of IOIT. The previous analysis has shown that the CST effectiveness depends on two factors, the learning ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency $\mathcal { L } ^ { * }$ and the reward cost $R ^ { * }$ , which are both determined by the optimal reward rate $\theta ^ { * }$ . We develop a measure of CST effectiveness that can combine these factors together as follows: $\begin{array} { r } { E F F = \frac { C _ { s } ^ { N } - C _ { s } ^ { S } } { C _ { s } ^ { N } - C _ { s } ^ { I } } = \mathcal { L } ^ { * } - \frac { R ^ { * } } { \omega _ { s } \Delta } , } \end{array}$ , where $C _ { s } ^ { N } - C _ { s } ^ { S }$ is the manufacturer’s cost saving from CST relative to NIT, and $C _ { s } ^ { N } - C _ { s } ^ { I }$ is the cost saving from IOIT relative to NIT (ignoring the side payment), and the term $\frac { R ^ { * } } { \omega _ { s } \Delta }$ represents the CST reward loss. Then we evaluate these factors and CST effectiveness under the current $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ the data used in this simulation) and other potential market conditions with different values of autocorrelation coef<sup>fi</sup>cient λ.<sup>3</sup>

We <sup>fi</sup>rst examine the CST effectiveness under the market condition when $\lambda = 0 . 1 2$ . Figure 5(a) shows that the optimal reward rate $\theta ^ { * }$ remains at 0.1 when $\lambda = 0 . 1 2$ for all values of $\eta$ in the simulation range, which suggests the manufacturer is better off providing the minimal reward rate under this market condition. Moreover, Figure 5(b) shows that the learning ef<sup>fi</sup>- ciency $\mathcal { L } ^ { * }$ ranges from 0.61 to $0 . 6 7$ as $\eta$ increases when $\lambda = 0 . 1 2$ . Figure 5(d) indicates that the CST effectiveness increases from 5.79% to 11.92%, which means CST gen erates only a small fraction of cost savings of IOIT. The low CST effectiveness is due to high CST reward loss $\frac { R ^ { * } } { \omega _ { s } \Delta } = 5 6 \%$ when $\lambda = 0 . 1 2$ (see Figure 5(c)). However, we note that CST still generates cost savings relative to NIT; actually, the cost savings $C _ { s } ^ { N } - C _ { s } ^ { S }$ is positive (whose sign is implied by $E F F > 0 )$ ). Hence, we conclude that CST is inferior to IOIT but still helps reduce the manufacturer’s cost relative to NIT under this market condition.

We also examine the CST effectiveness for different values of λ. Figure $5 ( \mathrm { a } )$ shows that the optimal reward rate $\theta ^ { * }$ increases with λ (i.e., θ<sup>∗</sup> increases with $\lambda \in$ $[ 0 , 1 )$ and decreases with $\lambda \in ( - 1 , 0 ] )$ , and it has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant increase for $\lambda \in [ 0 , 1 ]$ . Figure 5(b) shows that the behavior of $\mathcal { L } ^ { * }$ synchronizes with that of $\theta ^ { * }$ , which implies that the reward rate is the main factor shaping the learning ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency. Furthermore, Figure $5 ( \mathrm { d } )$ is almost the inverse of Figure 5(c), which means the CST effectiveness is mainly affected by the CST reward loss. Note that when λ is close to 1, the CST effectiveness is higher than 90%, which means that CST can help the manufacturer recover more than 90% of cost savings that can be achieved by IOIT, suggesting CST can be an effective alternative to IOIT when λ is high.

## 6. Concluding Remarks

This paper examines a recent implementation of CST to improve supply chain management, which is in sharp contrast to the traditional reliance on interorganizational systems and collaboration within supply chains. Our theoretical and simulation results provide the following insights. First, the results demonstrate the value of ubiquitous and easy-to-use consumer-oriented technologies such as mobile apps even for upstream <sup>fi</sup>rms that do not directly interact with end consumers. These technologies need not be limited to the traditional marketing, selling, or support efforts, but they can be useful to improve the internal operations as well. Second, the results remind upstream <sup>fi</sup>rms to assess the value of these technologies based on not only their operational value but also their strategic value. In the supply chain context, the strategic value arises because the upstream <sup>fi</sup>rm is able to use CST to bypass the downstream <sup>fi</sup>rm and infer the demand information possessed by it. This suggests that evaluating the value of technologies such as CST based only on their operations value provides only a partial and possibly misleading characterization of their bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. These insights have been shown to be robust even if our model are generalized by relaxing some assumptions (e.g., considering <sup>fi</sup>xed cost) in the online appendix.

This paper can be extended in different directions. First, the demand is assumed to follow an AR(1) process. Although the AR(1) process is widely used in the literature and in practice, future research can consider more general demand processes (such as, e.g., autoregressive moving average or autoregressive integrated moving average). Second, this paper assumes that the retailer’s information loss is public information. An interesting extension is to consider the information asymmetric setting in which the information loss is the retailer’s private information. Third, the possible impact of CST on the retailer’s bargaining power is considered in the online appendix by assuming that CST makes the retailer lose some constant bargaining power, although there may be other meaningful ways to model such an impact that enable us to explore the supplier’s possibility of endogenizing the retailer’s bargaining power as a decision variable via CST.
