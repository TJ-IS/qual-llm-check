# Where You Live Matters: Local Bank Competition, Online Marketplace Lending, and Disparity in Borrower Benefits

Mohammed Alyakoob,<sup>a</sup> Mohammad S. Rahman,<sup>b</sup> Zaiyan Wei<sup>b</sup>

Abstract. In the past decade, the proliferation of online marketplace lending has been disrupting the consumer credit market, especially personal loans used for debt consolidation. These lenders, for example, Lending Club, transcend the geographic boundaries within which local banks operate and offer homogeneous access and terms to borrowers. However, the ultimate bene<sup>fi</sup>ts borrowers derive from marketplace lending can differ signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly because local alternatives may be used to replace marketplace loans when they are available and favorable. Correspondingly, if local bank competition drives the substitution of an existing marketplace loan with a traditional bank loan, the promise of equal bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to all borrowers from marketplace lending is unlikely to fully materialize. This competitive dynamic also has implications for policy making, particularly in judging the rami<sup>fi</sup>cations of bank mergers and acquisitions. We utilize data from Lending Club, a major peer-to-peer (P2P) lending platform, to study whether local market structure drives heterogeneous incentives of traditional banks to convert borrowers of marketplace lending to conventional bank loans. The results indicate that a borrower who resides in a more competitive market is more likely to pay off a P2P loan early by making a large, one-time payment compared with a borrower from a less competitive market. Thus, borrowers from different market do not bene<sup>fi</sup>t equally from online marketplace lending disrupting the consumer credit market. Our study has implications for online marketplace lending, other FinTech-based markets, and the consumer credit market in general.

## 1. Introduction

The rise of online marketplace lending threatens traditional banking by removing intermediaries and facilitating direct funding between investors and borrowers. Marketplace lending platforms offer a wide range of <sup>fi</sup>- nancial products, including personal, business, and mortgage loans, among others. These platforms transcend the geographic boundaries within which traditional credit providers operate and can utilize lower <sup>fi</sup>xed costs to potentially offer superior terms. For example, the largest personal loan marketplace lending platform in the United States, Lending Club (LC), lists two main drivers of its growth in its 10K <sup>fi</sup>lings: (1) “a fast and easy-to-use online and mobile application process” and (2) “a wide range of borrower interest rates that are lower on average than the rates charged by banks for credit cards.” Importantly, most Lending Club borrowers (more than 80%) utilize the platform to reconcile their debts, often focused on reconciling costly credit card debts. Related research suggests that, when traditional banks face credit crunches or do not lend to riskier borrowers, online marketplace lending may serve as an alternative (Tang 2019). Correspondingly, in the United States, FinTech lenders accounted for about 38% of all new personal loans in 2018, skyrocketing from 1% in 2010 (Blumberg 2019). In other words, online marketplace lending has emerged as a credible substitute for conventional loan offerings.

With the rapid growth of consumer credit issued on marketplace lending platforms and the corresponding borrowers often improving their credit worthiness (e.g., because of debt consolidation), traditional banks should <sup>fi</sup>nd it incentive compatible to attract FinTech borrowers to conventional bank loans. However, the incentives of traditional banks depend on their local competitive dynamics (Dick 2007). Correspondingly, if local bank competition drives the substitution of an existing marketplace loan with a traditional bank loan, the promise of equal bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to all borrowers from marketplace lending is unlikely to fully materialize.

Also, for policy making, it is important to understand if and how local banking competition dictates traditional banks’ capacity and incentives to respond to the changes in the competitive landscape resulting from marketplace lending. This understanding is necessary for a variety of policy decisions, such as judging the rami<sup>fi</sup>cations of potential bank mergers and acquisitions (M&As) as well as informing various traditional bank stakeholders (e.g., shareholders and regulators). For instance, in the case of a proposed merger between banks, conventional practice only considers the impact on traditional bank clients in the local banking market. However, if such a merger also affects the traditional banks’ incentives to attract marketplace borrowers, policy enforcement would be ill-informed absent such understanding.

Past research and government agencies, including the Department of Justice, primarily focus on the market structure in assessing banks’ incentive to respond to changing market conditions (see, e.g., Liebersohn 2017). In this regard, it is important to note that traditional banks operate within market boundaries (or geographic clusters).<sup>1</sup> When evaluating the antitrust implications of a proposed bank merger, the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) examines markets in geographic clusters. Accordingly, we study whether local market structure, as measured by the number of banks or market concentration, creates incentives for local banks to persuade marketplace lending borrowers to utilize conventional bank loans. Furthermore, we examine how the local banks’ incentives adjust when branches close because of banking M&A activity.

A critical challenge in studying the incentive of traditional banks to online marketplace lending is that detailed information regarding the loan origination process, for example, what terms a borrower received from local banks and how they compare with loans from marketplace lending platforms, is practically impossible to obtain. Moreover, the initial loan origination process suffers from bias because banks often refrain from unsecured lending to lower quality borrowers, whereas these borrowers are a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant part of online marketplace lending.<sup>2</sup> Because the typical life cycle of a loan often involves re<sup>fi</sup>nancing by borrowers with an improving credit pro<sup>fi</sup>le, even if the data were available, an analysis involving initial loans would miss traditional banks’ efforts to convert existing borrowers of online marketplace lending. This issue would be quite signi<sup>fi</sup>cant given that most Fin-Tech borrowers’ motivation behind using the online channel is to consolidate past credit (which was held by traditional banks); therefore, the improved underwriting conditions justify traditional banks’ incentive in re<sup>fi</sup>nancing existing loans.

In our study, we obtain detailed loan-level data of marketplace lending borrowers. The data enable us to examine the role of local market competition on individual borrower-level differences in debt manage ment. Identifying differences in borrowers’ debt man agement behavior as it relates to differences in local banking competition, provides a proxy to measure changes in banks’ incentives to respond to marketplace lending. Moreover, because marketplace borrowers are clearly aware of the online channel and have previously utilized it, our analysis provides a conservative estimate of the impact of local market structure on banks’ incentives to target and serve the marketplace borrowers. Had we been able to track information about borrowers who chose not to borrow from marketplace lenders after considering it as an option, our estimates would have been larger.

To brie<sup>fl</sup>y illustrate our approach, by way of an example, consider two statistically equivalent borrowers who obtain identical loans from a marketplace lending platform. The borrowers have the same credit pro-<sup>fi</sup>le, and the economic conditions of each borrowers respective area, in terms of both current conditions and future economic developments, are similar. Given that these two borrowers are equivalent, their debt management should not be different if the local market structure does not play a role in banks’ incentives to respond to marketplace lending. Also, the borrowers have already decided to initially forego local options, meaning that the initial competition, for whatever reason, did not stop them from utilizing the online marketplace. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we examine each borrower’s propensity to pay off the balance of the loan after a certain period of time with a large, one-time payment. If we observe that the borrower from the area with higher competition is more likely to make such a large one-time payment before the completion of the loan, then, on average, the variation in local bank competition leads to circumstances in which banks have higher incentives to make local funding available to facilitate the large, one-time payment. This suggests that banks in these areas are incentivized to be more responsive to the marketplace lending alternative (than banks in less competitive areas). Despite the nascency of marketplace lending, there is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant evidence that traditional banks consider FinTech platforms as competitors and are cognizant of the challenges these platforms introduce.<sup>3</sup>

Although similar borrowers receive equivalent rates on marketplace lending platforms, there are many reasons why local bank competition leads to circumstances in which banks have higher incentives to attract marketplace borrowers. With regard to pricing, borrowers from more competitive markets have access to funds with more favorable terms (Hannan and Prager 2004, Degryse and Ongena 2005, Dick 2007). This implies that the difference between the marketplace platform rate and the of<sup>fl</sup>ine rate is smaller in more competitive markets. This is meaningful because the vast majority of marketplace borrowers utilize the platform for debt consolidation, enabling borrowers to signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly improve their credit pro<sup>fi</sup>les. An improved credit pro<sup>fi</sup>le conceivably allows these borrowers to avail local alternatives with favorable rates when their bank market is more competitive.

Furthermore, areas with less bank concentration (higher competition) are generally faster to react to macro level changes, such as discount rate adjustments (Adams and Amel 2011). The quicker response to macro changes, coupled with improving borrower credit pro<sup>fi</sup>les, means that local competitive dynamics can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly impact the debt management decisions of marketplace borrowers. Notably, traditiona banking institutions are increasing their unsecured personal loan marketing efforts at the expense of credit card marketing, suggesting that the changing landscape has resulted in greater focus on unsecured loans.<sup>4</sup> Anecdotal evidence suggests that banks are, in fact, better at identifying risk prospects in the unsecured loans market.<sup>5</sup>

Local banking competition is also important in determining the general availability of local funding for marketplace borrowers, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally those with relatively bad credit pro<sup>fi</sup>les. Those residing in more competitive banking environments have greater access to bank <sup>fi</sup>nance (Beck et al. 2004, Cetorelli and Strahan 2006). Furthermore, the charter value hypothesis predicts that more banking competition can cause an increase in risk-taking behavior (Keeley 1990). Increased bank competition leads <sup>fi</sup>nancial institutions to more nontraditional banking services and risky loan provisioning (Jiang et al. 2019, Jimenez et al.´ 2013). This is evident from the positive relationship between bank competition and banks’ provision for loan losses (Berger et al. 2004, Dick 2006). Marquez (2002) <sup>fi</sup>nds that an increase in the number of banks in an area results in greater access to credit for lower quality borrowers.

Our rich data set is from the largest peer-to-peer (P2P) marketplace lending platform for personal loans in the United States, LendingClub.com.<sup>6</sup> The data set has all loans funded on Lending Club since its inception and keeps track of all payments made by each borrower. The data set also contains a comprehensive set of borrower and loan characteristics, such as borrower location, credit scores (speci<sup>fi</sup>cally FICO scores), the purpose of the loan, and other variables used to assign credit scores. We also obtain the number of consumer credit offering institutions from the business pattern data maintained by the U.S. Census Bureau. From the FDIC, we gather branch-level deposit data as well as a comprehensive list of all M&As between banks. Finally, to control for local market characteristics, we combine a rich set of variables ob tained from the U.S. Census Bureau, the Bureau of La bor Statistics (BLS), and the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD).

We utilize an instrumental variable (IV) approach as well as regulatory changes that led to exogenous shocks to bank lending to address potential endogeneity issues. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that, controlling for a detailed set of borrower and market characteristics, borrowers who live in more competitive markets (i.e., with more local lending institutions) are more likely to make a large, one-time payment to pay off their marketplace loans early than borrowers from othe markets. The conclusions still hold if we use the number of FDIC-insured bank branches and the deposit amount held by FDIC-insured banks as the measures of local market structure.<sup>7</sup> An examination of market concentration, measured by the Her<sup>fi</sup>ndahl–Hirschman Index (HHI) of local consumer credit markets based on FDIC data sets, consistently <sup>fi</sup>nds that borrowers in less concentrated (i.e., more competitive) markets are more likely to make large, one-time payments.

To inform regulators concerned about the impact of bank mergers, we also conduct a comprehensive assessment of the impact that bank mergers have on banks’ incentives to respond to marketplace lending. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that M&As between banks, which often lead to an increase in the concentration of local markets, are associated with a lower likelihood of large, one-time payments made by marketplace borrowers. Moreover, we examine the impact of branch closures that result from bank M&As on the debt management behavior of marketplace borrowers in affected areas. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that, in areas with fewer bank branches, branch closures caused by bank M&As are associated with even lower probabilities of large, one-time payments.

In seeking evidence consistent with the underlying mechanism (i.e., marketplace borrowers utilize local funding options to pay off their marketplace loans), we explore the moderating role of FICO score improvement at the individual loan level. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that a marketplace borrower from a more competitive market is even more likely to make a large, one-time payment if their FICO score improves and passes the 700 threshold. This evidence lends support to our proposed mechanism because, as a result of minimum FICO score restrictions, many local lending institutions would previously have overlooked such bor rowers. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that, in areas with more competitive markets, banks and other lending institutions are more incentivized to provide funding for these borrowers once they pass the acceptable FICO score threshold. Other tests exploring Lending Club’s interest rate assignments and repeated borrowers on the platform provide further evidence supporting the underlying mechanism.

As discussed earlier, our empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings have substantive implications for the burgeoning marketplace lending industry and policy implications for regulators. Besides, our study also has implications for traditional consumer credit providers. Our empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that local bank competition dictates the incentives for local lending institutions to respond to the changed competitive landscape because of the rise of online marketplace lending. Given the large market share of FinTech lenders in the new issuances of personal loans (Blumberg 2019), commercial banks and other lending institutions (e.g., credit unions) may have to respond to the new phenomenon more strategically. For example, to compete with FinTech lenders, local lending institutions can offer more customizable consumer credit rather than simply offering better terms.<sup>8</sup> In addition, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that FinTech borrowers utilize local markets to re<sup>fi</sup>nance loans obtained from marketplace lending platforms. This presents an opportunity but, simultaneously, some potential risks to local lending institutions. On the one hand, local lending institutions may view FinTech loans as a new and potentially pro<sup>fi</sup>table asset class. On the other hand, the risks associated with FinTech loans (most of which are unsecured) may be exacerbated in the process of re<sup>fi</sup>nancing these loans or extending a consumer’s credit by the local lending institutions.

## 2. Literature and Contributions

We <sup>fi</sup>rst contribute to the burgeoning literature on online marketplace lending or, more speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the literature on P2P lending. Early studies on P2P lending focus primarily on investors’ behaviors on the platforms and, consequently, the implications for funding outcomes and market ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency (see, e.g., Berkovich 2011, Pope and Sydnor 2011, Zhang and Liu 2012, Lin et al. 2013, Iyer et al. 2015, Lin and Viswanathan 2016).<sup>9</sup> To the best of our knowledge, our paper is the <sup>fi</sup>rst study examining the potential for local bank competition to drive disparate bene<sup>fi</sup>ts for online marketplace borrowers. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we provide systematic evidence that marketplace borrowers bene<sup>fi</sup>t disproportionately from the local market in their debt management.

Two studies are closest to ours. First, Tang (2019) studies whether P2P lending is a substitute or a complement of banks. She <sup>fi</sup>nds that, in areas in which a regulatory change causes a reduction in credit supply, there is an increase in lower quality P2P loan applications. This suggests that P2P loans can be considered as substitutes for bank loans in terms of borrowers creditworthiness. The reduction in local credit means that lower quality borrowers might be refused by their local banks, which implies that, in markets with less competition (and, therefore, less credit supply), lower credit quality borrowers may seek out P2P loans. In contrast, we study the role of local bank competition in driving online marketplace borrowers to re<sup>fi</sup>nance their loans, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally those that were initially refused by these same local banking markets. Despite the fact that marketplace borrowers have utilized a platform that seemingly transcends geographic boundaries, local banking competition creates incentives that drive geographically diverse outcomes for these same marketplace borrowers. As such, contrary to Tang (2019), the stakeholders of our research question are traditional banks and regulators who need to fully incorporate the interplay between online marketplace borrowers and local bank competition. In another related study, Butler et al. (2016) explores the impact of access to local funds on the issuance of P2P loans. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, they study how local market competition affects the terms of P2P loans during a period when Prosper adopted auctions as its funding mechanism. In contrast, we examine the role of local banking competition in driving disparate outcomes for bor rowers after the online borrowers have obtained their loans.

Our study also contributes to the extensive <sup>fi</sup>nance literature on access to local <sup>fi</sup>nancing opportunities for households and small businesses. Recent regulations eliminated several important restrictions that prevented large banks from opening branches in certain locations. Much of the literature, therefore, explores whether the regulations blurred the geographic boundaries within which market competition (among banks) is de<sup>fi</sup>ned. For example, despite the regulatory changes, Elliehausen and Wolken (1992), Kwast et al. (1997), and Amel and Starr-McCluer (2002) draw on household surveys on consumer <sup>fi</sup>nance usage and <sup>fi</sup>nd that households continue to rely on their local banks for their <sup>fi</sup>nancial needs. In another study, Cyrnak and Hannan (1999) reinforce these <sup>fi</sup>ndings with evidence from the market of small business loans. In contrast, Petersen and Rajan (2002) <sup>fi</sup>nd that the distance between small businesses and their credit providers is increasing. We complement this strand of literature by studying a new, emerging consumer credit segment: marketplace lending. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that borrowers who obtain funds from this online channel continue to utilize local funding in their debt management. More importantly, our results demonstrate that geographic boundaries continue to impact individuals’ funding and debt management decisions.

We are also related to an extensive literature in information systems (IS) that studies the dynamics between online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels (see, e.g., Brynjolfsson et al. 2009, Forman et al. 2009, Zhou et al. 2020). First, much of this literature focuses on whether the online channel is a complement or a substitute for the corresponding of<sup>fl</sup>ine channel. In the crowdfunding literature beyond marketplace lending, Kim and Hann (2019) <sup>fi</sup>nd that a decrease in housing prices (which impacts the potential for collateral to be used in mortgage applications) leads to an increase in crowdfunding projects.<sup>10</sup> We, in contrast, focus on providing empirical evidence that the of<sup>fl</sup>ine channel responds to the rise of an emerging online channel. Second, our paper extends this literature by studying a <sup>fi</sup>nancial service: consumer credit provision. The consumer credit market has special characteristics to which the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from the literature in IS do not apply. For example, most of the consumer credit providers often consider existing personal loans as assets and offer opportunities to re<sup>fi</sup>nance these loans. Moreover, because marketplace lending is a credit provider itself, it poses speci<sup>fi</sup>c geography-dependent, regulatory challenges. Therefore, this line of research is unique in the literature studying the dynamics between online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels.

## 3. Peer-to-Peer Marketplace Lending and Debt Management

P2P marketplace lending is considered a revolutionary form of consumer credit and an internet-based alternative to traditional, local lending institutions (Morse 2015). P2P lending platforms primarily facilitate the direct transaction between borrowers and (individual or institutional) investors. A typical funding process on P2P lending platforms is as follows. First, a potential borrower places a personal loan request on the platform after some basic checks that the platform completes in the background. In many P2P lending platforms today, platforms assign an interest rate based on the borrower’s history with the platform and the borrower’s credit information.<sup>11</sup> In the request, the borrower chooses the loan amount and describes the purpose (e.g., debt consolidation, home improvement, etc.). In addition to the basic information about the loan, the borrower needs to provide some nonveri<sup>fi</sup>ed information (such as income and homeownership) and, more importantly, the borrower’s credit information obtained from credit bureaus. Typical credit variables include debt-to-income ratio, concurrent credit lines, delinquencies in the past, etc. As soon as the request is listed on a P2P lending platform, interested investors can decide whether and how much to lend to this particular borrower. Investors generally fund only a portion of the loan. Most investors lend fewer than \$500 dollars in one P2P loan. Once the request is funded within a prespeci<sup>fi</sup>ed period (usually 7 or 14 days), it transfers to a fully amortized, unsecured personal loan. The borrower then receives the funds and starts the typical paying process. P2P lending platforms usually take some service fees before transferring the payment from the borrower to investors.

P2P lending platforms generally allow the borrower to pay off the loan before the maturity date without punishment. Because the focus of this study is on early payoff, we de<sup>fi</sup>ne it as a scenario in which the borrower pays back the remaining principal and all accruing interests well before the maturity date of the loan. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, our main de<sup>fi</sup>nition requires that the early payoff has to happen (1) after the sixth payment but before half of all payments made and (2) with more than 35% of the principal unpaid.<sup>12</sup> We adopt these restrictions because some P2P borrowers may utilize the platforms to improve their credit scores strategically (e.g., by obtaining a P2P loan and paying it back immediately). Moreover, we require the last payment to be a one-time, large, lump-sum payment so that it is more likely funded by a re<sup>fi</sup>nancing agent.

## 4. Data and Samples

We combine data from multiple sources in our empirical analysis.<sup>13</sup> Our main P2P lending data were obtained from LendingClub.com. Lending Club is the largest P2P marketplace lending platform in the United States, having facilitated the funding of more than three million personal loans by June 2018. The data set contains all funded loans on Lending Club since its inception in 2007. Our main sample covers a period between January 2011 and March 2014. We removed the loans from the early stage of the platform to control for constant policy and regulatory changes that significantly affected the funding process on marketplace lending platforms. We do not examine more recent data because Lending Club stopped providing the borrower’s city on March 31, 2014. We also excluded the loans that had not matured by the date of our data analysis. Consequently, there are, in total, 165,780 personal loans in our main sample.

For each personal loan in our main sample, we observe the borrower’s city (which we match to the relevant county), the loan funding amount, the borrower’s income, the borrower’s FICO score, homeownership, and other credit information collected from FICO (e.g., debt-to-income ratio, the number of delinquencies, current credit lines, etc.). Based on the borrower’s credit information and history of borrowing on Lending Club, the platform assigns a lettered credit grade to indicate the riskiness; these range from A1 (least risky) to G5 (riskiest). In addition, we also observe the monthly payments made by each borrower. Table 1 provides summary statistics of the variables.

We obtain variables from multiple sources to measure the local banking market structure. From the business pattern data managed by the U.S. Census Bureau, we obtain the number of lending institutions in a county in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c year. Local lending institutions in the Census Bureau de<sup>fi</sup>nition include commercial banks, savings institutions, and credit unions.<sup>14</sup> From the FDIC, we obtain the number of FDIC-insured bank branches, a widely adopted measure in the literature (Becker 2007, Butler and Cornaggia 2011). For each branch, we obtain the total dollar amount of deposits (including checking, savings, and money market accounts as in Becker 2007).

Based on the FDIC deposit data, we also calculated the HHI as a measure of local market concentration. 15 HHI is widely adopted by government agencies, such as the FDIC, Department of Justice (DOJ), and the Federal Trade Commission as a standard measure of market concentration. Therefore, it is used to monitor the potential impacts of M&As on market competition. In the banking industry, FDIC speci<sup>fi</sup>es geographic boundaries when de<sup>fi</sup>ning markets. These boundaries are collected from the Competitive Analysis and Structure Source Instrument for Depository Institutions (CASSI-DI). The Federal Reserve and DOJ use this tool to evaluate competition among banks. The rule of thumb is that a market with an HHI below 1,000 is considered “low concentration,” meaning the deposits are more evenly distributed across banks, and an HHI over 1,800 indicates that the market has “high concentration.”<sup>16</sup> From the Federal Financial Institutions Examination Council (FFIEC), we also obtain data on small business lending to provide additional empirical evidence in support of our proposed mechanism.

We also obtain a rich set of variables from a variety of sources as controls of the local market conditions. We <sup>fi</sup>rst follow the literature by incorporating demographic variables, such as race, origin (or ethnicity), and education attainment obtained from the U.S. Census Bureau. In particular, Anderson and VanderHoff (1999), Ambrose and Capone (1998), and Pedersen and Delgadillo (2007) all document the role of race in debt management. To control for this, we calculate the proportion of residents who identify themselves as Black or African American (Race: Black) and the proportion of Hispanic residents (Origin: Hispanic). Second, one may argue that the heterogeneity in the literacy of <sup>fi</sup>nancial management may contribute to the variation in borrowers seeking re<sup>fi</sup>nancing across counties. To control for that, we calculate the proportion of the population with a four-year college degree in business in a county. We assume that borrowers from a county with a larger fraction of such population will have a better understanding of the trade-off between risk and return and is, hence, more likely to have a higher ability of debt management (e.g., more likely to utilize the local fund to re<sup>fi</sup>nance existing loans).

In addition, we obtain multiple variables from the BLS and HUD to control for the overall economic condition in a county. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, based on the unemployment rate data, we calculate the % change unem ployment.<sup>17</sup> We include this variable to control for the overall economic environment (either improving or deteriorating) because, arguably, the overall economic environment has an impact on a borrower’s ability to pay off a loan. A borrower has better access to local funds if the economic environment is generally improving in the county. From Zillow.com, we obtain monthly home value index (the Zillow home value index) data and calculate the % change housing index. This is the percentage change in the housing index from six months before obtaining a loan to 18 months after. It is analogous to the calculation used for the % change unemployment. We also obtain the value of capi tal gains reported by residents in a county on their IRS tax returns.<sup>18</sup> We calculate the percentage gain as well (from six months before a loan to18 months af ter). We refer to this variable as % change capital gains.

As another measure of the overall economic condition, we control for the Poverty rate in the county. To control for a borrower’s overall ability to pay back personal loans, we calculate Income/cost of living as the ratio of the borrower’s income and the fair market rent of a two-bedroom property ( borrower income/ fair market rent of a two-bedroom property). In addition, to account for potential disparities in the population served per lending institution, we calculate the Lender–population ratio as the number of lending institutions (from the business pattern data by the Census) divided by the total population. We also include Population density (population in thousands per square mile) to capture the potential differences in debt management between urban and rural populations.

## 5.1. Empirical Evidence

Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we borrow from Goetz et al. (2016) and adopt a similar IV to instrument for the endogenous banking market structure. In our speci<sup>fi</sup>c context, a valid IV needs to be correlated with the local banking market structure (number of lending institutions, deposits, etc.) and conditionally exogenous to the early payoff behavior of marketplace borrowers during our study period (between 2011 and 2014). To construct our IV, we capitalize on the fact that the local banking market structure during our sample period is a result of bank branch expansion decisions in the past. Most prominently, the literature establishes that these decisions are impacted by the geographic distance from banks’ headquarters (Goetz et al. 2013) and local regulatory environments (Rice and Strahan 2010). Therefore, the geographic distance to banks’ headquarters and historic state-level regulatory conditions likely determine the local market structure in a particular location, that is, the relevance of the IV. However, it is unlikely that the geographic distance (from banks’ headquarters) and/or state-level interstate banking regulation changes (most of which occurred prior to 2007) impact marketplace borrowers decisions to pay off their loans early during our period except through their impact on the local market structure—the exogeneity and exclusion restriction of the IV. We next provide a full exposition of the instrument construction and note that a variant of this instrument has been used previously in related contexts (Goetz et al. 2016, Jiang et al. 2019, Levine et al. 2020).

The IV we utilize is the predicted relative total local bank deposits available to marketplace borrowers. The prediction of deposits is based on two components: branch distance to headquarters and state regulations. The <sup>fi</sup>rst component of our IV is based upon the Goetz et al. (2013, 2016) <sup>fi</sup>nding that banks are more likely to expand into markets that are geographically closer to their headquarters. This conclusion is also supported by other studies that have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the role of geographic distance in the banking industry (Degryse and Ongena 2005, Agarwal and Hauswald 2010, Aguirregabiria et al. 2016). We <sup>fi</sup>rst use this relationship to estimate a model predicting a bank’s deposits from each county as a share of its total deposits. We use the following speci<sup>fi</sup>cation:

$$
\text { Deposit   } \text { Share } _ {b, c} = \beta_ {1} \ln \left(\frac {\text { Population } _ {c}}{\text { Population } _ {b , c}}\right) + \beta_ {2} \ln \left(\text { Distance } _ {b, c}\right) + \epsilon_ {b, c},\tag{2}
$$

where Deposit $S h a r e _ { b , c }$ is the proportion of bank $b ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ total deposits that are attributed to branches in county c and $D i s t a n c e _ { b , c }$ is the distance between bank b’s home county and county c. Note that this variable has a value of zero if c refers to bank b’s home county. Popula-$t i o n _ { b , c }$ is the population in the county of the bank’s headquarters, and Population is the population in the target county. Therefore, $P o p u l a t i o \bar { n } _ { c } / \bar { P } o p u l a t i o n _ { b , c }$ is the population size of the target county c relative to the bank’s headquarter county. This speci<sup>fi</sup>cation (Equation (2)) formalizes the idea that, controlling for the market size, banks prefer to open branches closer to their headquarters. For example, a bank with headquarters in New York City, when considering two equally sized markets in Connecticut and Illinois, would prefer to expand to the former state. In addition, the <sup>fi</sup>rst component of the right-hand side of Equation (2) takes into account the (relative) population size of the target county.

We estimate the parameters of Model (2), using data from 2007, for bank holding companies with assets greater than \$10 billion.<sup>20</sup> Using the estimated coef<sup>fi</sup>cients, we predict Deposit $S h a r e _ { b , c , y }$ for each county and year combination between 2011 and 2014 (our main sample period). It is important to note that, because Model (2) only considers geographic distance to headquarters and (relative) market size, other factors that are relevant to the expansion decision are not included. For example, a bank may decide to open a branch in a location because the population of that county exhibits risk tendencies that the bank values, but Deposit $S h a r e _ { b , c , y }$ would not capture this effect.

Then, we use the actual total deposits held by each bank during our sample years (Total $D e p o s i t s _ { b , y } )$ in conjunction with our predicted deposit share in county $c \ ( D e p o s i t \ S h a r e _ { b , c , y } \ )$ ) to calculate the estimated deposits that bank b has in county c in year y as $\begin{array} { r } { D e p o s i t s _ { b , c , y } = D e p o s i t S h a r e _ { b , c , y } } \end{array}$ Total $D e p o s i t s _ { b , y }$ . Finally, to compute the total predicted deposits in each county, we sum all bank deposits in each county and obtain $\begin{array} { r } { \widehat { D e p o s i t s _ { c , y } } = \sum _ { b } \widehat { D e p o s i t } s _ { b , c , y } . } \end{array}$ , which is the <sup>fi</sup>rst component of our IV.

Importantly, $D e p o s i t s _ { c , y }$ only uses the relative market size and distance to all possible bank headquarters to predict the total deposits in each county. Prior literature suggests that this variable satis<sup>fi</sup>es the relevance condition that it is correlated with the endogenous variable in our context, that is, the market competition structure in our sample period. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, it is likely that a market with more lending institutions has more (predicted) total deposits.<sup>21</sup> Regarding the exogeneity of a valid IV, we argue that the unobserved factors that impact marketplace borrowers’ early payoff decisions are unlikely to be correlated with the predicted total deposits considering the geographic distance to the banks’ headquarters. For example, although a marketplace borrower’s unobserved risk preference (as a potential omitted component of the error term in Model (1)) may impact the borrower’s probability to pay off the loan early, the risk preference is unlikely to be correlated with the geographic distance of the borrower’s home county to the local banks’ headquarters. Finally, when a borrower makes the decision whether to pay off the marketplace debt early, the borrower is unlikely to be taking into account the geographic distance of the borrower’s home county to all local banks’ headquarters. Therefore, we argue that the IV we propose satis<sup>fi</sup>es the exclusion restriction of a valid IV.

One potential issue that prior literature has identi<sup>fi</sup>ed with Model (2) is that it does not account for the fact that banks with headquarters closer to the states with more interstate restrictions may have been historically limited in their expansion efforts. Therefore, we further incorporate the effect of interstate banking regulations into our instrumental variable (Goetz et al. 2016). Although the Interstate Banking and Branching Ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency Act of 1994 removed most remaining federal restrictions on interstate banking, states are still able to implement their own legislations. According to Rice and Strahan (2010), states could limit interstate branches (whether through mergers or branch openings) by utilizing one of the following four approaches: (1) setting requirements on the minimum age of institutions for acquisitions, (2) allowing de novo interstate branching, (3) interstate branching by acquisition of a single branch or a portion of an institution, and (4) statewide deposit caps on branch acquisition.

We follow Celerier and Matray (2019), Goetz et al. (2016), and Rice and Strahan (2010) and incorporate the historical interstate banking restrictions. Operationally, for each state–year combination from 1994 to 2007, we construct an annual deregulation index, ranging from zero to four, where zero means that the state had implemented all regulatory conditions listed and four means that the state was completely deregulated. We sum up the values of the deregulation indicator in all years (1994 to 2007) for each state s and normalize $D e r e g u l a t i o n _ { s }$ to a (0, 1) scale. A value closer to one indicates the state is less regulated, and a value closer to zero indicates the state is highly regulated. We factor this state regulation component into our IV for the local <sup>fi</sup>nancial market structure:

$$
I V _ {c, s, y} = \widehat {\text {Deposits}} _ {c, y} * \text {Deregulation} _ {s}.\tag{3}
$$

Bank Expansion $I V _ { c , s , y }$ predicts the level of deposits a bank has in a county based upon the distance to the bank’s home county. It also incorporates the fact that these historical expansion decisions are limited by differences in state-level interstate banking regulations. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, Deregulation discounts the predicted deposits to account for potential regulatory restrictions. Similar to $D e p o s i t s _ { c , y }$ satisfying the exclusion restriction, Deregulation is unlikely to impact marketplace borrower’s debt management decisions, especially given that our sample period is between 2011 and 2014.

5.1.3. Evidence from Leveraging Dodd–Frank Supervisory Stress Test Regulation Changes. In this section, we examine exogenous shocks to local bank lending attributed to regulatory changes in light of the endogeneity concerns. In response to the economic downturn from 2007 to 2009, referred to as the “Great Recession”, the United States Congress enacted the Dodd–Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act (the Dodd–Frank Act hereafter). This act requires the U.S. Federal Reserve to conduct annual stress tests of the largest banks to examine whether they have enough capital to sustain operations in the case of extreme adverse economic conditions. Although these stress tests are designed to improve the long-term stability of <sup>fi</sup>- nancial markets, they have been documented to have a negative effect on the lending volume and rates offered by the stress-tested institutions.<sup>25</sup> These reductions are a consequence of banks reducing lending to comply with the minimum capital ratios needed to pass the stress tests.

The <sup>fi</sup>rst Dodd–Frank Act Stress Test (DFAST), whose outcomes were released in March 2013, was applied to 18 bank holding companies with assets more than \$100 billion. Because of subsequent regulatory changes, the minimum threshold decreased from \$100 billion to \$50 billion after the March 2013 report. In the March 2014 DFAST report, the results of stress tests on the 30 bank holding companies that met the revised minimum threshold of \$50 billion were reported. Because of the change in the threshold, the lending of the 12 banks newly subject to the Act (not stress tested in 2013 but stress tested in 2014) arguably decreased (Acharya et al. 2018, Cortes et al.´ 2020). To further examine the impact of local market competition on the debt management behavior of marketplace borrowers, we exploit these regulatory changes in the minimum threshold for the stress tests (\$100 billion to \$50 billion) as an exogenous shock on local market competition.

If the local market structure has an impact on local banks’ response to the marketplace alternative, we would expect a reduction in marketplace borrowers’ early payoff rate in areas in which the negative impact of stress testing is highest. Arguably, banks that are subject to stress testing because of the change in policy have to reduce their lending (to ensure that they meet stress test requirements) and likely increase the interest rates offered. As a result, they are less likely to successfully compete for marketplace borrowers even if those borrowers may have improved their credit pro<sup>fi</sup>les.

## 5.2. Supplementary Analysis

5.2.1. A Placebo Test: The Effects of Local Market Structure on On-Time Payment. Our analysis thus far has focused on borrowers who pay off their loans early with large, one-time payments. We focus on large, one-time payments because they are more likely representative of borrowers’ re<sup>fi</sup>nancing activities. In contrast, borrowers who make equal payments over the loan term are unlikely to utilize the local market for re<sup>fi</sup>nancing because their payments are small relative to the total loan size. Therefore, conditional on the controls, we should not see a statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationship between local market structure and whether a borrower pays a loan with equal payments. Again, this is because these payments do not require that the borrower obtains a large amount of money to pay off the loan.

Operationally, we consider a borrower as Paid on time if the borrower made the exact number of contracted payments, each at the value of the original contract.<sup>26</sup> That is, these borrowers did not default, but they never made a large, one-time payment. If we <sup>fi</sup>nd that, even after controlling for borrower, local, and economic level changes, the level of banking com petition impacts the probability that a borrower pays a loan on time, we have reasons to doubt the credibility of our main <sup>fi</sup>ndings. However, if we do not, this lends evidence that our results are driven by the fact that the borrowers in more competitive markets utilized these markets to obtain a large, one-time payment to re<sup>fi</sup>nance their loans. The results indicate that the number of local lending institutions does not have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect on the likelihood of paying back a personal loan on time without a large one-time payment.<sup>27</sup>

5.2.2. Specification Tests and Robustness Checks. We conduct several robustness checks of our main speci<sup>fi</sup>cation (results reported in Online Appendix A2). First, we estimate Equation (1) using a logit regression, and the results are consistent with the Probit speci<sup>fi</sup>cation (Online Tables A7 and A8). Second, we repeat the same tests on Prosper’s data set. Prosper is the second largest and the <sup>fi</sup>rst marketplace lending platform in the United States. Similar to our main speci<sup>fi</sup>cation, we obtain all Prosper loans funded between January 2011 and March 2014. The results are also qualitatively the same as the results from the Lending Club sample (Online Table A9).

As the third set of robustness checks, we examine our de<sup>fi</sup>nition of early payoff. The main de<sup>fi</sup>nition requires that an early payoff happens after the sixth payment but before half of all payments are made and with more than 35% of the principal unpaid. We repeat our regressions in Table 2 with three extensions: (1) early payoff happens after the third payment but before half of all payments are made, (2) early payoff happens with more than 45% of the principal unpaid, and (3) early payoff happens with more than 55% of the principal unpaid. The results are consistent with our main <sup>fi</sup>ndings in all three extensions (Online Table A10).

Finally, we use an alternative measure of market competition. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, in addition to the number of lending institutions (or FDIC-insured bank branches), we consider the number of employees in these lending institutions. We estimate the total number of employees using data from the business pattern data by the Census Bureau.<sup>28</sup> Then, we repeat our estimation of Equation (1) using this estimated number of employees in all lending institutions as the alternative measure of local market competition. Not surprisingly, the results are consistent with our main <sup>fi</sup>ndings (reported in the last column of Online Table A10).

## 5.3. Evidence Supporting the Underlying Mechanism

Our results suggest that marketplace borrowers with access to a more competitive banking market are more likely to pay off their marketplace loans early. In this section, we provide evidence supporting the underlying mechanism that higher competition leads to environments in which local banks are more incentivized to respond to marketplace lending. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we provide evidence that local markets have incentives to exploit competitive interest rates (Section 5.3.1), more actively expand the customer base (Section 5.3.2), and that marketplace borrowers do utilize local lending options (Sections 5.3.3 and 5.3.4).

5.3.1. Interest Rate Experiment. We explore the underlying mechanism related to banks in more competitive markets incentivized to offer superior rates by exploiting Lending Club’s interest-setting mechanism. LC grades are assigned to borrowers by the LC proprietary formula and used to assign interest rates to borrowers. The interest rate assigned to each grade is assigned by the LC Interest Rate Committee.<sup>29</sup> The committee assigns interest rates based on four criteria: (1) general economic conditions; (2) the balance of funds and demand for credit through the LC platform, considering whether borrowing requests exceed investor commitments; (3) the estimated default likelihood of loans in the category; and (4) consumer credit rates by other lending platforms and major <sup>fi</sup>nancial institutions. Among the criteria, the second implies that the interest rate changes are potentially a result of platform-speci<sup>fi</sup>c differences that are not driven by local market conditions.

At each speci<sup>fi</sup>c point in time, Lending Club assigns the same interest rate for all loans of the same grade. These borrowers receive the same interest rate because, as they were assigned the same grade, LC deems their credit risk to be equivalent. Lending Club’s grade-level interest rate changes provide us a unique opportunity to further investigate the underlying mechanism. Consider two borrowers who are assigned the same grade but obtained their loans before and after the date of interest rate changes, respectively. Therefore, although the borrowers are comparable based on credit characteristics (assuming they apply for loans within a short time window), one is assigned a higher interest rate by the platform.

Rationally, local banks would be more successful in attracting marketplace borrowers who are charged a relatively higher interest rate because of the exogenous intervention in determining interest rates charged for their marketplace loans. Under our proposed mechanism, this success is even more likely in more competitive markets where rates offered by the local banks are relatively lower. In other words, the testable prediction is that increases in interest rate (because of changes in Lending Club’s rate assignment considerations) positively moderate the effect of local market competition on individual borrowers’ likelihood of early payoff. Findings that are consistent with this prediction provide evidence that the positive relationship we <sup>fi</sup>nd between local market competition and the rate that marketplace borrowers pay off their loans early with a large one-time payment is driven by borrowers’ utilizing local re<sup>fi</sup>nancing options.

Operationally, for each personal loan, we generate a new dummy variable Interest rate (1/0). A loan is assigned a value of one if the loan was (1) funded within a three-month window before or after the associated increase in the interest rate and (2) funded at the higher interest rate. Otherwise, Interest Rate (1/ 0) is assigned a value of zero. To ensure that loans are comparable in the analysis, we remove loans if Interest rate (1/0) equals zero and the loans are funded outside the three-month window.<sup>30</sup> We estimate our main results for this sample of impacted loans and include an interaction term between Local lending institutions and Interest rate (1/0). The results are reported in Table 3.

First, consistent with our main <sup>fi</sup>ndings, the coef-<sup>fi</sup>cient for the number of local lending institutions is, again, positive and statistically sound. On top of that, the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of the interaction term is also positive and statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, which is consistent with our prediction and lends further support to the underlying mechanism.

5.3.2. Evidence from FICO Score Improvements. Our proposed mechanism contends that banks in more competitive markets have greater incentives to compete for marketplace borrowers. One obstacle facing traditional banks is minimum FICO score requirements. Banks often utilize near-prime credit scores (high 600s to low 700s) as the exclusion criteria for offering personal loans.<sup>31</sup> Therefore, potential borrowers that do not meet this criteria must seek alternative <sup>fi</sup> nancing. This is supported by the data: in 2013, approximately 62.5% of LC borrowers had FICO scores lower than 700, and 32% had FICO scores lower than 680. Borrowers who utilize the online marketplace may, as a result, increase their credit scores to near prime or prime levels, becoming potential marketing targets for traditional banks. This potential is highlighted by the fact that the majority of marketplace borrowers use their loans to re<sup>fi</sup>nance existing credit card debt, which would have been provided by traditional lending institutions.

Therefore, if more competitive local markets are more incentivized to respond to online marketplace lending, changing the unsecured lending landscape, this effect should be positively moderated by increases in FICO scores. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, borrowers whose FICO scores increase beyond the traditional cutoffs (i.e., 700) should be most impacted by having access to more competitive local lending opportunities. Fortunately, our data from Lending Club provide a unique opportunity to test this prediction (lending support to the proposed mechanism). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, for each personal loan on Lending Club, we observe changes in the borrower’s FICO score throughout the loan period. Therefore, we can determine whether, and by how much, the borrower improved their credit score during the payment process. We examine the change in borrower FICO score from the time the loan request was initially made to the 12th (20th) month of a 36 (60) term loan.<sup>32</sup> This corresponds to one third of the loan timeline and approximately the time point we begin evaluating whether a borrower pays off the loan early. If the increase in FICO score is greater than or equal to 40 points, we assign a FICO increase dummy variable to be one for the loan (and zero otherwise). We argue that such an improvement in credit scores is suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to have access to better terms from local lending institutions. Based on the construct, we modify our main speci<sup>fi</sup>cation by incorporating the FICO increase label, 1(FICO increase), as a moderator.

Given the importance of the near-prime cutoff (high 600s to low 700s) for local banks’ personal loan decisions, we split our sample of borrowers into two groups based on whether their initial FICO score was below/above 700. The regression results are reported in Table 3. We conduct the analysis on two separate subsamples. Column (2) reports the regression results based on the subsample of borrowers who had a FICO score lower than 700 at the time when the loan was funded. Column (3) repeats the regression on other borrowers (who had a FICO score greater than 700 at the time of funding). Arguably, if the prediction is true, then a borrower with a lower initial FICO score bene<sup>fi</sup>ts even more from the credit score improvement than a borrower with a higher initial score. Indeed, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that, <sup>fi</sup>rst, the coef<sup>fi</sup>cients for the number of local lending institutions are positive and statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in both regressions. More importantly, the interaction term is positive and statistically sound in the subsample that had lower initial scores (column (2)). As expected, for the borrowers with a FICO score greater than 700, we fail to <sup>fi</sup>nd a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant moderating effect of credit score improvement on our main effect (of local market competition on a borrower’s likelihood of early payoff) as in column (3) of Table 3.<sup>33</sup> These results are overall consistent with the prediction and lend support to the underlying mechanism.

## 6. The Impact of Bank M&As on Marketplace Borrowers

## 6.1. Bank M&As and Early Payoff

We show that the local market structure has a signi<sup>fi</sup>- cant impact on marketplace borrowers’ debt management behavior. To further examine the potential policy implications of this relationship, we study the role of M&As between banks on marketplace borrowers’ debt management behavior. M&As between banks are shown to have an unambiguous impact on the market structure of the banking industry, which, in turn, may affect the supply and demand of consumer credit. For example, Fraisse et al. (2018) <sup>fi</sup>nd that the overall bank lending decreases by approximately 2.7% in areas with the presence of branches from two merging banks. Liebersohn (2017) <sup>fi</sup>nds that bank M&As increase the deposit rate by about 0.13 percentage point. Nguyen (2019) uses bank M&As as an instrumental variable for branch closures and <sup>fi</sup>nds that bank closures lead to a decline in the supply of consumer credits.

Bank M&As are often evaluated by regulators based on their impact on geographically de<sup>fi</sup>ned local markets. However, as more banking activities migrate to the online channel, it is important to examine how bank M&As impact marketplace borrowers. Given that the online channel transcends geographic boundaries, marketplace borrowers are generally overlooked when M&As are evaluated by regulators. However, if the online borrowers are also affected by the M&A induced changes to the local market competition, these borrowers must also be considered.

We obtain the data of bank M&As from the FDIC. We focus on all M&As in our study period (January 2011–April 2014) in which both participating banks had assets greater than \$100 million. ## 6.2. Bank M&As and Branch Closures

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that bank mergers have a significant impact on marketplace borrowers. Importantly, marketplace lending, in general, is often seen as an avenue to democratize access to credit.

This is especially critical as bank restructuring has resulted in many smaller cities, which are very dependent on local lending, to become banking deserts. As such, we extend our analysis and study how branch closures resulting from bank M&A activity impact the propensity of marketplace borrowers to utilize the local market to re<sup>fi</sup>nance their loans. Following Nguyen (2019) and because of the issues surrounding banking deserts, we conduct this analysis at the smallest geographic level available to us, the city level.<sup>35</sup>

Because the M&A branch closures analysis seeks to examine areas with a small number of local lending institutions, we <sup>fi</sup>rst assess the heterogeneity of our main results with respect to market size. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we are interested in understanding the heterogeneity of our results relative to borrowers with very few local lending options. We replicate the procedure from Section 5.2.2, in which we utilize CEM matching, except that we focus only on borrowers from counties with fewer than the median number of local lending institutions (250 lending institutions; recall in Section 5.2.2, we refer to this as the Low market competition sample). We split this sample in half based on the 25th percentile of local lending institutions (94 lending institutions). We construct a dummy variable called Lending institutions > 25th percentile (1/0), which is assigned a value of one if the borrower is from a county with more than 94 lending institutions and zero otherwise. ## 7. Concluding Remarks

Online marketplace lending has clearly established itself as a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant force in the unsecured personal loan market. The online channel can transcend geographic boundaries, potentially equalizing access to consumer credit regardless of borrower locations. These platforms offer borrowers, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally those previously unviable to local lenders, an opportunity to consolidate their debts and improve their <sup>fi</sup>nancial situation. Initially, local lending markets might overlook these borrowers because of subprime credit and/ or the fact that they carry more lucrative loan products from the same institution (e.g., credit cards that generally charge higher interest rates than personal loans). As these borrowers’ risk pro<sup>fi</sup>les improve, their creditworthiness to local lending markets changes. Because local <sup>fi</sup>nancial market competition dictates geographically diverse offerings of rates for re<sup>fi</sup>nancing and marketing efforts by traditional banks, the bene <sup>fi</sup>ts derived from the online marketplace by borrowers consequently become unequal.

Because borrowers who utilize local lenders in the <sup>fi</sup>rst place already bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the market competition, on the margin, they are less likely to bene<sup>fi</sup>t from re<sup>fi</sup>nancing and local competition. This is also because, unlike the marketplace borrowers, they are more likely to be prime borrowers to begin with and have little room to improve creditworthiness. Consequently, the marketplace, particularly through debt consolidation and improved credit, expands the serviceable borrower segments for local banks. Therefore, regulators, especially when considering local competitive issues, such as the evaluation of the antitrust implications of proposed M&As, would be ill-informed absent a thorough treatment of incumbent banks’ response to the changing dynamics because of marketplace lending.

To study this issue, we use detailed loan-level data from the largest personal loans marketplace lending platform in the United States. By combining the rich data set with an extensive set of market competition measures (together with controls at the local market level), we <sup>fi</sup>nd that local market structure impacts the incentives of traditional consumer credit providers to respond to the rise of marketplace lending. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, a borrower from a more competitive market is more likely to pay off a marketplace debt early with a large, onetime payment. Numerically, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that, if the number of local lending institutions increases from the 25th to the 75th percentile, the probability of early payoff increases from 18.3% to 19.7%. This indicates a 1.4 percentage point increase and an increase of 7.7% ( 1.4%/ 18.3%). The required change in borrower income to achieve the analogous increase would be \$75,000 to \$225,000. Notably, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that this effect is more pronounced for borrowers who utilized the online lending platform to signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly improve their <sup>fi</sup>nancial pro<sup>fi</sup>les (an initial FICO score lower than 700 with a large subsequent increase in FICO score). To address potential endogeneity issues, we utilize an instrumental variable method, a coarsened exact matching, as well as Dodd–Frank initiated regulatory changes to the stress testing criteria of banks.

We also provide extensive evidence of the underlying mechanism: higher market competition leads to greater incentives for banks to respond to marketplace lending. Supporting this mechanism, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the local market competition does not have predictive power in anticipating marketplace borrowers paying off their debt on time and without a large, one-time payment. Similarly, marketplace borrowers with higher initial interest rates assigned are more likely to pay off their loans early. Moreover, we also <sup>fi</sup>nd that repeat marketplace borrowers who paid off their previous loans have higher utilization of local credit markets. Finally, our evidence based on the small business lending data obtained from the FFIEC also lends support to the proposed mechanism. Online Table A11 summarizes all our empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

For bank stakeholders, it is encouraging that banks are able to successfully respond to and leverage the rise of marketplace lending. In particular, they are converting borrowers who have demonstrated creditworthiness and may have been too risky initially. Nevertheless, this path of gaining credit market share through re<sup>fi</sup>nancing is not necessarily a net market expansion for banks. This is because the majority of marketplace loan borrowers are consolidating credit card debts, which are primarily issued by traditional banks. Although banks are losing customers in the credit card segment to marketplace platforms, they may recoup some of these losses when the market conditions provide the necessary incentives for local banks to respond. In fact, our analysis reveals that marketplace borrowers who obtain loans for debt consolidation have a higher likelihood to pay off their loans early through a large one-time payment.

Our analysis of bank M&As provides important policy implications. Traditionally, the impact of mergers on the incentives of local banks to respond to online marketplace lending is not considered by regulators. This is especially important given the extensive regulatory concerns regarding the speci<sup>fi</sup>c process through which online personal loans are initialized (i.e., in Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) v. CashCall et al. in 2019, the CFPB argued that the intermediate bank is the true lender and not the peers). As such, there is debate surrounding which market Fin-Tech lenders comprise, but at the same time, regulators generally use local markets to assess antitrust implications of mergers, which we demonstrate should adjust because of the connection between local bank competition and the disparity in marketplace borrower bene<sup>fi</sup>ts.

Although we provide ample evidence of the importance of local bank competition in driving the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts derived by online marketplace borrowers, investigating further nuances regarding this relationship is important. For example, one may explore heterogeneity of bank types in terms of how market competition changes their incentives to respond. Furthermore, we call for future studies to explore similar questions in other FinTech-based markets, such as the response of traditional payment companies (e.g., credit card issuers) to the rise of social payment or the response of traditional trading companies to the rise of social trading.
