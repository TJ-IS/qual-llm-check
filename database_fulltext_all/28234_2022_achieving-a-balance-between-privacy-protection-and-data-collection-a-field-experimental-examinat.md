# Achieving a Balance Between Privacy Protection and Data Collection: A Field Experimental Examination of a Theory-Driven Information Technology Solution

Abstract. Companies face a trade-off between creating stronger privacy protection policies for consumers and employing more sophisticated data collection methods. Justice-driven privacy protection outlines a method to manage this trade-off. We built on the theoretical lens of justice theory to integrate justice provision with key privacy protection features and conceptualized the extent to which these features affect privacy concerns and information disclosure behavior. Notably, we proposed an information technology (IT) solution to balance the trade-off between privacy protection and consumer data collection. In the context of mobile banking applications, we prototyped a theory-driven IT solution, referred to as negotiation, active-recommendation privacy policy application, which enables customer service agents to interact with and actively recommend personalized privacy policies to consumers. We benchmarked our solution through a <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment relative to two conventional applications: a non-negotiation privacy policy application (only a nonnegotiable privacy statement is posted) as a base method and a negotiation, non-active-recommendation privacy policy application (only a negotiation feature is integrated with the privacy policy). The results showed that the proposed negotiation, active-recommendation privacy policy application de creased privacy concerns and increased consumers’ information disclosure intentions and actual disclosure behavior. A post hoc analysis corroborated these <sup>fi</sup>ndings, indicating that our design enhanced perceived procedural justice, interactional justice, and distributive justice among consumers and made them feel comfortable to disclose their personal infor mation. Likewise, companies would be able to collect additional personal information from consumers, thereby contributing to a privacy-friendly environment. We discuss contributions and the implications of our proposed IT solution for consumers, companies, de velopers, and public policy of<sup>fi</sup>cials.

## 1. Introduction

Information privacy is considered one of the biggest issues in cyberspace (Chai et al. 2009, Smith et al. 2011) because of the ever-increasing collection and use of personal information by companies. Largely driven by the proliferation of mobile commerce, companies now have more direct access to consumers’ private data (e.g., real-time location, biometrics) than ever before, thus opening the door to privacy invasion (Gu et al. 2017). The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the Breach Level Index revealed that in the <sup>fi</sup>rst half of 2018, 944 data breaches led to almost 3.3 billion data records being compromised worldwide, an increase of 72% versus 2017 (Gemalto 2018). Data breaches raise privacy concerns that prevent consumers from disclosing their personal information, especially private information, thus preventing companies from obtaining and leveraging consumer data. Data collection must be balanced with protecting private information and consumer privacy; otherwise, companies’ credibility and abili ty to collect data from consumers would be jeopardized (Lyons et al. 2016).

Research has consistently shown that the perception of justice plays a crucial role in privacy protection (Son and Kim 2008, Lyons et al. 2016). Consumers who believe that their companies handle their data fairly are encouraged to disclose private information (Culnan and Armstrong 1999, Culnan and Bies 2003). Accordingly, if information privacy protection practices encouraged perceived justice, consumers would feel that their personal information was being treated fairly and their privacy concerns would thereby decrease (Lyons et al. 2016, Zhou 2016). Moreover, consumers would be more willing to share their personal information with companies (Li 2014), therefore allowing companies to collect and leverage consumer data.

Perceived justice re<sup>fl</sup>ects a set of fairness perceptions that have been widely used in the privacy literature (Xu et al. 2009, Zhao et al. 2012, Choi et al. 2016). Culnan and Bies (2003) identi<sup>fi</sup>ed three types of justice related to privacy, that is, procedural, interactional, and distributive justice. Procedural justice is concerned with the fairness of handling personal information; interactional justice captures the treatment of people by others; and distributive justice focuses on one’s perception of gain and loss. Together, these three justice types affect privacy concerns and information disclosure (Son and Kim 2008, Zhou 2016). Posting a privacy statement is the main privacy protection approach currently offered by most companies, and it is viewed as an approach that aims to instill a sense of procedural justice (Zhao et al. 2012, Liu 2014). However, a privacy statement is not effective in protecting people’s personal information because it lacks interactional and distributive justice, according to the justice theory. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, privacy statements are usually unidirectional (typically set by the company) and do not give consumers a chance to express their privacy preferences, plus they are often long, complex, and dif<sup>fi</sup>- cult to understand, especially on mobile devices (Meinert et al. 2006), thereby making consumers feel that reading privacy statements is a useless task. Accordingly, consumers have no other choice than to accept privacy statements with feelings of helplessness or refuse them and forego interactions with the company given the lack of perceived fairness of interaction. Second, privacy statements are the same for all consumers without considering their own privacy needs. Consumers who may not be very concerned about their privacy may be willing to provide private information in exchange for more personalized services. In contrast, consumers who are concerned about their privacy and want to provide the least information needed to use basic services are either forced to accept the privacy statement in its entirety or bail out, leading to feelings of unfairness between cost and bene<sup>fi</sup>t (lack of distributive fairness).

Some scholars suggest that, if a value of justice drives privacy protection approaches, making consumers feel their information is fairly treated, consumers will experience a higher level of trust and lower privacy concerns (Lyons et al. 2016, Zhou 2016). Companies understand that privacy protection often con<sup>fl</sup>icts with obtaining consumer information. They have little incentive to provide <sup>fl</sup>exible choices and individualized privacy agreements to consumers, leading to a failure to align consumer behavior with their stated preferences (Acquisti et al. 2017). In many cases, companies use privacy statements to abide by laws and regulations, rather than viewing them as a proactive privacy protection approach. However, as mentioned above, existing research has found that the sense of fairness motivates consumers to disclose their personal information. Thus, companies can improve the justice perceptions of their consumers by offering privacy protection mechanisms that may help close the gap between privacy protection and information collection.

In this paper, to better align consumer behavior with their stated preferences (Acquisti et al. 2017) while encouraging appropriate information disclosure, we build on the justice theory (e.g., Culnan and Bies 2003, Ashworth and Free 2006) to integrate privacy protection approach features with key justice provisions. We seek to identify and embed negotiation and activerecommendation features in privacy policies by integrating both interactive justice and distributive justice. A two-way negotiation feature would effectively miti gate privacy con<sup>fl</sup>ict between companies and consumers, enabling both sides to compare and discuss the privacy policy and seek a mutually-agreed solution (Preibusch 2006). Similarly, companies with an activerecommendation feature would be more likely to actively recommend personalized privacy policies to different consumers. We theorize that the provision of both negotiation and active-recommendation features would lead consumers to enjoy a lower level of privacy concerns and a higher information disclosure versus an online privacy statement and a privacy policy with only a simple negotiation (non-active-recommendation) feature.

Building upon these arguments, we propose and design a theory-driven information technology (IT) solution in a mobile banking context, considering the pri vate information often required by banks. Importantly, the small screen of mobile devices makes the design of our solution more challenging compared with online environments, such as PCs. The proposed theorydriven IT solution—negotiation, active-recommendation privacy policy application, aims to provide better procedural, interactive, and distributive justice perceptions by utilizing service agents to recommend personalized privacy policies to individual consumers based on their privacy preferences. The solution offers consumers choices to help them express their privacy preferences<sup>1</sup> (privacy policies) and <sup>fi</sup>nally help them reach an agreement with the agent by conducting interactive negotiations. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, to promote distributive justice perception by supporting the active-recommendation feature, the service agent is in charge of evaluating consumers’ heterogeneous privacy preferences based on their self-reported responses by employing the methods of three-parameters interval-valued fuzzy set (e.g., Zadeh 1975, Chen and Tan 1994, Zhu et al. 2007, Lan and Fan 2009). Based on this evaluation, the service agent actively recommends to each consumer a privacy policy drawn from a database of predesigned privacy policies by integrating the privacy compromise value method (Ghazinour et al. 2014) with information generalization (Squicciarini et al. 2006). To promote interactive justice perception by supporting the negotiation feature, the IT solution also evaluates the consumer’s submitted privacy policy proposal versus the company’s existing privacy policy, and the service agent determines whether to accept, deny, or continue the negotiation.

To capture consumers’ reactions to our proposed solution, we conducted a <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment in the context of mobile banking. We empirically demonstrated that the proposed theory-driven IT solution improves consumers’ perceived procedural justice, interactive justice, and distributive justice, making consumers feel that the company (mobile bank) respects their privacy and is willing to exert more effort to protect their privacy. This, in turn, reduces their privacy concerns, enhances their privacy awareness, and increases their willingness and actual disclosure of their personal information in exchange for more personalized services from the company.<sup>2</sup> The <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment benchmarked our negotiation, active-recommendation privacy policy application against both a baseline method (referred to as non-negotiation privacy policy application) where only a privacy policy statement is posted and a privacy policy with a negotiation feature but without an active recommendation (referred to as negotiation, non-active-recommendation privacy policy application), showing a uniform privacy policy to all consumers (based on which it seeks to reach an agreement between the service agents and consumers). We then conducted a structural equation model analysis to examine our proposed solution's effects on consumers justice perceptions and their psychological states to explain our experimental results. Given the additional time our proposed IT solution requires from consumers than reading a traditional privacy statement, our solution is mostly appropriate for applications and settings that rely on long-term relationships with consumers and deal with sensitive private information.

Our study contributes to the privacy literature by using justice theory to propose and design an IT solution to balance privacy protection and information collection. Although justice theory has been applied in the Information Systems (IS) literature, it focuses main ly on explaining and predicting, such as identifying the existing privacy intervention approaches to justice provisions (Xu et al. 2009, Zhao et al. 2012). Few studies have attempted to propose a new IT solution design to help companies increase their consumers justice perceptions regarding their privacy, even though justice-driven privacy approach has been regarded as a promising way to help companies experience fewer privacy incidents (Lyons et al. 2016). This study answered the call for research on the design of IT artifacts for privacy protection (e.g., Belanger and´ Crossler 2011, Pavlou 2011). This call has been mostly answered by computer scientists (not by IS researchers). However, most research on privacy in computer science has been conducted in isolation from actu al consumers. We identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the IT features of privacy protection measures based on the social science theory, justice theory, and IS theories. We proposed and validated an IT design by taking advantage of methods in IS, mathematics, design science, computer science, and social sciences. Thus, our study sought to promote a multidisciplinary research approach in overcoming the fundamental trade-off between privacy protection and data collection.

## 2. Literature Review

## 2.1. Justice Theory

Justice theory, proposed by American psychologist John Stacey Adams in 1965, is an effective theoretical framework to analyze privacy concerns (Culnan 1995), as perceived fairness could affect the willingness to disclose personal information (Culnan and Armstrong 1999). Several studies have applied justice theory to study privacy intervention approaches. For instance, Xu et al. (2009) identi<sup>fi</sup>ed three privacy intervention approaches (compensation, industry self-regulation, and government regulation) and two types of justice provision (distributive justice and procedural justice) to examine their effects on individuals’ intention to disclose personal information in location-based services. Zhao et al. (2012) applied justice theory to investigate the role of privacy intervention approaches used by service providers in enhancing perceived justice in the location-based social network, including incentives provision, interaction promotion, privacy control, and privacy policy. Past studies have shown that justice theory provides a relevant theoretical basis for studying privacy protection approaches. However, most scholars using justice theory have focused on the effects of existing privacy protection approaches on consumers’ disclosure intention. In other words, there is a dearth of research into the design of new privacy protection approaches based on justice theory.

Because justice theory is so useful for studying privacy protection, we theoretically linked justice provisions to privacy protection features, and we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed negotiation and active-recommendation features as possible extensions of the privacy policy. As explained earlier, the negotiation feature enables two-way interactions between consumers and companies, enabling them to agree on what information the consumer will disclose and how that information would be used. Companies with an active-recommendation feature will actively recommend personalized privacy policies to different categories of consumers, such as privacy fundamentalists, the pragmatic majority, and the marginally concerned (Ackerman et al. 1999).

2.2. Existing Privacy Policy Protection Approach Although several empirical studies have shown that posting a privacy policy statement is useful for reducing consumers’ privacy concerns and enhancing their disclosure intentions, some studies doubted its effectiveness. For example, Liu (2014) demonstrated that the privacy policy is criticized as a burden for most consumers in mobile commerce, as it lacks meaningful choices and thus is not very useful in enhancing consumer control. Few people would read long, complex, and tedious privacy policies, especially when they perceive the cost of reading the policy will not outweigh the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (McDonald and Cranor 2008). These competing <sup>fi</sup>ndings con<sup>fi</sup>rm the need for more theory-grounded studies to gain a deeper understanding into what and how privacy policy features affect consumers’ privacy concerns and disclosure behavior.

Nowadays, mobile apps rely on permission-based approaches and allow consumers to control access to sensitive information and functions. Gu et al. (2017) argued that perceived permission sensitivity makes consumers more concerned about their privacy, whereas permission justi<sup>fi</sup>cation reduces privacy concerns. In contrast, other studies have shown this permissionbased approach to be unrealistic, as consumers are often unaware of many of the permissions with which they are forced to agree (Almuhimedi et al. 2015). Consequently, some researchers have developed technology tools to help consumers manage their privacy permissions. For instance, Lin et al. (2014) identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a small number of privacy pro<sup>fi</sup>les; Liu et al. (2016) operationalized this approach by developing a technical solution to generate recommended permission settings (or pro<sup>fi</sup>les) for consumers to con<sup>fi</sup>gure permissions based on their selected pro<sup>fi</sup>le, which was further deployed in the context of the Internet of Things (Das et al. 2018). In sum, this work focused on helping consumers manage their privacy permissions for all apps installed on mobile phones, which can alleviate the knowledge burden that consumers face while allowing them to interact with privacy permissions. Most prior studies are consumer-protection centric (Acquisti et al. 2017) and have not examined companies' perspectives, limiting companies' ability to invest in privacy protection. Wang and Wu (2014) showed that if e-service providers proactively inform customers about their privacy practices and demonstrate their respect for customers rights, customers would be more willing to disclose their personal information to them. Thus, we highlight the negotiation and active-recommendation features to augment companies' privacy policies. We proposed a theory-driven IT solution to help companies respect their consumers’ privacy by actively offering them better justice provisions. This solution aims to balance pri vacy protection and company gains from leveraging consumer data. Our study seeks to encourage companies to make larger investments in their privacy protection strategies to obtain a competitive advantage.

## 3. Hypotheses Development 3.1. Privacy Concerns

The literature has treated privacy concerns as an individual’s internal worry about the potential loss of pri vacy (Smith et al. 1996, Dinev and Hart 2006). Some authors have indicated that an individual’s privacy concerns vary across contexts and it is necessary to distinguish context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c from general privacy concerns, notwithstanding that it is easier to understand privacy concerns in a given context than in a general setting (Xu et al. 2012). Therefore, in this paper, we focus on context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c privacy concerns and we de<sup>fi</sup>ne privacy concerns as consumers’ worries about the possible loss of information privacy in online environments.

3.2. Procedural Justice: Privacy Policy Statement Procedural justice has been described as “visible fairness,” which refers to the consumer’s perception of fairness in procedures. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, procedural justice is at play when consumers perceive whether a decision maker’s use of procedures or policies to reach a certain negotiation result is fair (Thibaut and Walker 1975). When consumers perceive that a company uses fair processes to handle their personal private information, their privacy concerns decrease and their willingness to disclose information increases (Xu et al. 2009). Re search has indicated that a company’s privacy policy statement re<sup>fl</sup>ects its practices and enhances consumers’ perception of procedural justice (Xu et al. 2009, Zhao et al. 2012). The privacy statement describes how consumers’ personal information is collected, proc essed, and used as well as the company’s responsibility when handling consumers' private information. In location-based social network services, an effective privacy policy statement can reduce privacy concerns and prompt consumers to disclose their locationrelated private information by augmenting procedural justice perceptions (Zhao et al. 2012). The privacy policy statement, as a self-regulation privacy protection action, can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly increase consumers’ perceived control over their personal information and can thus decrease their privacy concerns over location-based services (Xu et al. 2012). In sum, a fair and persuasive privacy policy statement can in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumers’ privacy concerns and information disclosure willingness.

## 3.3. Interactional Justice: A Negotiation Feature

Distinct from procedural justice, interactional justice emphasizes fairness in the way people treat each other (Holmvall and Sidhu 2007). In our context, interpersonal treatment refers to the relationships between consumers and companies. In contrast to the privacy policy statements, which are unidirectional and do not allow consumers to express their privacy preferences, negotiations are said to be an “interpersonal decisionmaking process necessary whenever we cannot achieve our objectives single-handedly” (Thompson 2014, p. 1), as they ensure that both consumers and companies come away with a sense of fairness in the way the result is developed. The inclusion of a negotiation feature enables two-way interactions between consumers and companies based on their privacy preferences. Consumers feel they are treated fairly when companies respond to them (Turow 2003). Thus, compared with a unidirectional privacy statement, these two-way interactions make consumers feel that they are being treated fairly. Therefore, interactional justice is mapped to a negotiation feature that allows consumers to interact with companies on submitting information elements, enabling consumers and companies to work together to reach an agreement about what information they are willing to disclose and how companies would use the information according to the consumers’ privacy preferences. A detailed description of the negotiation feature in our context is shown in Online Appendix B.

Negotiations can close the gap between the information that companies require from consumers and the personal information that a consumer is willing to share with companies. Thus, reciprocal relationships can be built through negotiation to make consumers feel they are treated fairly (Blau 1964). Studies have suggested that it is important for companies to provide consumers with the ability to negotiate the information they are asked to provide (Sparks and McColl-Kennedy 2001). Companies could explain the use of personal information during their negotiations with consumers by specifying the information the consumer must disclose and the ways in which this information would be used. This type of negotiation (interactive communication) could raise consumers’ willingness to disclose their personal information to companies (Turow 2003).

The negotiation feature also increases information transparency by informing consumers about what information is collected and how it is used in an understandable way, thereby alleviating consumers’ privacy concerns (Spears 2013). Gu et al. (2017) also found that declaring the purpose of a permission request and the subsequent handling of collected infor mation decreases consumers’ concerns about their privacy.

Together, transparency and fairness principles help build trust and con<sup>fi</sup>dence in relationships where personal information is required (EPIC and Junkbusters 2000). Therefore, by fostering trust and con<sup>fi</sup>dence, negotiation between consumers and companies helps alleviate privacy concerns and drive information disclosure. We argue that a privacy policy with a negotiation feature reduces consumers’ privacy concerns and motivates their intentions to disclose personal information. Thus, we hypothesized the following:

Hypothesis 1a. A privacy policy with a negotiation fea ture results in lower level privacy concerns compared to a privacy policy without a negotiation feature.

Hypothesis 1b. A privacy policy with a negotiation fea ture results in a higher level of willingness to disclose per sonal information compared to a privacy policy without a negotiation feature.

## 3.4. Distributive Justice: Active-Recommendation Feature

Distributive (or outcome) justice refers to one’s perceived fairness of outcomes (Zhao et al. 2012). This form of justice is associated with an individual’s sense of gain and loss. In social exchanges, comparing the output (an individual’s gain) and the input (the individual’s investment) affects his or her distributive justice perception. In the context of privacy, distributive justice usually refers to measures aimed at enhancing consumer perception of fair outcomes (Xu et al. 2009) and decreasing their privacy concerns (Culnan and Bies 2003). For example, by offering some tangible (e.g., <sup>fi</sup>nancial) or intangible (e.g., services) bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (Hui et al. 2007), consumers would be more likely to disclose their private information (Zhao et al. 2012). The output should be perceived to be equal to consumer investments (information disclosure). However, different individuals have different value measurements of the same investments because of heterogeneity in individuals’ attitudes and preferences toward privacy information (Westin 1967, Dwork 2008, Lin 2019) that could affect their privacy disclosure behaviors. We de<sup>fi</sup>ned this phenomenon as heterogeneous privacy preferences.<sup>3</sup>

Westin’s Privacy Segmentation Index has been widely used to categorize individuals into three priva cy groups according to their privacy preferences (Ackerman et al. 1999, Kumaraguru and Cranor 2005): (a) privacy fundamentalists, who are very concerned about any use of their personal information and generally unwilling to give companies their personal information even under common privacy protections; (b) the pragmatic majority, who are also concerned about their personal information use but less so than privacy fundamentalists; and (c) the marginally concerned, who often show only slight concern about their privacy and are generally willing to offer their personal information under most conditions. Thibaut and Walker (1975) pointed out that privacy pragmatists are willing to disclose their private information to obtain some bene<sup>fi</sup>ts if they see a fair trade-off, whereas privacy fundamentalists are generally unwilling to disclose personal information. Consumers in different categories have different privacy preferences and privacy concerns, all of which affect their intention to disclose their personal information (Taddei and Contena 2013). Thus, it is essential for companies to actively consider consumers’ heterogeneous privacy and show their concerns and respect by actively recommending personalized privacy policies for different categories of consumers and aligning service designs with different consumers’ privacy preferences (Karwatzki et al. 2017).

Different categories of consumers have different attitudes toward disclosing personal information (investment). Privacy pragmatists think their personal information can be exchanged in return for bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. They deem disclosing their private information in exchange for high-quality services is fair (Hui et al. 2007). Still, rather than high-quality services, privacy fundamentalists value their personal information more. If privacy fundamentalists are asked to disclose the same personal information (investment) as privacy pragmatists in exchange for high-quality services (output), they may not prefer the exchange, even though they view it to be fair. Instead, they could feel that providing the least information in return for basic services (e.g., account management in mobile banking) is more acceptable and fairer. Unlike privacy fundamentalists and pragmatists, marginally concerned consumers do not consider their personal information as valuable. They think it is a fair and acceptable trade to disclose private information to receive personalized services.

We proposed the active-recommendation feature as a means to provide distributive justice to consumers while considering their privacy preferences. The company actively recommends a personalized privacy policy to consumers to satisfy their privacy needs based on their heterogeneous privacy preference. We take mobile banking as an example. Consumers with a high level of heterogeneous privacy preferences can be requested to disclose more private information, such as a speci<sup>fi</sup>c amount of monthly household income to obtain a higher service quality, like getting personalized <sup>fi</sup>- nancial advice as a reward, whereas consumers with a low level of heterogeneous privacy preferences could be asked to disclose the least personal information, such as surname and phone number, to attain basic services, like account management. Therefore, to help consumers perceive a higher degree of fairness, a different amount of personal information can be required from different categories of consumers to offer differential services, thereby offering distributive justice.

The active-recommendation feature enables consumers to access personalized privacy policies ef<sup>fi</sup>- ciently. Consumers favor personalization as it reduces their cognitive load by offering services that match their needs (Chin 2007), which can facilitate the interaction between consumers and companies. Personalization also makes consumers feel that they receive special treatment from the company (Nyheim et al. 2015), thus promoting interactional justice. The activerecommendation feature is particularly important for a solution with a negotiation feature that requires higher consumer engagement than simply reading a privacy statement.

Therefore, to ensure consumers perceive a sense of fairness to reduce their privacy concerns, we proposed a privacy policy negotiation in which the company actively recommends a personalized privacy policy by describing data collection and purposes for data processing according to the categories to which consumers belong. Accordingly, consumers are offered a certain quality of services, allowing them to conduct selective settings in the recommended privacy policy according to their privacy preferences, thus generating a unique personalized privacy policy for each consumer. Such a design incorporates both a negotiation feature and an active-recommendation feature to the privacy policy to provide consumers with a higher sense of procedural justice, interactional justice, and distributive justice. Taken together, we hypothesized the following:

Hypothesis 2a. A privacy policy negotiation with an active-recommendation feature results in a lower level of privacy concerns compared to a privacy policy negotiation without an active-recommendation feature.

Hypothesis 2b. A privacy policy negotiation with an active-recommendation feature results in a higher level of willingness to disclose personal information compared to a privacy policy negotiation without an active-recommendation feature.

## 4. Research Methodology

We designed a <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment to manipulate the independent variables (i.e., negotiation and activerecommendation). The dependent variables were privacy concerns, disclosure intention, and disclosure behavior (re<sup>fl</sup>ected by the number and sensitivity of participants’ disclosed personal information).

## 4.1. Proposed IT Solution

Three web-based mobile banking applications were developed for this study: (1) an application that only provides a privacy statement without negotiation $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ a non-negotiation privacy policy application); (2) an application that allows consumers to negotiate with service agents the various data elements they are asked to disclose according to their privacy preferences (i.e., a negotiation, non-active-recommendation privacy policy application); and (3) an application that allows consumers to negotiate with service agents the various data elements they are asked to disclose based on a personalized privacy policy actively recommended by service agents (i.e., a negotiation, active-recommendation privacy policy application). The proposed negotiation, active-recommendation privacy policy solution (App 3) was developed to offer consumers all three types of justice by actively considering the privacy needs of different consumers and actively highlighting companies’ respect of consumers’ heterogeneous privacy preferences. The service agent evaluates each consumer’s heterogeneous privacy preference based on the consumer's individual preferences and actively recommends a privacy policy drawn from a database of predesigned privacy policies approved by the company. Both sides interact to develop the privacy policy. Figure 1 gives an overall architectural view of the proposed IT solution.

A large volume of empirical research has accumulated on consumers’ factors of disclosure intentions (Xie et al. 2006, Xu et al. 2009, Zhao et al. 2012, Li 2014, Sharma and Crossler 2014, Wang and Wu 2014, Bansal et al. 2016). 4.1.2. Privacy Policy Design. To achieve a negotiation, active-recommendation privacy policy application, we designed a set of privacy policies that we then recommended to different consumers based on their privacy preferences. To determine the types of personal information that banks desire and the reason they need such personal information, we interviewed three managers at three different banks in China. Consumers can register for online banking with only a phone number. However, they need to provide additional personal information if they want to use personalized services, such as personalized <sup>fi</sup>nancial advice and <sup>fi</sup>- nancing information. From these interviews, we chose 12 items asking for personal information from subjects in our study (Table A3 in Online Appendix A). The information was collected for <sup>fi</sup>ve reasons, account management, safety and security, value-added services, third-party access, and research and data analysis (Ghazinour et al. 2014).

Next, we integrated the privacy compromise value method (Ghazinour et al. 2014) with information generalization (Squicciarini et al. 2006) to design sets of privacy policies. First, we obtained the sensitivity scores of the 12 personal information items with different granularity and the level of concern about their data usage for each purpose. We surveyed 120 subjects by asking them to specify their data sensitivity and level of concern on a discrete scale from 0–10, where 0 represents being not sensitive/concerned at all and 10 represents being extremely sensitive/concerned. Second, ascending the sensitivity score of each item, a pointer can be set to the position where the number of scores on its left is equal to x% of the total number of the score (x [0, 100]) (Ghazinour et al. 2014). We de<sup>fi</sup>ned x% as the compromise level, meaning that at least x% consumers are willing to provide this information. In this study, we set the compromise level of privacy fundamentalists, the pragmatic majority, and the marginally concerned at 85%, 50%, and 20%, respectively. Meanwhile, given the mandatory personal information that almost all mobile banks require in China, we developed the lowerand upper-limit privacy policies for privacy fundamentalists $( S _ { 0 } ^ { - } , ~ S _ { 0 } ^ { * } )$ , the pragmatic majority $( S _ { 1 } ^ { - } , S _ { 1 } ^ { * } )$ and the marginally concerned $( S _ { 2 } ^ { - } , S _ { 2 } ^ { * } )$ , as shown in detail in Table A3 of Online Appendix A. These privacy policies showed hierarchical relationships, that is, $\bar { S _ { i } ^ { - } } \bar { \prec } S _ { i } ^ { * } , i = 0 , 1$ , 2. Besides, the lower limit of the privacy policy for the pragmatic majority was stricter than the upper limit of the privacy policy for privacy fundamentalists $( S _ { 0 } ^ { * } \preccurlyeq S _ { 1 } ^ { - } ) .$ , and the lower limit of the privacy policy for the marginally concerned was stricter than the upper limit of the privacy policy for the pragmatic majority $( S _ { 1 } ^ { * } \preccurlyeq S _ { 2 } ^ { - } )$ .

Given that the negotiation, active-recommendation privacy policy application is equipped with both focal features (i.e., negotiation and active-recommendation), assessing the effects of these two individual features on consumers’ privacy concerns and their disclosure behaviors required us to develop two benchmarked mobile applications: a base version that only presents a static privacy policy statement (non-negotiation privacy policy application) and another version that also provides a privacy policy negotiation function (negotiation, non-active-recommendation privacy policy application). In the base version's privacy policy, the information request and data usage were equal to the lower limit for the marginally concerned. In the other version (the negotiation, non-active-recommendation privacy policy application), the lower-limit and upper-limit privacy policies were equal to the privacy policies for the marginally concerned.

## 5.1. Manipulation Checks

Experimental manipulations were checked in three steps. First, we recorded the time each participant spent on the experiment. Data from the participants who spent too little time (less than <sup>fi</sup>ve minutes) were dropped. Second, data from participants who failed to respond to the manipulation check questions correctly were dropped. Third, we included the justice perception questions to test the effectiveness of the manipulations. All t-tests showed that all treatments were manipulated effectively. ## 5.3. Behavioral Analysis

We also explored the effects of negotiation and activerecommendation feature on consumers’ actual disclosure behaviors. In the experiment, participants who agreed to the privacy statement or achieved successful negotiation results were required to disclose their personal information. We viewed their disclosure behaviors as a real number ranging from zero to one, which is calculated by the following function:

$$
d i s c l o s u r e b e h a v i o r = \frac {\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {m} i t e m _ {s e n s _ {j}}}{\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} i t e m _ {s e n s _ {i}}},
$$

w $\mathsf { n e r e } \{ m , n , i , j \} \in N ;$ m represents the number of disclosed information; n represents the total number of the requested information in Session 4 of the experiment; and $i t e m _ { s e n s _ { j } }$ and $i t e m _ { s e n s _ { i } }$ indicate the mean sensitivity scores of the jth item and ith item of personal information, respectively, which was assessed by participants in the <sup>fi</sup>rst session of the experiment. We incorporated sensitivity scores because companies not only care about the quantity but also the quality of information disclosure. The more sensitive the information, the less willing consumers are to disclose their private information.

To control for the effect of different privacy policy levels on disclosure behavior, we incorporated an additional control variable, that is, privacy policy level. Because the relationships between privacy policies were hierarchical, we assigned values of one, two, and three to the privacy policy of marginally concerned, pragmatic majority, and privacy fundamentalists, respectively. The estimation results are shown in the <sup>fi</sup>rst column of Table 6. We found that the active-recommendation feature indeed increased actual disclosure behaviors $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ ). Interestingly, we observed that the negotiation feature had a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and negative effect on participants’ disclosure behaviors $( p \ < \ 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . Yet, the effect of the activerecommendation feature on disclosure behavior was stronger than that of the negotiation feature. Therefore, our solution (privacy policy with both negotiation and active-recommendation feature) promoted participants actual disclosure behaviors compared with the tradition al privacy statement and the privacy policy negotiation without an active-recommendation feature. This further con<sup>fi</sup>rms the effectiveness of our proposed mechanism.

As a robustness test, we tested several alternative models. First, Model 1 uni<sup>fi</sup>ed the value of disclosure behavior ranging from zero to one in all three treatments. However, the denominator of disclosure behavior measure varied by the experimental conditions. To account for this <sup>fi</sup>nding, we changed the measurement of disclosure behavior (dependent variable) to (a) the quantity of information disclosure and (b) the sensitivity score of the information disclosure, respectively, variables that companies truly value. The results are shown in the second (Model 2) and third column (Model 3) of Table 6. Next, in the <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment, par ticipants were only asked to rate the sensitivity score of 12 information items without considering their granularity to avoid potential participant fatigue. To eliminate the potential effect of information granularity on disclosure behavior, we redid the analysis without considering the personal information referring to granularity, that is, we did not consider the name, address, amount of personal debt, monthly household in come, and personal monthly expenditure in the disclosure behavior analysis. The results are shown in Table A7 of Online Appendix A. Generally, all the alternative models' results were similar to those obtained from the basic model, strengthening the con<sup>fi</sup>dence in our basic model’s results.

## 6. Discussion

## 6.1. Key Findings and Contributions

This study sought to provide useful insights to companies on balancing between protecting consumers information privacy and leveraging their data. Consumer data are valuable to companies, although collecting them often con<sup>fl</sup>icts with privacy protection. We theorized that a justice-driven privacy policy approach helps overcome this trade-off, avoid privacy breaches, and retain consumer trust. Using the justice theory lens, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed two key justice provision features of privacy protection, negotiation and activerecommendation. We empirically examined the role of these privacy protection features in privacy concerns and disclosure behaviors with a randomized <sup>fi</sup>eld ex periment. The results showed that only a negotiation feature, which is similar to emerging privacy protection mechanisms, such as privacy permissions, does not reduce privacy concerns. Consumer disclosure intention did not increase signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly; more interest ingly, we observed that a single negotiation feature had a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant negative effect on actual disclosure behavior. The plausible explanation is that consumers are already accustomed to agreeing with companies privacy statements without being aware of the content. Participants in our experiment had to disclose their personal information to use the bank’s app services. In App 1 (a non-negotiation privacy policy application), participants disclosed their personal information when asked to do so, habitually assuming that they were using the banking application. The negotiation feature in App 2 increased information transparency by making participants clearly aware of what personal information they must provide, how their personal information will be used, and who can access this personal information. However, transparency is not only a signal of fairness but it can also be a warning sign, increasing people’s privacy concerns (Knijnenburg et al. 2012). Gu et al. (2017) also showed that the practice of prominently disclosing all permission requests in the app download stage makes consumers more concerned about the loss of their privacy by cautioning them against privacy-invasive apps that over-request permissions. Thus, the negotiation feature has dual characteristics that help increase information transparency and make privacy issues explicit (Karwatzki et al. 2017). Hence, participants using App 2 may have hesitated when asked to disclose their personal information. This may also be a reason why App 2 had a low success rate (Table 3). In other words, although the negotiation feature enhanced the consumers’ privacy consciousness by increasing information transparency, the negotiation feature also reduced the amount of information companies get from consumers, which is not a desirable outcome for companies. Thus, this single feature cannot single-handedly balance privacy protection and data collection, which actively considers companies' needs, whereas most prior work is consumer-protection centric.

Through the lens of justice theory, we argued that integrating distributive justice to the privacy policy negotiation feature would result in a lower level of privacy concerns and a higher level of intention to disclose private information among consumers. As such, this study proposed a new design to address the trade-off between privacy protection and data collection of consumers that enables companies to actively recommend a personalized privacy policy to consumers according to their privacy preferences. Therefore, companies would negotiate with each consumer based on the recommended privacy policy and his or her privacy preferences, offering consumers procedural, interactional, and distributive justice.

To prevent the control paradox (Brandimarte et al. 2013), our solution not only provides negotiations related to information collection but it also includes negotiations for information usage and third-party access. To enhance the transparency of companies’ privacy practice, all privacy-related information is shown concisely and understandably and consumers can easily change the set of recommended privacy policies presented to them. This way, information asymmetry between consumers and companies is alleviated and consumers can make more conscious privacy decisions.

The experimental results demonstrated that our active-recommendation privacy policy negotiation design not only alleviates consumers’ privacy concerns but also enhances their information disclosure intention and their actual disclosure in practice. Post hoc analysis con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that our design signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly enhances consumer perceptions of procedural, interactional, and distributive justice, making them believe that the company values and respects their privacy. This, in turn, increases their psychological comfort in using our application design, making consumers feel more at ease when sharing their personal information with the company. Therefore, our proposed design can provide consumers better privacy protection while ensuring that consumers voluntarily disclose personal information desirable for companies.

Acquisti et al. (2017) proposed that aligning people’s behavior to their stated preferences is the desired outcome of implementing privacy nudges. This is exactly one of the motivations of our solution. Speci<sup>fi</sup> cally, our proposed solution enables companies to actively recommend personalized privacy policies to consumers based on their heterogeneous privacy preferences; consumers and companies can negotiate the privacy policies on an equal basis; <sup>fi</sup>nally, companies provide corresponding services associated with the negotiation results, in turn to satisfy the privacy needs of consumers. Thus, our proposed solution not only improves feelings of comfort but also provides objec tively better outcomes for consumers in practice.

## 6.2. Feasibility and Implementation

Additionally, we analyzed the feasibility of our approach in terms of participants’ negotiation behavior, success rate, perceived ease of use, perceived useful ness, use intention, and time spent on each approach.

To understand whether consumers actually negotiate or simply accept what they are shown, we collected data on participants' negotiation behavior in App 2 and App 3. As shown in Table 3, the average number of rounds of negotiations in App 2 and App 3 were 2.13 and 1.46, respectively. Eighty participants in App 2 and 79 participants in App 3 adjusted the default settings, whereas 69 participants in App 2 and 78 par ticipants in App 3 had negotiation results that were different from the default settings. These results indicate that most participants actually negotiated rather than passively accepting what they were given. It seems that consumers like to express their privacy preferences when privacy practices are presented to them easily and understandably and communicate their preferences when it is convenient for them to do. The results showed that consumers are willing and able to use the negotiation and active-recommendation feature

Although App 1 had the highest success rate, this may be the result of habitual acceptance. It is further con<sup>fi</sup>rmed by the record that only 19 out of 112 participants clicked the link to take them to the privacy statement, which means most participants had no awareness of the privacy statement. In contrast, App 2 had a much lower success rate with the same request for required information and data usage. This indicates that a considerable proportion of consumers are likely to reject the content of the privacy statement if it is shown to them in a concise and understandable way. With the integration of the active-recommendation feature (App 3), the negotiation success rate was signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly enhanced and interactive negotiations with consumers actively took place. This is consistent with our expectations that the active-recommendation feature not only provides distributive justice but it also reduces consumers’ cognitive load by offering personalized privacy policies and promoting interactions between consumers and companies. Finally, as mentioned earlier, we set the compromise level of privacy fundamentalists, the pragmatic majority, and the marginally concerned at 85%, 50%, and 20%, respectively. Because the compromise level represents the proportion of consumers who are willing to provide an item of personal information, if we increased the compromise level by requiring less personal information, the success rate of App 3 could be further enhanced. For example, if we changed the lower limit of the privacy policy for privacy fundamentalists by requiring only a phone number (which is enough for registering in mobile banking in China), more participants would potentially reach successful negotiations in App 3. In practice, companies can set compromise levels according to their own needs.

Given the effectiveness of our proposed solution (App 3), we compared App 3 with the most commonly used approach, that is, a traditional privacy statement (App 1). The optional survey analysis (in Online Appendix E) showed that the perceived ease of use of our design is not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different from agreeing or disagreeing with a privacy policy statement (p 0.108). Our simple technical design allows consumers without any technical knowledge to easily use our application in practice. Because default recommendations are provided, consumers who do not want to rate questions for evaluating their heterogeneous privacy or participate in the negotiation process can simply click the buttons to proceed without delay, which is as easy as agreeing or disagreeing with a privacy statement. The perceived usefulness and use intention for our design were signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly higher compared with the simple standard privacy statement (p < 0.001). This indicates that participants believed that our proposed design is useful in terms of protecting their privacy and they were more willing to adopt our approach and use more services if the banking application provided our proposed active-recommendation solution, recon<sup>fi</sup>rming the effectiveness of our proposed solution.

On average, the participants spent only 16.54 seconds on the privacy statement while spending much more time on our solution in App 3 (118.8 seconds). Actually, participants using App 3 could simply click the buttons to proceed without any delay, albeit the results showed the perceived ease of use of App 3 did not differ signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly from that of App 1. This implies that participants were willing to spend more time on App 3 to make more conscious privacy decisions, which may be comparable to the central versus peripheral routes in the Elaboration Likelihood Model (Petty and Cacioppo 1986). Our proposed solution acts both as a signal and as a persuasion attempt. When providing only a privacy statement, participants may be motivated to think about the privacy statement. Super<sup>fi</sup>cial processing is likely to follow, focused only on surface features, such as the presence of a privacy statement (peripheral route), without much attention to its contents. On the contrary, our proposed solution may enhance participants' motivation and ability to think about privacy-related information and engage in deeper processing of the message, more focused on the content provided by our solution. This then drives a cognitive response (higher perceived justice) that encourages a higher willingness and higher actual disclosure of private information (central route). Given the additional time our solution requires from users, it may be more appropriate for applications that rely on long-term relationships with consumers and require sensitive private information, such as online banking, healthcare information systems, and related applications, because consumers may be more amenable to investing time in these contexts.

Moreover, in practice, consumers' heterogeneous privacy preferences can be learned without explicitly asking them questions. Compared with our proposed method, more advanced arti<sup>fi</sup>cial intelligence methods can be used to estimate consumer preferences, such as machine learning techniques and prediction approaches (Lin et al. 2014, Liu et al. 2016, Wijesekera et al. 2017, Das et al. 2018, Smullen et al. 2020). However, the development of an evaluation algorithm is beyond the scope of this study. To predict consumers’ privacy preferences inevitably requires consumers to disclose their personal information; companies can still realize the active-recommendation feature by choosing appropriate means to predict their privacy preferences.

In terms of implementation concerns, companies may think of the negotiation module of the Platform for Privacy Preferences Project (P3P), which was dropped because of concerns about the complexity of the process (EPIC and Junkbusters 2000). Although our solution contains a negotiation feature, it is different from P3P in the sense that our negotiation feature is provided by service providers, which is consistent with the idea of P3P’s critics who think the access to personal data within a service (negotiation) might be performed by the service providers according to thei own rules. Others voiced the concern that the P3P Version 1 removed the negotiation feature, highlighting the necessity of negotiation (Kwon et al. 2011). Negotiation supports communication among consumers and companies, and it also gives consumers control over their information disclosures. Without the negotiation function, P3P Version 1 is deemed as providing not a perfect but an internationally effective tool; the handling of the negotiation is considered as the right path to follow (Grimm and Rossnagel 2000).

Adopting our proposed design may change the way companies determine privacy policies, but it deserves serious consideration. Some companies would make efforts to protect their customers’ personal information privacy even if it increases the costs and changes the process (Internet Law Research Center 2016). Besides, our proposed solution should not replace but rather complement the companies’ traditional privacy statement. That is, for online transactions that would not require the disclosure of sensitive information, the traditional privacy statement may be suf<sup>fi</sup>cient. However, for mobile apps and online platforms that require sensitive information and need to maintain long-term relationships with consumers, the companies deserve to try to integrate both the negotiation and the activerecommendation feature to their privacy policies to balance data collection and privacy protection.

## 6.3. Implications for Theory and Research

This study contributes to privacy theory and research in three ways: First, to our knowledge, this study is the <sup>fi</sup>rst to incorporate justice theory into the design of privacy protection approaches. We identi<sup>fi</sup>ed two privacy protection features—negotiation and activerecommendation—to provide consumers with interactional justice and distributive justice. Our study indicates that the single negotiation feature is regarded as a warning sign by consumers concerned about their privacy and it does not have a positive effect on their disclosure behavior. This puts companies in a passive position to take effort to protect consumer privacy. However, the inclusion of an active-recommendation feature alongside the negotiation feature, which simultaneously re<sup>fl</sup>ects procedural justice, interactional justice, and distributive justice, puts companies in a position to actively consider consumers’ privacy needs, reduce their privacy concerns, and enhance their information disclosure willingness, unlike approaches that re<sup>fl</sup>ect only one or two forms of justice. Such <sup>fi</sup>ndings contribute to justice theory by showing how privacy protection design features can reduce privacy concerns and encourage consumers’ intention and actual behaviors to disclose their personal information, extending the application of justice theory to the design of privacy solutions.

Second, our study showed that IT solutions could effectively balance consumers’ privacy protection and data collection by companies. Our empirical studies (<sup>fi</sup>eld experiment and surveys) consistently revealed that our proposed design (which actively recommends a different privacy policy to different categories of consumers by a company’s service agents by allowing interactions with consumers) promotes the sense of psychological comfort among consumers by enhancing their perceived fairness, making consumers believe that the focal company respects their privacy.

Accordingly, they are more privacy aware and more willing to provide their personal information to the company. Such <sup>fi</sup>ndings contribute to justice theory by demonstrating how our proposed design can help achieve an equilibrium, allowing companies to extract the maximum value from consumer data while also ensuring some level of justice provision in privacy protection to prevent privacy breaches and sustain a trusting relationship with consumers. We hope this implication will stimulate an exciting direction to explore a range of possible technological designs that would incorporate such privacy protection features beyond what is proposed in this study. Accordingly, consumers could experience an additional level of perceived justice to increase their sense of comfort. Such a stream of research would be a valuable complement to the extant privacy protection research in this study.

Third, we integrated an IT design with a <sup>fi</sup>eld experi mental study. The privacy literature has focused mainly on explaining and predicting, whereas only a few studies have focused on prescriptive design. There has been a call for more design and action research that fo cuses on designing IT artifacts to protect and control consumers’ information privacy (Belanger and Crossler´ 2011, Pavlou 2011). This call has been mostly answered by computer scientists (alas not by IS researchers). However, most research on privacy in the computer science literature has been conducted in isolation from actual consumers. Few studies have included both pri vacy design and subsequent evaluation of the solution by its expected consumers. Thus, Belanger and Crossler´ (2011) called for multidisciplinary studies. To answer this call, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the IT features of privacy protec tion measures that affect consumers’ privacy concerns and information disclosure intentions through a social science theory—justice theory—and IS theories. Based on our theorization, we proposed an IT design by applying mathematics, design science, and computer science methods. The proposed IT solution was imple mented in mobile commerce; its effectiveness and effects were evaluated through randomized <sup>fi</sup>eld experimentation, which combines strategies from the IS, computer science, and social sciences. Thus, our study seeks to promote the development of a multidiscipli nary research approach in overcoming privacy protec tion versus information collection.

## 6.4. Implications for Practice, Public Policy, and Society

This study has useful implications for companies, information systems developers, users (or consumers), public policy of<sup>fi</sup>cials, and society. As Tim Barker, CEO of DataSift, stated, companies that actively respect and protect consumer data will be the winners (Nunns 2015). While companies extract value from consumer data, they must also ensure some level of justice to provide consumers with a sense of fairness and build trusting relationships with consumers. Thus, our study suggests that companies change their privacy protection principles from a passive position, such as unilaterally posting a privacy statement or showing uni<sup>fi</sup>ed privacy permissions to all consumers, to actively recommending personalized privacy policies to different categories of consumers, especially in contexts where privacy issues are more important and privacy concerns are salient among consumers. Our solution not only encourages consumers to disclose more sensitive information but it also helps companies experience fewer privacy incidents and build trust with their customers. Our results are valuable for companies in industries that collect sensitive information, as these companies may <sup>fi</sup>nd it useful to incorporate such features (negotiation and activerecommendation) into their privacy policy designs. Even for traditional (physical) retailers who obtain sensitive personal information from consumers, if there was an interface in physical stores that enabled customers to negotiate and personalize their privacy policy, our method might also cautiously generalize to traditional commerce. Finally, we hope to encourage companies to take privacy investments as added value and a competitive advantage source rather than passive compliance with applicable privacy laws and regulations.

Our study offers practical guidance to both companies and IS developers on developing effective technical solutions for balancing the con<sup>fl</sup>ict between privacy protection and data collection, even in mobile commerce where mobile screens are small. By building on the notion that procedural justice, interactional justice, and distributive justice can be achieved simultaneously by integrating negotiation and active-recommendation features in a privacy policy, IS developers can design effective privacy policy solutions by combining objective techniques with consumers’ subjective preferences. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, in designing and developing privacy policies, IS developers should comprehensively include consumer heterogeneous privacy preferences and individual psychological factors, thus encouraging consumers to disclose their private information.

Our results call for public policy of<sup>fi</sup>cials to give companies more latitude on their privacy policy design under the precondition of ensuring that consumers' personal information will not be collected excessively. Usually companies would provide many types of services, and they have to design a uniform privacy policy to collect personal information for all services they provide. Different services need different information. Suppose companies can design different sets of privacy policies for different services and associated personal information. In that case, companies can promote more innovations in terms of privacy protection solutions by achieving a balance between consumers’ privacy protection and the collection of their private information.

To build a healthy privacy environment, companies must provide consumers with useful and easy-to-use privacy protection mechanisms and consumers must enhance their understanding of privacy policies by actively adopting some modest privacy protection techniques to safeguard their privacy, such as our proposed IT solution. The proposed solution allows consumers to perceive more justice in disclosing their personal information, recognizing that, on the one hand, they are active participants and decision makers in the develop ment of privacy policies they accept, whereas, on the other hand, companies actively consider and respect their privacy, forming a forced mechanism for companies to regulate ways to treat consumers’ personal in formation and promote stronger privacy protection. This, in turn, creates a virtuous circle promoting the healthy development of society. Finally, promoting privacy protection also profoundly affects all aspects of social development, such as personal awareness, organizational honesty and ethics, and others.

## 6.5. Limitations and Suggestions for Future Research

This research has some limitations that create opportunities for future research. First, the proposed solution does not suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently consider the privacy con<sup>fl</sup>ict issues inherent in the negotiation process. We only suggested some nontechnological measures based on existing empirical studies. To address privacy negotiation con<sup>fl</sup>icts, future research should pay more attention to technological and nontechnological solutions that could resolve privacy policy negotiation con<sup>fl</sup>icts more ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently.

Second, future work could expand our design by proposing other strategies to help consumers manage their privacy settings, as decided during the negotiation process, which is beyond this paper's scope.

Third, the negotiation procedure brought up aspects of the brand and the reward to encourage participants to compromise. Perhaps consumers who are highly concerned about their privacy are also concerned about the security level with which the company stores their data. Maybe those consumers would be more convinced by information regarding security investments or security procedures. Therefore, combining security and privacy is an interesting idea to pursue in the future.

Fourth, our method is appropriate for a highly sensitive context. People may not go through the negotiation process for a casual mobile application or online platform with whom they do not have a longstanding relationship. Future research can extend the proposed solution to make it more feasible for other contexts.

## 7. Conclusion

Privacy concerns are one of the biggest challenges in the evolution of cyberspace and electronic commerce. Although it is necessary to design effective privacy protection approaches, most privacy studies in the IS literature have focused on explaining and predicting; only a few studies have focused on designing IT artifacts to protect people’s privacy. To this end, we proposed and designed a theory-driven IT solution to achieve a balance between consumers’ privacy protection and the collection of their private information by companies. We believe that this research not only provides an effective practical solution for companies but it also highlights how companies should change their privacy protection practices from passive to active to give consumers a sense of perceived justice through negotiation via bilateral communication and activerecommendation measures to individual consumers. We hope this study will stimulate more research on the intelligence augmentation design with privacy protection mechanisms to help companies succeed in an age where personal data protection could be a source of competitive advantage.
