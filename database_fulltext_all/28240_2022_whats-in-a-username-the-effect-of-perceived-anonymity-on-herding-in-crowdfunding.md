Abstract. This research examines the role of perceived anonymity in shaping herding be havior in online crowdfunding markets. Drawing on theories from social psychology literature, we argue that a lender forms different credibility perceptions toward preceding peers based on their perceived anonymity state; the lender then uses such perceptions to adjust the lender’s herding momentum toward them. Using data collected from a leading debt-based crowdfunding platform, we classify an individual’s username as either anonymous or real-seeming, with the latter referring to as a user identi<sup>fi</sup>cation that seems to reveal one’s real name. The results show that successors demonstrate weaker herding momentum toward predecessors who are presented with real-seeming usernames than anonymous ones. This <sup>fi</sup>nding, which we attribute to a lower extent of perceived credibility derived from a nonconforming behavior, challenges the conventional wisdom that considers anonymity a negative factor for source credibility. We further show that the uncovered positive effect of perceived anonymity on herding is accentuated in the early stage of the fundraising period; nevertheless, we <sup>fi</sup>nd no such discrepancies between listings that are assigned with high-risk and low-risk credit grades. Our study contributes to the literatures dealing with anonymity and herding in online environments.

## 1. Introduction

Debt-based crowdfunding is a collective process where the crowd pool together and lend money to unknown borrowers for interest earnings (Lin and Viswanathan 2016). Information asymmetry inherent in this market poses a tough challenge to individual lenders; they must evaluate the potential risk of each loan listing with very little information about the borrower (Burtch et al. 2016). To facilitate decision making, they resort to observe and follow the decisions made by preceding peers, giving rise to herding behavior (Zhang and Liu 2012, Xiao et al. 2021). The rationale is that the crowd is uncertain about the potential risk of a listing; nonetheless, each individual may hold some private knowledge about it, perhaps through the individual’s previous experience with similar listings or through personal connections with the borrower (Agrawal et al. 2015). Consequently, lenders can infer a listing’s potential risk through observational learning, a mechanism under which pred ecessors’ lending decisions are treated as signals that carry useful information in successors’ eyes.

Then, are these individual signals perceived as equally credible? Prior research suggests that individuals tend to attach uneven weights to information sources that have different attributes, such as network ties and expertise (Lee et al. 2015, Kim and Viswanathan 2019). The cognitive process underlying this differentiation behavior is that, upon receiving signals, individuals <sup>fi</sup>rst assess source credibility based on some observable personal cues (Petty et al. 1983). They then use such perceived source credibility to moderate the value of the information, which, in turn, impacts their subsequent decision making. Following this notion, we anticipate that lenders are likely to incorporate a similar differentiation process into their observational learning. If they indeed distinguish the informational value of previous lending transactions (i.e., signals), we should observe successors demonstrating different herding momentum toward distinct types of predecessors (i.e., information sources).

In online environments, a username is a unique and persistent personal cue that is presented to and perceived by others (e.g., Ma and Agarwal 2007, Forman et al. 2008, Burtch et al. 2016). This role of usernames could be particularly salient in contexts where other personal information is hidden for privacy preservation (as in debt-based crowdfunding markets). Indeed, empirical research has documented that usernames can carry informational value that affects a beholder’s perception in e-commerce settings (Silva et al. 2017, Silva and Topolinski 2018). Along this line, concealment of usernames is further shown to discourage subsequent crowdfunding activities, because an absence of identity information about others triggers a sense of unease from a socially normative perspective (Burtch et al. 2016).

Although it has been recognized that usernames, albeit evaluated in various dimensions, can affect a beholder’s perception, little is known about their implication in shaping observational learning behavior, which manifests as herding. To <sup>fi</sup>ll this gap, we investigate whether and how predecessors’ usernames—as evaluated from a perspective of perceived anonymity— affect successors’ herding momentum through the varying extent of perceived source credibility. Moreover, as the main purpose of lenders engaging in observational learning is to infer investment risk (Zhang and Liu 2012), a natural follow-up question to ask is whether lenders would adjust their reliance on observational learning when dealing with different levels of perceived risk. Thus, we are also interested in examining whether and how the effect of perceived anonymity on herding is moderated by listing characteristics that could signal a listing’s potential risk.

We conceptualize perceived anonymity as the beholder perception that arises from observing predecessors who present themselves with an anonymous username. Conventional wisdom suggests that anonymity provides no personal cue about information sources, which lowers the perceived source credibility and reduces the informational value of the signal (Hass 1981, Kruglanski et al. 2006, Wagenknecht et al. 2016). Intuitively, a prospective lender is likely to perceive predecessors presented with an anonymous username as less credible, and accordingly exhibit a weaker herding momentum toward them. Recent work on online anonymity, however, implies that an opposite effect may emerge as well. Debt-based crowdfunding is an online community (Burtch et al. 2016) wherein members have little incentive to reveal identity cues (e.g., real names) to others. As remaining anonymous to “lie low” emerges as an agreed behavioral standard in such an investment-oriented context (Pritchard 2020), the behavior of keeping a high pro<sup>fi</sup>le by voluntarily revealing one’s identity in a username is likely to arouse disapproval and skepticism from the beholders (Van Kleef et al. 2015), attenuating the magnitude of the herding effect. Bearing the competing arguments presented earlier, we leave whether perceived anonymity has a positive or negative effect on lender herding as an empirical question worth academic examination.

To study our research questions, we collect data from a leading debt-based crowdfunding platform in Asia. Our data record crowdfunding activities on this platform, including characteristics at the listing, borrower, and transaction level. The platform provides an ideal setting for answering the posed question for two reasons. First, the platform conceals all personal information about all lenders for privacy preservation purposes, leaving usernames the only salient identity cue that can affect a beholder’s perception of source credibility. This contextual feature allows us to isolate the role of usernames in impacting herding behavior, after controlling for other listing attributes observable to subsequent lenders. Second, unlike some platforms where transactional information is partially disclosed (e.g., the context studied by Burtch et al. 2016), prospective lenders in our context observe detailed information about all previous funding activities in a salient fashion. With this complete transaction log, we can recover the cumulative lending amount a listing has received at a given point of time. With this key measure for the herd volume, we can quantify the herding effect, allowing us to empirically investigate whether successors exhibit different herding magnitudes toward predecessors presented with different types of usernames.

Our preliminary result con<sup>fi</sup>rms the existence of herding, as re<sup>fl</sup>ected by a positive correlation between lending amounts funded by precedent and subsequent lenders. To investigate the role of perceived anonymity, we <sup>fi</sup>rst classify each individual’s username as either anonymous or real-seeming, with the latter referring to as a user identi<sup>fi</sup>cation that seems to reveal one’s real name. We then construct our herding measure (i.e., cumulative lending amount a listing has received at a given time) and decompose it into two based on the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation result. The empirical results suggest that successors demonstrate weaker herding momentum toward predecessors who are presented with real-seeming usernames than anonymous ones. This <sup>fi</sup>nding, which we attribute to a lower extent of perceived credibility derives from a nonconforming behavior (i.e., deviating from others by presenting oneself with a real-seeming username), challenges the conventional wisdom that considers anonymity a negative factor for source credibility. Further, in examining the moderating role of risk-related attributes, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the effect of perceived anonymity on herding is more pronounced in the early stage of the fundraising period. Nonetheless, no such systemic difference is found between listings that differ in credit grades assigned by the platform.

The current research makes several contributions to the literature. First, our study extends prior work dealing with anonymity in the information systems literature. Unlike past work that takes a socially normative perspective and therefore considers perceived anonymity a factor that undermines source credibility (e.g., Forman et al. 2008, Jiang et al. 2013, Burtch et al. 2016), we argue observational learning as a distinct mechanism under which the effect of perceived anonymity can manifest in either direction. Our empirical analyses demonstrate that perceived anonymity intensi<sup>fi</sup>es herding in the debt-based crowdfunding market. Though seemingly surprising, our main <sup>fi</sup>nding is not uncommon. In this regard, we add to the emerging research stream that underscores the bright side of anonymity in a variety of online settings, such as product reviews (Hong and Li 2017) and user-generated content (Lee et al. 2014). Second, prior credibility literature (e.g., Sternthal et al. 1978, Harmon and Coney 1982, Wagenknecht et al. 2016) has largely focused on the effect of source credibility on persuasive communication (direct, initiated by information sources). Our work adds to this literature by showing the possibility that source credibility can also play a role in observational learning (indirect, information receivers are proactive). Furthermore, our work also contributes to the literature stream focusing on the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of herding, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally in online crowdfunding markets (e.g., Zhang and Liu 2012, Jiang et al. 2018). Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide a nuanced yet important understanding of the mechanism that underlies herding behavior. Instead of mimicking precedent transactions in a homogenous manner, successors <sup>fi</sup>rst form different credibility perceptions toward predecessors based on observable personal cues (De Vries and Pruyn 2007); they then use such perceptions to adjust the informational value derived from observational learning accordingly. Results from our extended analysis help better understand that the observed behavior of differentiated herding can vary with some risk-related characteristics that can potentially convey the investment risk associated with a listing.

Our study is closely related to Burtch et al. (2016), who also analyze backers’ behavior on a rewardbased crowdfunding site. In their study context, individual backers can decide whether to conceal their usernames or not at the transaction level. Despite some similarities, our research differs from theirs in three major aspects. First, the two studies explore distinct social dynamics. Burtch et al. (2016) observe a positive association between backers’ decision to conceal identity cues (i.e., usernames), citing social norms as the driving mechanism. However, we investigate the sequential correlation in lenders’ lending amount, arguing herding arises as a result of observational learning. Our extended analysis suggests that listing characteristics that may signal a listing’s risk level can moderate herding, con<sup>fi</sup>rming observational learning, rather than simply mimicking or socially normative behavior, being the mechanism that underlies lenders decision making. Second, the main <sup>fi</sup>ndings are opposite. Their work <sup>fi</sup>nds that early peers’ decision to remain anonymous has a negative in<sup>fl</sup>uence on later backers’ contributions, which pertains to the “I’ve got nothing to hide” argument from the law literature (So love 2007). By contrast, our results suggest that predecessors presented with an anonymous username induce a stronger herding effect, in that such privacypreservation behavior is subject to fewer disapprovals in an online investment community. Finally, their work leverages backers’ choice of information control at the transaction level as a unique contextual feature and helps understand how the effect of identity hiding manifests within a crowdfunding project. Different from that, our research explores the variation in perceived anonymity at the lender level, allowing us to uncover the broader impact of usernames as a persistent personal cue, in a more general, online environment. Taken together, these conceptual and contextual differentiations help explain why the uncovered effects of perceived anonymity are quite different between the two studies.

## 2. Theoretical Background and Hypotheses 2.1. Herding

Herding, de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the tendency for an individual to follow the actions of preceding peers (Banerjee 1992; Bikhchandani et al. 1992, 1998), has drawn growing research attention in online environments. One of the most prominent behavioral mechanisms behind herding is observational learning which describes situations in which people draw inference from mere observations of others’ actions (Zhang 2010). For example, Cai et al. (2009) examine the effect of observational learning on diners’ dish choices in a restaurant setting. They <sup>fi</sup>nd that when diners are presented with information about popular dishes ordered by others, the demand for these dishes increases. Using data from a <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment, Tucker and Zhang (2011) study how the popularity information affects consumers’ choices on a website that lists wedding service vendors. They show that narrow-appeal vendors attract more visits than their broad-appeal counterparts after the introduction of popularity informa tion. Zhang and Liu (2012) investigate whether the observed herding behavior is rational or irrational in microloan markets. Their empirical results reveal that the magnitude of herding is accentuated by unfavorable listing characteristics but attenuated by favorable ones, providing evidence of rational observational learning.

Drawing on observational learning theory, we argue that lenders are observational learners who regard predecessors’ lending transactions as informative signals re<sup>fl</sup>ecting the collective assessment of a listing. Having little to no information about a borrower, each individual lender observes the cumulative amount collectively funded by preceding peers, and uses this information to draw an inference of the listing’s potential risk. As such, we expect that lenders would exhibit a tendency to make a larger lending amount to a listing that has received more funds.

There is a stream of literature that examines the role of early decision makers’ attributes in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing follow-up user activities in various online communities. Research in this stream conveys a same message: predecessors (and their actions) are not perceived as equal by successors. For example, Lee et al. (2015) examine users’ reviewing behavior in response to previously posted ratings on a social movie site. They <sup>fi</sup>nd that moviegoers value ratings posted by their online friends more than those by the crowd. Kim and Viswanathan (2019) <sup>fi</sup>nd a similar differentiation behavior on a crowdfunding platform for mobile applications. For apps in the prerelease stage, investors value preceding peers with app development experience more than those with investment experience; however, they behave conversely for apps that are already being sold in the market. An important cognitive process that rationalizes such differentiation behavior is that individuals tend to gauge <sup>fi</sup>rst the credibility of an information source based on some observable personal and identity cues (Petty et al. 1983); they then use the perceived credibility to adjust the value of the information obtained from the source (Hovland et al. 1953, Ohanian 1990).

## 2.2. The Role of Perceived Anonymity

Prior literature has documented that the anonymity state of information sources may in<sup>fl</sup>uence a beholder’s perception of source credibility (Rains 2007a, b). In most online settings, individuals can freely choose a username, which serves as a unique and persistent personal cue presented to others (e.g., Ma and Agarwal 2007, Forman et al. 2008, Burtch et al. 2016). This role of a username would be even more salient when other identity-related information is unobservable to beholders. Such freedom of username choice grants individuals a means to present themselves, which, in turn, would determine how they are perceived by others in the same community. In our framework, we do not use anonymity as a term to describe a status of whether one’s of<sup>fl</sup>ine identity (i.e., real name) being known (or unknown) to others. Instead, we conceptualize anonymity, or perceived anonymity to be more exact, as the beholder perception that arises upon observing other individuals presenting them selves with a username that seems (or doesn’t seem) to re<sup>fl</sup>ect their real name. This conceptualization is consistent with much prior work that considers perceived anonymity dichotomous (e.g., Postmes and Spears 1998, Hite et al. 2014). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we consider a username either anonymous (associated with a high state of perceived anonymity) or real-seeming (associated with a low state of perceived anonymity). In what follows, we <sup>fi</sup>rst propose a link between the perceived anonymity state of a username and the perceived credibility of the information source. We then use the proposed link to rationalize the role of perceived anonymity in shaping herding behavior in debt-based crowdfunding markets.

Marx (1999) argues that we live in a culture where the norm is that one must be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed, and names anchor us in the world as a “little detail in which big social meanings may reside.” Refusing to disclose such information disregards our culture and makes it dif<sup>fi</sup>- cult for others to assess the credibility of the source (Hovland et al. 1953, Kruglanski et al. 2006). Early work has shown that a high anonymity state undermines the perceived trustworthiness, lowering the perceived credibility of information sources (Hass 1981, Kruglanski et al. 2006). More recent research suggests that the same cognitive process exists in online environments as well. For example, information contributed by anonymous (Forman et al. 2008) or unidenti<sup>fi</sup>ed (Racherla and Friske 2012, Wagenknecht et al. 2016) sources is perceived as less trustful by shoppers in e-commerce settings. Individuals are also found more hesitant to transact when the informational cues about preceding peers are missing, as an absence of personal information results in negative attitudes, such as skepticism (Gneezy et al. 2012) and uncertainty (Burtch et al. 2016). Applying this cogni tive process to our context, we argue that a high state of perceived anonymity is likely to result in a lower extent of perceived credibility, which in turn attenu ates the magnitude of the herding effect.

Perhaps more interestingly, sources presented with a low state of perceived anonymity may not necessarily be viewed as more credible either. Before we proceed to discuss the reasoning, it is useful to underscore the feature that distinguishes our study context from others. In many online settings, such as social media and knowledge-sharing sites, users are motivated, either intrinsically or extrinsically, to present and establish themselves by revealing some identity cues (e.g., using their real names as part of the usernames). On a debt-based crowdfunding platform, by contrast, individual lenders have relatively less incentive to do so. To increase the transparency of the crowdfunding process, most platforms publicize all funding activities (Burtch et al. 2015) at the expense of lender privacy. The high visibility and traceability of a lender’s past transactions may give rise to a worry of being watched by an unauthorized third-party entity, or even a fear of receiving unwanted solicitations (from either friends or strangers) should one’s identity is exposed. Such concerns attribute to a real-world observation that most lenders have a strong desire for privacy preservation and accordingly prefer to present themselves with a high state of anonymity (i.e., through using an anonymous username).<sup>1</sup> As remaining anonymous to lie low emerges as an accepted standard in an investment-oriented community (Xu et al. 2008, Bansal et al. 2016, Pritchard 2020), the behavior of keeping a high pro<sup>fi</sup>le—by voluntarily revealing one’s real name in a username<sup>2</sup>—is likely to be perceived as nonconforming, arousing disapprovals and skepticism from other community members even when no actual harm has occurred (Helweg-Larsen and LoMonaco 2008, Van Kleef et al. 2015). These unfavorable attitudes undermine the perceived credibility of predecessors who are presented with a low state of perceived anonymity, dampening successors’ herding momentum toward them.

The previous discussion proposes that the perceived anonymity of predecessors could pose two opposite effects on source credibility perceived by successors, and which effect prevails may depend on the nature of the context. In determining source credibility, we argue that the negative effect of perceived anonymity plays a predominant role in a general, consumption-oriented context, whereas the positive effect of perceived anonymity manifests as a critical determinant in a more specialized, investment-oriented context. The reasoning is twofold. First, in a consumption-oriented context such as an e-commerce site, users voluntarily contribute to the community (e.g., writing reviews to share product experiences) with motives like altruism and helping the company (Hennig-Thurau et al. 2004). As individuals have an innate need to be appreciated or recognized for their contribution (Ma and Agarwal 2007), they may have an incentive to present themselves with a low state of anonymity such that their identity can be associated with the good reputation they have established. However, this is less unlikely to occur in an investment-oriented context wherein individuals’ core task is to make pro<sup>fi</sup>ts. Since online investors have strong privacy concerns (Xu et al. 2008, Bansal et al. 2016, Pritchard 2020), they are in general more inclined to present themselves with a high state of anonymity. Second, in a consumption-oriented context, the concern of content manipulations (e.g., fake reviews) casts serious doubt on the perceived credibility of user-generated information (Ho et al. 2017), resulting in distrust toward information sources presented with a high state of anonymity. In an investment-oriented context, however, the contents of peer-generated signals (i.e., actual lending amount) are more objective (as op posed to online reviews that tend to be more subjective) and less likely subject to manipulations. This contextual nature diminishes lenders’ reliance on perceived anonymity as a means of assessing source credibility. Bearing this reasoning this mind, we anticipate that the positive effect of perceived anonymity on herding is likely to outweigh its negative effect. Formally, we propose the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 1. The positive relationship between predecessors’ cumulative lending amount and successors’ lending amount toward a listing is stronger for predecessors presented with an anonymous username than those with a real-seeming one.

2.3. The Moderating Role of Risk-Related Factors To facilitate decision making with little information, lenders observe and learn from the decisions made by preceding peers. As we previously argued, during this process, lenders tend to demonstrate dissimilar herding momentum toward predecessors who are presented with different states of perceived anonymity. Given the main purpose of engaging in observational learning is to better evaluate a listing’s potential risk, it is anticipated that lenders may further adjust such a differentiation behavior as the perceived risk level varies across listings. In this subsection, we hypothesize that the predicted effect of perceived anonymity on herding (Hypothesis 1) is likely to be moderated by two listing characteristics that could signal the investment risk to potential lenders: fundraising stage and listing credit grade.

In our context, a listing can materialize into a loan only when the requested amount is reached. Under this mechanism, a listing situated in the early stage of its fundraising period is likely to be regarded as less certain with respect to loan materialization (Burtch et al. 2018, Kuppuswamy and Bayus 2018), compared with the other otherwise identical listing situated in its late fundraising stage. When dealing with such a listing, a prospective lender may tend to rely more on observational learning, making the pre dicted positive effect of perceived anonymity (Hypothesis 1) more pronounced. Consistent with this logic, prior literature on online reviews holds that the uncertainty of a product is related to its life cycle, which then affects the value of the credibility signal conveyed by reviewers (e.g., Yazdani et al. 2018). In addition, a listing in its early fundraising stage receives fewer transactions such that a prospective lender can differentiate predecessors per their anonymity state in a relatively manageable manner. However, as the listing progresses into its late fundraising stage, the uncertainty of loan materialization diminishes and the number of total transactions increases, together weakening lenders’ motivation of engaging in observational learning. Therefore, we expect that the differentiated herding behavior toward distinct predecessor groups would diminish in the late stage of a listing’s fundraising process.

Hypothesis 2 (a). The predicted positive effect of predecessors’ perceived anonymity on herding is weaker in the late fundraising stage than in the early fundraising stage.

To help mitigate information asymmetry, the platform derives and assigns a credit grade to each listing based on various listing attributes as well as the <sup>fi</sup>nancial status of the borrower (Iyer et al. 2015). Such an aggregate measure is found to have the ability to predict loan default (Herzenstein et al. 2008, Zhang and Liu 2012) and thus can signal the potential risk of a listing. As such, a prospective lender may regard a listing that has a low credit grade as riskier with respect to the likelihood of default, ceteris paribus. Given that anonymous predecessors are perceived as more credible in assessing a listing’s potential risk under our theoretical framework (Hypothesis 1), lenders (as observational learners) are likely to pay more attention to their lending decisions when dealing with a listing that might ultimately turn into delinquency or default. By contrast, for a listing that has a high credit grade, prospective lenders would reduce their dependence on the signal originated from different predecessor groups due to reduced concern on the investment risk. Therefore, we anticipate that the predicted positive relationship between predecessor anonymity and herding magnitudes would be stronger on listings that receive low credit grades.

Hypothesis 2 (b). The predicted positive effect of predecessors’ perceived anonymity on herding is stronger on listings with high-risk credit grades than those with low-risk credit grades.

## 3. Methods and Data

## 3.1. Empirical Context

Our research context is one of the leading debt-based crowdfunding platforms in Asia. As of August 2019, the platform has been in operation for nine years, facilitating nearly 1.67 million transactions. In what follows, we provide the details about the lending process and the relevant information from a prospective lender’s perspective.

To participate in any activity on the platform, an individual <sup>fi</sup>rst needs to register for an account and choose a username. The platform imposes nearly no naming restriction such that a new user can freely use a combination of Chinese characters, English letters, and numbers as a username. The lending process starts with a borrower submitting a loan application, in which the borrower is required to provide informa tion such as the requested amount, the loan duration, the <sup>fi</sup>xed interest rate the borrower would like to offer, the borrower’s age, as well as the listing title. The borrower could also voluntarily disclose additional personal information such as education, marriage, employment, and income. Loan applications approved by the platform would turn into listings for prospective lenders’ browsing. For an interested listing, a prospective lender can observe the listing- and borrower-speci<sup>fi</sup>c characteristics in the top section of the listing’s main page. In the bottom section of the same page, the lender can view detailed information about all previous lending transactions, including the preceding lender’s username, the lending amount, and the timestamp of each transaction.<sup>3</sup> Unlike other crowdfunding sites on which the transactional information is partially available (e.g., the context studied in Burtch et al. 2016), the entire transaction log is displayed on a single page for easy browsing. This contextual feature provides us an ideal setting to quantify how a successor’s lending amount is associated with the cumulative lending amount collectively made by predecessors.

## 3.2. Data Description

We collect data from all listings that were posted on the platform from March to August in 2015.<sup>4</sup> To make our sample comparable to those posted on other major sites (e.g., LendingClub and Prosper), we restrict our attention to listings that were requested online and were not guaranteed by of<sup>fl</sup>ine institutions.<sup>5</sup> We also restrict our sample to listings that received at least two transactions. The resulting data set for our main analysis contains 3,504 listings, which attracted a total of 64,925 transactions from 17,816 distinct lenders. We construct our panel data at the transaction level. For each transaction, we observe characteristics at the listing, the borrower, and the transaction level. Listing characteristics include the requested amount, the interest rate, the loan duration, and the credit grade. Borrower characteristics include each borrower’s username, demographic information, and the lending history on the platform. Transaction characteristics include the lender’s username, the lending amount, the sequential index of the transaction associated with a given loan, and the channel through which the transaction is made (desktop vs. mobile).

Next, we discuss how perceived anonymity is operationalized and how it affects the perception of beholders. In the spirit of prior work on online anonymity (Nissenbaum 1999, Qian and Scott 2007, Tsikerdekis 2012), we ask research assistants to label all usernames appearing in our data as either real-seeming or anonymous.<sup>6</sup> Based on the theoretical background provided in Section 2, we argue that distinct types of usernames may carry dissimilar informational values, which may lead to heterogeneous perceptions held by beholders (Hite et al. 2014)—the primary focus of this research.

It is imperative for us to emphasize that the objective of this research is to explore the informational role of perceived anonymity in shaping the perceived source credibility from a subsequent lender’s perspective. Although it is likely for a user to choose a realseeming username that doesn’t match the user’s real name, such a scenario is less of a concern in our research framework. Since the platform discloses no identity cue about a lender, there is no way for other users to discern whether a real-seeming username indeed re<sup>fl</sup>ects the focal lender’s real name. As such, a subsequent lender is likely to regard predecessors who are presented with real-seeming usernames as the same kind, and consequently forms the same attitude toward them. To see if lenders demonstrate such differentiation behavior in our data, we calculate the partial correlation between the lending amount of a transaction and the cumulative lending amount made by different predecessor groups given the percentage of the unfunded amount. The correlation is positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant for the all-predecessor group (0.052, $p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ). If we split all predecessors based on their username types, we see correlations deviating to some extent, with 0.043 for the anonymous group $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and 0.023 for the real-seeming group $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$

## 3.3. Model Specification and Estimation

A straightforward test of herding is to look for the sequential correlation in the lending amount. If herding exists, we should expect to see a positive correlation between (1) the lending amount of a given transaction and (2) the cumulative lending amount the listing received prior to that transaction. Such examination of transaction-level dynamics based on the sequential order is commonly used in studying herding (Banerjee 1992, Bikhchandani et al. 1992, Zhang 2010). Leverag ing the detailed transaction log, we can observe the sequential order of all transactions and construct our herding measure—the cumulative lending amount made to the same listing up to any given point of time.

We next specify our amount model. Let i denote the lender and j denote the listing. The logarithm of the lending amount of the tth transaction made to listing j by lender $i , y _ { i j t } ,$ is modeled as:

$$
y _ {i j t} = \alpha + \beta Y _ {j, t - 1} + X _ {j t} \gamma_ {1} + Z _ {j} \gamma_ {2} + W _ {i} \gamma_ {3} + \lambda_ {w} + \varepsilon_ {i j t},\tag{1}
$$

where $t = 2 , \dots , T$ and T is the total number of transactions for listing j, and $Y _ { j , t - 1 }$ denotes the logarithm of the cumulative lending amount listing j has received prior to the arrival of its tth transaction. The parameter of interest is $\beta ,$ which captures the magnitude of herding. A positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant estimate of $\beta$ will indicate that subsequent lenders’ lending amount is increasing in the cumulative lending amount made to the same listing, con<sup>fi</sup>rming the existence of herding.

The time-variant listing characteristics $X _ { j t }$ include the cumulative number of transactions prior to the current transaction (Lag Num of Transactions, prior to t), the time gap between two consecutive transactions (Time Interval), and whether the transaction is made through a desktop (Lender Device). To control for the payoff externality effect at the listing level, we include the percentage of the unfunded amount prior to the current transaction (Lag Percent Needed) in $X _ { j t } . \mathrm { ~ A ~ }$ dummy variable is also included to indicate whether a listing is situated in its late fundraising stage if the percentage of the unfunded amount is less than 50% (Late Fundraising Stage 1). Time-invariant listing and borrower characteristics $Z _ { j }$ includes Amount Requested, Interest Rate, Loan Duration, Credit Risky, Paid Loans, Overdue Loans, Age, Education, Marriage, Employment Length, Monthly Income, and a dummy variable indicating the borrower perceived anonymity (Real Sm Borrower). We include in W a dummy variable that indicates the lender perceived anonymity (Real Sm Lender). We also insert week <sup>fi</sup>xed effects $\lambda _ { w } ,$ which helps capture potential unobserved temporal dynamics be yond the listing level. The error term is $\varepsilon _ { i j t } .$

To further differentiate the heterogeneous herding effects induced by different groups of predecessors, we decompose $\boldsymbol { Y } _ { j , t - 1 }$ and use $Y _ { j , t - 1 } ^ { a }$ and $Y _ { j , t - 1 } ^ { r }$ to denote the logarithm of the cumulative lending amount made by predecessors who present themselves with anonymous and real-seeming usernames, respectively.

The relative magnitude of these two coef<sup>fi</sup>cients will inform us of the role of perceived anonymity in lenders’ observational learning process.

Multiple Sources of Unobserved Heterogeneity. Our amount model so far explores herding at the transaction level. However, the variation in lending amounts could be further attributed to some higher-level sources of variation where each transaction resides. For example, there could be unobserved heterogeneity beyond the observable listing characteristics we have controlled for. Similarly, lenders with different characteristics may demonstrate different funding behavior. Ignoring these unobserved heterogeneities could lead to an inconsistent estimation and misleading inference. To deal with this concern, we use a cross-classi<sup>fi</sup>ed multilevel modeling approach (Gelman and Hill 2006, Rasbash and Browne 2008). In our data set, we observe transactions made to different listings from distinct lenders. Thus, transactions can be viewed as lowest-level units nested within two higher levels: listings and lenders. Listings and lenders are not nested within each other (i.e., crossclassi<sup>fi</sup>ed), because neither all transactions made by a given lender are made to the same listing, nor do all transactions made to a given listing come from the same lender. Accordingly, we enhance (1) and (2) by inserting lender-speci<sup>fi</sup>c intercepts (η ) and listing-speci<sup>fi</sup>c intercepts (η ) to better control for unobserved heterogeneity at the lender and listing level, respectively.

Participation Decisions of Lenders. Another concern is that our analysis of lending amount does not consider individuals who do not participate in lending (\$0). Potential selection bias may arise if factors that affect a lender’s choice of which listing to participate also affect the lender’s decision of how much to lend. The following steps are taken to address this concern. First, for each lender who made at least one transaction on a given day, we construct the lender’s consideration set using all listings that were active on that day.<sup>8</sup> Then, we adopt a two-stage estimation procedure (Heckman 1979) to correct the potential selection bias. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage, we model an individua lender’s decision of whether to participate in a listing (in the lender’s consideration set) using a standard probit speci<sup>fi</sup>cation. We refer to this speci<sup>fi</sup>cation as the participation model. The dependent variable here is a vector of dummy variables indicating whether a lender participates in a listing, and the independent variables include corresponding listing and lender characteristics. To satisfy the exclusion restriction, we include the maximum title length of other listings, that is, all except the focal listing, in a lender’s consideration set (Max Title Length). This variable choice can be justi<sup>fi</sup>ed by lenders’ decision-making processes. When a prospective lender chooses which listing to participate, the lender is likely to evaluate all listings in the consideration set in an exploratory manner (akin to online shoppers’ behavior discussed by Janiszewski 1998). At this stage, useful soft information can be inferred from listing titles to help grasp a general impression about a speci<sup>fi</sup>c listing, and to also allow a quick comparison with plausible alternatives of which the listing with the maximum title length is likely a good representative. When proceeding to the next stage to determine a lending amount, the lender will evaluate a focal listing’s attributes in a more deliberate manner, focusing on detailed information posted on that listing’s main page. The information about other listings, though not directly displayed, is not expected to play a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in this decision. Hence, the maximum title length of alternative listings only indirectly affects the lending amount through the participation decision. To further justify our variable choice from a statistical perspective, we follow the strategy of Kumar et al. (2018) and include Max Title Length in our models to test the previous justi<sup>fi</sup>cation. The results indicate that the exclusion restriction is well satis<sup>fi</sup>ed: the maximum title length of alternative listings has good explanatory power in the participation model (reported in Table 2) but turns insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant in the amount model (reported in Table A1 of the online appendix). Finally, we compute the inverse Mill’s ratios $( \hat { \lambda } _ { i j } )$ at the lender-listing level and include them as an additional variable in the amount model for the secondstage estimation.

Based on the previous discussion, we can rewrite the full model for our herding analysis as:

$$
\begin{array}{l} y _ {i j t} = \alpha + \beta Y _ {j, t - 1} + X _ {j t} \gamma_ {1} + Z _ {j} \gamma_ {2} + W _ {i} \gamma_ {3} + \gamma_ {4} \hat {\lambda} _ {i j} + \eta_ {i} + \eta_ {j} \\ \quad + \lambda_ {w} + \varepsilon_ {i j t}, \end{array} \tag {3}
$$

where $\eta _ { i } \sim N ( 0 , \sigma _ { \eta _ { i } } ^ { 2 } )$ and $\eta _ { i } \sim N ( 0 , \sigma _ { \eta _ { i } } ^ { 2 } )$ . The means for both parameters are zeros as any nonzero mean will be absorbed into α. $\sigma _ { \eta _ { i } } ^ { 2 }$ and $\check { \sigma } _ { \eta _ { j } } ^ { 2 }$ are the variance parameters that re<sup>fl</sup>ect the degree of heterogeneity at the lender and listing level, respectively.

Our <sup>fi</sup>nding challenges the conventional wisdom concerning how anonymity is perceived in the eye of beholders. A predominant notion that has long been used to justify the negative effect of anonymity is that one must be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed (Marx 1999). Extensive literature has documented that anonymity provides few cues about the information source and therefore would undermine the trustworthiness perceived by others (Postmes et al. 2001, Jiang et al. 2013). In contrast to this point of view, our results lend empirical support to a competing argument in which anonymity poses a positive impact on source credibility. As we previously argued, this seemingly counterintuitive <sup>fi</sup>nding is quite reasonable due to lenders’ strong desire for privacy in an investment-oriented context (Xu et al. 2008, Bansal et al. 2016, Pritchard 2020) like debtbased crowdfunding. Our <sup>fi</sup>nding complements an emerging stream of research that uncovers the positive role of perceived anonymity in various online settings, such as the trustworthiness of online reviews (Hong and Li 2017), quality of online posts (Lee et al. 2014), and online charity giving (Peacey and Sanders 2014). Moreover, we show that the effect of perceived anonymity on herding is further moderated by riskrelated characteristics of various listings. This <sup>fi</sup>nding supports the established notion that lenders are rational observational learners who use observable attributes to moderate their perception of preceding peers credibility (Zhang and Liu 2012), endorsing observational learning as the behavioral mechanism that drives herding.

## 4.1. Robustness Checks

In this section, we perform a series of robustness checks. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we use an alternative measure for herding, include additional listing sample, account for serial correlation at the listing level, employ generalized method of moments (GMM) estimators, and estimate <sup>fi</sup>xed-effects models. The consistent parameter estimates demonstrate the robustness of our main <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

4.1.1. Alternative Herding Measure. In our main analysis, we use the logarithm of the cumulative lending amount made to a listing as our herding measure. To rule out the possibility that the uncovered heterogeneous herding effects are attributed to a potential change in the base of two predecessor groups, we use the logarithm of the average lending amount of previous transactions in this robustness check. Following a similar procedure, we decompose this alternative herding measure according to the group membership. As column (1) of Table 4 presents, the new results corroborate our main <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

4.1.2. Alternative Listing Sample. As previously discussed in Section 3.2, we restrict our sample to listings that are comparable to those available on other major platforms. In this robustness check, we re-estimate our models using the whole sample, which contains all listings posted during our sample period (i.e., including listings that are requested of<sup>fl</sup>ine and guaranteed by of<sup>fl</sup>ine institutions). The results, reported in column (2) of Table 4, are largely consistent with our main analysis.

4.1.3. First-Order Autoregression. So far, we assume that there is no serial correlation among error terms. If this assumption is violated, error terms would be correlated with lagged independent variables, introducing bias into the estimation. To address this potential issue, we <sup>fi</sup>t multilevel models that allow for <sup>fi</sup>rstorder autoregressive correlation structure for residuals at the listing level. As reported in column (1) of Table 5, despite changes in magnitude, the coef<sup>fi</sup>cients of our key independent variables continue to support our main <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

4.1.4. Multilevel GMM. If the random intercepts in our multilevel models are correlated with the herding measure, our estimation could be biased due to the endogeneity stemming from such correlations. To address this concern, we <sup>fi</sup>t models using the multilevel generalized method of moments (GMM) estimators (Kim and Frees 2007). The results are robust, as reported in column (2) of Table 5.

4.1.5. Reflection Problem. The re<sup>fl</sup>ection problem (Manski 1993) describes that the observed social interactions can arise from three sources: endogenous effects, exogenous (contextual) effects, and correlated effects. To identify social in<sup>fl</sup>uence (such as herding in our study) that arises from endogenous effects, researchers must account for the other two sources, that is, exogenous and correlated effects. Failure to control those two effects could lead to endogenous group formation due to homophily (Kim and Viswanathan 2019).

Put in our research framework, exogenous (contextual) effects could arise when the observed herding is caused by the shared characteristics at the listing level. For example, the correlated behavior between a predecessor and a successor may be driven by some unobserved listing characteristics, such as project type. In this case, the inclusion of listing <sup>fi</sup>xed effects can help control for homophily driven by listing-related factors (Kim and Viswanathan 2019). On the other hand, correlated effects could also arise when the observed correlated behavior is caused by similarity among lenders. For example, the correlated behavior between a predecessor and a successor may be driven by some unobserved lender characteristics, such as disposable income. In this case, adding lender <sup>fi</sup>xed effects can help control for homophily driven by lender-related factors (Burtch et al. 2016). Following the strategy employed by Kim and Viswanathan (2019) and Burtch et al. (2016), we conducted a robustness check by including <sup>fi</sup>xed effects at both the listing and lender level. As shown in Table 6, the observed differentiated herding behavior toward distinct predecessor groups continues to prevail.

## 4.2. Additional Analyses

We conduct additional analyses that may help researchers further understand or expand our study.<sup>9</sup> Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we differentiate the herding effect from earlier or recent predecessors, account for the sentiment contained in usernames, and consider a subsequent lender’s investment history during our observation period.

4.2.1. Primacy or Recency Effects. Prior literature suggests that individuals tend to remember information that is presented at the beginning (primacy) and end (recency) of a list (e.g., Haugtvedt and Wegener 1994, Murphy et al. 2017). In our context, if the primacy effect or the recency effect exists, lenders may rely more on the easiest or the most recent transactions appearing in the transaction log. To account for this possibility, we re-estimate (4) by investigating the role of the (<sup>fi</sup>ve or 10) earliest transactions (reported in Table A3 of the online appendix) and the (<sup>fi</sup>ve or 10) most recent preceding transactions prior to a given lender funding activity (reported in Table A4 of the online appendix). The results support our main <sup>fi</sup>ndings that successors exhibit a stronger herding behavior toward predecessors presented with an anonymous username than those with a real-seeming one.

4.2.2. Sentiment in Usernames. ## 5. Conclusion

Online crowdfunding provides researchers an opportunity to gain new insights into the role of perceived anonymity in herding. This research pursues such an inquiry based on the data collected from a leading debt-based crowdfunding platform. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that subsequent lenders demonstrate stronger herding momentum toward preceding peers who present themselves with an anonymous username than those with a real-seeming one. This <sup>fi</sup>nding, which we attribute to a lower extent of perceived credibility resulting from a nonconforming behavior, challenges the conventional wisdom that considers anonymity a negative factor for source credibility. An extended analysis further reveals that the positive effect of perceived anonymity on herding is accentuated in the early fundraising stage. Nonetheless, we <sup>fi</sup>nd no differentiated herding effect between listings that have high-risk and low-risk grades.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings complement prior research on herding. Recent work <sup>fi</sup>nds empirical evidence of herding in various contexts, such as dish choices from diners, kidney refusals from patients, and lending activities from lenders (Cai et al. 2009, Zhang 2010, Herzenstein et al. 2011). Some research further identi<sup>fi</sup>es factors that may affect the magnitude of herding, such as network ties (Lee et al. 2015), listing attributes (Zhang and Liu 2012), platform attributes, and government-imposed regulations (Jiang et al. 2018). We show that subsequent lenders may exhibit dissimilar extents of herding behavior toward predecessors who are presented with different states of perceived anonymity. Such a difference is more prominent under high-risk circum stances, such as the early stage of the fundraising period. Besides, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings also complement the emerging stream of literature that identi<sup>fi</sup>es the positive role of perceived anonymity in a variety of settings, such as trustworthiness of online reviews (Hong and Li 2017), quality of online posts (Lee et al. 2014), and so on.

Our main <sup>fi</sup>ndings lead to actionable recommendations for managers of debt-based crowdfunding platforms. For example, the role of the uncovered user heterogeneity with respect to perceived credibility (manifested in observable personal cues such as usernames) in herding should be better utilized to facilitate lenders’ decision making. To illustrate, we show that predecessors who are presented with anonymous usernames in our case are perceived as more credible and hence induce a stronger herding magnitude from their successors. Accordingly, to encourage subsequent lending activities (in amount), web designers can consider displaying previous transactions made by those lenders in a more salient fashion (e.g., listing them on the top of the transaction log), rather than list all prior transactions in chronological order as is currently done. This new design feature is also likely to be useful in mitigating information overload on the lenders’ side, particularly when they face listings that have received a large number of transactions. Moreover, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the previous heterogeneity is contingent on the fundraising stage. During the fundraising period, a prospective lender may face varying degrees of risk, and hence may have different needs for peer-generated lending information (<sup>fi</sup>nd signals) and peer differentiation (<sup>fi</sup>nd credible signals). Crowdfunding platforms should be cognizant of such needs and develop information-disclosure strategies that provide useful risk-related information to facilitate lenders’ decision making. For example, though the measure of fundraising progress is usually constructed as the percentage of the requested amount that is funded, platforms could develop an alternative measure by incorporating additional information such as the number of days left. Meanwhile, in designing the fundraising progress bar, platforms could use different colors to highlight different fundraising stages (e.g., a dark color for early and late fundraising stages). Such a design may enable lenders to quickly get the fundraising progress information.

Empirical results from this work also have important implications for borrowers who desire to seek money from the crowd. For example, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that lenders are less prone to participate in listings that offer high interest rates, perhaps because they rationally associate a high return to a high level of risk. As such, borrowers who are in urgent need of money should refrain from overcommitting to interest rates that are much higher than market prices as this strategic move is likely to back<sup>fi</sup>re in the end. To encourage participation from the crowd, borrowers instead should exert effort in providing more information about their listings, at least in the length of listing title as our empirical results suggest.

This work has some limitations that can point directions for future research. As with most empirical studies of crowdfunding, we have limited information about individual lenders. For example, in controlling for lenders’ choice of listings, we construct lenders consideration sets based on their transaction records within a <sup>fi</sup>xed time period (e.g., one day). Future research may leverage data on lenders’ browsing history to accurately recover their consideration set at the time of choice, accounting for lenders’ selection of listings in a more precise manner. To mitigate the concern about the re<sup>fl</sup>ection problem, we carry out <sup>fi</sup>xedeffects models as an alternative speci<sup>fi</sup>cation (similar to Kim and Viswanathan 2019 and Burtch et al. 2016) and con<sup>fi</sup>rm that our results are robust. This strategy can largely account for the correlated behavior in crowdfunding settings like ours, where investors release little personal information and have little room for direct communication or information sharing (as discussed by Kim and Viswanathan 2019). When demographic and geographic data are accessible in other settings, researchers may also account for the potential correlation among lenders according to other model speci<sup>fi</sup>cations (e.g., a spatial autoregressive form).<sup>10</sup> Further, in addition to the perceived anonymity that is present in our setting, perceived expertise may also play a role in forming the perceptions about source credibility (Hovland et al. 1953, Ohanian 1990) in oth er online environments (e.g., Kim and Viswanathan 2019). Researchers may explore whether and to what extent observation learning is induced by source credibility stemming from peer expertise based on detailed information on peers’ knowledge and skills (self-reported or platform-generated).

To conclude, our work is among the <sup>fi</sup>rst empirical studies to investigate the effect of online anonymity on subsequent lenders’ herding behavior in a debtbased crowdfunding platform. Our <sup>fi</sup>nding regarding online anonymity challenges the common wisdom that considers anonymity a negative factor for source credibility. This <sup>fi</sup>nding would seem to be particularly relevant in similar investment-oriented contexts, such as social lending. Because individuals face great risks in such contexts, they may actively engage in sophisticated cognitive processing of peer anonymity. Perhaps more broadly, however our work highlights the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of understanding online anonymity through the lens of observational learning. Observational learning has become increasingly important in individual decision making in online environments due to improved visibility and traceability. Considering this trend, our work may inspire further interest in understanding the role of online anonymity and uncovering interesting behavioral mechanisms in other settings where a similar observational learning mechanism has a strong presence, such as online reviews, online health, or online education.
