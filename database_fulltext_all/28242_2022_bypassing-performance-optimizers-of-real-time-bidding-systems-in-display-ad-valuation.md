# Bypassing Performance Optimizers of Real Time Bidding Systems in Display Ad Valuation

Abstract. Demand-side platforms (DSPs) that purchase digital ad space using real-time bidding (RTB) systems employ “black-box” performance optimizers to adjust bids at run time. Advertisers using <sup>fi</sup>eld experiments to estimate the marginal value of display ads need to contend with the selective targeting of users by optimizers that adjust bids to target users with a greater propensity to respond favorably (i.e., click or conversion). In this paper, we propose an alternative approach for advertisers who choose to bypass their DSP’s performance optimizers for the purpose of assessing the value of their ads. We show that external frequency caps that set upper limits on the number of ad impressions outside the purview of bidding algorithms can serve as a suitable instrumental variable. Eliminating performance optimizers allows the advertiser to value ads without relying on the support services of the DSP with the added bene<sup>fi</sup>t of a broader customer reach and a markedly lower cost. As the focal advertiser disables performance optimizers, any overbidding or underbidding vis-a-vis competition that employs them results in a negative correlation between the\` numbers of ad impressions won and their underlying quality in real time. Using two large-scale randomized <sup>fi</sup>eld experiments in different geographies (United States and Asia) and different devices (PC and mobile), we validate the proposed approach and report a positive effect of ad impression count after adjusting for net negative bias.

## Introduction

Despite the continuing growth in the global adoption of real-time bidding<sup>1</sup> (RTB) for digital ad campaigns, a key challenge facing advertisers is their dif<sup>fi</sup>culty in computing the private value of incremental ad impressions in order to optimize their campaigns and prevent suboptimal spending. A variety of biases underlie the problem of accurately measuring a display ad’s effectiveness from an advertiser’s perspective. Recent literature has examined two broad categories of bias associated with the estimation of the marginal impact of ads, the customer activity bias and the selective targeting bias (Hoban and Bucklin 2015, Johnson et al. 2017a, Hoban and Arora 2018, Sahni et al. 2019).

Customer activity bias occurs when speci<sup>fi</sup>c customer segments self-select to receive ad impressions owing to unobserved individual-speci<sup>fi</sup>c characteristics such as the amount of time spent online and their propensity to search before purchase. Selective targeting bias ensues when market participants target a speci<sup>fi</sup>c segment of customers based on their past purchase behavior, browsing behavior, or past exposure to ads. The state of-the-art methods of valuation correcting for these biases include randomized <sup>fi</sup>eld experiments that employ a variety of approaches to identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, such as the use of public service announcements (PSAs; Hoban and Bucklin 2015), ghost ads (Johnson et al. 2017a, Lewis and Wong 2018), and tagged campaigns with frequency cap treatments (Sahni et al. 2019).

The recent advances in approaches to valuation notwithstanding, major impediments remain for advertisers from an implementation perspective. The demand-side platforms (DSPs) that execute the auctions employ performance optimizers to achieve prespeci<sup>fi</sup>ed goals such as maximizing click or conversion probability in real time, reaching a speci<sup>fi</sup>c number of impressions, or a spending limit, among others, unless they are explicitly bypassed by the advertiser. Performance optimizers are automated machine learning–based algorithms that in<sup>fl</sup>uence the bidding mechanism in terms of both the decision to bid when an ad slot becomes available at any given time and the actual size of the bid. Consequently, the DSPs’ performance optimizers remain a dominant source of bias even if the advertisers refrain from selectively targeting their customers as part of their advertising campaigns.

Furthermore, as advertisers typically rely on their DSPs to run their campaigns, the cost of support services for a valuation exercise can be prohibitive for small-scale advertisers. For instance, the implementation of a PSAbased controlled experiment entails signi<sup>fi</sup>cant cost owing to the showing of PSAs instead of targeted ads. The implementation of a ghost-ad-based controlled experiment requires that the advertiser adopt a platform that can support it (e.g., DoubleClick).<sup>2</sup>

In this paper, we propose and demonstrate an alternative approach to valuation that explicitly bypasses the DSP’s performance optimizers. In this regard, we partnered with advertisers<sup>3</sup> whose express goals were to assess the marginal value of their ads by directly specifying bid values unaltered by the DSP during the valuation exercise. We randomly assign users to a range of external frequency caps and use the exogenous variation in frequency caps for the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of causal impact in a two-stage least squares (2SLS) framework. Frequency caps are outside the purview of bidding algorithms and serve to stop the bidding process once the user reaches the cap, a preset upper limit for the number of impressions.

We rely on a simple bidding rule comprising a <sup>fi</sup>xed bid amount throughout the experimental duration and compensate the DSP based on the actual cost of impressions as opposed to the cost per click (CPC) or other performance measure. We ensure that the DSP’s optimizers do not alter the <sup>fi</sup>xed bid amount at run time. As a result, the bids are completely independent of users, their responses during the experimental duration, any past impressions they received, or any real-time changes to the content of the ad hosts<sup>4</sup> within publishers. This also ensures a broad reach of unique customers in terms of ad impressions. In addition to lowering the cost of implementation, our approach eliminates the prospect of selective targeting bias at the outset.

We report results from two large-scale <sup>fi</sup>eld experiments in which we collaborated with advertisers in different geographies (United States and Asia) involving different types of devices (mobile and desktop) to demonstrate the validity and usefulness of this approach. The results from our analyses indicate a bias-adjusted and robust positive incremental impact of ad impressions with satiation in line with recent <sup>fi</sup>ndings employing other techniques such as PSAs and ghost ads. However, we report a strong net negative bias using ordinary least squares (OLS) estimates pertaining to the underlying data sets. The negative bias persists even after accounting for user <sup>fi</sup>xed effects and publisher- and time-related controls.

We suggest that the net negative bias can be largely attributed to two different theoretical mechanisms. First, as our approach disables performance optimizers, it simultaneously jeopardizes the focal DSP’s ability to assess the probability of a favorable customer response and adjust bids accordingly in real-time. This competitive disadvantage vis-a-vis competing DSPs, which use\` their own “black-box” performance optimizers, contributes to a negative correlation between the quantity of ad impressions won by the focal DSP and the underlying quality of those purchased impressions. We refer to this type of bias as the quantity–quality (QQ) bias. Second, prior empirical literature has suggested that timevarying customer activity may contribute to negative bias under circumstances where customer response is negatively correlated with time spent online (Hoban and Bucklin 2015).

This article contributes to the information systems (IS) and related literature pertaining to both digital advertising through auctions and the assessment of a digital ad campaign’s performance (e.g., Liu et al. 2010, Abhishek and Hosanegar 2013, Li et al. 2013, Abhishek et al. 2015, Gong et al. 2018). Moreover, we document the unintended consequence of a key information technology artefact, the performance optimizer, within a multistakeholder ecosystem, an important research prerogative in IS literature (Orlikowski and Iacono 2001, Majchrzak et al. 2016).

We describe and demonstrate an easy-to-implement approach to identifying the incremental impact of a digital ad beyond the <sup>fi</sup>rst exposure in RTB by using frequency caps. In contrast to the use of contextual variables as suitable instruments (Ghose and Todri-Adamopolous 2016), frequency caps serve as strong instruments by design. This approach is not only suitable for advertisers that cannot afford relatively expensive alternatives such as PSAs and ghost ads, but also for advertisers who particularly wish to eliminate all forms of customer targeting, including those employed by the DSPs in real time.

Moreover, we highlight a new type of negative bias, the QQ bias, related to discrepancies in the competitive performance of the focal DSP vis-a-vis competing DSPs\` that employ proprietary performance optimizers. QQ bias is, by de<sup>fi</sup>nition, a negative bias unlike other sources of bias, which can be either positive or negative. We believe that QQ bias is an important addition to the taxonomy of biases related to valuation of an ad impression under the RTB environment. Empirical researchers should anticipate it when advertisers resort to DSPindependent approaches to valuation or choose to collaborate with DSPs that do not possess the sophistication of industry leaders.

We organize the rest of this article as follows. In the following section, we provide the research background, citing relevant literature. We then describe our empirical approach and contrast it with other contemporary methods. Subsequently, we describe the design of our <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment followed by empirical analysis and the explication of the results, where we particularly contrast our estimates with OLS regression analysis. Finally, we provide a general discussion, where we elaborate on the theoretical mechanisms underlying the observed biases followed by implications of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings for advertisers and academic researchers.

## Background and Literature

Previous research has used a variety of empirical approaches to valuing display ads that do not rely on collaboration with industry partners, such as surveybased controlled experiments, natural experiments, and the use of suitable instrumental variables (Goldfarb and Tucker 2011a, b; Bart et al. 2014; Lewis and Nguyen 2015; Ghose and Todri-Adamopolous 2016). However, the use of large-scale <sup>fi</sup>eld experiments in collaboration with advertisers and their DSPs has been particularly fruitful given the adoption of experimental approaches by practitioners.

## Field Experiments for Display-Ad Valuation

Hoban and Bucklin (2015) used PSAs as controls in a <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment to account for several sources of bias, including targeting algorithms. However, in the RTB environment, automated performance optimizers are likely to treat PSAs differently from regular ad impressions, thereby confounding the experiment. To counter this, Johnson et al. (2017a) proposed the concept of ghost ads as a suitable alternative, where users in the control group are exposed to the next best alternative as the buyer forfeits the opportunity to serve an impression, albeit after recording the winning event. More recently, Sahni et al. (2019) used frequency caps, which are upper limits to the number of impressions for a given customer, as experimental treatments. They show how tagged campaigns where regular ads are tagged (combined) with PSAs such that the customers who received PSAs but were also assigned to a frequency cap of zero (i.e., no exposure) can be used as a control group.

Irrespective of the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation strategies used, researchers employing <sup>fi</sup>eld experiments have reported a positive main effect for the impact of display ads contingent on factors such as past customer behavior (Rutz et al. 2012), customers’ product preferences (Lambrecht and Tucker 2013), product category (Goldfarb and Tucker 2011a, b), and customers’ stage in the purchase funnel (Hoban and Bucklin 2015, Sahni et al. 2019). Furthermore, the direction of bias in the underlying data comprising endogenous impressions has been largely positive, with the rare exception of Hoban and Bucklin (2015). For example, Sahni et al. (2019) report a particularly large positive bias in the range of 100%–200%.

## Types of Biases

Extant empirical literature does not provide clear guidance on the nature and relative contribution of the different types of biases. Researchers employing <sup>fi</sup>eld experiments have attributed the net bias irrespective of its valence to a combination of two broad classes of bias, namely, customer activity bias and selective targeting bias. Hoban and Arora (2018) suggest that selective targeting alone may contribute to roughly two-thirds of the total bias using a large-scale secondary data analysis.

Heterogeneous customer activity can introduce bias in the estimation of digital ad effectiveness in at least two ways. First, customers differ widely in when and how long they are available online. Naturally, the number of exposures to digital ads will be dependent on customer availability online. Second, prospective customers of a certain product type may choose to browse websites related to the product prior to purchase. For example, prospective online furniture shoppers may have visited websites related to interior design and furniture review. Consequently, the number of ad exposures on such websites may be more re<sup>fl</sup>ective of customer willingness to buy the product.

Customer activity bias has been cited as a possible theoretical reason for both a net positive and net negative bias (Hoban and Bucklin 2015, Johnson et al. 2017a). One way to address activity bias is to estimate the marginal value of an ad by explicitly accounting for individual customer-level variation such as by using customer <sup>fi</sup>xed effects in regression models. However, this type of bias cannot be eliminated in its entirety when customer activity is time varying. Discrete time controls such as the time of day, day of week, etc., are useful yet imperfect.

Unlike customer self-selection, the auction participants in RTB frequently match ad recipients and the frequency of digital ads as part of their customized ad campaigns. Advertisers frequently target previous purchasers to upsell or cross-sell their products, in cases of “positive targeting.” Similarly, they may choose to reduce their spending on recent purchasers and instead target ad spending on acquiring new customers, a case of “negative targeting.” The design of a <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment can eliminate selective targeting arising from advertiser’s customization of the campaign by ensuring that all customers are treated equally.

Although selective targeting bias has been cited as a possible source of net positive bias in recent literature (Johnson et al. 2017a, Lewis and Wong 2018, Sahni et al. 2019), the contributing role of the DSP’s performance optimizer and its implication for experimental design has not been addressed adequately. Although the different designs of <sup>fi</sup>eld experiments in the current literature show important adaptations contingent on the purposes of their implementation, a common aspect is their reliance on automated performance optimizers in their bidding processes.

## The Role of Performance Optimizers

In an RTB environment, DSPs often use machine learning algorithms that optimize the bidding performance during the execution of the campaign and simultaneously maximize pro<sup>fi</sup>ts for the platform depending on the bidding goal chosen by the advertiser. DSPs typically maintain the implementation of these optimizers as proprietary information, as they provide an important competitive advantage in the digital ad market.<sup>5</sup> Such performance optimizers use a variety of information pertaining to customer behavior to compute customer propensity to enact a desired behavior (e.g., click) in real time, which they use to selectively target customers. The resulting positive correlation between the number of ad impressions and customer response serves as a natural explanation for the net positive bias, a case of selective targeting introduced by the automated algorithm.

As many advertisers seek to move their end-to-end ad campaign management in-house,<sup>6</sup> the computation of return on investments becomes central to custom designing their campaigns. However, the reliance on the DSPs’ performance optimizers for executing ad campaigns is detrimental to computing the marginal value of an ad. Even as the performance optimizers seek to optimize for goals speci<sup>fi</sup>ed by the advertisers (e.g., clicks), they are not suitable for identifying causal linkages between ad impressions and the desired customer behaviors owing to the aforementioned selective targeting bias. Consequently, they can impede advertisers seeking to develop and estimate simpler explanatory models that can inform the design of ad campaigns, such as, for example, the ideal number of ad impressions to retarget a recent customer.

The use of performance optimizers can lead to other unintended consequences for advertisers availing the services of a DSP. First, DSPs can limit the type of bidding contracts for advertisers such as CPC-only contracts or cost-per-mille impressions (CPM) contracts without the manual-bidding option. This can lead to a misalignment of campaign goals if the advertiser seeks to maximize a speci<sup>fi</sup>c type of customer response while the performance optimizer seeks to maximize a different type of response such as the number of clicks or impressions. Second, any attempt by the DSPs to optimize for campaign goals while simultaneously maximizing their pro<sup>fi</sup>t can inadvertently restrict the reach of display ads to certain groups of customers. For example, as performance optimizers rely on past information on customer behavior, DSPs can choose to not bid or bid conservatively on ad hosts visited by users with no prior information to rely on. In this paper, we focus on designing <sup>fi</sup>eld experiments that explicitly bypass the performance optimizers of the DSP.

## The Quantity–Quality Bias

The bypassing of DSPs’ performance optimizers as part of the design of the experiment is not without consequences. Although it eliminates the problem of selective targeting at the outset, it renders the focal DSP at a disadvantage in terms of winning competi tive auctions in real time. During the experimental period, competing DSPs have the advantage of being able to harness the power of their algorithms to assess the changing quality of ad impressions and thereby adjust their bids accordingly to increase their odds of winning at prices commensurate to anticipated value. Consequently, turning off the DSP’s performance optimizer leads to a negative correlation between the number of ad impressions won and their respective quality within a given time period. We refer to the underestimation of the marginal value arising from this negative correlation as the quantity–quality bias. Quality of the ad impression in this context refers to the ability of the ad impression to elicit a favorable customer response, which depends on the exact goals of the performance optimizer, such as clicks.

QQ bias is a consequence of real-time discrepancy in the ability of competing DSPs to estimate the latent quality of the ad impressions and adjust bids accordingly. As a result, to the extent the competing DSPs are heterogeneous in the capability of their bidding algorithms, QQ bias is endemic to the RTB environment, although a deliberate bypassing of performance optimizers can exacerbate it. In the following section, we describe our empirical approach to recovering the unbiased estimate of a display ad’s marginal value by explicitly bypassing the performance optimizers of DSPs.

## Empirical Approach

We collaborate with two major online retailers that regularly run display-ad campaigns using RTB, each via two different DSPs, in two different markets (United States and South Korea), and targeted at two different types of devices (mobile and nonmobile). Our central goal was to design randomized <sup>fi</sup>eld experiments that can be implemented without relying on the DSP’s support services, including performance optimization, together with a relatively simple analytical procedure to recover unbiased estimates. One of the retailers had a stated interest in moving all of its future campaign management in-house, necessitating a low-cost and easy-to-use procedure that it could resort to as necessary.

There are two major aspects to our approach that contrast it with other experimental methods in the literature. First, we ensure that the frequency cap treatments have a suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently large range, for frequency caps to serve as a strong instrumental variable. Sahni et al. (2019) recently used frequency caps as experimental treatments. They used three levels (none, 5, and 15) and compared the 5 and 15 frequency groups with the control group (PSA ads) to assess the impact of frequency and timing of ad impressions. In contrast, we do not rely on DSP support services to create a control group and rely solely on a larger range of frequency cap treatments to estimate the marginal value of an ad using a 2SLS estimation procedure. Although we are unable to assess the marginal value of the <sup>fi</sup>rst ad shown, the primary goal of our industry collaboration was to assist our partnering advertisers in ascertaining the incremental value of ad impressions beyond the <sup>fi</sup>rst exposure. Consequently, our estimates are local average treatment effects for the targeted population.

Second, we preset the size of the bids and maintain the same value throughout the experiment. More importantly, we explicitly turn off the performance optimizer of the DSP using the manual-bidding option and compensate the DSP on a cost-per-impression (CPM) basis.

Our objective is to maintain a uniform bidding strategy that does not discriminate between customers based on their past responses or future anticipated responses. Most <sup>fi</sup>eld experiments in the literature have used CPC compensation and, to the best of our knowledge, have not made any effort to bypass the performance optimizers of the DSP.

Our identi<sup>fi</sup>cation strategy relies on the fact that although the numbers of ad impressions within groups are endogenous, the random assignment of customers to frequency caps creates an exogenous source of variation in the number of ad impressions. We eliminate selective targeting owing to advertisers and the DSP as part of the design of the experiment. As frequency caps are uncorrelated with customer activity or unobserved customer- or publisher-speci<sup>fi</sup>c characteristics, we can use frequency caps as an instrumental variable to correct for endogeneity arising from both customer activity bias and QQ bias.

## Experimental Design

We collaborated with a leading South Korean online retailer to measure the effectiveness of display ads among customers who had already installed their mobile app. Accordingly, the ads are served via the mobile channel, a priority and area of growth for our partnering retailer.<sup>9</sup> Moreover, an app-based identi<sup>fi</sup>- cation does not suffer from the problem of potential mismatch between website cookies and unique customers. Whereas early empirical studies relied on cookies, more recent research has relied on cookie-free techniques to identify customers (Johnson et al. 2017b), including the use of DSP proprietary services to identify and track customers (Sahni et al. 2019).

Our sample for the experiment contained a random set of 150,000 customers for a period of 18 days from October 22, 2019, to November 9, 2019. Our partnering <sup>fi</sup>rm selected a product to promote for each day and showed the exact same banner ad to the entire sample of customers. We imposed six different external frequency caps as treatment conditions to randomly assigned customer groups within the sample. The caps were 3, 6, 9, 12, 22, and 30. However, we limit each random assignment to a <sup>fi</sup>xed period, each lasting three days. At the end of each period, we repeat the random assignment to the six different treatments to create exogenous variation within customers and within periods. A day in the experiment ends at 4:00 a.m. local time, as customers have shown the least activity around that time, per historical data. Customers who respond by clicking the ad enter the product page where they can obtain detailed information about the advertised product prior to making purchase decisions. We ensured that the <sup>fi</sup>rm excluded all

150,000 customers comprising our sample from all other ongoing advertisement campaigns. Figure 1 shows a schematic of our experimental design.

In order to maintain a uniform bidding strategy on all customers, we used the manual-bidding mechanism of the DSP that participates in the RTB auction. This allows for <sup>fi</sup>xed bid amounts and frequency caps to be set by the advertiser. We worked with the DSP to turn off all performance optimization mechanisms and entered into a payment agreement that was based on actual costs of buying banner spaces in RTB auctions together with a <sup>fi</sup>xed service fee. We <sup>fi</sup>xed the bid amount for all auctions considering the historical average winning price. The DSP’s role in this approach is limited to bidding the preset amount whenever an ad slot is available in the RTB ad exchange (i.e., a customer included in our experiment opens a mobile app or web page that serves available ad space through the ad exchange) without relying on automated tools to inform either the decision to bid or determine the bid size in real time. Furthermore, there was no budget constraint at the individual customer level other than the natural upper limit per day set by the chosen frequency caps throughout the experimental duration.

In contrast to the bidding strategy followed for this <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment, previously, the retailer had used a cost-per-click or cost-per-transaction approach to compensate the DSP. For instance, for campaigns that focused on customer clicks, they paid 14¢ per click in 2019. Under this approach, the DSP participates in <sup>fi</sup>rst-price<sup>10</sup> pay-per-impression RTB auctions in all major networks (e.g., Google, DaumKakao, Smaato, Admixer) to purchase banner ads but uses machine learning algorithms to target customers and select time of display and even ad space to maximize their pro<sup>fi</sup>t function. However, as noted earlier, this approach is a key driver of selective targeting bias that we seek to circumvent to ensure uniform bidding in this paper.

## Data and Randomization Checks

The number of customers who received one or more banner ads is 62,207, and the total number of customerday observations is 236,099. The total count of impressions is 1,034,651, and the total count of clicks is 4,626. In order to reliably estimate a customer-level <sup>fi</sup>xed effect model, for the main analysis of the data, we focus on customers who have three or more customer-day observations in order to use a user-level <sup>fi</sup>xed effect model to account for customer-level time-invariant characteristics. We have 31,834 customers, 195,111 customer-day observations, 868,502 ad impressions, and 4,042 clicks in the main data set.<sup>11</sup>

Table 2 reports the key summary statistics from the main data set. Because we randomly assigned customers into different treatment groups, we expect the numbers of customers exposed to our experimental ads to be comparable. We <sup>fi</sup>nd this to be the case from a formal test (F 0.11, p 0.99). In Section 2 of the online appendix, we provide a variety of additional randomization checks using historical customer data pertaining to their interactions with the retailer (e.g., online store visits, purchases) during the <sup>fi</sup>ve weeks prior to the experiment.

We note again that the frequency caps do not in<sup>fl</sup>uence the bidding strategy. The bidding algorithm merely checks whether a customer has reached the predetermined cap for the day prior to bidding in response to ad slot availability from ad networks in real time. Therefore, frequency caps do not guarantee that a customer will see a set number of ads. However, for some customers, the number of impressions can end up being slightly greater than their respective daily cap. This is because the number of impressions earned for a customer from a single auction cycle can be more than one. The DSP is unable to prevent this, and we observe such cases in our data. However, such multiple impressions from a single auction occur randomly, and the daily cap successfully introduces exogenous variations in the number of impressions across different cap groups. Table 2 shows that the average number of daily impressions increases as the cap increases, as expected.

In order to estimate the marginal effect of individual ad impressions beyond the <sup>fi</sup>rst exposure on customer response, we start with a simple OLS regression by estimating the following regression model:

$$
Y _ {i t} = \alpha_ {0} + D _ {i t} \cdot \alpha_ {t} + I m p s _ {i t} \cdot \beta + \varepsilon_ {i t},\tag{1}
$$

where $\alpha _ { 0 }$ is a constant. The term $D _ { i t }$ is a $( 1 \times 1 7 )$ vector of day indicators (if t  2, the (t  1)th element is equal to one, and all others are equal to zero, and if $t = 1 ,$ , all elements are equal to zero; the time effect of day 1 is absorbed in $\alpha _ { 0 } )$ . Term $\alpha _ { t }$ is the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of the time effect to account for the day-speci<sup>fi</sup>c variations in clicks due to day effect (e.g., weekend versus weekdays) and the product selected for the banner ad on that day. The term Imps<sub>it</sub> is the count of ad impressions to i at t. The term $\varepsilon _ { i t }$ is an idiosyncratic random shock and can be decomposed as $\varepsilon _ { i t } = \mu _ { i } + \xi _ { i t } ,$ , where $\mu _ { i }$ is the time-invariant individual effect and $\xi _ { i t }$ is a time-varying deviation from $\mu _ { i }$ or a time-varying unobserved shock. Using this decomposition, we can rewrite the Equation (1) as follows:

$$
Y _ {i t} = \alpha_ {0} + D _ {i t} \cdot \alpha_ {t} + I m p _ {i t} \cdot \beta + (\mu_ {i} + \xi_ {i t}).\tag{2}
$$

In our context, $\mu _ { i }$ represents an individual-speci<sup>fi</sup>c tendency to click ads. Because customers are heterogeneous in their response to ads, whether it be in terms of their online activity in the mobile channel or their tendency to click more frequently, we cannot rule out correlation between $\mu _ { i }$ and Imps<sub>it</sub> resulting in the familiar customer activity bias. However, our experimental design allows us to account for this issue by incorporating customer-level <sup>fi</sup>xed effects to the extent they are time invariant. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we <sup>fi</sup>rst apply the <sup>fi</sup>xed effect transformation to Equation (2) as

Formally, we estimate the following equation:

$$
(I m p s _ {i t} - \overline {{I m p s}} _ {i}) = (D _ {i t} - \overline {{D}} _ {i}) \cdot \gamma + (I V _ {i t} - \overline {{I V}} _ {i}) \cdot \delta + \omega_ {i t}.\tag{4}
$$

Because we randomly assign customers into cap groups in each period, $I V _ { i t }$ is unrelated to $( \xi _ { i t } - { \overline { { \xi } } } _ { i } )$ . We con<sup>fi</sup>rm that the estimated coef<sup>fi</sup>cients of $\left( I V _ { i t } - \overline { { I V } } _ { i } \right)$ on impressions are highly signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. Consequently, $( I \dot { V } _ { i t } - \overline { { I V } } _ { i } )$ serves as a valid instrumental variable. We then use the predicted values of $( I m p s _ { i t } - \overline { { I m p s } } _ { i } )$ to estimate Equation (3).

## General Discussion

## Mechanism Underlying Net Negative Bias

In the absence of selective targeting owing to advertisers and DSPs via their performance optimizers, we provide two possible explanations for the underlying net negative bias in the data set informed by both our empirical approach and prior <sup>fi</sup>ndings in the litera ture: QQ bias and residual time-varying customer activity bias. In the context of our experimental design, the QQ bias mechanism provides a parsimonious and natural explanation for the consistent net negative bias. As the advertiser disables the DSP’s performance optimizer during the experimental period, the focal DSP loses its ability to adjust bids in response to the changing quality of the ad impression. As a result, when the competing DSPs’ optimizers correctly assess the realtime quality to be higher and adjust their bids upward, the focal DSP is more likely to lose the auction.

Similarly, when the competing DSPs’ optimizers correctly assess the real-time quality to be lower and adjust their bids downward, the focal DSP is more likely to win the auction. Consequently, the focal DSP suffers from a negative correlation between the number of impressions won and their respective real-time quality.

Performance optimizers can assess real-time quality using multiple inputs that include both recent customer behavior and the characteristics of ad hosts as they vary over time. For example, it can adapt to changing content and format of the ad host web pages that in<sup>fl</sup>uence customer behavior, even if it involves the same customer and same publisher. Furthermore, the machine learning algorithms underlying these optimizers are goal directed; that is, they can optimize for different types of customer responses, such as clicks, views, purchases, etc., depending on the contract between the DSP and the advertiser. As a result, the presence and magnitude of negative bias owing to QQ bias in an experimental data set generated using our approach depends on both the relative performance and the goals of competing DSPs’ performance optimizers. Nonetheless, QQ bias is always negative, as opposed to customer activity bias, which can also be a source of positive bias.

Time-varying customer activity unrelated to the QQ bias mechanism and more granular than what we control for in our models can also contribute to the net negative bias. Although the customer-speci<sup>fi</sup>c effect that is time invariant shows evidence for positive bias in our analysis, an individual mobile app’s intraday usage pattern or behavior can still drive bias in the opposite direction. However, in such a scenario, we need additional assumptions to justify a negative correlation where high impression count results in lower propensity to click. This is possible if high app usage co-occurs with low attention span, which is not conducive to initiating the purchase cycle for certain classes of products. For example, Hoban and Bucklin (2015) justify a negative bias owing to website-speci<sup>fi</sup>c customer behavior by positing a negative correlation between browsing intensity and customer purchase decisions that require high concentration or privacy.

## Implications for Practitioners

Our proposed valuation approach is bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to advertisers in two important ways. First, our approach is accessible to advertisers of all sizes by virtue of its low cost and ease of implementation. A major cost advantage comes from the avoidance of simultaneous PSA campaigns or DSP support services such as the ghost ad campaigns. Admittedly, the control group–based identi-<sup>fi</sup>cation allows for the estimation of average effects pertaining to the <sup>fi</sup>rst exposure and therefore can inform the decision to bid on a prospective customer who has not been exposed to an ad before. However, in the retargeting context, our approach allows for small budget advertisers to avoid overspending on customers who have already been exposed to ads earlier.

Second, for advertisers with large advertising budgets and resources to manage digital ad campaigns in-house (as in the case of our partnering <sup>fi</sup>rms), the prospect of measuring the effectiveness by bypassing the DSP’s performance optimizers is attractive. It eliminates any mismatch between the goals of the

DSP and the advertisers and allows for greater control and transparency of the valuation exercise. For example, the advertiser might prioritize a broader reach of prospective customers together with other customer response goals. However, the DSP algorithms might deprioritize new customers on whom the past behavioral information is lacking.

Our research also highlights the importance of choosing the appropriate pricing mechanisms in an RTB environment that counters possible goal mismatch between the advertiser and DSP. Under the most common clickbased payment contract, DSPs will maximize the clicks while minimizing the cost (i.e., the actual CPM-based cost of buying ads in RTB auctions). This scheme has at least two downsides. First, DSPs will target customers who have historically shown high click tendency, and these customers may not overlap with the main target of the ad campaign. Second, if the click rates are the same, DSPs would rather use low-CPM publishers than high-CPM publishers. Customers’ after-click behaviors at low-CPM publishers might be different from those at high-CPM publishers. Under such circumstances, advertisers can bene<sup>fi</sup>t by trying an alternative payment scheme such as ours (i.e., CPM plus <sup>fl</sup>at fee).

## Implications for Scholars

Our contribution to the literature is twofold. First, we demonstrate an empirical approach to display ad valuation in the RTB environment that explicitly bypasses the performance optimizers of the DSP. Although extant empirical literature has highlighted the dominant role of selective targeting bias and proposed methods to correct for it, there has not been an attempt to isolate or eliminate DSP-driven selective targeting as part of the valuation exercise.

Second, we document and address a new source of negative bias, the QQ bias, in the context of programmatic advertising involving RTB, as previous empirical work has mostly reported the overestimation of effectiveness (i.e., positive bias) as a primary concern. Although our disabling of the DSP’s performance optimizers as part of the experimental design contributes to the QQ bias mechanism, we cannot de<sup>fi</sup>nitively eliminate it from the underlying data sets in other experimental designs. Consequently, any suboptimal bidding by the DSPs participating in an RTB auction can potentially contribute to QQ bias. As we cannot guarantee that all competing DSPs are of equal sophistication, we believe that prior empirical work has not isolated this source of bias owing to the dominance of positive bias driven by selective targeting and heterogeneous customer activity.

## Conclusion

The valuation of display ads using randomized <sup>fi</sup>eld experiments in RTB settings is an active area of research given the multiple challenges it presents. In addition to the involvement of multiple participants such as the advertiser, DSP, publisher, ad exchange, etc., in a dynamic environment, advertisers must contend with the increasing sophistication of black-box performance optimizers of the DSP. In this paper, we demonstrate an experimental design that prioritizes the reduction of DSP interference and the cost of implementation by solely relying on exogenous variation in frequency caps. In addition to helping low-budget advertisers, we show that large advertisers who seek to manage their display ad campaigns in-house can bene-<sup>fi</sup>t from bypassing the DSP’s performance optimizers to eliminate all forms of selective targeting at the outset, rendering greater control of the valuation exercise. Our approach is particularly appropriate when advertisers internal goals for their ad campaigns do not readily align with the goals of the DSP’s automated systems.

Furthermore, considering the evidence from two large randomized <sup>fi</sup>eld studies involving advertisers from different geographies and platforms, practitioners adopting this approach to valuation should also anticipate a net negative bias before correcting for endogeneity. We suggest two possible underlying mechanisms, namely, the QQ bias caused by the discrepancy in the focal DSP’s ability to correctly assess the quality of ad impressions in real time vis-a-vis its \` competition and the residual time-varying customer activity bias unaccounted for in the regression model.

A major limitation of our approach is that the empirical results we report are local average treatment effects in the context of display ad campaigns. Consequently, our estimates pertain to the value of showing incremental ads to customers who have already been exposed to an ad and are available for additional impressions. However, the estimates are vitally important for advertisers in designing ef<sup>fi</sup>cient ad campaigns. RTB algorithms commence bidding only in response to noti<sup>fi</sup>cation of an available ad slot. Even though DSPs can adjust bids to prioritize customers that are more likely to respond favorably, a key source of inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency in the display ad market is the continuation of automatic bidding on all available customers until the DSP reaches the budget limit set by the advertiser. Our approach to marginal valuation provides advertisers a tool to reduce this inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency in ad spending.
