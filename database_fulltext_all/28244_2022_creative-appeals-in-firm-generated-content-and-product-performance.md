# Creative Appeals in Firm-Generated Content and Product Performance

Jifeng Mu,<sup>a,b</sup> Jonathan Zhang,<sup>c</sup> Abhishek Borah,<sup>d</sup> Jiayin Qi<sup>e,f</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Microfoundation Institute, Madison, Alabama 35758; <sup>b</sup> School of Business, Alabama A&M University, Normal, Alabama 35762; <sup>c</sup> College of Business, Colorado State University, Fort Collins, Colorado 80523; <sup>d</sup> INSEAD, 77305 Fontainebleau, France; <sup>e</sup> Institute of Arti<sup>fi</sup>cial Intelligence and Change Management, Shanghai University of International Business and Economics, Shanghai 200336, China; <sup>f</sup> Key Laboratory of Data Science and Management Decision, Shanghai University of International Business and Economics, Shanghai 200336, China Contact: microfoundation@outlook.com, https://orcid.org/0000-0003-1134-5971 (JM); jonathan.zhang@colostate.edu https://orcid.org/0000-0002-9559-0626 (JZ); abhishek.borah@insead.edu, https://orcid.org/0000-0002-8313-0948 (AB); qijiayin@139.com (JQ)

Abstract. The authors of this research investigate the effects of hedonic and utilitarian message appeals in <sup>fi</sup>rm-generated content on product performance measured by daily sales. The results from a multiproduct category, large-scale empirical study with data collected from a variety of sources show that hedonic appeals have a higher positive impact than utilitarian appeals on product performance. The authors also illustrate that message appeals have positive effects on product performance only when message quality and message goal consistency surpass a certain threshold and that these positive effects are stronger for hedonic than for utilitarian appeals. The authors further demonstrate that message complexity negatively moderates the effects of hedonic and utilitarian appeals on product performance with a stronger negative moderating effect for hedonic appeals. The research provides guidance on the proper combination of message styles and suggests that <sup>fi</sup>rms creative capabilities to curate online messages that resonate with consumers can produce signi<sup>fi</sup>cant performance advantages.

## 1. Introduction

Although research shows that <sup>fi</sup>rm-generated content (FGC), that is, <sup>fi</sup>rm-initiated marketing communications on social media sites, can improve outcomes such as message sharing (e.g., Goh et al. 2013, Hewett et al. 2016, Kumar et al. 2016, He et al. 2018, Tellis et al. 2019, Chung et al. 2020), other studies offer divergent results, such as countervailing effects of informative and brand personality messages on message sharing (Lee et al. 2018) and diminishing returns on customer (Homburg et al. 2015) and employee engagement (Miller and Tucker 2013). What is surprisingly lacking among these insightful studies is an integrated approach to investigate message appeals theoretically grounded in marketing communications and psycholinguistics and linking message appeals with content characteristics to product performance metrics, such as sales, one of the most important and usable downstream metrics for <sup>fi</sup>rms. Sales is the prime driver of a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s survival and growth. Content characteristics and psycholinguistic appeals are inherently inseparable from FGC communications, and their collective impact demands empirical examination. This current work attempts to <sup>fi</sup>ll the research gap.

First, conceptually, psycholinguistic research provides the foundational roots for studying language in use in persuasive marketing communication (Shiv and Fedorikhin 1999, Bagozzi 2007, Ordenes et al. 2017, Brisset 2018), that is, how online messages by marketers are to be comprehended and believed by consumers and subsequent consumer behavioral changes evoked by such messages. Methodologically, psycholinguistic theory offers researchers fundamental approaches for classifying messages and integrating rhetoric (e.g., <sup>fi</sup>gure of speech, intertextual meta-acts, use of words and writing style, tones, similes, analo gies, and metonymies) in message classi<sup>fi</sup>cation (Chitturi et al. 2007, Carr et al. 2012, Kronrod et al. 2012). Accordingly, rooted in the literature on the psycholinguistic appeals of messages, we investigate the impact of hedonic and utilitarian message appeals (hereinafter referred to as hedonic or utilitarian appeals) on daily sales. We extend the FGC literature by complementing extant studies, such as the informative/brand personality perspective (e.g., Lee et al. 2018), with a psycholinguistic angle that provides a nuanced view of the effect of message content on product performance.

Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we employ a hedonic–utilitarian (H/U) perspective to investigate FGCs on product performance. The H/U perspective has been applied in marketing and information systems (IS) literature (Hirschman and Holbrook 1982, Voss et al. 2003, Chitturi et al. 2007, Kronrod et al. 2012, Shin et al. 2020). However, the effects of H/U message appeals have not been empirically tested in large-scale FGC research. Categorizing FGCs into hedonic and utilitarian appeals re<sup>fl</sup>ects the affective and instrumental motives of a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s communication strategy to consumers. An examination of FGC from a psycholinguistic angle is conceptually and empirically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant because it offers marketers a new perspective to creatively stimulate marketing messages based on how messages are processed and represented in the minds and brains of consumers as intended by message senders, that is, marketers. In doing so, we extend the H/U literature by testing its predictions in the FGC online message context (Batra and Keller 2016). In literature, because of the constraints of obtaining rich, daily, and granular sales data, which is usually not available to researchers (e.g., Lee et al. 2018), most studies explore types of FGC on customer message sharing and engagement (Tellis et al. 2019). A key question is whether FGC can differentially affect performance in sales. Our conceptual distinction suggests that hedonic appeals have a stronger positive impact than utilitarian appeals on sales. Our research contributes to the growing debate surrounding the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of FGC utilizing psycholinguistic appeals that in<sup>fl</sup>uence the audience’s attention and interpretation.

Second, integrated marketing communication and IS research suggest that researchers not only need to consider the main effect of any communication, but also look into the interactive effects of message characteristics (Batra and Keller 2016, He et al. 2018, Chung et al. 2020). We explore the differential moderating roles of FGC psycholinguistic characteristics over which the <sup>fi</sup>rm has direct control, that is, message quality, goal consistency, and complexity in the relationship between message types and product performance. Much empirical research on FGC and product performance focused on spending and message types rarely explains when FGC has positive versus negative effects on performance (e.g., Goh et al. 2013, Hewett et al. 2016). Accordingly, extant literature offers limited guidance on how the characteristics of message appeals affect the relationship between FGC and downstream outcome variables, such as product sales. Therefore, instead of seeking formulas for generalized effectiveness, researchers should recognize that the effects of message types on performance might be content characteristic dependent. Such boundary conditions are critical as they regulate the limitations in applying the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from extant research. The important question is, thus, to understand the conditions under which a given main effect is most appropriate. Understanding the roles of message psycholinguistic characteristics in the relationship between FGC and sales would highlight the importance of message content in addition to the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of the message types. Given FGC’s short shelf life and the continuous pressure for <sup>fi</sup>rms to create new content to attract consumer attention online, an investigation of how message psycholinguistic characteristics affect sales managerially speaks to the trend that creative capability can be a source of competitive advantage in the digital age. Our research offers the <sup>fi</sup>rst empirical test of the effectiveness of message psycholinguistic characteristics on product performance measured by daily sales, complementing the growing body of literature on FGC.

The remainder of this article is organized as follows: we <sup>fi</sup>rst provide the conceptual foundations and research hypotheses establishing links among different types of message content in FGC and product performance. We then follow with sections on the research design, empirical results, and discussion.

## 2. Conceptual Foundation and Hypotheses

## 2.1. Literature Review

Researchers propose different categorizations of FGC<sup>1</sup> with informative and brand personality (I/B) being the closest to our categorization (Lee et al. 2018). The I/B perspective highlights the different customer engagement behaviors associated with FGC’s informative and brand-personality message orientation. Lee et al. (2018) classify informative content as any product-oriented facts, such as details about products, promotions, availability, price, and brand, and brandpersonality content refers to content that has a set of human characteristics associated with the brand. They <sup>fi</sup>nd that brand-personality messages increase consumer engagement, but informative messages do not affect customer engagement. However, the combination of brand personality–related and informative messages drive engagement (Lee et al. 2018).

Our proposed H/U perspective is different from the I/B perspective conceptually and empirically. Conceptually, whereas the I/B perspective is built on psychology, marketing, and advertising and readily deals with branding (hence, brand personality), the theoretical root for H/U categorization is more foundational and draws from psycholinguistics and communication (e.g., Wittgenstein 1953, Austin 1962, Searle 1976, Chitturi et al. 2007, Erler 2010, Brisset 2018). The H/U perspective emphasizes bidimensionality from the affective and instrumental motives of consumers in information processing, which makes it theoretically and practically possible for us to quantify messages based on established scales by Voss et al. (2003). Empirically, we demonstrate that hedonic and utilitarian appeals and informative and brand-personality messages are different with hedonic and utilitarian resulting in superior model <sup>fi</sup>t given our research context as a better measure to capture consumers’ response to FGC’s creativity.<sup>2</sup>

## 2.2. Hedonic vs. Utilitarian Appeals

Language is used to accomplish objectives within speci<sup>fi</sup>c situations (Wittgenstein 1953, Austin 1962, Searle 1976, Erler 2010, Carr et al. 2012, Brisset 2018). Accordingly, message senders achieve their objectives through the use of language, often to evoke attitudinal and behavioral changes in the message recipients. In FGC contexts, content marketers generally create content to arouse consumers’ intrinsic affection and imagination or provide speci<sup>fi</sup>c instrumental bene<sup>fi</sup>ts about their products. These two functions of messages correspond to hedonic and utilitarian message appeals. In social media FGC communications, hedonic appeals tend to highlight the product’s positive affective bene-<sup>fi</sup>ts, such as emphasizing emotional attachment focusing on fun, playfulness, enjoyment, and excitement. In contrast, utilitarian appeals emphasize the product’s practical bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, such as the need to complete specific tasks ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently and effectively. Hedonic appeals are experiential and multisensory (Voss et al. 2003), whereas utilitarian appeals are more functional, practical, and task related (Kronrod et al. 2012). In this study, both types of appeals are speci<sup>fi</sup>c messages communicated by the <sup>fi</sup>rm on its social media sites only. We analyze both the volume and the degree of the two types of appeals on product performance.

When <sup>fi</sup>rms create marketing messages on social media, mentioning the product’s hedonic nature can stimulate high arousal in customers, providing customers with fun, exciting, pleasurable, and inspiring moments, such as feelings of satisfying one’s taste and stylistic uniqueness. Psycholinguists suggest that high activation levels in affective language engender active exercise of power and aspiration for consumers (Chitturi et al. 2007). Hedonic appeals trigger message recipients emotional and aroused consumption levels (Austin 1962, Searle 1976), and consumers process hedonic appeals more holistically than utilitarian appeals (Chitturi et al. 2007, Kronrod et al. 2012). According to the cognitive tuning model (e.g., Fiedler 2000), a promotional affective state, such as elation and relaxation, leads consumers to feel unconstrained and to take risks and explore new possibilities and instills feelings of interest and pleasure associated with the anticipation of obtaining and consuming something indulgent. Theories of feelings as information (e.g., Schwarz and Clore 1983)

suggest that consumers might, in fact, have dif<sup>fi</sup>culty decoupling the positive affect they experience from their feelings toward the object of the affect—a transference that would boost attitudes and behaviors. As such, in social media FGC communications, affective-rich hedonic appeals are more likely to trigger strong drives for purchasing. In contrast, utilitarian appeals trigger lower levels of arousal, which lead to inactivity, avoidance, and deterioration of consumer attitudes toward the product (Chitturi et al. 2007, Kronrod et al. 2012). As utilitarian appeals are generally presented as objective statements (Searle 1976), which are more conclusive than hedonic appeals, these message appeals leave less room for consumers’ imagination and action stimulation associated with the product ownership. Thus, we argue that hedonic appeals in social media FGC communications have a stronger positive impact than utilitarian appeals on product performance, which we measure through sales revenue, a customer-oriented metric and a direct, consequential performance outcome. We hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 1. In social media FGC communications, he donic appeals have a higher extent of positive association with product performance than utilitarian appeals.

## 2.3. Message Quality

Message quality refers to the uniqueness, novelty, and believability of the content messages released by a <sup>fi</sup>rm (Chandy et al. 2001, Tellis and Johnson 2007, Kopalle et al. 2017). A high-quality message can (1) attract consumers’ limited attention and lead to a change in consumer attitudes about the products or brands (MacInnis et al. 1991), (2) reduce information asymmetry between <sup>fi</sup>rms and consumers and lower consumer information search and processing costs (Meyers-Levy and Malaviya 1999), and (3) give consumers strong rational criteria for decision making (Fabrigar et al. 1998).

For many consumers, accurately assessing the quality of even simple products or services is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult (Kopalle et al. 2017) and even more challenging for complex or experience goods. In the social media environment, consumers use FGC message quality cues to infer product quality (Batra and Keller 2016). In social media FGC communications, quality messages can arouse consumers’ curiosity and motivation, which stimulates consumer cognitive evaluation and elaboration of the messages, such as encoding the attributes, personal relevance of the products, reliability, and competency of the products (Setia et al. 2013, Batra and Keller 2016). Online content messages are more effective when <sup>fi</sup>rms can frame the idea of “quality” the way their customers see it.<sup>3</sup>

In social media FGC communications, message quality should in<sup>fl</sup>uence the effectiveness of message appeals on consumer purchases. If the message quality is low, consumers discount or ignore the message because consumers use message quality associations to infer product performance given information overload and time pressure of users in a social media context (Lambrecht and Tucker 2013). Accordingly, consumers respond best to high-quality messages, and low-quality messages can lead to negative perceptions that could ultimately diminish the message’s effectiveness. In particular, message quality is particularly important for processing hedonic appeals because hedonic appeals are aimed at promoting brand image, building consumer identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and connections with the brands, enabling consumers to explore, decide, and enact who they are or want to become. These factors are less concrete, more experiential, more aesthetic and affective, more subjective and interpretive, and offer more freedom for consumer imagination and stimulation than utilitarian appeals. Consumers should thereby rely more on message quality for cues for hedonic appeals than utilitarian appeals in making product evaluations and purchasing decisions in social media context. Although message quality would positively moderate both types of message appeals, its moderating effect should be stronger for hedonic appeals. We hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 2 (a). In social media FGC communications, message quality positively moderates the association of utilitarian/hedonic appeals with product performance.

Hypothesis 2 (b). In social media FGC communications, the moderating effect of message quality on product performance is stronger for hedonic appeals compared with utilitarian appeals.

## 2.4. Message Goal Consistency

Message goal consistency refers to communicating a common brand representation to articulate the unique identity and value of the brand to customers utilizing multiple means of communication (Duncan and Moriarty 1998). In the social media context, FGCs are goal consistent if the information conveyed shares meaning and content with the purpose of creating and reinforcing common brand associations and positioning (Lee and Park 2007).

In the social media FGC communication context, message goal consistency should in<sup>fl</sup>uence the magnitude of the impact of FGC on consumers because their motivation to engage in detailed cognitive processing is low given that they have limited access to processing resources and when other bases of evaluation are ambiguous or unrevealing (Lau-Gesk and Meyers-Levy 2009). Cues of psycholinguistic appeals in consistent message communications elicit automatic responses, requiring few processing resources, emerging rapidly, and guiding attitudes and actions (Chitturi et al. 2007, Kronrod et al. 2012). A consistent message goal helps consumers to understand and predict better what the brand stands for when they interact with different messages each time (Edell and Keller 1989), enhances the motivation of message processing, and produces more enduring and favorable judgments (Erdem and Swait 2004). In contrast, inconsistent messages violate consumer schema congruity and lead to interruption of expectations and predictions in the activated schema for message processing (Meyers-Levy and Malaviya 1999).

Although message goal consistency can enhance the effect of both hedonic and utilitarian appeals, we posit that its moderating effect would be stronger for hedonic appeals in social media FGC communications. Message goal consistency makes language processing more <sup>fl</sup>exi ble and heuristic and less analytical and systematic, a key feature in hedonic message appeals processing based on elaboration likelihood model (Petty and Cacioppo 1986, Petty et al. 2003). Ful<sup>fi</sup>llment of promotion goals intended from message consistency for hedonic message appeals can introduce positive mood, evoking the arousal-oriented feelings of excitement and cheer fulness (Chitturi et al. 2007), altering language usage and communication expectations, and making users feel higher positive experiential elevation (Fiedler 2000, Schwarz and Clore 2003). In social media FGC commu nications, for hedonic appeals, message consistency, then, should enhance the realism of consumers’ experiences of what the brand stands for, triggering strong affective responses, such as the feeling of pleasure, enjoyment, or fun with sensory, affective, intellectual, symbolic, and stimulating experiences. In contrast, goal-consistent messages in utilitarian messages <sup>fi</sup>t expectations of what the product can do for consumers (Kronrod et al. 2012). Psycholinguistic research demonstrates that weak and less persuasive response occurs when the language <sup>fi</sup>ts the receiver’s expectations (e.g., Brown and Levinson 1987, Forgas 1998). The convergence of consumer expectation and the products’ actual functionality makes consistent utilitarian message appeals appear natural for consumers and accordingly produces less strong responses from consumers compared with hedonic message appeals. The principle of hedonic dominance suggests that meeting or exceeding desired message consistency expectation on both hedonic and utilitarian appeals makes consumers prefer the hedonically superior option in social media FGC communications (Chitturi et al. 2007). Therefore, the effect of message consistency is less intense for utilitarian message appeals than it is for hedonic message appeals. We hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis 3 (a). In social media FGC communications, message goal consistency positively moderates the association of hedonic/utilitarian appeals with product performance.

Hypothesis 3 (b). In social media FGC communications, the moderating effect of message goal consistency on product performance is stronger for hedonic appeals compared with utilitarian appeals.

## 2.5. Message Complexity

Message complexity refers to the degree of dif<sup>fi</sup>culty in understanding or comprehension of message content. Message complexity exists when a message is packed with detailed information, complicated words, or intricate reasoning. Clear and easy-to-understand language is bene<sup>fi</sup>cial in social media communication, whereas message complexity increases the cognitive effort of understanding the message (Batra and Keller 2016). Cognitive effort refers to the amount of cognitive resources that a user must allocate to accommodate the task representation’s information-processing demands. The more complex the message description and the information cues embedded in the message, the higher the mental effort needed to distill the relevant message for comprehension (MacInnis et al. 1991). Cognitive resource-matching theory (Anand and Sternthal 1989) suggests that persuasion is maximal when the amount of cognitive resources required to process the message is matched to the individual’s ability. In other words, consumers process a message to a greater extent when the message is tailored to their ability to process than when the message is mismatched. Consumers thus stay longer in social media FGC communications with easier-to-understand material and <sup>fi</sup>nd it more interesting if the writing matches their preferred comprehension level. When the message complexity exceeds the capacity of an individual’s cognitive ability to comprehend the message, the cognitive elaboration of the readers is inhibited (MacInnis et al. 1991). Thus, it matters little whether the underlying message contains rich and relevant information if readers stop information processing because of message complexity. Accordingly, in social media FGC communications, message complexity reduces the effect of hedonic and utilitarian appeals on product performance.

In social media FGC communications, message complexity should be particularly likely to in<sup>fl</sup>uence hedonic appeals processing more than utilitarian appeals. Research suggests that consumers rely more on the central route reasoning to process utilitarian messages while taking peripheral route reasoning to process hedonic messages (Petty and Cacioppo 1986, Petty et al. 2003, Voss et al. 2003). Attitudes formed and changed via the central route are considered to be relatively enduring and resistant to change even when the communication complexity increases (Petty and Cacioppo 1986, Huskinson and Haddock 2004). Therefore, the effect of instrumental utilitarian appeals focusing heavily on product attributes changes slowly when message complexity increases.

In contrast, consumers employ peripheral cues, such as message attractiveness, value-expressiveness, brand personality, and multisensory pleasure, fantasy, nonproduct-related attributes, and experiential and perceived symbolic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to judge the hedonic value of a message (Hirschman and Holbrook 1982, Petty and Cacioppo 1986, Chitturi et al. 2007, Kronrod et al. 2012). Peripheral cues of hedonic messages allow message receivers to process the message heuristically, be relaxed, and enter the realm of life that brings them enjoyment, happiness, excitement, and satisfaction (Hirschman and Holbrook 1982, Fiedler 2000, Schwarz and Clore 2003). However, message complexity constrains the ability of consumers to heuristically process the messages associated with hedonic affect content, inhibiting the freedom of association and imagination required in processing hedonic appeals (Chitturi et al. 2007), lowering the value of feelings as information, and eliciting counterproductive effects by reducing diagnosticity, the symbolic and intangible value of hedonic appeals (Schwarz and Clore 2003, Andrade 2005). Consequently, consumers may question the credibility of messages, leading them to correct for the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of affect when making their product evaluations and choices (Petty et al. 2003, van den Berg et al. 2006). Further, message complexity makes hedonic affective content that provides consumers rapidly accessible and diagnostic signals about products more ambiguous or unrevealing than utilitarian content that provide consumers objective descriptive facts (Greifender et al. 2011). Extant research suggests that the accessibility, diagnosticity, psychological synchrony and conveyance of hedonic affective cues diminish rapidly more than their utilitarian counterpart when the evaluative clarity decreases (Searle 1976, Giles and Smith 1979, Zajonc 1980, Pham et al. 2001, Erler 2010). We hypoth esize the following:

Hypothesis 4 (a). In social media FGC communications, message complexity negatively moderates the association of hedonic/utilitarian appeals with product performance.

Hypothesis 4 (b). In social media FGC communications, the moderating effect of message complexity on product performance is stronger for hedonic appeals compared with utilitarian appeals.

## 3. Method

## 3.1. Research Context

We test our conceptual framework using two years of rich and daily data from January 1, 2016, to December 31, 2017, collected on a major global consumer electronic and manufacturing <sup>fi</sup>rm whose name is concealed per our corporate partner’s request. The <sup>fi</sup>rm leads the world market across various brand categories, such as smartphones, computers, tablets, digital appliances, and TVs. In our study period, the <sup>fi</sup>rm market share breakdown by product segments is

19.35% in smartphones, 3.3% in computers, 12.95% in tablets, 16.69% in digital appliances, and 20.8% in TV. The <sup>fi</sup>rm uses its Facebook page and Twitter account to engage with its customers. We collected information written in English from its North American social media accounts on Facebook and Twitter. We derive our variables and measurements from multiple sources as detailed in the following sections.

## 3.2. Dependent and Independent Variables (IVs)

3.2.1. Product Performance (Daily Sales Revenue). We use daily sales revenue as a measure of product performance. The <sup>fi</sup>rm provides us with its daily sales revenue for each product in our analysis from its 2016–2017 marketing and sales program database. The sales revenue is measured by U.S. millions of dollars per day. In the analysis, we use the logtransformed sales revenue.

3.2.2. Hedonic and Utilitarian Appeals. We wrote a Javascript based on the Facebook application programming interface (API) to retrieve all <sup>fi</sup>rmgenerated messages on Facebook. The <sup>fi</sup>rm-generated content on Twitter was purchased from Gnip. After obtaining the data, we followed an intensive coding process to determine if the messages sent by the <sup>fi</sup>rm are hedonic or utilitarian. Based on psycholinguistic speech-act theory for a language analysis unit (e.g., Austin 1962, Searle 1976, Erler 2010, Brisset 2018), three researchers manually broke down each message into sentences, resulting in 23,197 unique sentences on Facebook (average of 2.53 sentences per post) and 16,859 unique sentences on Twitter (average of 2.17 sentences per tweet). Given the relatively low number of Facebook posts and Tweets and the absence of ready-to-use dictionaries, the three researchers manually labeled each sentence as hedonic or utilitarian appeals. As there is always the possibility of a lack of precision in labeling content using machine learning, our approach tackles the issue of misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cation.

More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we iteratively developed a reliable, context-sensitive, and noncomputerized contentanalytical instrument to categorize hedonic and utilitarian appeals in messages. The coding process had three phases. First, we developed preliminary coding instructions, including concise de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of the hedonic or utilitarian ingredients of message appeals (i.e., tones, words, <sup>fi</sup>gures of speech, similes, analogies, and metonymies). We developed a comprehensive coding protocol (Miles and Huberman 1994), such as anchoring examples, coding criteria, and intersubjective comparable guidelines that illustrated how to reliably classify messages as hedonic or utilitarian. We discussed inconsistent codings until we arrived at an agreement (Krippendorff 2004).

Second, we revised the initial coding instructions and supplemented them with various examples of hedonic and utilitarian appeals. For utilitarian appeals, we have four criteria: the messages (1) emphasize the product’s practical bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, such as completing specific tasks ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently and effectively; (2) inform users how to resolve speci<sup>fi</sup>c user problems with the product; (3) highlight the practical value of the product to users; and (4) provide certain concrete functional attributes/bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of the product to users. For hedonic appeals, we use four criteria: the messages (1) highlight the product’s positive affective bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, emphasizing emotional attachment and focusing on fun, playfulness, enjoyment, excitement, feelings of interest and pleasure; (2) are experiential and multisensory; (3) employ affective language; and (4) provide exercise of power, imagination, and aspiration for consumers. Online Appendix 3 provides examples of anchoring message appeals in our message labeling process.

Third, we applied the coding guidelines in the sample and optimized our coding instruments. Following the <sup>fi</sup>nal guidelines, the three researchers independently coded all the sentences into hedonic or utilitarian ap peals in messages. Because some FGC messages include mixed sentences of both hedonic and utilitarian appeals, decomposing messages at the sentence level avoids the problem of dual components and ambiguity in classifying message appeals. If an FGC message has two sentences and both sentences are classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as he donic (or utilitarian), the FGC message is classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as hedonic (or utilitarian). If a message has both a hedonic and utilitarian sentence, then the three researchers judge based on the tone (use of words and writing style)<sup>4</sup> of the message to determine if the message should be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as hedonic or utilitarian by voting.<sup>5</sup> This approach is consistent with Lee et al. (2018) in that some elements of content classi<sup>fi</sup>cation are necessarily subjective and based on human judgment. A systematic test (Lacy and Riffe 1996) showed high acceptable interrater reliability (Krippendorff (2004) 0.86).

After labeling the messages into hedonic and utilitarian, we assessed the degree of hedonic and utilitarian appeals.<sup>6</sup> To determine the degree of hedonic and utilitarian appeals of each sentence, we <sup>fi</sup>rst asked 10 trained research assistants from different institutions on three different continents (North America, Asia, and Europe) to independently evaluate each sentence using the seven-point semantic differential items developed by Voss et al. (2003).<sup>7</sup> Second, we recruited and trained 50 students at a large Southwest University and asked these students to evaluate the degree of hedonic and utilitarian appeals of the sentence using the same scale by Voss et al. (2003). The scores from the two groups of evaluators are highly correlated (r 0.95 for hedonic and r 0.97 for utilitarian, p < 0.001). The measurement has a Cronbach alpha of 0.85 for hedonic and 0.91 for utilitarian at the sentence level. At the message level, we used weighted mean based on recency or primacy effects of H/U sentence locations in a message (to account for whether readers focus on the <sup>fi</sup>rst few sentences or the last few sentences of a message).<sup>8</sup> We then used the average score on aggregation each day for each product as the measures for message hedonic and utilitarian appeals, respectively.<sup>9</sup>

## 3.3. Moderator Variables

3.3.1. Message Quality and Message Goal Consistency. Message quality is measured by the average score of three commissioned independent content practitioners’ evaluation of the message. Consistent with message quality research (e.g., Chandy et al. 2001, Tellis and Johnson 2007, Kopalle et al. 2017), the experts evaluated each message on a 0 to 10 rating scale of three dimensions in terms of the magnitude of uniqueness, novelty, and believability (in which 0 indicates “not at all,” 5 denotes “neutral,” and 10 represents “very”). Interrater agreement analyses (LeBreton and Senter 2008) suggested that the measure exhibits good reliability and interrater agreement and provided support for aggregation: intraclass correlation coef<sup>fi</sup>cient (ICC)(1) 0.42; ICC(2) 0.83; F-ratio 727.16, p < 0.001; median rwg(j) 0.79, Cronbach alpha 0.92.

Message goal consistency is measured by the average rating of three graduate research assistants who were blind to the purpose of the research. The three assistants were instructed to independently assess each message on three criteria: incompatibility/compatibility, inconsistency/consistency, and incongruity/congruity (Edell and Keller 1989, Duncan and Moriarty 1998, Lee and Park 2007) referenced on the brand image of the <sup>fi</sup>rm “cutting-edge, future-oriented, tech-savvy innovation; world-class; experiential” on a semantic differential scale continuum from 3 to 3 (here, 3 stands for “very,” 0 means “neutral,” and 3 denotes “very”). We conducted an aggregation test, the results of which suggest there was a high degree of interrater agreement (ICC(2) 0.86; LeBreton and Senter 2008). The measurement has a Cronbach alpha of 0.95.

Online Appendix 4 provides examples of highquality, low-quality, consistent, and inconsistent messages. It is worth noting that we tried to measure message quality and message goal consistency by employing machine learning and text-mining approaches. However, we were not successful because we neither found an existing corpus for message quality and message goal consistency, nor could such a corpus be meaningfully constructed because of dif<sup>fi</sup>cult levels of processing and interpretation for these two constructs. Therefore, we adhered to the practice in extant IS and marketing research of using human coders (e.g., Setia et al. 2013, Kopalle et al. 2017) to measure constructs as complex as message quality and message goal consistency. In our research design, we use different scales for message quality and message goal consistency to increase response variability, differentiation, and analytic validity and reliability in the evaluation response (Mac-Kenzie et al. 2011).<sup>10</sup>

3.3.2. Message Complexity. We use the new Dale–Chall readability (DCR, Chall and Dale 1995) formula to calculate message complexity.<sup>11</sup> Readability is a good proxy for measuring message complexity because scores from readability formulas have high correlations with the conceptual properties of text and message complexity and are the strongest predictors of reading speed and comprehension dif<sup>fi</sup>culties (Kintsch and Miller 1984). For each product each day, we use the mean score of the messages that the <sup>fi</sup>rm posted to calculate the message complexity score for our analysis—higher values indicate higher message complexity.

## 3.4. Control Variables

We include the following set of control variables to rule out confounds.

3.4.1. User-Generated Content (UGC). We collected consumer comments (UGC) to FGC posted by the <sup>fi</sup>rm on Facebook by developing a custom-designed Apache Hadoop Hive–based crawler on a distributed computing platform. This tool allows us to obtain relevant data about users and comments simultaneously and can handle massive amounts of data and computation. We obtained the daily timestamp of the posts/ comments and the anonymized unique user identi<sup>fi</sup>- cation data. Our data set contains detailed information, such as consumer comments, posts, shares, and “likes.” The data for user comments regarding the studied company at Twitter was purchased from Gnip. We employed lexicon-based sentiment analysis SentiStrength to capture the sentiment of usergenerated content (Thelwall et al. 2012). For given content, the text-mining tool <sup>fi</sup>rst decomposes the con tent into words and phrases based on its large library. It then performs the extraction of positive, negative, or neutral sentiment. Each extracted word or phrase is assigned a corresponding type indicating the sentiment type (positive, negative, or neutral), and the overall sentiment of the UGC is then generated using counts of sentiment types. We use the log-transformed total volume of consumer’s positive, negative, and neu tral messages to measure positive, negative, and neutral sentiment in UGC for each product.

3.4.2. Brand Love and Brand Distance. Brand love and distance, respectively, represent consumers’ posi tive or negative affective feelings about the brands or products of a company (Batra et al. 2012). We use a linguistic inquiry and word count program (Pennebaker et al. 2015, Ordenes et al. 2018) to automatically analyze emotions of brand love (positive emotion category: e.g., love, nice, sweet) and brand distance (negative emotion anxiety, anger, and sadness category: e.g., hurt, ugly, nasty, worried, annoyed, crying) with the products of the <sup>fi</sup>rm from Facebook and Twitter in the user-generated content as described in Section 3.4.1. We aggregated all message scores for the product to derive a mean level for each day for each product. We mined and conducted separate calculations for brand love and distance with the products of the <sup>fi</sup>rm. The calculations for brand love/distance are

3.4.3. Google Search Index. To capture unobserved time-varying demand shocks for products from consumers, we measure the extent to which the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s products are subject to online search (Archak et al. 2011), consistent with the recommended data-rich approach for addressing endogeneity in Chen et al. We obtain Google search index data for each product from Google Trends and use the mean of the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s daily keyword search frequencies for all different variations (using Google Trends) of the product name on Google to construct the search index. We collect the daily Google Trends search data across the observation period. To account for time trends and seasonality effects, we regress the search index on the time trend and use the residuals in the model.

3.4.4. Traditional Media Spending and Social Media Spending. The data on traditional and social media spending for the <sup>fi</sup>rm are collected from Nielsen and Ad\$pender. Media spending is measured by U.S. millions of dollars. Nielsen and Ad\$pender report weekly <sup>fi</sup>rm media spending. We use gross media expenditures of the <sup>fi</sup>rm on TV, print, and radio to measure traditional media spending. We use gross spending of the <sup>fi</sup>rm on various social media platforms to measure social media spending of the <sup>fi</sup>rm. For both spending types, we average the spending amount one week prior to the focal day of sales and divided it by seven to get the daily spending. Note that this control variable is at the <sup>fi</sup>rm level.

3.4.5. Firm Social Media Promotions. We control for daily <sup>fi</sup>rm social media promotion activities as promotions are salient stimuli for attracting customers (Pauwels et al. 2002). Firm social media promotion is a dummy variable with one denoting there are promotion activities in day t for product i and zero otherwise. Firm social media promotion information is mined from the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s Facebook and Twitter postings, and we used keywords, such as discounts and deals, to classify the messages as promotional.

3.4.6. Message Impression. Message impression is measured by the total daily log-transformed number of likes, shares, and reposts of users to the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s messages on the Facebook page and Twitter account of the <sup>fi</sup>rm. We counted the daily numbers of likes, shares, and reposts for each FGC message for each product posted on Facebook or Twitter, adding them up to get the measure for message impression for each product each day.

3.4.7. Industry Events. We include the number of daily major industry events, such as the release of new products and consumer electronics technologies, major service failures, and major quality improvements of products. The industrial events are measured by the number of news releases from major news outlets in both traditional media and social media via Facti va.<sup>12</sup> This measure is at the industry level.

3.4.8. Product Category and Time Fixed Effects. In order to reduce product category heterogeneity, we add product category dummies for FGC involving smartphones, computers, tablets, digital appliances, and TVs. We use week and seasonal dummies to account for any time effects.

3.4.9. Competitor Media Spending and Messages. To control for competitive reaction to the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm’s marketing activities, we include the major competi tor’s media spending and daily hedonic and utilitarian message volume.<sup>13</sup> The competitor’s media spending data are collected from Nielson and Ad\$pender and are measured by U.S. millions of dollars and use the same procedure for creating the measure as the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm’s media spending. We employ the same approach for collecting data, classifying, and creating hedonic and utilitarian appeals for the competitor’s messages as we do for the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm. Note that we use the overall spending of the competitor <sup>fi</sup>rm rather than for each product.

3.4.10. Product Quality and Price. Product quality is measured by the number of awards and top 10 product rankings accumulated in the past nine (for the year 2016) or 10 years (for the year 2017). Awards and ranking information are extracted from major report outlets, such as Customer Reports, The New York Times, Washington Times, Forbes, Fortune, Economists, CNET, brandindex.com, yougov.com, marketwatch.com, ranker.- com, thedailyrecords.com, and manufacturingglobal.com. We assigned one for each major award that a product received; we assigned value 1, 0.9, 0.8, 0.7, 0.6, 0.5, 0.4, 0.3, 0.2, and 0.1 corresponding with ranking orders 1 to 10; we then added up the scores from awards and the ranking order value to get the total quality score for each product. Product price is measured by the daily price listed on the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s North American website.

3.4.11. Customer Satisfaction. Customer satisfaction positively affects product performance (Fornell et al. 1996). To measure customer satisfaction, we <sup>fi</sup>rst randomly selected <sup>fi</sup>ve daily customer comments each day about each product of the company from Facebook and then employed the service of 25 trained coders (two waves of coding, the <sup>fi</sup>rst wave involving <sup>fi</sup>ve coders and the second wave involving 20 coders with <sup>fi</sup>ve months apart; the two waves of coding are highly correlated, $r = 0 . 9 5$ , Cronbach alpha 0.91) to code satisfaction of different product users of the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm (e.g., TVs, smartphones, refrigerators, computers). The coders independently evaluate these statements employing the widely used customer satisfaction scale (Fornell et al. 1996).<sup>14</sup> We used the daily average scores from the 25 coders to measure customer satisfaction at the product level.

3.4.12. News Media Positive and Negative Sentiment. We collect news media positive and negative sentiment from RavenPack.<sup>15</sup> RavenPack provides various types of sentiment and relevance scores. For the purposes of our research, we consider the composite sentiment score (CSS). RavenPack computes this score only for news articles or press releases that are highly relevant for a company. The CSS is set by a computer algorithm using a coding system established by experts who have classi<sup>fi</sup>ed entity-speci<sup>fi</sup>c news and determined whether the news generally conveys positive or negative sentiment about a company. RavenPack reports daily sentiment scores for each news item in its database on a scale from 0 to 100, on which values above 50 indicate more positive sentiment, and values below 50 denote more negative sentiment. We compute daily positive and negative news sentiment by averaging CSS values across all news items about the <sup>fi</sup>rm published in RavenPack on a daily basis to match with our data on hedonic and utilitarian appeals.

3.4.13. Photos, Videos, and Hyperlinks. We control FGC features, such as whether there are photos, video, and hyperlinks, in the focal FGC as these message features might generate higher virality and sales.

3.4.14. Prior Week Sales. Firms use prior sales to adjust their strategic initiatives. Prior sales might in-<sup>fl</sup>uence current day sales. To alleviate this issue, we control for the prior week sales.

## 4. Analysis and Results 4.1. Model Specification

To detect the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the degree of hedonic and utilitarian appeals on sales revenue,<sup>16</sup> we include lagged hedonic and utilitarian appeals in the model and examine the relationship of these lagged variables on the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s sales revenue to mitigate the potential for reverse causality and simultaneity issues (e.g., Goh et al. 2013, Hewett et al. 2016). We determine the opti mal number of lags based on the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) (Franses and van Oest 2007, Wooldridge 2010). Too many lags in<sup>fl</sup>ate the standard errors of coef<sup>fi</sup>cient estimates, resulting in the proliferation of parameters and an increase in the estimation error, and omitting lags that should be included in the model may result in an estimation bias. BIC is designed to consistently estimate the lag structure as a uniform criterion that minimizes the sum of squared errors while taking model complexity into account. In comparison with the maximum signi<sup>fi</sup>cant lag criterion, minimizing BIC has the advantage of model parsimony. We test whether adding one to <sup>fi</sup>ve lags improves model <sup>fi</sup>t. In line with the BIC, a lag of L  2 is optimal (see Online Appendix 5). Pairwise Granger causality tests suggest that sales revenue does not Granger-cause hedonic and utilitarian messages (none of the F-tests were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at $p < 0 . 0 5 )$ ). The Hausman test $( x ^ { 2 } = 1 1 2 . 7 5 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ) suggests a <sup>fi</sup>xed-effect model, which can alleviate problems such as omitted variable bias. To overcome possible heteroscedasticity (Wooldridge 2010), we use robust errors clustered by product category. Our analysis is at the daily product level.

We use brand love and distance to control for consumer self-selection to the <sup>fi</sup>rm and its products. Unobservable factors can be factors related to both hedonic and utilitarian appeals and <sup>fi</sup>rm sales revenues but are unobservable to the researchers. For example, hedonic and utilitarian appeals could be a function of product features, product quality, and <sup>fi</sup>rms’ strategic intent. Such factors could induce perturbation in the error terms because of which the estimates could be biased. The <sup>fi</sup>rm could also strategically orchestrate hedonic and utilitarian appeals to capitalize on additional, unobserved demand shocks, which makes these variables potentially endogenous. For example, <sup>fi</sup>rms could strategically select time and events to in-<sup>fl</sup>uence demand (Proserpio and Zervas 2017, Wang and Chaudhry 2018). To control for unobservable demand shocks that may be correlated with the predictors, we include Google searches to control for otherwise unobserved demand shocks (Archak et al. 2011). We use news media sentiment scores from RavenPack to control for unobservable consumer behavior changes, investor behavior changes, and <sup>fi</sup>rm strategic policy changes resulting from news cycles on product performance. We model the endogenous variables (hedonic and utilitarian messages) as a function of exogenous and instrumental variables. We use lagged differences in hedonic and utilitarian appeals (daily average difference in the volume of hedonic or utilitarian message appeals between <sup>fi</sup>ve and six months prior preceding each focal post) as instruments because these differences can capture trends in the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s message posting behavior indicative of the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s overall activity level for each post type. Our selection of the instruments takes advantage of the sequential nature of the key variables (Wooldridge 2010). Logically, using lagged message types alleviates the concern for the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s current competitive response and strategic intent. Practically, the older FGC is not very visible on the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s social media, such as Facebook and Twitter, and <sup>fi</sup>rms are less likely to create messages based on what they did a long time ago (in social media time). The further back in time of the lag, the more true this becomes.<sup>17</sup> Customers, therefore, almost exclusively react to the most recent post. Thus, it is unlikely that the posting behavior independent of unmeasured confounders from <sup>fi</sup>ve to six months earlier have a direct in<sup>fl</sup>uence on customer purchase behavior; rather, any possible in<sup>fl</sup>uence goes through the latest messages.

Despite our reasoning, one can still argue that a company likely has consistent marketing strategies in multiple channels. As a result, the company’s current hedonic and utilitarian message appeals in other channels (such as traditional media and search ads, which can be more in<sup>fl</sup>uential compared with FGCs) are affected by the proposed IVs, and these variables are unobservable to the researchers. In order to have con<sup>fi</sup>dence in our results, we employ the widely used the impact threshold of a confounding variable (ITCV) test. The test results suggest that, for an omitted variable to revoke our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, 47.47% cases for hedonic appeals and 52.35% cases for utilitarian appeals would have to be replaced with cases for which there is an effect of zero. Thus, the pattern of correlations suggests that the main results are reasonably robust to potential correlated omitted variables.

## 5. Discussion

## 5.1. Theoretical Implications

This study has several novel theoretical implications for information systems and digital marketing. First, the current work contributes to the body of literature on FGC by examining the psycholinguistic nature of message appeals. The theoretical underpinnings for hedonic/utilitarian appeals are drawn from the psycholinguistic literature that considers both affective and instrumental aspects of online messages (e.g., Wittgenstein 1953, Austin 1962, Searle 1976, Voss et al. 2003, Chitturi et al. 2007, Erler 2010, Brisset 2018). Such perspective allows for a granular assessment of the role of FGC on consumer purchases and offers prescriptions on FGC content design. To our knowledge, as important as content strategy is in the digita era, the roles of hedonic and utilitarian appeals in <sup>fi</sup>rms’ social media communication in impacting daily sales have not been empirically examined. Using extensive coding of the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s FGC across two of the most popular social media sites, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that hedonic appeals have a more substantial positive impact (0.449) than utilitarian appeals (0.151) on sales; that is, the elasticity is 2.11 for hedonic appeals and 0.65 for utilitarian appeals. Our research suggests that it is crucial to consider message appeals, which have differential effects on product performance. In terms of effect size, our results are comparable to reported results in FGC research. For example, Tellis et al. (2019) report effects for information content ranging from 0.39 to 0.46 on shares and 0.25 to 2.38 for emotional content on shares of different types of messages at different social media platforms. Meire et al. (2019) report a nonsigni<sup>fi</sup>cant effect of information content of 0.057 and a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect of emotional content of 0.122 on customer sentiment. Kumar et al. (2016) report the effects of FGC on consumer spending ranging from 0.985 to 1.164 using different model speci<sup>fi</sup>cations. Compared with these three studies, Lee et al. (2018) report smaller effects of messages on sharing and likes. However, depending on the speci<sup>fi</sup>c category of the messages, the effect of brand-personality messages ranges from 0.545 to 1.084 (EdgeRankcorrected) and 0.923 to 1.162 for mixed messages in terms of odds ratio (table 7, Lee et al. 2018). The effect of informative messages in Lee et al. (2018) is not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant as in Meire et al. (2019).

This study extends research on FGC by exploiting strategies to manage FGC that are under the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s control (Batra and Keller 2016, He et al. 2018, Chung et al. 2020). Though researchers generally consider the main effects of FGC types on outcomes (e.g., Lee et al. 2018), studies exploring the joint effects of FGC psycholinguistic characteristics and content types are rare. Our results suggest that it is important to take content characteristics into consideration when study ing FGC types on performance outcomes. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that both hedonic and utilitarian appeals signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affect performance but in differential and complex ways. The results suggest that both hedonic and utilitarian appeals hurt product performance when message quality and message goal consistency are at low levels. By demonstrating that hedonic and utilitarian appeals are effective for product performance only when message quality and goal consistency reach certain levels, our study stresses the value of a coherent and integrated marketing communication infrastructure. This insight is reinforced by the results suggesting message quality and goal consistency moderate hedonic more than utilitarian appeals. Our research also shows that message complexity can dilute the positive effects of message types on product performance, and message complexity hurts hedonic more than utilitarian messages. Accordingly, our research might offer an explanation to the divergent results in extant research, such as the countervailing effects of informative and brand-personality messages on message sharing (Miller and Tucker 2013, Homburg et al. 2015, Lee et al. 2018, Meire et al. 2019). Our research offers empirical support for the principle of quality, consistency, and simplicity in online marketing com munication (Batra and Keller 2016), which speci<sup>fi</sup>es that the elements of marketing communication should be consistent, easy-to-comprehend, and of high quali ty, therefore mutually reinforcing to be effective.

This research also makes contributions to psycholinguistic research. Psycholinguistic theory offers means to study messengers’ intentions of language in use and the subsequent behavioral outcomes in message recipients. For example, in speech act theory, languages are used to understand what people intend to achieve by saying something or to capture the effects of utterances on audiences (Austin 1962, Searle 1976, Erler 2010, Brisset 2018). However, understanding the messenger’s meaning and accepting the messenger’s beliefs or attitudes and the subsequent behavioral changes are two different processes. Marketing persuasion is not a one-sided affair produced by the marketers onto the customers. Marketing communication works smoothly when messages are sent and achieve the intended results by the message senders. We extend the psycholinguistic theory to the FGC domain via hedonic and utilitarian appeals and the psycholinguistic characteristics of message appeals. Though psycholinguistic research suggests that language in use intends to stimulate and elicit actions, re<sup>fl</sup>ective of speakers’ intentions and predictive of recipients’ responses, our research suggests that FGC messages vary in the extent to which they evoke consumer behavioral changes and the subsequent product performance outcomes. A theory of marketing communication, and an adequate account of marketing communication persuasion, must be able to systematically explain how marketing stimuli are understood and the degree to which their persuasive intention is ful<sup>fi</sup>lled. Our research suggests that marketers can intentionally design FGC messages to in<sup>fl</sup>uence receivers’ perceptions and interpretations through eloquent, persuasively espoused and curated languages, arousing consumers’ emotions, evoking desires for a situation, product, or service.

## 5.2. Practical Implications

This study provides critical managerial implications. First, our research suggests that creative and original message appeals stimulate customers, generating arousal through novelties, meanings, associations, inspirations, human emotions and connections, and the degree to which the message appeals resonate and get the attention of the customers. The higher positive effect of hedonic appeals demonstrates that hedonic appeals, which are affective, interactive, and creative, are what win the attention, hearts, and minds of consumers. Hedonic appeals imbue desirability around a product, endowing it with mystery or coolness, subtly invoking intentions, anticipation, and agency. As technological, analytical, and logistic factors, which constitute competitive advantages of the past, increasingly converge in the current digital era, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that the creative capabilities and the ability to craft messages that resonate and empathize with consumers will constitute competitive advantages of the future. Our research suggests that creative appeals affect how consumers interpret their interactions with products, brands, services, other consumers, and experiences. Consumers use and respond to creative message appeals, whether it be consumers’ own consumption-related message processing; how they organize experiences, create order, explain unusual events, gain perspective, and make evaluations; or their implicit or explicit use of marketers’ creative appeals and user experiences to persuade others. Given the affective and cognitive nature of online messages and our empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings, <sup>fi</sup>rms should highlight more about the hedonic rather than utilitarian features of products with high quality, highly consistent, or less complex FGC messages to persuade consumers to purchase.

Second, in essence, the results suggest that, for message appeals to have intended performance outcomes, message quality must reach high levels. High-quality content is original and virtuosic, novel and compel ling, curated and accurate, insightful and relevant, relatable and credible, authentic and immersive, and engaging and resonating with customers. Our results reveal that, at the low levels of message quality, the total effect of hedonic appeals on daily sales revenue is between 0.525 and 0.177 and between 0.208 and 0.026 for utilitarian appeals. In comparison, at the high levels of message quality, the total effect of hedonic appeals on daily sales revenue ranges from 0.277 to 0.892 and 0.089 to 0.381 for utilitarian appeals. High-quality content is the conversation starter and problem solver in social media. When <sup>fi</sup>rms create FGC communications, they can ask the following questions:

i. Is there a point to the message? Each FGC should provide value by helping users solve their real problems, answer their questions and concerns, pique and expand their interests, or build trust in the prod uct—and, <sup>fi</sup>nally, provide users with concrete and practical insights they can use after processing. If there is no clear point, then the message will have little effect and be considered as noise by consumers. FGC messages must resonate with real and live users so they can land and genuinely connect with their intended audiences. The most successful FGC messages on social media respond to customer comments and engage with users who are engaging with them.

ii. Does the message <sup>fl</sup>ow well aesthetically and logically? High-quality messages can emphasize the ideal aesthetics to aid brand recognition and brand affect. If the FGC message execution is not informative or entertaining, users won’t click to learn and explore more. Spelling, styling, grammatical mistakes, and verbosity can damage message credibility and consumers’ attitudes toward the branded products.

iii. Is the message optimized to drive desired consumer actions using best-practice formatting, headers, style, and link structures? Firms can use a variety of available tools, such as clicks, inquiries, likes, shares, comments, email contact submissions, phone calls, and purchase incentives, to engage with customers. Planning an FGC message around one of these action tools helps establish a narrative framework for the message and focus the creative style of the content. Purposeful, result-driven FGC planning, combined with an aesthetically pleasant narrative, will result in higher customer engagement and better product sales performance.

iv. Does the message include different types of multimedia such as videos, audios, tables, charts, slideshows, and photos? High-quality FGC contains different formats and visuals that explain the information in the best way possible.

Third, message goal consistency calls for alignment to central brand guidelines (i.e., what the brand stands for, its value proposition, and its purpose) and adherence to the brand DNA regarding look and feel, tone and manner in FGC creation. Our results indicate that at the low levels of message goal consistency, the total effect of hedonic appeals on daily sales revenue is between 0.269 and 0.079 and between 0.197 and 0.036 for utilitarian appeals. In comparison, at the high levels of message goal consistency, the total effect on daily sales revenue ranges from 0.115 to 0.609 for hedonic appeals and 0.051 to 0.328 for utilitarian appeals. Higher consistency in FGC makes the brand more recognizable, credible, and stable in consumers minds. Message goal consistency, therefore, mandates a clear brand positioning and style guidelines communicated throughout the entire <sup>fi</sup>rm. In reality, a <sup>fi</sup>rm communicates on many different topics, uses unique creative layouts for various audiences, and employs distinctive languages and idioms when addressing various markets. The <sup>fi</sup>rm also needs to adapt its messages to the speci<sup>fi</sup>c features on each social media platform (e.g., Twitter, Instagram, Youtube, and Facebook have different length and media requirements). It is imperative that across all of these topics, creatives, markets, and platforms, there has to be an overarching consistency in communications rules to ensure that the look, tone, terminology, voice, and framing are consistent across all FGC messages.

Consistent messaging can be achieved when <sup>fi</sup>rms <sup>fi</sup>rst create clear brand goal consistency guidelines to follow. The established brand goal consistency guidelines must be implemented across all touchpoints and clearly communicate what the brand stands for. It is critically important that all FGC staff members, across functional areas, geographic markets, product divisions, and social media platforms are well versed in these guidelines and stick to them. At <sup>fi</sup>rst glance, creating, educating, and enforcing the branding guideline might seem like extra work. Still, such efforts can pay off and make subsequent executions easier; a clear guideline clari<sup>fi</sup>es the marketing purpose in the employees’ minds and creates a strong foundation from which to build. The results of these conscious efforts are consistent brand messages, ensuring that product communications, customer service, and brand initiatives elicit strong consumer engagement for each message.

For example, when it comes to message goal consistency, <sup>fi</sup>rms such as Apple and Walmart rely on speci<sup>fi</sup>c message guidelines that govern all aspects of their brand messaging. Apple has built up its message goal consistency over time with meticulously curated messages that reinforce its central identity: sleek and elegant products that push the boundaries of innovation. Walmart is consistently talking about offering low prices for customer value creation in all its digita touchpoints at various social media sites. Without a message goal consistent effort, FGC communications appear disjointed and don’t convey the desired brand image, sending mixed signals to consumers. For instance, consider the mishmash of FGC messages commonly seen for many automobile brands. The brand’s message goal is slick, professional, and conveys a speci<sup>fi</sup>c image although the local dealer’s message, in general, is unprofessional, poorly executed, and confuses the prospective buyer about the brand.

Fourth, message simplicity is what makes a message strong and memorable. Writing clearly and concisely in a novel approach creates intrigue by making FGC communications stick. Excellent writing and easy readability make for easy comprehension and, consequently, better persuasion. Our results demon strate that at the high levels of message complexity, the total effect of hedonic appeals on daily sales revenue is between 0.519 and 0.103 and between 0.327 and 0.058 for utilitarian appeals. In comparison, at the low levels of message complexity, the total effect on daily sales revenue ranges from 0.251 to 0.793 for hedonic appeals and 0.075 to 0.448 for utilitarian appeals. When a message is clear and easy to grasp, it helps to keep it strongly implanted in the mind of the targeted audience. Crisp and highly ar ticulated messaging makes it possible for customers to imagine what results they could expect from using the branded product. The more complex that the message is, the more likely it is to be ignored. The more clear, concise, and consistent the messages are, the more recognizable they are to consumers, evoking positive emotions and providing a feeling of reliability and con<sup>fi</sup>dence. The negative moderating impact of message complexity on product perfor mance suggests that <sup>fi</sup>rms get their messages tested for readability before posting them on their social media pages. If not, both hedonic and utilitarian appeals suffer, and the negative impact is more severe for hedonic appeals.

## 5.3. Limitations and Future Research

Despite the rich data and the triangulating empirical efforts in this study, several limitations remain. First, similar to the extant FGC literature, we collected FGCs from Facebook and Twitter as they have the largest market share on social media by the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm (e.g., same settings as in Hewett et al. 2016). Studying other platforms, such as Snapchat, WhatsApp, Instagram, and YouTube, could extend the generalizability of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Second, the sample products used in this study are all digital (i.e., smartphones, computers, tablets, digital appliances, and TVs). Though the <sup>fi</sup>rm markets these products as hedonic artifacts in its marketing communications, one can argue that these products are very utilitarian/functional oriented. Hedonic-oriented products (e.g., <sup>fl</sup>owers, music, movies) could differ from utilitarian/functional oriented products. We believe that our results could apply to more hedonic-oriented products because the hedonic–utilitarian distinction lies on a continuum. In general, for utilitarian-oriented products, <sup>fi</sup>rms highlight their hedonic nature as a marketing strategy, which we see in our data. For hedonic-oriented products, <sup>fi</sup>rms emphasize their functional utilities as a marketing strategy. Because we <sup>fi</sup>nd that hedonic appeals work for more functional-oriented products in our sample, future research involving more hedonicoriented products, such as music and movies, could validate our results reported here.

Third, we focus on the differential effects of psycholinguistic and textual characteristics, such as message quality, goal consistency, and complexity. Future research could also consider the moderating effects of product involvement or types of textual, photo, or video content posted as FGC. In this aspect, future research could apply the digital visual content analytics that can algorithmically transform unstructured image data into useful representations for computer vision tasks as developed in Shin et al. (2020). Fourth, the absence of focal <sup>fi</sup>rm internal data, such as market segmentation, targeting, and customer service strategy, does not allow us to completely rule out the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of <sup>fi</sup>rm strategic action on the effect of message types documented here. Investigating the boundary conditions employing <sup>fi</sup>rms’ internal data, such as marketing communication techniques and strategic priorities, may be of interest for future research.

Fifth, platforms such as Facebook and Twitter implement complex algorithms that target FGC to nonrandom groups of consumers. As a result, hedonic and utilitarian appeals may be shown in different amounts to different types of consumers. Even though we controlled for message engagement, such as likes, shares, and reposts, it is not the same as the actual impression/visibility of the message simply because not all consumers exposed to a message would engage with it. Moreover, different customers have different characteristics and content preferences. The degree of deviation in terms of customer preferences and characteristics, relative to the expectation of a speci<sup>fi</sup>c audience, might determine the level of attention and processing and, ultimately, the impacts of message appeals. Ideally, individual customer data can capture customer heterogeneity precisely, but data limitations preclude such analyses. Though we employ a control function approach to alleviate the endogeneity issues, we cannot completely rule out endogeneity caused by the unobserved message exposure and customer heterogeneity. Future research can investigate this issue by combining customer-level variables, such as demographic, psychographic, and behavioral; content preference; the platform’s decision model; and the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s decision model. Finally, we manually classify the message types. Future research can develop methods in machine learning to classify message types based on the message classi<sup>fi</sup>cation methods deployed in this research.
