# What Questions Are You Inclined to Answer? Effects of Hierarchy in Corporate Q&A Communities

Abstract. An increasing number of companies have started to implement corporate knowledge-sharing communities. Consistent with the observation in the of<sup>fl</sup>ine setting, employees are less likely to share knowledge with individuals who have higher job ranks (i.e., higher-ups) in corporate communities such as online wikis and discussion groups. Given the importance of managers’ engagements in the community and the needs for knowledge sharing across the hierarchy, we examine whether such observation persists in the corporate question-and-answer (Q&A) community, another popular type of corporate knowledgesharing community. On the one hand, as in the of<sup>fl</sup>ine setting and other types of communities, employees can still be reluctant to share knowledge with the higher-ranked individuals in the Q&A community. On the other hand, a Q&A community has some unique attribute that can potentially motivate employees to engage more with the higher-ups. Using a unique data set from a large corporate Q&A community and a potential-dyads approach, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that a user is inclined to respond to a knowledge seeker whose job rank is higher than (versus lower than or the same as) the user’s rank in the corporate Q&A community. We further show the causality of the result with a quasi experiment that leverages the promotions announced in our study period. Because these promotions are based on employees’ perform ances before the existence of the community, the promotion announcements are largely exogenous to our research interest. We also <sup>fi</sup>nd that knowledge providers exert greater effort when answering questions from the higher-ups. Finally, our analyses show that knowledge providers who post more answers to higher-ranked seekers and who display greater effort in those answers are more likely to get promoted in subsequent years. Given the critical role of knowledge sharing and the increasing prevalence of online communities, our study offer a better understanding of the knowledge-sharing pattern in the corporate Q&A community of the hierarchical organizations and delivers useful managerial implications.

## 1. Introduction

How best to motivate knowledge sharing has been a persistent challenge for companies (e.g., Lu et al. 2017). Lew Platt, the former chief executive of Hewlett-Packard (HP), realized the importance of knowledge and observed that “If only HP knew what HP knows, we would be three times more productive” (Hagel and Brown 2008). Despite its critical role, knowledge is often insulated of<sup>fl</sup>ine, which makes it challenging to acquire, assimilate, and synthesize (Hwang et al. 2015). In particular, knowledge sharing is normally constrained by an organization’s formal hierarchy (Michailova and Husted 2003). Hierarchy asymmetry is one of the biggest barriers to knowledge sharing in the organization (e.g., Riege 2005), and in the of<sup>fl</sup>ine setting, employees are less likely to share knowledge voluntarily with individuals who have higher job ranks (i.e., higher-ups; e.g., Keltner et al. 2003).

The knowledge-sharing disinclination re<sup>fl</sup>ects the worry of many employees that sharing knowledge with a higher-up can jeopardize their careers (e.g., Stasser and Titus 2003), because imperfect or incorrect input can make the higher-ups have negative opinions of the employees. Employees fear that they will be rejected or ignored if their thoughts do not align with the higher-ups’ intent (Keltner et al. 2003), because the objectives and actual intents of higher-ranked employees often are uncertain (Lelic 2001). Furthermore, lower-ranked employees are more likely to feel the pressure of hierarchy asymmetry and respond by withholding their true opinions even though they may have valuable information (Sunstein 2005, Qiu et al. 2017).

Recently, corporate online knowledge-sharing communities have become increasingly popular. According to McKinsey’s 2016 report on organizational social tools, 80% of executives (compared with 69% in 2014) mention that their companies use tools such as an online knowledge-sharing community for internal communication (Bughin et al. 2016). The online community can potentially facilitate knowledge sharing among employees, as it enables any employee to seek and provide knowledge from any place, at any time. Hierarchical companies launching online communities expect to motivate knowledge sharing across the hierarchy (Hwang et al. 2015, Faraj et al. 2016). Also, in corporate knowledgesharing communities, managers’ leadership and engagement are important (e.g., Stocker et al. 2012, Argyris and Ransbotham 2016). To better understand the outcomes and the knowledge-sharing pattern when the managers proactively participate in the community, it is inevitable to discuss whether employees behave differently when they face the higher-ups (i.e., managers). Recent studies have started to examine whether and to what extent the organizational hierarchy affects knowledge sharing in corporate online communities, such as corporate wikis and online discussion groups (e.g., Behrendt et al. 2015, Meske et al. 2020). The conclusion so far is consistent with the observation in the of<sup>fl</sup>ine setting: employees are disinclined to share knowledge with their higherups in corporate online communities.

The corporate question-and-answer (Q&A) community is another popular type of corporate knowledgesharing community.<sup>1</sup> It offers an open discussion board for users to pose and answer questions (Beck et al. 2014). In this study, we examine the effects of hierarchy on knowledge sharing in the corporate Q&A community. Because this type of corporate knowledge-sharing community has features of both a hierarchical organization and an online Q&A community, we cannot simply infer the conclusions from the existing studies. On the one hand, the knowledge-sharing pattern in the Q&A community can be similar to that in of<sup>fl</sup>ine and other types of online communities, where the pressure from hierarchy asymmetry is salient; therefore, it is possible that users in the Q&A community are still disinclined to share knowledge with higher-ups.

On the other hand, however, three unique characteristics of the Q&A community can potentially make users inclined to share knowledge with higher-ups. First, in the Q&A community, knowledge sharing happens only when a knowledge seeker posts a question; compared with the other knowledge-sharing channels, the provider can have fewer concerns about making mistakes because the interaction starts with the seeker’s inquiry and an imperfect answer is within the knowledge seeker’s expectations (Wang et al. 2018). Second, the expectation for future reciprocal behaviors motivates the users to contribute in the Q&A communities (e.g., Kankanhalli et al. 2005, Faraj and Johnson 2011); a higher-ranked knowledge seeker is able to offer more valuable reciprocal bene<sup>fi</sup>ts because a higher job rank is associated with more resources and abilities (Nahapiet and Ghoshal 1998). Finally, community users’ answers in the Q&A community can help improve their reputations (e.g., Wasko and Faraj 2000, Shi et al. 2021); to better gain reputation, users tend to strategically engage more in the impact ful posts (Faraj and Johnson 2011) and to interact more with the in<sup>fl</sup>uential individuals (Kuk 2006).

These unique characteristics of corporate Q&A communities should act against the pressures of hierarchy asymmetry that otherwise would be expected to deter users from sharing knowledge with higher-ups. Therefore, the net effect of hierarchy on knowledge sharing in the corporate online Q&A communities is an open empirical question. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, in this paper, we ad dress the following research questions: In a corporate online Q&A community, is a user relatively disinclined or inclined to answer a question posted by a knowledge seeker with a higher (versus the same or a lower) job rank as th user? Moreover, does a knowledge provider exert greater ef fort when answering a question posted by a higher-ranked (versus a lower-ranked or same-ranked) knowledge seeker?

We then explore an intriguing follow-up question: Does sharing knowledge with higher-ranked individuals affect the knowledge provider’s probability of getting promoted? Analyzing this question further helps us understand the potential costs or bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of interacting with the higher-ups in the corporate Q&A community and whether the extents of these costs or bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are aligned with (or different from) those in the of<sup>fl</sup>ine setting and other types of communities. For instance, if sharing knowledge with higher-ranked individuals leads to a lower probability of promotion, then the argument that employees have career concerns when interacting with the higher-ups should still be valid in the Q&A community (e.g., Michailova and Husted 2003). If answering the higher-ups’ questions results in a higher probability of promotion for the knowledge provider, then the corporate Q&A community should have some unique characteristics (versus other knowledge-sharing contexts) in terms of the costs of interacting with the higher-ups; in this case, users do not necessarily need to worry about their careers when sharing knowledge with higher-ranked individuals in the corporate Q&A community.

We obtain a unique data set from a corporate online Q&A community in the marketing segment of a large company in China. The company’s formal hierarchical structure is based on the consultant team, market area, department, and market section. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the company clearly de<sup>fi</sup>nes eight job ranks, ranging from the junior business consultant to the member of the top management team. On each question page in the Q&A community, in addition to the textual content and the content generation time, the users’ real names are next to the questions and answers. Those who log in to the community can also access a separate page with all the users’ information, including their work sections, job ranks, work types (departments), work cities, and the total numbers of generated answers.

We <sup>fi</sup>rst use a series of tests to analyze whether a user is disinclined or inclined to share knowledge with a knowledge seeker of a higher (versus the same or lower) rank as the user. With a potential-dyads approach, we show that when a prospective knowledge provider faces all the available questions, they are more likely to answer a question from a knowledge seeker with a higher (versus the same or lower) job rank. We extensively control for the question’s text features and the knowledge relevance of the knowledge provider’s expertise by applying the latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA) and constructing custom dictionaries. We also control for the users’ other organizational attributes and take into account the peer knowledge providers’ answers. The results are consistent across all our robustness tests.

We further design a quasi experiment involving promotions announced in our study period, such that we have data on users’ knowledge-providing and -seeking behaviors before and after the promotions. These promotion announcements are largely exogenous to our research interests because the Q&A community is launched just prior to our study period, and promotions announced in our study period are based on the performance indicators before the existence of the community. Meanwhile, a promotion directly alters the relative hierarchical rank between the promoted employee and their colleagues who remain at the promoted employee’s original job rank, and between the promoted employee and the employees who are at the promoted employee’s new job rank. We show that after (versus before) an employee’s promotion, knowledge providers who are at the promoted employee’s original job rank respond more to that employee; however, after (versus before) the promotion, the promoted employee responds less to knowledge seekers who are at the promoted employee’s new job rank.

We then examine whether knowledge providers exert different levels of effort when responding to higher-ranked (versus lower-ranked or same-ranked) knowledge seekers. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that knowledge providers exert greater effort when responding to higher-ranked seekers; the number of non-stop words in the answer, the diversity of topics in the answer, and the answer’s relevance to the question jointly re<sup>fl</sup>ect the knowledge providers’ answering effort.

Finally, we estimate a two-stage least squares (2SLS) model with an instrumental variable to explore whether a user is more likely to be promoted if they answe more to the higher-ups. In the Q&A community, all questions are listed in an inverse chronological order, and users are more likely to encounter the recent questions. For each user, we count the number of higherups’ questions generated one day before the date when the user logs in the community and posts an answer, and apply this as the instrumental variable for the number of user’s responses to higher-ups. This instrumental variable should have a positive relationship with the user’s number of responses to higher-ups, but should not be directly related to the user’s future promotion because the questions are posted by others (i.e., whether the user can encounter those questions is rela tively exogenous). We <sup>fi</sup>nd that users who answer more to the higher-ups are more likely to get promoted in subsequent years.

Our study contributes to the literature by examining whether and to what extent hierarchy affects knowledge sharing in the corporate Q&A community. Our results show that employees are more inclined to share knowledge with other employees of a higher rank (versus the same or lower rank) in the Q&A community, a <sup>fi</sup>nding that is different from the knowledge-sharing disinclination to higher-ups in the of<sup>fl</sup>ine setting and other types of corporate knowledge-sharing communities (e.g., Behrendt et al. 2015, Meske et al. 2020). We further investigate the effects of answering to the higher-ups on the knowledge providers, and the <sup>fi</sup>ndings expand our understanding of the effects of users community behaviors in public online communities (e.g., Roberts et al. 2006, Hann et al. 2013) to the internal corporate online communities.

As practitioners are increasingly interested in online communities for knowledge sharing, our research, together with the existing studies on other types of corporate communities, helps companies understand employees’ knowledge-sharing patterns and decide which type of community to adopt. Also, the community designers can motivate the knowledge sharing in the Q&A community more effectively by considering the answer inclination to the higher hierarchy. Finally, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the positive relationship between employees’ responses to the higher-ups and their future promotions offer implications for companies regarding promotion decisions and criteria.

## 2. Literature Review

Hierarchy is the formal vertical integration of of<sup>fi</sup>cial positions within one explicit organizational structure (Diefenbach and Sillince 2011). In a hierarchical organization, the members’ of<sup>fi</sup>cial roles and positions are clearly de<sup>fi</sup>ned and demarcated from each other. Hierarchy affects not only the formal monitoring and supervising relationships between employees with different job ranks but also the <sup>fl</sup>ow of information and knowledge within the organization (e.g., Friebel and Raith 2004). The hierarchy asymmetry is one of the biggest barriers to knowledge sharing, as employees are disinclined to share knowledge voluntarily with higher-ups in the of<sup>fl</sup>ine setting (e.g., Riege 2005). An open and <sup>fl</sup>exible organizational structure can facilitate knowledge sharing (e.g., De Long and Fahey 2000), but an emphasis on hierarchy creates a workplace climate that constrains effective knowledge sharing (Michailova and Husted 2003). The knowledge-sharing disinclination related to hierarchy re<sup>fl</sup>ects employees worries that sharing knowledge with higher-ups can jeopardize their careers (e.g., Lelic 2001). For instance, employees in a Russian <sup>fi</sup>rm are reluctant to share knowledge with their managers, who often express dissatisfaction when lower-ranking employees are more knowledgeable (Michailova and Husted 2003).

As companies have increasingly adopted corporate knowledge-sharing communities, information systems scholars have started to analyze the relationship between hierarchical structures and knowledge-sharing patterns in these emerging corporate communities. Corporate wikis, for instance, are useful for knowledge sharing because a wiki system hosts multiple freely editable pages that can facilitate internal conversation (e.g., Argyris and Ransbotham 2016). However, employees often are reticent to contribute to or revise posts, especially those created by higher-ups, because the original creators of posts may perceive revisions as judgmental or evaluative (Beylier et al. 2009, Grudin and Poole 2010). Overall, wiki users are inclined to discuss with colleagues who have the same job rank and rarely discuss with those who have different job ranks (Behrendt et al. 2015). Similarly, in the company’s Skype discussion groups, employees have fewer and shorter conversations with higher-ranked (versus lower-ranked or same-ranked) employees (Meske et al. 2020). The general conclusion of the literature, so far, is that, consistent with the observation in the of<sup>fl</sup>ine setting, users are disinclined to share knowledge with individuals with higher job ranks in the corporate knowledge-sharing communities.

This study contributes to this group of literature by documenting the effects of hierarchy on knowledge sharing in a corporate Q&A community, another popular type of corporate knowledge-sharing community. Compared with the of<sup>fl</sup>ine setting and other types of knowledge-sharing communities, the Q&A community is unique in terms of the costs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of sharing knowledge with higher-ups. Given the importance of improving managers’ engagement in corporate online communities (e.g., Argyris and Ransbotham 2016) and the practical needs for knowledge sharing across the hierarchy (Hwang et al. 2015, Faraj et al. 2016), it is necessary to understand the knowledge-sharing patterns in the corporate Q&A community when users have different hierarchical ranks.

To better understand whether users should have career concerns when they share knowledge in the corporate Q&A community, we further examine the effects of answering higher-ups’ questions on the knowledge provider’s future promotion. We contribute to the literature that analyzes the material effects of knowledgesharing behaviors on community users. Different from individuals’ overall contribution levels in open online communities (Roberts et al. 2006, Hann et al. 2013, Huang and Zhang 2016), we focus more on the quantity and effort of employees’ knowledge-sharing activities to higher-ups in an internal Q&A community.

## 3. Hypotheses Development

As the corporate Q&A community has features of both a hierarchical organization and an online Q&A community, we adopt two perspectives, in turn, to theorize the effects of hierarchy on users’ knowledge-sharing behaviors in the corporate Q&A community. On the one hand, the literature on hierarchical organizations consistently implies that employees are less likely to share knowledge with the higher-ups. On the other hand, however, the corporate Q&A community has some unique characteristics that differentiate it from of<sup>fl</sup>ine and other corporate knowledge-sharing communities, and these characteristics can motivate knowledge sharing with higher-ups.

In a hierarchical organization, employees are less likely to voluntarily share knowledge with higherranked individuals in of<sup>fl</sup>ine communication. Discussing with higher-ranked individuals usually leads to extra costs for the employees. Because a higher job rank normally re<sup>fl</sup>ects more power and a greater level of authority (Nahapiet and Ghoshal 1998), an employ ee fears that a discussion with a higher-up will harm their career (Stasser and Titus 2003) or that they will be rejected or ignored if their thoughts do not align with their higher-up’s intent (Keltner et al. 2003). Moreover, employees are worried that acting more knowledgeable than a higher-up may cost them the higher-up’s favor (Michailova and Husted 2003, Wil kesmann et al. 2009). This social pressure also leads some employees to hide their true opinions from higher-ups (Sunstein 2005, Qiu et al. 2017).

A similar pattern can also be seen in certain types of corporate knowledge-sharing communities, such as wikis and online discussion groups; that is, users still suffer from the potential costs of sharing knowledge with higher-ups even in emerging corporate communities, and they fear that sharing knowledge with higher-ups in the communities threatens their careers. In theory, wikis enable knowledge contributors not only to add their own knowledge but also to integrate and modify others’ existing inputs (e.g., Wang et al. 2018). In reality, however, users in the corporate wikis are reluctant to edit others’ existing posts— especially the ones from their higher-ups (Hasan and Pfaff 2007; Argyris and Ransbotham 2016, pp. 228–229), and the users are disinclined to communicate directly or collaborate with higher-ranked (versus lower-ranked or same-ranked) users (Klier et al. 2017). Similarly, in the company’s Skype discussion groups, employees conversations with higher-ups tend to be shorter and are more likely to end soon (Meske et al. 2020).

It is possible that a corporate Q&A community, as another type of corporate knowledge-sharing community, is simply supplementary to those communities where the potential costs of replying to the higher-ups persist and the knowledge-sharing disinclination to high ranks still exists. In this regard, we can reasonably expect that knowledge sharing in the Q&A community exhibits a similar pattern. After all, the online community can be simply an extension of the organization itself, so the users’ knowledge-sharing behaviors in the community are probably still under the pressures of hierarchy asymmetry. A user in the Q&A community might still worry that an imperfect response, or even the act of providing a response to a higher-up’s question, will be treated as inappropriate or inaccurate.

Moreover, the common ground between the knowledge provider and seeker is vital for a successful answer (Hwang et al. 2015). A knowledge provider is often more comfortable and con<sup>fi</sup>dent when responding to a knowledge seeker of the same or lower job rank, as in this case, the provider and seeker share a similar work environment and background knowledge (Behrendt et al. 2015, Klier et al. 2017). Finally, prospective knowledge providers tend to consider how helpful their answers will be before deciding whether to answer the questions at hand (Wasko and Faraj 2005), and higher-ranked seekers are less likely to appreciate the responses of lower-ranked providers. Accordingly, we propose our <sup>fi</sup>rst hypothesis.

Hypothesis 1a. In the corporate Q&A community, users are less likely to share knowledge with knowledge seekers who have a higher (versus the same or lower) job rank within the hierarchy.

However, the corporate Q&A community has some unique characteristics and represents a departure from of<sup>fl</sup>ine and other types of corporate online knowledgesharing communities in several important regards, and these differences can potentially shape a different effect of hierarchy on knowledge sharing. Here, we discuss, in the corporate setting, how the Q&A com munity differs from other knowledge-sharing contexts, including the of<sup>fl</sup>ine setting and other types of online communities, in terms of the extent of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and costs of sharing knowledge with higher-ups.

First, knowledge providers have fewer career concerns when answering higher-ups’ questions in the corporate Q&A community than communicating with the higher-ups in the other contexts. The potential costs of sharing knowledge with the higher-ups are less in the Q&A community than those in the other contexts. Knowledge providers posting an answer in the Q&A community expect that any given answer can lead to one of three outcomes: a satisfactory solution, helpful information but not an actual solution, or a futile or inconclusive discussion (Wang et al. 2014). In the Q&A community, an imperfect answer or inconclusive discussion to a question is within the knowledge seeker’s expectation and it is acceptable. The Q&A community has a one-to-many relationship and one question can have multiple answers, so having one imperfect answer does not hurt the knowledge seeker or provider much. This is different from othe types of corporate communities, such as wikis and discussion groups, where the knowledge providers post content or revise others’ content, which needs to be accurate, because otherwise the audience will have a negative opinion of them (Holtzblatt et al. 2010, Arazy and Gellatly 2012). Corporate wikis and discussion groups normally involve many-to-many relationships, so one knowledge provider’s content is likely to be checked by other knowledge seekers, and one imperfect contribution can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly cost the knowledge provider. Thus, compared with the other corporate communities, inaccurate content in the O&A commu: nity is less inappropriate, and users in the Q&A community are less worried that their contributions will result in the higher-ups’ disapproval. Similarly, em ployees should be more comfortable sharing knowledge with higher-ups in the Q&A community than in the of<sup>fl</sup>ine setting because information in the face-toface setting is also expected to be accurate

Second, providing knowledge in the Q&A community bene<sup>fi</sup>ts knowledge providers more in terms of the knowledge seeker’s reciprocal behaviors, and responding to a seeker with a higher job rank potentially brings more reciprocal bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. Reciprocity re<sup>fl</sup>ects embedded obligations created by exchanges of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts or favors (Gouldner 1960), and it is one of the key mechanisms that explain knowledge-sharing activities in online communities (e.g., Wasko and Faraj 2005, Faraj and Johnson 2011, Kokkodis et al. 2020). In wikis and discussion groups, the recipient is often the crowd that views the content or engages in the discussion (Beylier et al. 2009). By contrast, in the Q&A community, the knowledge provider attributes an answer to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c question, and the recipient is the knowledge seeker who asks the question. In turn, the knowledge seeker tends to reciprocate because the relationship between the bene<sup>fi</sup>t provider and recipient is clear in the Q&A community (Wasko and Faraj 2005). Therefore, the community users would anticipate future reciprocal behaviors and consider the extent to which the knowledge seekers could repay with substantial bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (Wasko and Faraj 2000, Zhao et al. 2016). As a higher job rank is associated with more resources (Nahapiet and Ghoshal 1998), a higher-up is more likely to be able to reciprocate both in the community and the daily work. For example, in the community, a higher-up’s experience can enable him to provide high-quality answers if the current knowledge provider posts a question in the future, and in the daily work, a higher-up can potentially repay the knowledge provider with their unique resources.

Third, knowledge providers in the corporate Q&A community can better boost their own reputations through answering questions from the higher-ranked seekers, as contributed knowledge in the Q&A community is visible to all the community users. Reputation is a prominent motivation for users to contribute in online communities (e.g., Wasko and Faraj 2005, Hu et al. 2012). In the Q&A community, knowledge providers’ names are right next to their answers, which all the community participants can easily observe. However, in wikis, the content contributor’s name is not right next to the contribution but on a separate page of the revision history (Zeng et al. 2006), and normally, the contributors do not get recognized by their colleagues (Arazy and Gellatly 2012). Similarly, of<sup>fl</sup>ine talks are more likely to take place in private, and the act of knowledge provision can hardly get recognized by other coworkers either (Wasko and Faraj 2000). Hence, compared with the other contexts, knowledge providers in the Q&A community are more likely to boost their reputations and more likely to bene<sup>fi</sup>t by answering questions from the higherups. In addition, knowledge providers tend to enhance their reputations by engaging more in posts that are already impactful, following the preferentia attachment mechanism (Faraj and Johnson 2011, Johnson et al. 2014), as community users have a shared sense of the most desirable partners for interaction. In our context, the job rank could indicate a knowledge seeker’s desirability (Sauder et al. 2012), and questions from higher-ranked seekers tend to attract more attention, as these questions are likely to be viewed as more important (even if they may be not; Thye 2000, Bunderson and Reagans 2011).

To sum up, different from the of<sup>fl</sup>ine setting and the other types of online communities (e.g., wikis and discussion groups), a corporate Q&A community can offer an environment that alleviates the costs (e.g., career concerns) and adds the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (e.g., reciprocal behaviors and reputation) of replying to the higher-ups. Because of these characteristics, the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of answering to the higher-ups can outweigh its costs, and it is possible that users are inclined to respond to seekers with higher (versus the same or lower) job ranks in the Q&A community. Our second hypothesis re<sup>fl</sup>ects this possible effect of hierarchy on knowledge sharing.

Hypothesis 1b. In the corporate Q&A community, users are more likely to share knowledge with knowledge seeker who have a higher (versus the same or lower) job rank within the hierarchy.

## 4. Research Context and Data

We obtained a unique data set from the marketing segment of a large company that markets and sells food and daily necessities in China. We are interested in the effects of the company’s formal hierarchy on knowledge sharing in the corporate Q&A community. The descriptions of the research context and data are as follows.

## 4.1. The Company

The company in our study consists of <sup>fi</sup>ve geographically dispersed market sections (headquarters/center, east, west, north, and south), and each section comprises several dispersed market areas. Each area has multiple consultant teams, and one or more teams are responsible for the business in one city within that area. For any given consultant team, the employees interact with and market to the customers in the assigned territory of that team. Apart from the clear hierarchical boundaries that delineate the company’s sections, areas, and consultant teams, every employee is in one of the company’s six work departments. Four departments are differentiated by their target customers, and the other two departments provide support services and other necessary assistance. The company has a formal hierarchical structure that is based on the consul tant team, area, department, and section. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, there are eight well-de<sup>fi</sup>ned job ranks in the company, ranging from the junior business consultant to the member of a top management team. In this study, we code the job ranks as R1 (lowest rank) through R8 (highest rank). Table 1 describes each job rank.

An employee can rise through the job ranks via promotions; there is no demotion in the company. For employees with a job rank of R1, R2, or R3, the company uses two factors for promotion decisions: key performance indicators (KPIs), such as product sales, and the direct supervisors’ comments. The promotion standards for employees with a job rank of R4 or higher are more complicated; interviews with managers in the human resources department indicate that these standards include the employee’s work experience and other unquanti<sup>fi</sup>able elements of their performance.

## 4.3. Data

To answer our research questions, we collected a unique data set from the company. We <sup>fi</sup>rst worked with the IT department to collect detailed information on all the questions and answers generated in 2015. For each piece of content (i.e., a question or answer), we gathered the real name and user ID of the content generator, the content posting time, and the text. Our data set comprises 14,759 questions from 857 knowledge seekers and 687,083 answers from 1,265 providers; there are 1,269 users in total. Moreover, we collaborated with the human resources department to obtain each user’s detailed organizational attributes, such as working city, department, and job rank. The hierarchy follows a typical pyramidal structure such that there are fewer employees at higher job ranks. At the beginning of 2015, 869 of the 1,269 users were junior business consultants (i.e., R1), 199 were senior business consultants (i.e., R2), 127 lead a consultant team (i.e., R3), 44 are area department managers (i.e., R4), 23 were area managers (i.e., R5), 3 were section department managers (i.e., R6), 3 were directors (i.e., R7), and 1 was from the top management team (i.e., R8).

During our study period, 106 employees were promoted; 76 were promoted from R1 to R2, 24 were promoted from R2 to R3, 3 were promoted from R3 to R4, 2 were promoted from R4 to R5, and 1 was promoted from R5 to R6. ## 5. Estimation Strategies and Results

The objective of this study is to determine whether a user of the online Q&A community is disinclined (Hypothesis 1a) or inclined (Hypothesis 1b) to respond to a knowledge seeker with a job rank that is higher than (versus lower than or the same as) the user’s. Our empirical tests proceed as follows. We <sup>fi</sup>rst adopt a potential-dyads approach and show the effect of hierarchy on knowledge-sharing probabilities. We then validate the result with a quasi experiment, in which we explore promotions announced in our study period as exogenous variations to the users’ knowledgesharing decisions in the Q&A community.

## 5.1. A Potential-Dyads Approach

In this section, we use a potential-dyads approach to construct a data set that includes all possible pairwise combinations of the questions posted during our study period and the users who provided at least one answer during our study period. Knowledge sharing happens in some combinations but not in others. This approach essentially adopts the perspective of the prospective knowledge providers, who can browse all available questions but ultimately will respond to only one or a few (e.g., Hwang et al. 2015). A potential-dyads approach that considers all possible combinations is necessary for an effective test of whether a user is more likely to respond to knowledge seekers with a certain attribute (in this case, a higher job rank than the user). With this approach, we can comprehensively control for both user-level and question-level factors that may also affect knowledgesharing behaviors. To ensure a fair comparison, we focus on the dyads in which both the user’s and seeker’s job ranks are unchanged during the study period (i.e., the nonpromoted users).

We construct our dyadic data set as follows. For each question posted during our study period, we <sup>fi</sup>rst create all the potential dyads of this question and the users who provide at least one answer during our study period, including dyads in which knowledge sharing happens and dyads in which it does not. To illustrate this using an example, let us assume that during the study period, knowledge seekers post K questions $( Q _ { 1 } , Q _ { 2 } , \ldots , Q _ { k } , \ldots , Q _ { K } ) .$ , and I users provided at least one answer and thus are prospective knowledge providers $( P _ { 1 } , P _ { 2 } , \ldots , P _ { i } , \ldots , P _ { I } )$ for each of the K questions. Thus, our data set includes a total of $K \times I$ dyads (i.e., observations) because knowledge providers $( P _ { 1 } , P _ { 2 } , \ldots , P _ { i } , \ldots , P _ { I } )$ are all the candidates that can share knowledge to answer the questions (Q<sub>1</sub>, $\boldsymbol { Q } _ { 2 } , \dots , \boldsymbol { Q } _ { k } , \dots , \boldsymbol { Q } _ { K } )$ . ## 5.2. A Quasi Experiment

Our analysis with the potential-dyads approach shows that in the corporate Q&A community, a prospective knowledge provider is more likely to share knowledge when the knowledge seeker’s job rank is higher than that of the provider. A typical limitation of the empirical identi<sup>fi</sup>cation strategy with observational data is that some unobservable factors may simultaneously affect the dependent and independent variables. In our context, such unobservable con founders can relate to both the users’ answer decisions (i.e., our dependent variable) and their relative job ranks (i.e., our independent variable). Although the potential-dyads approach considers all the alternative questions and includes extensive controls for alternative explanations, it is still possible that the hierarchy is not the actual cause of the observed effect. We illustrate this concern with panel (a) in Figure 2. Suppose we observe that knowledge provider i answers a question from knowledge seeker $j ,$ and $j ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s }$ job rank is higher than $i ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s . }$ The knowledge-sharing activity, Answer<sub>ijk</sub>, can be affected by $S k H R a n k _ { i j }$ (i.e., the independent variable and hypothesized driver of the effect) as well as other observable factors (in $C o n t r o l _ { i j k } )$ and/or unobservable confounders that we are unable to include in the analyses (i.e., $C o n f o u n d e r s _ { i j k } )$ . For example, j’s job rank is higher than $i ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s } ,$ but at the same time, j also may have established a reputation for generating a lot of high-quality content in the community. In this scenario, i may respond to j simply because j is known for posting meaningful, worthwhile questions. In this case, i’s preexisting perception of j can be the actual cause of $i ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ higher probability of responding to j (the dashed lines). To alleviate this concern, we design a quasi experiment.

In the analyses concerning the promoted user and the postpromotion-ranking users, the features of the questions from the postpromotion-ranking users should be largely unchanged. Moreover, in the analyses concerning the prepromotion-ranking users and the promoted user, the features of the questions from the promoted user should not change signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly, because the knowledge accumulation is a long-term process (Niemi et al. 2009), and the questions that the promoted user posts and their question interests should remain largely unvaried in a short time after the promotion.

We empirically test the proposed design with two analyses, one at the dyad level and one at the question level, with each approach being a robustness check to the other. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, dyad-level analyses indicate whether promotions change users’ answer probabilities, and question-level analyses show whether promotions shift the composition of knowledge providers on a given question. Our data set records 106 promotion cases in our study period, including the exact promotion announcement time and the promoted employee’s original and new job ranks. No user is promoted twice during our study period, and there is no demotion in our context.

## 6. Additional Analyses

Our analyses so far demonstrate that organizational hierarchy signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affects employees’ knowledgesharing decisions in the corporate Q&A community: a user is inclined to share knowledge with a seeker whose job rank is higher than the user’s. To gain a more nuanced understanding of this issue, in this section, we conduct additional analyses to examine (i) whether knowledge providers exert more effort when answering questions of the higher-ranked knowledge seekers and (ii) whether the future promotions of the knowledge providers are related to their answers to the higher-ups’ questions.

## 6.1. The Effect of Hierarchy on Answer Text Features as Proxies for Answer Effort

In the Q&A community, people comprehend the answers via the text, and the level of effort the knowl edge provider exerts on their answer should not be overlooked (Qiu and Kumar 2017, Pu et al. 2020). Apart from the knowledge-sharing probability, we are also interested in whether knowledge providers exert more effort when answering the questions from the higher-ranked individuals. We focus on the answer’s three text features that can serve as the proxies of the knowledge provider’s answer effort.

First, the number of words in an answer or the answer length provides a crude representation of the provider’s answer effort (Godes and Silva 2012, Pu et al. 2017, Liu et al. 2019). In this study, we quantify provider $i ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ answer length with the log-transformed number of non-stop words in $i ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ answer to seeker $j ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ question k (i.e., NonStopWords<sub>ijk</sub>). Non-stop words include nouns, adjectives, verbs, and adverbs because they normally have concrete meanings (Lu 2012, Pu et al. 2020).<sup>7</sup>

Second, the diversity of topics within the answer re-<sup>fl</sup>ects the scope of the information in the answer. Answers with more diversi<sup>fi</sup>ed topics provide readers with more information or knowledge (Son et al. 2019) and can re<sup>fl</sup>ect a greater effort from the knowledge provider. In this study, we calculate each answer’s topic diversity with Shannon’s entropy, a common measure of diversity in the knowledge-sharing literature $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , Kuk 2006). For example, if knowledge provider i’s answer to question k is described by a W-topic-vecto $\it { \Psi } ; \mathrm { < } T _ { i k , 1 } , T _ { i k , 2 } , \dots , T _ { i k , W } >$ , then we calculate Diversi-$t y _ { i j k }$ with the formula $\begin{array} { r } { D i v e r s i t y _ { i j k } = - \sum _ { w = 1 } ^ { W } T _ { i k , w } \mathrm { l o g } T _ { i k , w } . } \end{array}$

In the context of the Q&A community, answers congruent with the question in terms of several text features are perceived as more helpful (Peng et al. 2020). In this study, we measure the relevance between the answer and its preceding question by calculating the cosine similarity between the topic vector of user i’s answer to question k and the topic vector of question k $( \mathrm { i . e . , } R e l e v a n c e _ { i j k } )$ . ## 6.2. Future Career Promotion

Our analyses so far demonstrate that in the corporate Q&A community, users are more inclined to provide answers to questions from knowledge seekers with higher job ranks, and users tend to exert greater effort, cover more diverse topics, and provide more relevant answers when they respond to higher-ups. As previously discussed, the corporate Q&A community is different from the of<sup>fl</sup>ine setting and other types of knowledge-sharing communities in terms of the costs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of answering higher-ups’ questions. We propose that, compared with other knowledgesharing contexts, users should have fewer career concerns when they interact with higher-ups in Q&A communities. After examining the effect of hierarchy on knowledge sharing, it is intriguing to investigate the effects of replying to higher-ups on knowledge providers and to understand whether the knowledge providers should have career concerns when answering higher-ups in the Q&A communities.

As described in Section 4, the company uses different sets of promotion standards for employees with a job rank of R1, R2, or R3 and employees with a job rank of R4 or higher. Of the 1,265 knowledge providers in our study period, 1,189 have a job rank of R1, R2, or R3 at the end of 2015, whereas only 76 have a job rank of R4 or higher.<sup>8</sup> We mainly focus on the former group because the sample size is larger and the promotion standards are clearer (i.e., KPIs and the direct supervisors’ comments). In Online Appendix D.1, we conduct similar analyses for employees with a job rank of R4 or higher. It is worth noting that this group of analyses does not con<sup>fl</sup>ict with our previous claim in the quasi experiment that the promotions announced in our study period are exogenous to testing our main hypotheses. This is because the promotions in our quasi experiment are based on the performance indicators before the existence of the community.

The goal of our test is to analyze the relationship between a user’s knowledge-sharing behaviors toward higher-ups in 2015 and their probability of getting promoted in the future (in the present analyses, we consider promotions during 2016–2018). It is intuitive to conduct this analysis with an econometric model in which the dependent variable describes whether employee i is promoted between 2016 and 2018 (i.e., Promo\_Future ), and the independent variable is the log-transformed number of answers they have generated for higher-ups in 2015 (i.e., HRankAns ). For example, HRankAns may be related to i’s work ability, which also affects i’s promotion probability. ## 7. Conclusion and Discussion

In this study, we analyze whether and to what extent the company’s formal hierarchy affects knowledge sharing in the corporate Q&A community. Drawing upon the literature on knowledge sharing in hierarchical organizations and the literature on the Q&A communities, we propose two competing hypotheses—a user is disinclined (Hypothesis 1a) or inclined (Hypothesis 1b) to respond to a knowledge seeker who has a higher job rank than them. To empirically test the proposed hypotheses, we use a unique data set from a large corporate online Q&A community. We <sup>fi</sup>rst use a potential-dyads approach to consider all alternative questions available for answering, and the results show that a knowledge provider is more likely to answer the question from a knowledge seeker who has a higher job rank than (versus lower than and the same as) them. We then validate the causality of the result with a quasi experiment. With both dyadlevel and question-level analyses, we show that users who share the promoted employee’s original job rank are more likely to respond to the promoted user after the promotion than before, whereas the promoted user is less likely to respond to knowledge seekers who share the promoted employee’s new job rank after the promotion than before. Moreover, we use textmining techniques to quantify the effort displayed in each answer. Our results show that knowledge providers exert greater effort when answering questions from higher-ranked (versus lower-ranked or sameranked) seekers, as this greater effort manifests in the answers as more words with concrete meaning, more diverse topics, and greater relevance to the questions.

We further examine whether the act of sharing knowledge with higher-ranked knowledge seekers affects the knowledge providers. We employ an instrumental-variable design by exploring the different question generation time, and we show that employees with a job rank of R1, R2, or R3 who provide more responses to higher-ranked knowledge seekers during our study period are more likely to get promoted in subsequent years. We also <sup>fi</sup>nd that an employee’s promotion probability in the subsequent years is positively related to the level of effort in their answers to the higher-ranked seekers.

This study contributes to the literature on the effects of hierarchy on knowledge sharing. The existing studies mainly focus on of<sup>fl</sup>ine or other types of corporate knowledge-sharing communities (e.g., wikis and discussion groups), where employees are reluctant to share knowledge with higher-ups (e.g., Michailova and Husted 2003, Behrendt et al. 2015). If the online Q&A community is simply supplementary to existing communities, then we should expect a similar effect of hierarchy. However, the corporate Q&A community has some unique characteristics, and we theorize these unique characteristics from the perspectives of costs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of sharing knowledge to the higher-ups (i.e., career concerns, reciprocity, and reputation). Although users would encounter similar types of costs or bene<sup>fi</sup>ts for contributing in online communities, the extents of these costs or bene<sup>fi</sup>ts vary across different types of online communities. Therefore, we propose that users are inclined to share knowledge with higherups. We then provide robust evidence that in the corporate Q&A community, a user is more likely to respond to higher-ups, a <sup>fi</sup>nding that is different from the existing observations in the other knowledge-sharing contexts. Beyond examining the effect of hierarchy on the knowledge-sharing probability, we further show that hierarchy also affects the answer effort such that knowledge providers exert greater effort when responding to the higher-ranked knowledge seekers.

Moreover, this study adds to the literature on the relationship between employees’ community activities and outcomes (e.g., Roberts et al. 2006). Existing studies focus mainly on metrics of overall contributions in a public online community, where users signal their work effort and ability to the whole industry (Hann et al. 2013, Huang and Zhang 2016). By contrast, we examine the material effects of an employee’s behavior in an internal online Q&A community. We <sup>fi</sup>nd a positive association between users’ promotion probability and their knowledge-sharing behaviors (speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the number of answers and effort displayed in those answers) toward higher-ranked individuals. Our <sup>fi</sup>nding of the link between effort and promotion probability is especially noteworthy as (i) it demonstrates that users should not have career concerns when answering higher-ups’ questions and exerting effort in the answers, and (ii) it puts us one step closer to understanding whether employees community behaviors potentially lead to material out comes for them such as career promotions.

Our study offers important managerial implications. The managers’ engagement in the corporate knowledge-sharing communities is essential for the knowledge-sharing success (Argyris and Ransbotham 2016). To comprehensively assess the role of managers (i.e., the ones with high job ranks), practitioners need to understand the employees’ knowledge-sharing patterns when facing the managers in these emerging communities. However, employees are reluctant to share knowledge with higher-ranked employees when managers participate in the corporate wikis and discussion groups (Behrendt et al. 2015, Klier et al. 2017). Our analyses demonstrate that employees are more likely to answer questions from the higherranked users in the corporate Q&A community, and employees also exert more effort in these answers. Our research, together with research on other contexts, is useful for companies to take the role of the managers and their participation into account when considering which type of online community to adopt. If a company aims to enhance the engagement of managers and to facilitate knowledge sharing between subordinates and superiors, the corporate Q&A com munity is a good choice.

Moreover, based on our <sup>fi</sup>nding on the inclination to answer higher-ups' questions, community designers can better motivate knowledge sharing in the

Q&A community by taking into account the fact that the community users have different job ranks. For example, managers can proactively collect questions from the local team and post these questions on behalf of their subordinates to increase the probability that the questions will be answered and discussed. Whereas our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that the online Q&A community context helps employees with the higher job ranks receive more feedback, this also means that questions from the lower-ranked individuals may not get enough replies. If the core purpose of a corporate Q&A community is to help the lower-ranked employees to get constructive feedback, then the designers can introduce incentives for users to answer questions from lower-ranked or same-ranked employees. For instance, virtual badges (similar to the badge system in StackOver<sup>fl</sup>ow) or a virtual point system (similar to the system in Goes et al. 2016) could offer more rewards for answers provided to the less-answered seekers.

Finally, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings offer implications for companies on promotion decisions. Our results show that an employee’s future promotion is positively related to both the quantity and effort of their responses to higher-ups in the corporate Q&A community. If companies do not want employees’ online knowledgesharing behaviors to in<sup>fl</sup>uence their career development, then the companies should formally prohibit supervisors from considering employees’ activities in the community when they write comments for the subordinates. However, if companies hope the supervisors will take the employees’ online knowledgesharing behaviors into consideration when evaluating their job performance, it is necessary for the companies to provide a formal assessment guidance.

Our study is subject to several limitations that present future research opportunities. First, although our results show that employees who engage in more knowledge sharing with higher-ups are more likely to get promoted in the future, we do not analyze whether the existence of promotion opportunities affects knowledge sharing. This would require a setting in which one group of employees is exposed to the promotion potentials and the other group is not; our study context does not offer this opportunity. Future studies can use a different empirical setting or conduct experiments and surveys to further pinpoint the underlying mechanisms of our empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Second, we conceptualize that the corporate Q&A community is different from the other types of corporate communities in terms of the costs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of sharing knowledge with higher-ups. However, we cover only the aspects that matter to our research questions, and future studies can further explore the differences among the different types of corporate online communities. Third, as discussed in Section 6.2, when we analyze the material effects of knowledge sharing behaviors, although we examine our research context and employ an instrumental-variable design, our results still suffer from some data limitations. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the promotion records include only promotions announced within a three-year time frame, and records of other economic outcomes (e.g., wages) are not available because of their sensitivity. Future studies can conduct similar analyses in a research context in which promotion records are available for a longer period and other indicators are available. Finally, in this study, we are mainly interested in the effects of hierarchy on the probability of and effort involved in knowledge sharing, but we do not examine other linguistic features of the answers. Future studies can use other text-mining techniques to discuss whether the content of answers is different when knowledge providers face higher-ranked knowledge seekers.
