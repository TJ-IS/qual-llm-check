# Mitigating Risk Selection in Healthcare Entitlement Programs: A Beneficiary-Level Competitive Bidding Approach

Abstract. Healthcare entitlement programs in the United States represent a large and growing <sup>fi</sup>nancial outlay for taxpayers. In the pursuit of operational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies, program administrators often contract with private managed care organizations (MCOs) to procure insurance for bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries. This, however, encourages MCOs to attract the healthiest bene <sup>fi</sup>ciaries and avoid the sickest, a phenomenon known as risk selection. This paper investigates whether risk selection can be mitigated with a mechanism where MCOs bid to enroll each individual bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary. Although procurement auctions have been studied extensively in the literature, extant research has rarely discussed individual-level bidding. Digitization can contribute to the development and introduction of ef<sup>fi</sup>cient market structures and mechanisms for matching bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries with appropriate MCOs. We model demand- and supply-side aspects in a two-sided insurance marketplace to examine three mechanisms, risk adjustment, bidding, and a mix of prospective payment and bidding, with and without reserve prices. Analytical results show that traditional risk adjustment cannot optimally be used to eliminate risk selection, whereas the bidding mechanisms eliminate it entirely. Mixed bidding eliminates risk selection at a strictly lower cost than pure bidding. The proposed mixed bidding approach is a new type of mechanism with preauction offers that strictly dominates the second-price auction without requiring additional assumptions. Numerical analysis shows bidding dominates risk adjustment in 75.1% of simulated parameter sets. Compared with risk adjustment, bidding secures coordinated care for 12.1% more allocated bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries while lowering program costs by 9.2% and largely preserving MCO pro<sup>fi</sup>ts. This would amount to approximately \$27.2 billion in Medicaid program savings. Sensitivity analysis reveals that the proposed bidding mechanism dominates in scenarios that closely resemble real-world healthcare entitlement environments. These results show that digital markets that enable individual-level auctions are a promising approach for achieving the dual aim of <sup>fi</sup>nancial sustainability and expanded access to care for the most vulnerable.

## 1. Introduction

Federal and state governments in the United States spend approximately \$600 billion annually on Medicaid, the country’s largest healthcare entitlement program for certain groups of economically disadvantaged individuals. More than 73 million individuals are covered under various Medicaid plans. Nearly half of Medicaid spending is for state governments to contract with private managed care organizations (MCOs) (Howell et al. 2012) in an arrangement known as Medicaid managed care (MMC). State Medicaid agencies function as market intermediaries to bring bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries and plans together and are responsible for bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary assignment to MCOs.<sup>1</sup> The goal of MMC is to improve the healthcare services coordination for bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries while also lowering program costs for state Medicaid agencies. Despite the prevalence of the MMC model in 41 states in the United States, there is little evidence of savings to states from the capitated fee model (Stadhouders et al. 2019). As digitization of health records increases visibility into cost drivers and provides means for better population health management, digitization can also contribute to the development and introduction of ef<sup>fi</sup>cient market structures and mechanisms in matching bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries with appropriate MCOs. This research is motivated by the fundamental notion rooted in online and electronic auctions research that technology (including information availability, computing power, and digital platforms) enables impactful changes to market design and mechanisms (Chen et al. 2020).

Using the improved information availability in healthcare markets as the basis, we investigate a consequential and problematic source of inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency in capitated contracts of the type seen in MMC markets: supply-side preferred risk selection by MCOs (Ellis and Layton 2014). Risk selection is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the strategic practices by health plans, or providers, designed to enroll a group of bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries of lower cost or risk than the overall population on which the reimbursement is based (Eggleston 2000). Risk selection occurs when a uniform prospective payment is offered to facilitate care for a de<sup>fi</sup>ned group. Where there is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant within-group heterogeneity in cost or risk, however, uniform payment makes some individuals more pro<sup>fi</sup>table to enroll than others. This creates the incentives behind risk selection. Risk selection results in broken pooling arrangements wherein sponsors overpay for the enrolled and must <sup>fi</sup>nd alternative, often inferior, arrangements for those rejected (van de Ven et al. 2003). The central research objective in this paper is to investigate market designs that state agencies can put in place to ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently mitigate risk selection by MCOs, especially in a context where bene<sup>fi</sup>- ciaries frequently do not exercise their right to choose an MCO. Whenever bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries fail to choose an MCO, the state must select a process to assign the bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries across available MCO plans. The ratio of bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries automatically assigned to plans varies signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly across states, with Tennessee and Hawaii auto-enrolling 100% of the bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries. Eighteen states auto-enroll 50% or more bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries and additionally three more states auto-enroll about 40%.<sup>2</sup> In general, the state agencies use information in their databases on bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries, such as prior relationship with and distance to care providers, prior enrollment and enrollment of family members in a particular plan, plan performance (typically assessed via Healthcare Effectiveness Data and Information Set (HEDIS) measures<sup>3</sup>), plan capacity, and program goals in assignments.<sup>4</sup> No state agency has investigated the optimality of the assignments either from the bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary or from the MCO perspective. In essence, there is very little theoretical or practical insights to guide the ef<sup>fi</sup>- cient design of the MMC market, especially given the rapid advances in healthcare analytics.

It is important to note that risk selection occurs because the state agency offers a prospective enrollment contract to MCOs, which can respond differently based on each individual bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary’s expected profitability. We envision a process structured as an individually priced procurement auction where the state Medicaid agency “offers” separate contracts for each bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary. This work adds to the rich stream of auction related research in the information systems (IS) literature (Bapna et al. 2009; Cason et al. 2011; Lu et al. 2016, 2019a; Bichler and Goeree 2017; Qiu et al. 2019). The mechanisms evaluated in this study are inspired by the fact that digital platforms and information rich environments make it easier for state agencies to digitize processes and enable implementation of bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary-level bidding during enrollment periods (Bichler et al. 2010). Given that the current expenditure on healthcare is nearly 20% of the gross domestic product (GDP), the economic implications of implementing a digital platform can be enormous. By applying the auction mechanism to the problem of risk selection, with the state Medicaid agency as the principal, MCOs as bidders, and bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary enrollment as a contract, we derive theoretical results showing improved bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary access to coordinated care, as well as potentially lowering program costs and preserving the solvency of MMC plans.

Our research contributes to a growing body of IS research on auction markets and mechanism evaluation. Extant research on electronic auctions in the IS literature (Bapna et al. 2009, Bichler et al. 2010, Lu et al. 2019b, Qiu et al. 2019) has signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced the design of electronic markets, software agents, advanced decision support systems, and simulation tools to solve real-world problems (Giesen et al. 2009; Ketter et al. 2009, 2016; Sandholm and Likhodedov 2015; Valogianni et al. 2020). We extend this literature by demonstrating how bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary risk heterogeneity can be incorporated into electronic market design and identify ef<sup>fi</sup>cient mechanisms for sourcing care coordination. Recent investments in electronic medical records (EMR), population health management, and analytics enable governments and insurance companies to better pro<sup>fi</sup>le bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary groups. Whereas insurance companies can exploit the improving health information landscape to increase risk selection be havior, we demonstrate how auction mechanisms can effectively counter risk selection in this environment. To our knowledge, the use of auctions for solving inef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies in health insurance markets is unexplored in the academic literature.

This research also builds on the procurement auctions literature (Paringer and McCall 1991, Katzman and McGeary 2008, Lewis and Bajari 2011, Haruvy and Katok 2013). The use of competitive bidding in procurement allows buyers to minimize expenditure while preserving the winning bidder’s expectation of pro<sup>fi</sup>ts, which is a mutually bene<sup>fi</sup>cial exchange. The procurement and health economics literature, in particular, discuss auctions to source medical supplies and determine group-level reimbursement. Such auctions, however, are not designed to account for individual-level heterogeneity and risk selection. In this paper, we introduce bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary-level bidding, where prospective payments are distinct for each bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary and determined using a competitive bidding process. Such a mechanism was impractical in the past because of high transaction costs (Enthoven 1988, Newhouse 1996), but advances in information technology have made bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary-level bidding a feasible option today (Bichler et al. 2010). For instance, new algorithmic approaches allow sharing of patient microdata information without compromising con<sup>fi</sup>dential data of individual patients (Gar<sup>fi</sup>nkel et al. 2007).

Where this study fundamentally departs from existing mechanisms is the level at which MCOs bid for bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries. Past and proposed mechanisms use bidding to set prospective reimbursement rates for enrolling de<sup>fi</sup>ned groups of bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries. This may price group-level characteristics but leaves any remaining within-group heterogeneity unpriced and can preserve risk selection incentives. We propose bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary-level rather than group-level bidding. The closest realworld example of individual-level competitive bidding is Medibid.com, where surgeons bid through electronic auctions to serve the needs of individual patients for a speci<sup>fi</sup>c procedure. In contrast, the mechanisms proposed here use bidding to secure health insurance for bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries through enrollment with managed care plans.

Outside of the health context, the auction literature has proposed models where individuals are the subject of competitive bidding. For instance, the online search literature investigates online auctions that consider advertisement format/quality, customer pro<sup>fi</sup>le, and competition in targeting speci<sup>fi</sup>c types of individuals (Animesh et al. 2011, Agarwal and Mukhopadhyay 2016). A second stream of literature examines the use of bidding on freelancing platforms for outside individuals’ skills and expertise (Hong and Pavlou 2013). Hekimian and Jones (1967) proposed a mechanism where divisional managers bid on employees to establish individual valuations. In this research, we investigate whether bidding over individuals, rather than groups, can resolve an important source of market failure even when considering the associated transaction costs. The market intermediary (i.e., state Medicaid agency) may incur additional costs for each bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary enrolled into a plan via auction. The costs include both the administrative expenses of receiving and evaluating bids, as well as potential remedial expenses incurred in reallocating those bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries dissatis<sup>fi</sup>ed with their initial plan assignment. These costs are included in the tradeoff computations between mechanisms in our model.

In this paper, we model the MMC market by captur ing the essential parameters of interactions between bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries, state Medicaid agency, and MCOs capable of practicing risk selection. Bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries in the model have the option to investigate and choose an MCO. Those who do not choose an MCO are allocated to one by the state agency. If one of the allocated bene <sup>fi</sup>ciaries is found to be unpro<sup>fi</sup>table by all MCOs, then the bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary’s care is poorly coordinated. This implies higher costs, worse health outcomes, or both. Three primary allocation mechanisms are evaluated as potential candidates for market design. The <sup>fi</sup>rst is a traditional prospective risk adjustment system, where MCOs are offered a <sup>fi</sup>xed fee per enrollee, along with an adjustment for exogenous bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary characteris tics. The second is a pure competitive bidding mecha nism, offering no prospective payment but assigning each individual bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary to an MCO based on sub mitted bids. Finally, a mixed payment mechanism is evaluated, offering an initial prospective payment but then assigning any remaining unenrolled bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary to the lowest bidder. On one hand, the bidding mechanisms create match-level adjustments to MCO payments, making all bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries potentially pro<sup>fi</sup>table and allowing the state agency to price discriminate across MCOs, which represents a clear advantage. On the other hand, traditional risk adjustment allows payments to be set relatively low in cases where the bene <sup>fi</sup>ts of enrollment are small. Whereas the bidding mechanisms would incur signi<sup>fi</sup>cant expense making these bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries pro<sup>fi</sup>table, risk-adjusted payments could be set low enough to leave them unenrolled, thereby avoiding these costs without severe adverse consequences. To investigate this tradeoff, we compare the three mechanisms on both their ability to secure enrollment for bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries as well as the resulting expense for taxpayers. We also investigate the impact of reserve prices on outcomes of interest.

The results show that risk selection is never eliminated under the risk adjustment mechanism at the optimal baseline payment. This is true even if risk adjustment (as traditionally de<sup>fi</sup>ned) is perfect or if the health and <sup>fi</sup>nancial costs of nonenrollment are catastrophically high. In contrast, risk selection is always eliminated under competitive bidding, although at potentially great expense. Another result shows that, despite being an inef<sup>fi</sup>cient mechanism, the mixed bidding mechanism achieves universal enrollment at a strictly lower cost than pure bidding. Thus, a state agency using competitive bidding achieves the greatest savings by paying MCOs a low <sup>fi</sup>xed amount to enroll low-cost bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries and allocate only the most costly using bids. We identify conditions under which the bidding mechanisms dominate risk adjustment.

Numerical analysis shows that these conditions hold more than 75.1% of parameter sets from simulated entitlement programs approximating real-world conditions. Furthermore, relative to optimal risk adjustment, competitive bidding improves care coordination for 12.1% of allocated bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries and reduces the tax burden of the program by 9.2%, on average, and largely preserves MCO pro<sup>fi</sup>ts. Finally, although reserve prices can be used effectively to “carve out” the most costly bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries from the managed care portion of the entitlement program, there tend to be only modest (if any) savings from implementing the optimal reserve price. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings reveal the potential for the bidding mechanisms to increase access and reduce costs. Our results provide policymakers and managers with guidance regarding the market circumstances most conducive to this dual aim.

This research contributes to the understanding of market design and analytics in the IS literature by introducing a mixed payment mechanism for enrolling individual bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries. We theoretically model and demonstrate effective auction mechanisms that bene<sup>fi</sup>t from increased access to information, analytics, and digital markets. From this perspective, our research builds on extant literature on auction and procurement markets. Findings from our model show that buy-price auctions can be justi<sup>fi</sup>ed in an environment without assumptions of risk aversion, reference pricing, or participation costs. The research setting also uniquely models the implications of universal rejection of bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries and additional administrative costs incurred due to bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary-level bidding process. We extend the extant literature on market design by formally modeling bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary-level competitive bidding as a mechanism for mitigating risk selection in centrally administered health insurance programs. Whereas existing research frames competitive bidding and predetermined payment as alternative mechanisms, this paper outlines a single mixed mechanism utilizing both methods. We thus examine whether any complementarities arise from the joint use of bidding and predetermined payment.

The theoretical <sup>fi</sup>ndings from our research discover an important source of IT-related ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency gain for government-sponsored insurance programs. The individual-level bidding mechanisms proposed in our study can effectively scale as MCOs build digital capabilities and analytics to support population health management initiatives. Although the environment is described here in terms of health insurance and managed care, researchers can use variations of this mechanism in other applications areas such as scheduling scarce human resources, for example, nurses (De Grano et al. 2009), where the use of auctions has been explored but remains largely unsophisticated. Finally, our results indicate that there is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant potential for bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary-level bidding to mitigate risk selection, making even the most costly bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries pro<sup>fi</sup>table to MCOs and reduce the taxpayer burden. These results can be used to craft sustainable electronic markets for health insurance for eligible bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries and set the stage for more individual-level auction research in healthcare markets.

## 2. Research Context 2.1. MCO

An MCO is a healthcare delivery system that contracts with health insurance sponsors, such as Medicaid, to offer cost-effective and appropriate medical care to eligible individuals. Since their introduction into the United States in 1973, MCOs have shaped healthcare access and delivery signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly. Most Medicaid bene-<sup>fi</sup>ciaries now receive healthcare services through MCOs. In 2018, about 77% of the people covered by Medicaid at any time during the year were enrolled in managed care plans.<sup>5</sup> States pay MCOs a <sup>fi</sup>xed amount per enrollee, known as capitation, to cover the cost of services agreed on in the contract,<sup>6</sup> and MCOs assume <sup>fi</sup>nancial risk for the bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries under their plan. These capitation payments are set prospectively based on the expected costs of providing healthcare services for the covered population, allowing for some group-level adjustments based on certain observable characteristics. Historical cost and utilization data and projected trends are explicitly accounted for in the calculation of capitation rates.

The number of MCOs in operation varies widely across states. For example, California had 23 MCOs under contract in 2017, whereas Colorado and Delaware had only 2 MCOs. Most states use the competi tive bidding approach to select managed care plans (Gifford et al. 2019). States ask MCOs to propose capitation rates in their bids and provide coverage details on the services that will be included for the bene<sup>fi</sup>cia ry population. The average pro<sup>fi</sup>t margin for MCOs under these contracts is between 0.5% and 2.5%. States use bidding to induce competition among MCOs and encourage cost-effective service provision. In general, relative to state agencies, MCOs have <sup>fi</sup>rsthand and deeper knowledge about the cost and utilization of services from enrolling a particular Medicaid population, and the bids of MCOs provide strong signals to these agencies. Even when the state agencies do have historical data, they may not have information about the production function of the MCO, the in novations it plans to introduce, or evolving market conditions. In such cases, competitive bidding is an effective vehicle for states to induce MCOs to incorporate this information into their expected costs.

Medicaid programs across the United States spend approximately \$300 billion annually on managed care contracts.<sup>8</sup> Given that managed care contracts constitute a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant budget outlay for states and are the largest source of revenues for $\mathrm { { \bf M } \bar { C } O s , ^ { 9 } }$ we consider it important to examine whether more effective digitally enabled market mechanisms can be designed that expand bene-<sup>fi</sup>ciary access and/or reduce program expenditures.

## 2.2. Risk Selection

Risk selection presents a potentially large source of inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency in healthcare markets. Attempts to mitigate risk selection have included adjusting payments for high-cost patients (known as risk adjustment) and prohibiting discrimination based on health risks (Aizawa and Kim 2018). Despite this, the literature reveals that incentives for and evidence of risk selection are widespread. Although risk adjustment has had some success in pricing heterogeneity across Medicare Advantage plans (McWilliams et al. 2012), MCOs have responded by redirecting selection efforts toward unadjusted characteristics (Brown et al. 2014). This is accomplished in a variety of ways, including targeted advertising (Aizawa and Kim 2018), offering supplementary bene<sup>fi</sup>ts attractive to healthy individuals and unappealing to the disabled (Cooper and Trivedi 2012), and increased spending on services used by high-pro<sup>fi</sup>t patients (Ellis et al. 2013). Such behavior is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to verify as intentionally selective and is thus dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for administrators to ban or regulate. For instance, sickness funds in the German Social Health Insurance system selectively engage with bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries having pro<sup>fi</sup>table unadjusted geographic characteristics (Bauhoff 2012). Kuziemko et al. (2018) show that MCOs treated pro<sup>fi</sup>table and unpro<sup>fi</sup>table bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries differently once MMC was established, with the latter more often directed to alternate safetynet care. Ubiquitous adoption of EMR, health information exchanges, and aggregation of health data will potentially exacerbate information asymmetry and worsen the problems posed by risk selection.

A common explanation for why existing measures have failed to prevent risk selection is that adjustment mechanisms are imperfect and do not fully compensate plans for predictable cost differences within or across groups. A given bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries’ treatment cost partially depends on network restrictions and MCO structure, which are different across plans. Not only is this “match-speci<sup>fi</sup>c” heterogeneity signi<sup>fi</sup>cant but is also unaccounted for in traditional risk adjustment systems and could be used for selection (Einav et al. 2016). Granular approaches to risk adjustment to eliminate unpriced heterogeneity may create incentives for providers to “upcode” (Geruso and Layton 2020). Given that existing efforts at comprehensive risk adjustment have preserved incentives to select or created other distortions, it is important to study complementary or alternative mechanisms. The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) have recently sought guidance on improving the design and operation of MMC programs (Mathematica Policy Research 2019). Given this interest among policymakers, a thorough examination of market design alternatives would be especially timely.<sup>10</sup>

## 2.3. Procurement and Electronic Markets

Competitive bidding has long been proposed for the procurement of healthcare and health insurance (Diamond 1992, Keijser and Kirkman-Liff 1992, Feldman et al. 2012). In practice, Medicare has used procurement auctions to source durable medical equipment, prosthetics, orthodontics, and supplies (Katzman and McGeary 2008, Mechanic and Altman 2010). Additionally, healthcare systems in multiple states have used auctions to procure a variety of health services (Paringer and McCall 1991). Medicare uses bidding to set baseline capitation payments to MCOs in Medicare Advantage (McGuire et al. 2013; Song et al. 2013). Furthermore, at least 16 U.S. states use a competitive bidding process in their Medicaid programs (Howell et al., 2012).

Although not focused on individuals as the unit of examination, extant literature has examined procurement auctions in signi<sup>fi</sup>cant detail. Driven by the need to lower costs, many organizations have used auctions to source their needs (Bichler and Steinberg 2007). In fact, competitive bidding is an important component of procurement operations at more than 65% of organizations and accounts for 10% of the actual spending (Haruvy and Katok 2013). Procurement auctions are commonly used in the public sector as well (Lewis and Bajari 2011). Prior research suggests that the design of these auctions and the information display format have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant implications on the cost savings for buyers (Gupta et al. 2012).

Because of their <sup>fl</sup>exibility and the potential cost savings, online auction platforms are an increasingly important tool for policymakers. IS researchers have studied online and electronic auctions in detail, with organizations, individuals, and governments as participants (Bapna et al. 2009; Tunca et al. 2014; Lu et al. 2016, 2019a; Bichler and Goeree 2017). Recent research has experimented with agent-based implementations of auctions to mesh analytical aspects of online auction and mechanism design with computational aspects to simulate various scenarios and provide decision support for the auctioneer and bidders (Adomavicius et al. 2009, Bapna et al. 2020, Valogianni et al. 2020). A comprehensive review of this vast literature is beyond the scope of this paper, but its synthesis suggests that the fundamental aspects that separate the online and electronic auctions research from others is how technology changes existing assumptions used in the economics literature and facilitates newer auction designs and mechanisms. We focus on one such study, Gupta et al. (2012), which discusses the use of procurement auctions for health insurance. They investigate an initiative where several Fortune 500 <sup>fi</sup>rms procured health insurance for their employees by inviting MCOs to compete in open electronic auctions. The auctions generated signi<sup>fi</sup>cant cost savings in the <sup>fi</sup>rst year but failed to retain bidder participation during subsequent years and were shut down. The reasons for this outcome concern not the principle of competitive bidding but its execution, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally regarding auction winner determination, information feedback, ending rules, and price visibility. Furthermore, unlike the individual-level bidding mechanisms explored here, bidding in the procurement auction from Gupta et al. (2012) was conducted at the group level. In light of the paucity of theoretical and empirical research on the subject, our research proposes an innovative mechanism to eliminate risk selection in managed care. The theoretical results and the computational insights demonstrate that the individuallevel auction approach can contain healthcare costs and expand access to care for the most vulnerable bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries.

As explained below, risk type is an important consideration in the cost that an MCO expects to incur on enrolling a given bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary.

We assume that bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries have preferences over MCOs, although these preferences are unknown before investigation. All bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries wishing to choose a plan must investigate the set of MCOs participating in the entitlement program. Once the investigation is complete, the bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary may choose an MCO with which to enroll. Despite not knowing their exact preferences, each bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary holds subjective beliefs over the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of MCO selection. A given bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary expects to enjoy additional utility p from selecting their own MCO. Bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries who do not make a selection and instead have the entitlement program attempt to assign them to an MCO expect to enjoy an additional utility normalized to zero. A bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary investigating the set of participating MCOs incurs a utility cost of $g ,$ representing the time and cognitive costs of discovering and evaluating the pros and cons of each MCO. Therefore, before investigation, a given bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary’s expected utility from investigating is $V ( p , g ) =$ $\bar { U ( y , H _ { 1 } ) } + p - g .$ Conversely, should this bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary choose not to investigate, their expected utility would be $V ( 0 , 0 )$ . This expected utility would depend on the allocation mechanism used by the entitlement program. Assume that bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary preferences over MCOs are such that $p$ is uniformly distributed between zero and $\bar { p } .$ . Let S be the share of bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries that investigate (i.e., share $D \times S$ of all consumers) and choose their own MCO, whereas share 1 S rely on the entitlement program for placement.

There are n $\in \mathbb { Z } ^ { + + }$ MCOs participating in the entitlement program, where $n \geq 2 .$ . MCOs are risk-neutral pro<sup>fi</sup>t maximizers, receiving prospective payments for each bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary enrolled and bearing all costs associated with delivering care. Although real-world MCOs may differ in meaningful ways, they are assumed symmetric here because of informational limitations in the state agency’s problem. Although the payments received by the MCOs may differ depending on the reimbursement mechanism used by the program, the costs incurred on each bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary depend on the characteristics of both the bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary and the MCO. Some bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary characteristics would, on average, be equally costly for any MCO to manage. An example of such a characteristic is race, which can be correlated with labor and delivery charges (Kuziemko et al. 2018). As a bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary’s race is constant across MCOs, any enrolling MCO would expect to bear the associated costs. Let each combination of these characteristics map into one of the K different risk types and let $\mu _ { k }$ be the commonly observed cost that any MCO bears when enrolling a bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary of risk type k.

Depending on the characteristics of the enrolling MCO, there are other bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary characteristics that may have different implications for enrollment costs. For example, Einav et al. (2016) <sup>fi</sup>nd that certain bene<sup>fi</sup>- ciary characteristics (age, gender, or prior diagnoses) are predictive of care-seeking behavior under different network and contract designs. Because care-seeking behavior has implications for cost, differently structured MCOs could thus incur different costs from enrolling a given bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary. Given that an MCO has the most information about its own characteristics, it likely understands its own match-speci<sup>fi</sup>c costs better than any other participant in the entitlement program. Therefore, in addition to $\mu _ { k } ,$ , we assume that an MCO j enrolling bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary i of risk type k would bear the match-speci<sup>fi</sup>c cost $\nu _ { i j k } ,$ which is private information to MCO j. As a result, the total expected enrollment cost for MCO j from enrolling bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary i is $c _ { i j k } = \mu _ { k } + \nu _ { i j k }$ , which is also private information to MCO j. An advantage of this formulation is that the analytical results below hold regardless of the degree of correlation between MCOs enrollment costs, including scenarios where enrollment costs are near-perfectly correlated $( K > 1$ and $\nu _ { i j k } \approx 0 \forall$ $i , j , k )$ or completely uncorrelated K 1 .

The typical framework for common-value auctions allows for purely unobservable common costs with noisy signals. In contrast, the model structure for enrollment costs in this research includes both private and $o b \_$ servable common components. We exclude unobservable common costs for three reasons. First, beyond the symmetric MCOs modeled here, real-world health plans differ along many characteristics related to cost. These include pro<sup>fi</sup>t motive, business model, and provider networks. Both expected and <sup>fi</sup>nal enrollment costs are thus bound to differ across health plans and therefore are not purely common. Second, risk-pooling insurers can use past experience and data to accurately estimate enrollment costs for future bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries. As the common-value literature shows, experienced bidders tend not to over-rely on noisy idiosyncratic signals when selecting bids (Kagel and Richard 2001). Finally, the unobserved common component most relevant to health insurance results from a bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary’s private information regarding their expectations and preferences for using care. Although this is a well-known case of asymmetric information giving rise to adverse selection, the model here and its motivating example from MMC focuses on different contracting problems and is not designed to address this issue. Unobservable common costs are explored further in the discussion section.

The other participants in the entitlement program, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally the remaining MCOs and the state agency, do not observe the exact value of $c _ { i j k }$ but have suf<sup>fi</sup>- cient information to form expectations. Intuitively, each participant understands there can be different enrollment costs across MCOs for the same bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary, but because of informational limitations, does not understand why they are different and so proceeds as if they are random. They model $c _ { i j k }$ as an independent, type-speci<sup>fi</sup>c random variable $( c _ { k } )$ distributed accord ing to the function $F _ { k } ( c _ { k } )$ , with corresponding probability density function $f _ { k } ( c _ { k } )$ over the common support c, c¯ , where $\underline { { c } } \geq 0$ and $\overline { { c } } > \underline { { c } } . ^ { 1 3 }$ As MCOs are assumed symmetric, these distributions do not depend on $j .$ Each distribution $F _ { k } ( . )$ is smooth, continuous, atomless, and has <sup>fi</sup>rst-order stochastic dominance over the preceding distribution $F _ { k - 1 } ( . )$ for all $k \geq 2$ . This implies that $k = 1$ bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries are the least costly to enroll, on average, and that $k = K$ are the most costly. Thus, the state agency and each MCO understand the others enrollment costs to be correlated through risk type but independent conditional on risk type.

For each of the S share of bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries who has investigated, selected, and enrolled with a given MCO, that MCO receives an actuarially fair prospective payment $\begin{array} { r } { \mathbb { E } ( c ) = \sum _ { k = 1 } ^ { K } \alpha _ { k } \mathbb { E } ( c _ { k } ) } \end{array}$ and bears cost $c _ { i j k }$ . MCOs can engage in risk selection for the remaining share of the bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, MCOs match randomly and sequentially with each of the remaining 1 S share of bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries (Figure 1). Each MCO is unaware of its place in the order of these matches with a given bene-<sup>fi</sup>ciary. For each match, the MCO can entice pro<sup>fi</sup>table bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries to enroll while avoiding enrollment from any bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary they deem unpro<sup>fi</sup>table. In the real world, as explicit rejection is typically prohibited by program administrators, MCOs can use indirect risk selection techniques that are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for the state agency to verify or ban (Newhouse 1996, van de Ven et al. 2003). Thus, for simplicity, rejecting a bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary is assumed to be costless to an MCO and always successful. Bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary utility is assumed unaffected by the number of rejections as long as there is an eventual enrollment.

## 3.1. Risk Adjustment Mechanism

Every mechanism modeled in this section treats the share S of bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries in the same way. Regardless of allocation mechanism, bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries who investigate and select an MCO are enrolled with that MCO. The level of reimbursement is actuarially fair and thus exogenous. The mechanisms only affect the outcomes for taxpayers and the 1 S share of bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries that must be assigned by the program. Unless otherwise speci<sup>fi</sup>ed, references in these sections to bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries concern only those from the 1 S share.

The <sup>fi</sup>rst mechanism evaluated is risk-adjusted prospective payment (R). ## 3.2. Mixed Payment Mechanism

The mixed payment mechanism (X) begins similarly to risk adjustment. A share $S _ { x }$ of bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries choose to investigate the MCOs and make a selection. For the remaining $1 - S _ { x }$ who do not make a selection, MCOs are offered a <sup>fi</sup>xed payment w<sub>x</sub> for each bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary they enroll. MCOs match randomly and sequentially with each of these bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries, evaluate $c _ { i j k }$ for each matching bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary, and take on those they <sup>fi</sup>nd pro<sup>fi</sup>table. The difference is that under mixed payment, should bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary i be rejected by all n ${ \bf M C O s } _ { \ L }$ each MCO must report to the state agency an amount $( b _ { i j } )$ at which that bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary would be acceptable. These amounts are the MCOs’ bids. For those bene<sup>fi</sup>- ciaries rejected by all $n$ MCOs, the state agency awards the contract to enroll the bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary to the MCO submitting the lowest bid and pays this MCO an amount equal to the second-lowest bid. This reim bursement is funded by charging $\tau _ { x }$ to each taxpayer. In addition to the cost of reimbursement, the state agency incurs a cost $\lambda > 0$ for each bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary allocated according to competitive bids. This term captures any additional administrative costs from allocating a given bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary this way, as well as any potential costs from bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries who become dissatis<sup>fi</sup>ed with their assigned plan and seek new arrangements.<sup>22</sup>

In technical terms, the mixed payment mechanism is a second-price sealed-bid procurement auction with a posted sell price and n preauction offers. The state agency is the principal, all n rejecting MCOs are bidders, and the enrollment of a bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary is the contract. Each MCO, in random order, considers the same take-it-or-leave-it offer to enroll each bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary. By accepting a presented offer, an MCO can end the allocation process for this bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary and, in doing so, is guaranteed revenue equal to the offered payment. In rejecting, the MCO likely cedes this decision to a different MCO and risks losing the opportunity to enroll this bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary at a higher payment in the auction stage. This is the key departure from existing buy price auction mechanisms, where a bidder’s opportunity to accept the buy price is never ceded to another bidder (Bauner 2015, Grebe et al. 2016). The pure bidding mechanism (P), where all of share $1 - S _ { x }$ bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries are allocated by submitted bids, is a special case of the mixed payment mechanism. Setting an extremely low initial payment in the mixed mechanism, $w _ { x } < \underline { { c } } ,$ for example, would make every MCO unwilling to accept any bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary at the initial payment, leaving all bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries to be allocated based on bids. Although this already implies that the mixed mechanism weakly dominates pure bidding, the results below reveal the dominance to be strict.

Working backward, an MCO j that has rejected bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary i must decide on the amount to submit as a bid to enroll bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary i. If this bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary is subsequently accepted by a different MCO, then MCO j’s resulting payoff is zero regardless of its bid. MCO $j ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ payoff is affected by its bid if and only if the other n – 1 MCOs also reject, and the bids are used to allocate the bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary. It is well established in the literature that bidder $j ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s }$ optimal bid to enroll bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary i $( b _ { i j } ^ { \star } )$ in a second-price procurement auction is the opportunity cost of the contract $\left( c _ { i j k } \right)$ . As this bid weakly dominates all bids in the universal-rejection outcome, and payoff is independent of bid in any other outcome, this is bidder j’s optimal strategy ex ante.

MCOs must decide whether to accept or reject a given bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary before submitting a bid. An MCO j can estimate the probability that a bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary i is accepted following a rejection, the probability of $c _ { i j k }$ being the lowest bid, and the expected payment conditional on that event. Given the prospective payment offered, the value of accepting is $w _ { x } - c _ { i j k }$ . For any $w _ { x } < \bar { c } _ { \cdot }$ , the value to the MCO of rejecting is strictly positive because there is a positive probability that the $n - 1$ other MCOs also reject, and the bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary is allocated by submitted bids. These follow directly from Proposition 1 because bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries’ prospects for enrollment with MCOs are higher under either competitive bidding mechanism than under risk adjustment. With bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries unambiguously better off, a competitive bidding mechanism dominates risk adjustment if the entitlement program spending under the former is no greater than under the latter, leaving taxpayers no worse off. Based on Condition (2), and Propositions 3 and 4, the dominance conditions are most likely to fail when enrollment is costly to secure without delivering signi<sup>fi</sup>cant health improvements or savings through coordination.

## 4. Numerical Analysis

In this section, we report the results from numerical analysis designed to investigate the circumstances under which Conditions (9) and (10) tend to hold, as well as the model parameters that are most impactful on outcome variables. The numerical analysis explores the applicability of bidding mechanisms to various healthcare markets and projects the differences in overall costs across mechanisms. Because the implementation and <sup>fi</sup>xed costs associated with competitive bidding may be signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, the cost savings estimates would help clarify the feasibility of the proposed mechanisms. Although the model is simulated over a parameter space with values based on extensive review of the literature, this section does not present a true calibration. Numerical analysis in the absence of true calibration is common in cases such as this where accurate estimates of key parameters may not be readily available (Giesen et al. 2009, Mamani et al. 2013). Nevertheless, we caution that our numerical results should be considered useful guides rather than concrete estimates.

## 4.1. Parameter Settings

We derive equilibria for 20,000 randomly drawn parameter sets from a parameter space between 19 and 23 dimensions, depending on the number of bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary types. Each element of each parameter set was drawn independently from a uniform distribution between the bounds shown in Table 2. The bounds were selected to produce parameter means approximating those from real-world entitlement programs. Along with the expected values of outcome variables, this method reveals which parameters most affect the performance outcomes. We describe the parameter value settings in the following paragraphs.

4.1.1. Population Parameters. There are 55.5 million bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries enrolled in Medicare, 74.2 million in Medicaid, and 9.9 million dual eligibles (Kaiser

Family Foundation 2016; 2017a, b), meaning the share of bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries in entitlement programs (D) in the U.S. population is 36.7%. The mean for $m _ { 1 }$ is targeted at \$15,234, which is the weighted average of the incomes of all Medicare and Medicaid bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries (Jacobson et al. 2014, Department of Health & Human Services 2016). In the case of Medicaid, nationwide average enrollee income was unavailable. As a substitute, we used the ACA Medicaid expansion minimum income eligibility threshold (138% of federal poverty level). Assuming that the remaining 63.3% of con sumers are taxpayers, the U.S. per capita GDP of \$55,836.80 (World Bank 2017) translates into average taxpayer income m of \$79,377.44. We set the restriction $\dot { m } _ { 2 } > ( D / ( 1 - D ) ) \times ( \operatorname* { m a x } { \{ w ^ { \prime } , \bar { c } \} } )$ so that taxpayer consumption is always above zero in the simulations. Under this functional form, the solution to a hypothetical consumer optimization problem is to devote share $\beta$ of income to the pursuit of greater health. Skew is the mass of the unconditional probability density function distributed over the greater half of the support, $( 0 . 5 ( \underline { { c } } + \overline { { c } } ) , \overline { { c } } )$ . 4.1.4. Enrollment Parameters. The parameter mean for $w ^ { \prime }$ captures the treatment costs that enrolled bene-<sup>fi</sup>ciaries would have incurred if left unenrolled with an MCO. Other references for this parameter are obtained through comparisons of charges for primary care services in emergency departments, which are an important source of care for those with poor access, vs. those in more appropriate primary care settings. Emergency department charges are usually between 1.2 and 5.4 times greater (Blue Cross Blue

Shield 2009) than charges in primary care settings. To test all these scenarios, w is drawn randomly in each simulation such that its mean value is 3.56 times greater than the mean cost of the upper two quartiles of bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries (i.e., those most likely to face rejection) while using the 0.5 estimate of Coughlin et al. (2014) as a lower bound. The simulations therefore cover unenrolled bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary care scenarios that are more costly for the state agency, as well as those that are less expensive.

4.1.5. Risk Adjustment. The accuracy parameter of the risk adjustment scheme ρ  in each simulation is drawn from a uniform distribution over the interval 0, 0:3 . These bounds approximate the degree to which real-world risk adjustment mechanisms reduce the unexplained variance in bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary costs. The HCC adjustment model used in Medicare Advantage reduces it by 11% (Cooper and Trivedi 2012). Some private risk adjustment systems can reduce the variance by more than 50% in certain circumstances (Thomas et al. 2004). The bounds on $\rho$ result in simulated risk adjustment schemes that reduce the unconditional variance in costs by between 0% and 50%, with an average reduction of 16.4% across all simulations and 25% in simulations with multiple risk types. Thus, the simulated accuracy levels cover the full realistic range.

A <sup>fi</sup>nal note on the simulations concerns of Condition (1). Unlike in the analytical section, this condition is not required to hold over the simulated parameter sets. This is because the numerical section attempts to replicate real-world entitlement programs, where eligibility is typically determined by income thresholds or membership in vulnerable demographic groups rather than strictly by Condition (1). The main impact of relaxing the condition is the potential for scenarios where the state agency would be better off leaving the most costly bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries unenrolled. This could bias the numerical results against the mixed and pure bidding mechanisms because these mechanisms always secure enrollment for the entire set of bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries, no matter how costly. Even so, over all simulated parameter sets, Condition (1) ends up holding for 96.9% of bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries, on average. Therefore, although the bidding mechanisms ultimately secure enrollment for these “inef<sup>fi</sup>cient” bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries, they make up a relatively small share and so any bias against the competitive bidding mechanisms is likely modest. The simulations for reserve price mechanisms estimate the savings analogous to a “carving out” of those bene<sup>fi</sup>- ciaries the state agency does not wish to enroll.

In fact, expected MCO pro<sup>fi</sup>ts per bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary under pure bidding and mixed bidding were higher than under risk adjustment for more than 41.6% and 44.7% of parameter sets, respectively. There are multiple reasons for this. First, the bidding mechanisms are more likely than risk adjustment to allocate each bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary to the MCO with the lowest $c _ { i j k } .$ . Therefore, although the enrolling MCOs tend to receive lower payments, they also tend to bear lower costs. Second, the optimal riskadjusted payment tends to leave only 90% of the S share of bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries pro<sup>fi</sup>table enough for at least one MCO to enroll. Under optimal bidding strategies, however, 100% of these bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries are pro<sup>fi</sup>table to the winning MCO, regardless of the $c _ { i j k }$ values of the bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries. Finally, MCOs can pro<sup>fi</sup>t from their information advantage only over the share of bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries that are allocated by the state agency. The prospect of universal rejection under risk adjustment increases the share of bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries who investigate and select their own MCO by 5.4%, relative to the bidding mechanisms. This reduces the share of bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries allocated and thus reduces the MCOs’ opportunities for pro<sup>fi</sup>t. Although these results do not account for potential differences in MCO <sup>fi</sup>xed costs across mechanisms, they do indicate that symmetric MCOs could expect similar operating pro<sup>fi</sup>ts under any of the three mechanisms.

As a result, the state agency’s expenditure increases because each investigating bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary costs more in reimbursement (<sup>E</sup> c ) than when allocated through bids.

## 5. Discussion and Conclusion

This research investigates whether and how bene<sup>fi</sup>ciarylevel competitive bidding can mitigate the problem of risk selection in healthcare entitlement programs. We model these programs as a two-sided health insurance market where three mechanisms for allocating bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries across MCOs are compared: risk adjusted prospective payment, pure competitive bid ding, and a mix of prospective payment and bidding. The bidding mechanisms are best at securing coordi nated care for all bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries by removing unpriced heterogeneity, but this may be very costly to achieve. Therefore, it is not clear which mechanism best satis-<sup>fi</sup>es the goals of the entitlement program. Analytical results show that even the optimal risk-adjusted payment leaves a positive share of bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries rejected by all MCOs. This is because the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency gains from raising the baseline payment and inducing MCOs to accept more bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries are eventually outweighed by higher costs over those bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries already enrolled. In contrast, risk selection never occurs under the bidding mechanisms. This is because, where MCOs follow their optimal bidding strategies, the lowest bidder to enroll a given bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary always expects to make a pro<sup>fi</sup>t and is thus willing to enroll high-cost bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries. We show that the mixed payment mechanism achieves this result at a strictly lower overall cost than the pure bidding mechanism. Because of the differential impact across mechanisms on the practice of risk selection in equilibrium, a greater share of bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries rely on the state agency for allocation to an MCO under mixed and pure bidding mechanisms than under risk adjustment or reserve price auctions with implications for the tax burden of the entitlement program. Finally, comparative statics reveal how select model parameters affect the potential savings realized through competitive bidding.

Numerical analysis of 20,000 simulations approximating real-world healthcare entitlement programs reveals that the bidding mechanisms achieve both greater bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary enrollment and lower taxpayer burden in more than 75.1% of simulated parameter sets. On average, the bidding mechanisms improved care coordination for 12.1% of allocated bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries and reduced the tax burden of the program expenses by 9.2%. Parameter sets under which the bidding mechanisms dominated bore a greater resemblance to the circumstances of real-world entitlement programs than those where bidding did not. Despite these promising results, the bidding mechanisms were signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly more costly than risk adjustment in approximately 17% of the simulations, suggesting that policymakers should carefully evaluate market circumstances before pursuing bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary-level competitive bidding. The bidding mechanisms are most likely to dominate when the share of high-risk bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries is high and when the cost of universal rejection is high relative to the typical cost of enrollment.

## 5.1. Real-World Implementation of Beneficiary-Level Competitive Bidding

To our knowledge, a high-throughput auction platform facilitating bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary-level bidding does not yet exist. In the past, individual-level auctions for health insurance were impractical because of high transactions costs. However, recent technological advances have considerably lowered these barriers (Chen et al. 2020). Online transaction costs have plummeted over the last decade. Online auction platforms can accommodate large volumes of transactions at a negligible marginal cost. The computing, processing, and storage technologies available through Infrastructure-as-a-Service (IaaS) from public cloud vendors make it possible for organizations to run large-scale auctions at very high speeds. In the private sector, there are numerous auction platforms that facilitate auctions between individuals and organizations. In the public sector, the federal and state-level health insurance exchanges have facilitated coverage for more than 15 million individuals<sup>29</sup> with private health insurers. Although these exchanges do not allow bidding, these platforms are robust enough to handle large transaction volumes.

Risk scoring is a common practice in health plans (Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services 2016). The prevailing risk scoring frameworks can be the basis of bidding algorithms used in the proposed mechanisms. Automated bidding tools can facilitate real-world implementation of individualized auctions and obviate the need for online auction participants to monitor auctions continuously by specifying and using datadriven decision rules (Jank and Zhang 2011, Sandholm and Likhodedov 2015, Ketter et al. 2020). In fact, automation tools created through agent modeling can help bidders in all phases of the auction process, including bid preparation, bid planning, bid execution, bid revision, and bid sniping (Bapna et al. 2020).

In recent years, IS researchers have strongly advocated for the use of simulations to address research problems where closed form analytical solutions are extremely hard or where complete and reliable data are unavailable (Bapna et al. 2004, Giesen et al. 2009, Ketter et al. 2009, Day et al. 2012). In their research commentary, Bichler et al. (2010) have urged research ers to employ a variety of tools to extend computationally oriented auctions and market design research. In a recent review paper, Chen et al. (2020) emphasize that theoretical and computational approaches to market and mechanism design are complementary. Prior research has also speci<sup>fi</sup>cally suggested that numerical analyses can address real-world problems and provide recommendations for policy discussions and regulatory framework (Day et al. 2012, Ketter et al. 2016, Chen et al. 2020). In the healthcare context, Giesen et al. (2009) use actual market data to parameterize their simulations, an approach that we have used in our own simulations. These approaches are particularly applicable when public and private data sources may not be available or robust (Mamani et al. 2013).

An important insight from the study of Gupta et al. (2012) is that successful auctions need to codify the characteristics of the auctions carefully and unambig uously. This step is important to reach scale. The guid ing motto here is “If you can spec it, you can bid it” (Manoochehri and Lindsy 2008). If the bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary speci<sup>fi</sup>cation is clear and unambiguous, then both MCOs and state agencies can interpret the product the same way and participate in online auctions successfully. Thus, policymakers and MCOs need to collaboratively codify all bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary characteristics that are important for assessing bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary-level heterogeneity.

## 5.2. Limitations of the Research

Before we discuss the contributions of our work and its implications for policymakers and managers, we identify a few limitations that provide opportunities to extend the research model we have developed. The <sup>fi</sup>rst limitation concerns the prospect of collusion among MCOs, which this model assumes away. Like any mechanism based on competition among suppliers, collusion would drive up payments and make either of the two competitive bidding mechanisms more expensive. Notwithstanding the relative success of existing bidding arrangements in healthcare, there are features of bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary-level bidding that naturally mitigate the threat of collusion. Because of expectations of privacy, there is no need to publicly announce the winning bidder, thus removing one avenue for bidders to monitor and enforce collusive behavior. Bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary-level bidding involves many auctions for relatively small contracts, meaning there is little payoff from colluding in individual auctions and greater opportunity for state agencies to discover correlated bidding across auctions. Finally, concerns over collusion could be addressed with collusion-proof auction designs (Pavlov 2008, Che and Kim 2009). We also do not model potential asymmetry among MCOs. Modeling asymmetry among bidders can provide further interesting theoretical results in this domain. Additionally, we do not examine competitive dynamics between MCOs wherein they may compete to enroll the same bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary. Instead, our model assumes that MCOs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries match sequentially.

The model focuses on the complexities of the supply side of the entitlement program and, as a result, keeps the demand side relatively simple. As constructed, the cost characteristics of the S bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries who investigate end up the same as those who do not. We have not pursued some potential complexities on the demand side that could affect this outcome. For example, we do not explore correlations between bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary preferences and cost parameters. Such correlations could lead to adverse selection, which would not be addressed by the mechanisms explored here. We also do not allow MCOs to practice risk selection on the bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries who investigate and choose their own MCO. The impact of these complexities would likely depend on whether high risk types become more or less concentrated among the set of 1 S bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries. If alternate modeling assumptions make high risk bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries less likely to investigate, then (as the simulations show) the state agency would struggle to allocate them using risk adjustment. On the other hand, should high types investigate at greater rates than low types, then risk adjustment may perform better. Possible distributional considerations on the demand side and how they are impacted by different mechanisms would be a useful future research direction.

## 5.3. Research Contributions and Implications

The analysis presented here makes a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant contribution to the literature. Although researchers have mentioned bidding as an option for resolving selection problems (Enthoven 1988, Newhouse 1996), this is the <sup>fi</sup>rst study to formally model and evaluate bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary-level competitive bidding mechanisms in an environment susceptible to risk selection. Finding ef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies in this setting is particularly important given the immense challenge in keeping entitlement programs solvent while still covering all quali<sup>fi</sup>ed bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries. Although no theoretical model can capture every nuance of complex real-world healthcare entitlement programs, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings in this research nevertheless reveal the exciting potential for bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary-level bidding to accomplish important policy objectives. The potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts include the elimination of risk selection, expanded enrollment with private MCOs for even the most-costly bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries, and a reduced burden on taxpayers. We contribute to the information systems and mechanism design literature by demonstrating that, although the availability of more <sup>fi</sup>ne-grained information through the implementation of health information technologies may enable MCOs to risk select more <sup>fi</sup>nely, it also enables program administrators to design mechanisms that have the potential to lower costs while ensuring full bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary enrollment. Our research demonstrates the potential for information technology (IT) and analytics-enabled mechanism design to deliver societal bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. We also contribute to the nascent smart digital market design literature by providing insights on the implementation of algorithmically enabled mixed mechanism markets (Ketter et al. 2020).

A number of implications emerge from our analy sis. First, the mixed payment mechanism described here is new to the mechanism design literature. The analytical dominance over pure bidding of a mechanism incorporating prospective payment and preauction offers implies that these kinds of mechanisms can achieve ef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies in such large and important markets as healthcare and health insurance. The results from this study can be extended to use more elaborate combinations of competitive bidding with traditional payment methods in the pursuit of even better outcomes. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally in the healthcare context, competitive bidding allows an uninformed state agency to eliminate risk selection while keeping reimbursement fully prospective, and thus preserves MCO incentives to contain costs. As such, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings in this study present new insights for managing tradeoffs between various sources of inef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies in health insurance markets. Finally, competitive bidding eliminates risk selection regardless of the number of risk types or the variance in match-speci<sup>fi</sup>c costs across MCOs. This result suggests that competitive bidding mechanisms would be particularly effective in cases where it is dif-<sup>fi</sup>cult for traditional risk adjustment schemes to verify payment groups or otherwise price heterogeneity.

A potential extension inspired by the <sup>fi</sup>ndings in this research can examine outcomes under the assumption of MCOs receiving signals of an underlying unobserved common enrollment cost rather than observed common and match-speci<sup>fi</sup>c costs. This extension would require a substantially different theoretical framework than the one constructed here. At the outset, it is not clear how the ordering of mechanisms would change under this alternative framework. In a common value auction, rational bidders would adjust their bids above the cost indicated by their signal and would thus avoid a “winner’s curse” in equilibrium. Conversely, under risk adjustment, MCOs would have reason to consider rejecting bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries that appear to be pro<sup>fi</sup>table. Future research into this extension may determine whether mechanisms incorporating competitive bidding could mitigate other selection problems in health insurance.

## 5.4. Implications for Practice

Our results provide important insights for healthcare policy makers and administrators. First, the numerical results reveal that bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary-level competitive bidding tends to dominate risk adjustment over a wide range of realistic parameter values. As such, competitive bidding at least deserves consideration in most jurisdictions. Bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary-level competitive bidding is most effective in jurisdictions with high concentrations of high-risk types or where care coordination achieves substantial cost savings over unmanaged care. The <sup>fi</sup>nding that savings from competitive bidding increase with and are sensitive to the eligible share of bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries shows that this mechanism is scalable, which should appeal to program managers in jurisdictions with large bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary populations. Bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary-level bidding would be most effective in urban and suburban areas with large populations capable of sustaining multiple competing health plans. Interestingly, models promoting waste reduction through care coordination, such as accountable care organizations, patient-centered medical homes, and health information exchanges, improve the likelihood of competitive bidding mechanisms achieving their desired outcomes. Finally, high income levels and improved health outcomes from care coordination also increase the amount of savings due to bidding, although the numerical results revealed these are likely of secondary importance.

## 5.5. Conclusion

Despite signi<sup>fi</sup>cant efforts by healthcare administrators to curtail it, risk selection remains a pervasive and costly <sup>fi</sup>xture of U.S. healthcare entitlement programs. The conditions for risk selection arise when payment mechanisms leave individual-level heterogeneity in treatment cost unpriced. We suggest that having MCOs bid to enroll individual bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries may account for this heterogeneity and thus remove the incentive for risk selection. To explore this hypothesis, we construct a two-sided market where bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries have the option to investigate and choose an MCO to enroll with while MCOs can practice risk selection over the remaining bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries allocated by a state agency. We investigate the impact of bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary-level bidding on plan enrollment and cost using a mix of theoretical and numerical approaches. Our theoretical results indicate that a mixed mechanism, using both prospective payment and competitive bidding, achieves universal bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary enrollment at a lowe cost than bidding alone. Either of the bidding mechanisms achieves greater bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary enrollment than an optimally set risk-adjusted prospective payment. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that the practice of risk selection in equilibrium affects bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries’ willingness to rely on the state agency for allocation to an MCO, which has implications for the tax burden under risk adjustment and reserve price auctions. Additionally, our numerical analysis predicts that, relative to risk adjustment, competitive bidding secures coordinated care for an average additional 12.1% of allocated bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries at 9.2% lower cost. This would amount to approximately \$27 billion in program savings.

To our knowledge, this research represents the <sup>fi</sup>rst effort to formally model bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary-level competitive bidding and its impact on healthcare entitlement programs. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings strongly suggest that competitive bidding can be of use in achieving the dual aim of lower costs and expanded access. We caution administrators that implementation will necessitate a thoughtful, deliberate, and protracted process. The expected improvements in bene<sup>fi</sup>ciary enrollment and program solvency, however, represent an intriguing prospect. We hope this study stimulates additional research and demonstration projects to fully explore the potential for digital markets to facilitate bene<sup>fi</sup>ciarylevel bidding. There is immense potential for smart markets to improve the health outcomes of bene<sup>fi</sup>ciaries in a <sup>fi</sup>nancially sustainable manner for taxpayers.
