# Overcoming the Single-IS Paradigm in Individual-Level IS Research

Abstract. As the world turns digital, the number of information system (IS) that individu als use in their work and nonwork lives has increased signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly. These different IS are often subject to interactions and interdependencies among them, which creates new challenges and opportunities for individuals. Yet we observe a disconnect between this “multi-IS” reality and individual-level IS research, which is shaped by a dominant single-IS paradigm—that is, a deep-rooted focus on one-to-one relationships between users and IS. Although we do not question the accomplishments made by prior research, we observe at least three problems that emerge from the traditional single-IS perspective. First, the single-IS paradigm might produce incomplete theories that do not consider interactions between multiple IS that are relevant to a phenomenon. Second, existing theories that represent a phenomenon from a single-IS perspective might misrepresent the phenomenon when considering it from a multi-IS perspective. Third, we might simply lack theories fo certain phenomena that involve users’ interactions with multiple IS. To inspire future re searchers to overcome the boundaries of the single-IS paradigm, we <sup>fi</sup>rst illustrate its prevalence in existing research and then build on representation theory and general systems theory to offer a framework for theorizing about multi-IS phenomena. We also demonstrate how scholars can apply this framework to study challenges and opportunities that occur when individuals interact with multiple IS. This way, we offer a foundation for future research that helps to improve work and life in our multi-IS world.

## 1. Introduction

In today’s digital societies, we use a great variety of information system (IS) that affect many aspects of our lives. We routinely cycle through different mobile apps and naturally open multiple browser tabs, all while “alt-tabbing” back and forth between different windows on our computers. In other words, we interact with a multitude of different IS. In this “multi-IS” world, new challenges and opportunities arise that relate to interactions and interdependencies between different IS. For instance, different IS compete for the same mental resources such as our attention. Or we might process a lot of redundant information while using different IS for the same purpose (e.g., different news apps, different social media). On the bright side, we might be able to make more well-informed decisions by integrating information from multiple IS. In sum, our digital world has given rise to a great variety of phenomena that involve multiple IS.

Although it is obvious that individuals in modern societies are usually users of multiple IS, this is not adequately re<sup>fl</sup>ected in most IS theories at the individual level. Instead, a dominant single-IS paradigm is woven deeply into the fabric of the IS discipline, whereby studies focus on one-to-one relationships between users and IS. This paradigm constrains research on contemporary phenomena that involve multiple IS and their relationships (Kane and Alavi 2008, Lyytinen 2010, Stephanidis et al. 2019). We see at least three problems that the single-IS paradigm creates for IS theory:

1. Existing theories might be incomplete because they do not consider interactions between multiple IS that are relevant to a phenomenon. Extending these theories to account for multiple IS could increase their explanatory or predictive power.

2. Existing theories that represent a phenomenon from a single-IS perspective could also misrepresent the phenomenon when considering it from a multi-IS view. In this case, existing theories might include constructs or relationships that need to be removed or replaced to realign the theory with the phenomenon.

3. For certain phenomena that involve users’ interactions with multiple IS, theories might be completely nonexistent if the phenomena have remained overlooked or unstudied.

To date, the theoretical implications of multiple IS have remained largely unexplored. But given the pervasiveness of IS in today’s reality, external stakeholders (e.g., scholars from other disciplines and practitioners) might <sup>fi</sup>nd it odd that IS research lacks clear answers to many questions pertaining to multi-IS phenomena, let alone constructs or theories that represent those phenomena.<sup>1</sup> Our aim here is therefore to offer a framework that helps to go beyond the single-IS paradigm. To do so, we brie<sup>fl</sup>y review some key topics in the IS discipline, just as illustrative examples of the single-IS paradigm. Afterward, we introduce the idea of systems in which users interact with multiple interrelated IS. We call these systems multi-IS systems. From the idea of multi-IS systems, we construct our framework that can serve future theorizing concerned with multiple IS. We then illustrate how researchers can apply the framework to rethink extant theory from a multi-IS perspective or to theorize about unstudied phenomena that involve multiple IS. Overall, we hope to ignite new studies that go beyond the single-IS paradigm and realign IS research with today’s multi-IS reality. To focus, we limit our arguments to research at the individual level, excluding research concerned with other units of analysis (e.g., organizations and markets).

## 2. The Single-IS Paradigm in Individual-Level IS Research

Although the technological context has changed signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly over the years, certain assumptions in the IS discipline persist because of its cumulative nature and the path dependencies that result (Hirschheim and Klein 2012, Winter et al. 2014). One such assumption that still shapes current thinking is what we call the single-IS paradigm, which means that individual-level studies in IS largely focus on one-to-one relationships between users and IS. This paradigm has its roots in historical contexts where a user interacted with a given IS within given organizational boundaries (e.g., Winter et al. 2014). Historically, this single-IS focus made sense because the IS discipline was long driven by the question of how to maximize the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts offered by a given IS. We will now brie<sup>fl</sup>y illustrate the single-IS paradigm in four exemplary areas of IS research, although we found other areas to be subject to the single-IS paradigm, too.

## 2.1. IS Use

Understanding IS use has been one of the key concerns of IS scholars (e.g., Bayerl et al. 2016, Kim et al. 2016, Schmitz et al. 2016, and Burton-Jones et al. 2017a). IS use may be the most widely studied construct in the IS discipline (Burton-Jones et al. 2017a). However, prior studies on IS use do not consider the use of multiple IS. Instead, they typically focus on a given IS such as a data warehouse (e.g., Wixom and Todd 2005), a retail website (e.g., Devaraj et al. 2002), or a banking IS (e.g., Bhattacherjee 2001 and Beaudry and Pinsonneault 2010) while disregarding other IS that might be relevant to each context of use. A look at some core constructs in this area illustrates the single-IS focus. For instance, perceived usefulness is “the extent to which a person believes that using the system will enhance his or her job performance” (Davis 1989, p. 320), whereas IS use is “a user’s employment of a system to perform a task” (Burton-Jones and Gallivan 2007, p. 659). Interestingly, scholars interested in postadoptive use have acknowledged that IS are part of larger work systems, noting that an IS must be integrated well with other elements (e.g., other IS) of the system (Hsieh et al. 2011). Yet no constructs or theories capture the role of multiple IS within these larger systems.

## 2.2. Information Overload

Information overload is a state where the information load encountered by an individual exceeds that indi vidual’s processing capacities (e.g., Jacoby et al. 1974, Grise and Gallupe´ 1999, Eppler and Mengis 2004, and Oldroyd and Morris 2012). IS research on information overload points out that IS might provide more information than the user can handle (e.g., Morris et al. 1992, Grise and Gallupe´ 1999, Jones et al. 2004, and Saunders et al. 2017). To address this problem, most research in this area is concerned with reducing the information load offered by a given IS (Eppler and Mengis 2004). Despite their contributions, however, these studies restrict their perspective on overload from single IS such as a search engine, an electronic brainstorming system, or a blogging platform (e.g., Grise and Gallupe´ 1999, Guo et al. 2017, and Ghose et al. 2019). This single-IS perspective overlooks that individuals often depend on multiple IS to complete a given task. And although none of the IS involved might cause information overload alone, the combinations of information that these systems offer might be too much to process for the user.

## 2.3. IS Success

A key outcome of interest to IS researchers is the impact (or net bene<sup>fi</sup>t) that captures the value derived from IS. In this regard, the IS success model (DeLone and McLean 1992) spurred a wealth of impactful studies on the impacts and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of IS (e.g., DeLone and McLean 2004, Iivari 2005, Wu and Wang 2006, Kulkarni et al. 2007, and Wang et al. 2007). Yet again, the single-IS paradigm is inherent in this stream of research as well, which has focused on the impacts and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts that individuals and organizations can derive from a single IS (e.g., DeLone and McLean 1992, 2003; Iivari 2005; Wu and Wang 2006; Wang et al. 2007; and Petter et al. 2008). Some studies have investigated an ambiguous number of IS, sometimes referring to an unspeci<sup>fi</sup>ed number of IS and a single IS in the same study (e.g., Kulkarni et al. 2007 and Gorla et al. 2010). However, none of these studies has theorized potential interdependencies and interactions between multiple IS whereby the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts offered by a given IS might depend on other IS.

## 2.4. Technostress

Over the last decade, IS scholars have shown great interest in the phenomenon of technostress, which is “stress that individuals experience due to their use of information systems” (Tarafdar et al. 2019, p. 6). Technostress describes “the stress that users experience as a result of application multitasking, constant connectivity, information overload, frequent system upgrades and consequent uncertainty, continual relearning and consequent job-related insecurities, and technical problems associated with the organizational use of $\mathrm { I C T ^ { \prime \prime } }$ (Tarafdar et al. 2010, pp. 304–305). The language used in this area of research has acknowledged the existence of multiple IS and suggests that the stress experienced by users might result from the number of IS they face (e.g., Ragu-Nathan et al. 2008; Tarafdar et al. 2010, 2019; and Ayyagari et al. 2011). The underlying view is that more systems increase the demands on individuals, which increases stress when demands exceed users’ resources and capabilities (e.g., Tarafdar et al. 2010 and Ayyagari et al. 2011). Despite this valuable perspective, technostress research has somewhat “black-boxed” IS (Ayyagari et al. 2011, p. 832), which means that we currently lack constructs or relationships that represent multiple IS. This way, we believe that technostress research could bene<sup>fi</sup>t from a more explicit treatment of multiple IS and their interactions in the future.

## 2.5. Prior Multi-IS Studies

Some prior studies have considered constellations of multiple ${ \mathrm { I S } } . ^ { 2 }$ For instance, research has highlighted that work processes often involve multiple IS, which makes it important to understand how users bridge the gaps between inputs and outputs of different systems (Bailey et al. 2010). Furthermore, individuals’ IS adoption may be impeded by their habits of using other systems that preexist in an organization (Polites and Karahanna 2012). Prior work has also shown that using multiple IS can affect the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts gained from each system. In particular, internet users’ division of time and attention among multiple online communities can bolster the responsiveness of each community (Kim et al. 2018). Moreover, research on multitasking is concerned with a multi-IS phenomenon, demonstrating the effect of multitasking (that may or may not involve multiple IS; $\mathrm { e . g . }$ , see Twyman et al. (2020)) on various performance-related outcome variables $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , Nieuwenhuis and Monsell 2002, Adler and Benbunan-Fich 2012, Aral et al. 2012, and Goes et al. 2018). Overall, these studies indicate that different kinds of relationships may exist between IS and demonstrate that valuable insights can be gained from a multi-IS perspective. Yet these previous studies have only begun to scratch the surface of what is possible, as many other phenomena that involve multiple IS have remained unstudied.

## 3. A Framework for Theorizing About Multi-IS Phenomena

We now propose a framework for theorizing about an individual’s relationships with more than one IS. We proceed as follows: First, because our framework relies on the idea that users and the IS they use form systems, we draw on general systems theory and de<sup>fi</sup>ne what a system is. Second, we explain what we understand as an IS in this research. Third, we combine these concepts and present a framework for conceptualizing systems that involve a user as well as multiple IS.

## 3.1. Systems

A system is a set of interrelated components (i.e., “things”) and their properties (e.g., Ackoff 1971, Kast and Rosenzweig 1972, and Nevo and Wade 2010). Systems are ordered hierarchically. This means that they are composed of subsystems (i.e., their components), and every system can also be conceptualized as being part (i.e., a component) of a larger system (e.g., Ackoff 1971 and Kast and Rosenzweig 1972). A key idea associated with systems is the notion of “holism,” re<sup>fl</sup>ecting that a system is more than the sum of its parts (e.g., Ackoff 1971 and Kast and Rosenzweig 1972). This is because a system’s components interact with each other, producing effects that the components would not produce in isolation.

These ideas are foundational to general systems theory (e.g., Boulding 1956 and Von Bertalanffy 1968), which seeks to explain the fundamental concepts and principles that systems across disciplines (e.g., biolo gy, ecology, sociology, economics) have in common. Within IS research, researchers have applied general systems theory to explain phenomena as diverse as IS artifact evaluations (Prat et al. 2015), the role of a company’s information technology (IT) function (Porra et al. 2005), and the strategic potential of IT (Nevo and

Wade 2010). Moreover, given the IS discipline’s concern with artifacts (e.g., Hevner et al. 2004 and Lee et al. 2015)—a concept that re<sup>fl</sup>ects the ideas of general systems theory as well (Simon 1996)—IS scholars have implicitly adopted the perspectives of general systems theory for a long time.

## 3.2. Information Systems

To de<sup>fi</sup>ne what an IS means in the context of this work, we draw on representation theory (e.g., Wand and Weber 1995 and Burton-Jones et al. 2017b). Representation theory has been long established in the IS discipline but has also sparked signi<sup>fi</sup>cant interest in recent years (e.g., Burton-Jones et al. 2017b, Recker et al. 2019, and Gerlach and Cenfetelli 2020). The theory states that, aside from any speci<sup>fi</sup>c features, the core purpose of an IS is to represent the state of a real-world system (e.g., Wand and Weber 1995 and Burton-Jones and Grange 2013). For instance, a social media system represents the state of a user’s real-world social network, whereas an enterpriseresource-planning system represents the state of a company’s business.

Representation theory helps us to understand two things. First, as an IS represents the states of realworld systems, more IS increase the number of realworld systems that a user can know the states of. Because real-world systems interconnect, users of multiple IS can become aware of interactions between realworld systems or even trigger such interactions. For instance, an online social network might raise a user’s awareness of friends who need support $( \mathrm { e . g . } ,$ , Maier et al. 2014). This information might con<sup>fl</sup>ict (or create synergies) with information obtained from a workrelated IS $( \mathrm { e . g . , }$ an email system), showing that demands at work allow (or disallow) the user to leave work early and take care of those friends.

Second, representation theory highlights that IS have different components (i.e., subsystems). Whereas other perspectives besides representation theory can offer useful views on the components of IS (de pending on the research aim), the theory offers one such perspective by dividing IS into three compo nents (e.g., Wand and Weber 1995 and Burton-Jones and Grange 2013): a deep structure (i.e., how the real-world system is modeled in the IS), a surface structure (i.e., the IS’s interface to the user), and a physical structure $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ the IS’s physical characteristics). No matter how IS are conceptualized, the components (or the components’ components) of multiple IS can be subject to interdependencies. For instance, the deep structure of one IS (or the user’s perception thereof) might not be independent of the deep structure of another IS because the two realworld systems represented by the two IS have overlapping elements.

## 3.3. Putting the Pieces Together: Systems of Information Systems

We now make the simple argument that an IS can be part of a larger system in which multiple IS are relevant to a user and in which relationships of all different kinds might exist between these IS—we call such larger systems multi-IS systems. This argument is based on the idea that systems can be subsystems of other systems (e.g., Ackoff 1971 and Kast and Rosenzweig 1972). Figure 1 shows how this view of multi-IS systems goes beyond the traditional view of the single-IS paradigm.

Studying multi-IS systems explicitly considers that, in reality, multiple IS are often relevant to users who try to achieve certain things. For instance, a user may have two different mobile apps for the same purpose $( \mathrm { e . g . , }$ two news apps) and might choose one or the other, depending on situational conditions. Or a user might have to use a number of complementary IS $( \mathrm { e . g . } ,$ , a hotel booking app and a car rental app) to complete a given task (e.g., planning a trip), and the different IS might be more or less well integrated with each other. In multi-IS systems, many kinds of relationships may exist between the different IS at different levels. There can be relationships at the system level (e.g., multiple IS share technological resources), at the user level (e.g., multiple IS are part of a user routine), or at the organizational level (e.g., two IS are offered by the same company), just to name a few examples.

In general, a system can possess emergent properties—that is, properties of the system or its components that depend on interactions among the system’s components (Ackoff 1971, Kast and Rosenzweig 1972, Nevo and Wade 2010). For instance, in a multi-IS system, the usefulness of one IS might be reduced by another IS because the use of the <sup>fi</sup>rst depends on the use of the second, but the two IS are incompatible. This makes the usefulness of the <sup>fi</sup>rst IS an emergent property of the multi-IS system. Emergent properties can also be properties of a system that none of its components possesses in isolation. For instance, two IS might offer similar information, which creates a “redundancy of information” as a new property of the system, indicating the degree to which information offered by the two IS is redundant from the user’s perspective. As another example, consider an IS that offers information to a user, but, in isolation, using this IS does not create stress for the user. However, the user might also use a second IS that offers information that is inconsistent with information offered by the <sup>fi</sup>rst IS $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , an email app and a calendar app might show inconsistent dates for an upcoming meeting). Such inconsistency might stress out the user, who might be unable to make a decision without unambiguous information about the true state of the real world. Here, both stress and inconsistency are emergent properties of the multi-IS system that consists of the user and the two IS involved. In particular, inconsistency is an emergent property because it results from comparing information between two IS. The level of stress experienced by the individual is an emergent property because its values are affected by the inconsistency of information. Figure 2 illustrates this example.

## 3.4. A Framework for Theorizing About Multi-IS Systems

On the basis of the idea of multi-IS systems, we now offer a framework that consists of three simple steps to guide future theorizing about multi-IS phenomena: re<sup>fl</sup>ecting on (1) the relevance of multiple IS to the phenomenon of interest, (2) the nature of relationships between the IS involved, and (3) the emergent properties created through these relationships. Figure 3 shows an overview of this framework.

Departing from a phenomenon of interest, the first step is to re<sup>fl</sup>ect on whether multiple IS are actually relevant to this phenomenon (i.e., whether the phe nomenon should be viewed as a multi-IS system). This <sup>fi</sup>rst step is not straightforward, as the relevance of multiple IS might not be obvious—especially because individual-level phenomena have consistently been framed as single-IS problems in past studies. It might help to think about questions such as, is the outcome of interest affected by or does it affect multiple IS? Is the use of one IS followed or replaced by the use of another IS? Are multiple IS somehow used simultaneously? What other IS might be relevant from a user perspective to complete a given task? Overall, this <sup>fi</sup>rst step should help researchers get an idea of whether any thing important would be lost when reducing the phenomenon to a single-IS issue.

Once researchers have reached an understanding that multiple IS are relevant to their phenomenon of interest, the second step is to re<sup>fl</sup>ect on the nature of those relationships between the IS involved that ultimately affect the user. Such relationships can exist at different levels (e.g., technology, user, task, organization, and time). For example, IS can be related at the level of technology if they communicate directly with each other (e.g., IS exchange data through an interface) or rely on shared technological resources (e.g., a shared database, shared software services, and shared processing power). At a user level, for example, different IS might be related as a result of users’ cognitions or because users’ actions create relationships between the IS. For instance, a user might perceive two IS as being substitutes or complements in terms of their functionalities. Similarly, different IS might be used in parallel or in sequence as part of a user routine or a work process that the user is engaged in. There can be many other relationships between IS that go beyond those listed here. For example, different IS might be related because they are offered by the same company, which could affect a user’s perception of privacy risks as a result of a potential integration of personal user information across the different IS. Given the manifold ways in which IS can be related, we do not wish to impose a particular conceptualization of relationships between IS to avoid excluding relationships that fall outside of such a conceptualization. Instead, we appeal to the creativity of scholars to identify those relationships between IS that are relevant in each case.

After having gained a clear picture of the relationships between the IS that are part of the multi-IS system under investigation, the third step is to re<sup>fl</sup>ect on emergent properties of this system that may be associated with the relationships identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the prior step.

The basic aim of this step is to answer the question of what makes the multi-IS system more than the sum of its parts. For instance, in the stress example shown in Figure 2, the two IS may offer overlapping information that might, however, be inconsistent (e.g., contradicting dates for a meeting). In this example, the degree of stress felt by the user can then be seen as an emergent property: although a user may experience a certain level of stress even when using only a single IS, this stress level changes because of the inconsistencies between the two IS. Furthermore, the inconsistency of information itself can be seen as an emergent property because this property only exists as a result of the comparison between information offered by the two IS.

Having speci<sup>fi</sup>ed the emergent properties that exist in the multi-IS system of interest, researchers should have gained a concrete understanding of how multiple IS interact to affect the phenomenon of interest. They can then proceed in their theorizing by conceptualizing and operationalizing the phenomenon, including any emergent properties that are relevant to it, and theorize about the emergent properties’ relationships with other constructs.

## 4. Exemplary Applications of the Framework

The framework presented in the preceding is consistent with the few research topics that have studied multi-IS phenomena in the past (e.g., Bailey et al. 2010, Polites and Karahanna 2012, and Goes et al. 2018). For instance, a multitasking context can be seen as a multi-IS system that includes a user who is doing the multitasking and a number of IS that the user switches between. Research on multitasking is usually interested in the performance outcomes of such behavior (e.g., Adler and Benbunan-Fich 2012, Aral et al. 2012, and Goes et al. 2018). One of the key <sup>fi</sup>ndings of multitasking research is that switching between tasks creates additional switching costs that can reduce an individual’s performance (e.g., Aral et al. 2012, Gilbert and Shallice 2002, and Goes et al. 2018). When viewed through the lens of a multi-IS system, these switching costs can be seen as an emergent property because a single-IS context would not have such a property. Likewise, the user’s performance is an emergent property of the multi-IS system because it is affected by interactions between the tasks (i.e., the switching costs).

Multitasking is just one out of many phenomena where multiple IS may be relevant. Our framework offers a tool for scholars interested in those phenomena by helping them to structure a phenomenon based on a general systems perspective. We now demonstrate how this can be done. To do so, we revisit the three problems raised in the introduction that we believe are associated with the single-IS paradigm: incomplete theories, theories that misrepresent, and nonexistent theories. We address each of these problems by applying the previously presented framework to illustrate how it can be used to broaden extant theoretical perspectives in useful ways and to theorize about novel phenomena involving multiple IS.

## 4.1. Addressing Incomplete Theories

4.1.1. IS Use. One of the areas where we think that a multi-IS perspective could complement previous insights is the great body of literature on IS use. This lit erature is one of the most mature areas in IS research, but it does not consider that the adoption and use of a particular IS might depend on other IS that are relevant to an adoption or usage context. New perspectives could be found by taking a multi-IS view and by research question.

Taking a multi-IS perspective could therefore help to offer more complete theories of IS use.

4.1.2. Technostress. Another area of research where a multi-IS view could extend existing theories is the technostress literature. Technostress research has not offered constructs or theories that represent interactions between multiple IS that might jointly create technostress. We think that such interactions play a role in causing stress for IS users, and a variety of ideas seem to be rather low-hanging fruit for technostress researchers who could be guided by the following research question.

As long as these emergent properties are not taken into account, there may be unexplained variance in research models that seek to explain technostress but are unable to capture the causes of stress that are subject to relationships between multiple IS.

## 4.2. Addressing Misrepresentations of Multi-IS Phenomena

4.2.1. Redundant Information Processing. Existing theories and constructs that <sup>fi</sup>t phenomena from a single-IS perspective might also misrepresent these phenomena, when seen from a multi-IS point of view. One example is the information quality construct that is a central component of the IS success model (De-Lone and McLean 1992). One of the construct’s dimensions that is a central antecedent to IS success is completeness, which is the degree to which all aspects of the real-world system represented by an IS are included in the information offered by the IS (e.g.,

Nelson et al. 2005). The immediate implication following from this single-IS perspective is that an IS should represent a real-world system (as perceived by the user) as completely as possible. However, this overlooks that a user might use more than one IS to ad dress a certain information need and, thus, might end up processing redundant information offered by multiple IS. This way, a user’s scarce informationprocessing resources are used inef<sup>fi</sup>ciently. From a multi-IS perspective, the completeness construct might need to be removed or replaced from IS success models to represent the nature of users’ multi-IS information seeking. To better understand redundant information processing based on multiple IS, we propose the following guiding research question.

These examples illustrate that individual-level outcomes related to IS success, such as user satisfaction or net bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (e.g., DeLone and McLean 1992, 2003), can be affected by redundancies in information between multiple IS. For instance, studying the use of a single social media network in isolation could lead to false conclusions about the amount of information a network should offer. By contrast, a multi-IS perspective could reveal that redundancies in information offered by multiple IS might have negative effects on IS success. Unless redundant information processing is accounted for as a multi-IS issue, optimizing information quality in terms of completeness might lead to inef<sup>fi</sup>cient use of information-processing resources. A multi-IS perspective on this issue could pave the way for solutions such as metalevel tools or intersystem integration efforts that seek to remedy redundancy problems.

4.2.2. Information Overload. Another phenomenon of interest that might be misrepresented by single-IS theories when taking a multi-IS perspective is information overload. Research in this area has helped to avoid information overload that occurs while using a single IS (e.g., Guo et al. 2017 and Ghose et al. 2019). But addressing information overload for a single IS in isolation (e.g., by optimizing the user interface design or reducing the amount of information offered) neglects that information overload might not always be a single-IS phenomenon. Although recommendations focusing on a single IS prevent information overload occurring during the use of this single IS, these recommendations might not prevent information overload that occurs because of combinations of information from multiple IS. ## 4.3. Addressing Nonexistent Theories: Focus on the Present Moment

A multi-IS view also opens up opportunities to investigate phenomena, which have not sparked IS researchers’ broad interest to date. One example is individuals’ ability to pay focused attention to what is physically in front of them and how the multitude of IS they use affects this ability. Research on meditation practices highlights the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of focused attention, linking these practices to positive outcomes such as reduced anxieties and cognitive <sup>fl</sup>exibility (e.g., Jin 1992, Kabat-Zinn et al. 1992, and Moore and Malinowski 2009). The use of multiple IS could interfere with but also facilitate focused attention. On the one hand, individuals’ constant checking behaviors can impair their ability to pay focused attention in a given situation (Gerlach and Cenfetelli 2020). Initial evidence suggests that even the mere presence of access to multiple digital channels might interfere with an individual’s ability to focus (Ward et al. 2017). On the other hand, the use of multiple IS could free an individual’s cognitive load (e.g., Clark and Chalmers 1998, Barr et al. 2015, and Duus et al. 2018), which could bene<sup>fi</sup>t the individual’s ability to focus. It would be useful to learn more about how multiple IS affect individuals ability to pay focused attention. We therefore ask the following research question.

## 5. Implications and Future Research

Individuals use many interrelated and interdependent IS, and they are holistic human beings whose feelings, thoughts, and behaviors are not strictly compartmentalized into isolated areas of life (each of which might be represented by one or multiple IS). Instead, these areas are entangled with one another, making life more than the sum of a number of separated parts (e.g., Muraven and Baumeister 2000, Allen et al. 2014, and van Knippenberg et al. 2015). We illustrate that IS research has become somewhat disconnected from this reality, as the problem space that consists of all multi-IS phenomena has been left largely unexplored. To inspire scholars to overcome the boundaries of the single-IS paradigm, we offer a framework to guide multi-IS theorizing that serves as a foundation for a new paradigm in the IS discipline.

Realigning IS research with today’s multi-IS reality bears great potential to improve life and work in our digital world. As the number of IS has increased steadily over time, many challenges associated with relationships between different IS have crept into the world unnoticed. More attention to these challenges can pave the way for making users’ interactions with IS more user-friendly and mentally ef<sup>fi</sup>cient. As an example, consider the circumstance that individuals use a myriad of IS just to communicate with others (e.g., multiple email clients, multiple text messaging apps, multiple business chats, multiple social media chats), and it can be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to remember what was communicated with whom and where. Seen from a pure user perspective, this “mess” is unlikely a result of users personal preferences, but it has developed over time, and no solutions have been offered that help address the growing complexity from the user’s perspective. Thus, there might be a demand for simple solutions that help users manage or reduce the complexity created by multiple IS. Finding such solutions needs to start with identifying problems and untapped potentials, and our research could help other scholars to pinpoint such problems and potentials.

In addition to future work on speci<sup>fi</sup>c multi-IS phenomena, there is potential for extending the framework we have offered as well as our conceptualization of multi-IS systems. For instance, general systems theory offers more ideas than those discussed here (e.g., open versus closed systems; Ackoff 1971) and could therefore extend our perspectives. Likewise, future research could derive models that help to address more speci<sup>fi</sup>c problem de<sup>fi</sup>nitions. For instance, one could think of multiple IS in a time (sequential/simultaneous) and task (related/unrelated) context, which would lead to a 2 2 structure. First, IS might be used sequentially in unrelated tasks (e.g., a scholar uses an email app before using a word processor to write a paper but gets stuck in the email app, leaving insuf<sup>fi</sup>- cient time to write the paper). Second, an individual might use IS sequentially in related tasks (e.g., a doctor interacts with different IS while treating a patient, such as an IS that offers laboratory results, an IS that offers diagnostic advice, or an email program to ask a colleague for a second opinion). Third, IS might be used simultaneously for unrelated tasks (e.g., a scholar might try to write a paper using a word processor while a mobile app sends stock market alerts). Fourth,

IS could be used simultaneously for related tasks (e.g., an online day investor trading constantly might use multiple IS in parallel to gather relevant information for decision making).

Our framework can also aid practitioners such as IS designers and developers. Design processes could bene<sup>fi</sup>t from additional re<sup>fl</sup>ection about how certain aspects of IS might interact with and work together with other IS that individuals use. Optimally, thinking about challenges and opportunities with respect to multiple IS would become an integral part of the IS design and development process. Thereby, our framework could assist practitioners in identifying potential relationships between IS and help them re<sup>fl</sup>ect on emergent properties of multi-IS systems. This way, design decisions could facilitate bene<sup>fi</sup>cial emergent properties and mitigate undesirable ones early in the process.

## 6. Conclusion

We offer a framework for IS research to think about individual-level phenomena that involve multiple IS. We did this not because we believe that the extant single-IS perspective does not have its merits but because we do not believe that it is necessarily the best and only view to understand today’s users’ interactions with IS and the consequences thereof. We hope that our framework will serve other scholars who seek to establish a new multi-IS paradigm in individual-level IS research.
