# Social Media Marketing, Quality Signaling, and the Goldilocks Principle

Tingting Nian,<sup>a,</sup>\* Arun Sundararajan<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Paul Merage School of Business, University of California, Irvine, California 92697; <sup>b</sup> Leonard N. Stern School of Business, New York University, New York, New York 10012 \*Corresponding author

Abstract. Embraced by a rapidly increasing number of companies, social media marketing has become an integral part of companies’ business strategies. However, not all the <sup>fi</sup>rms plan on a big spend on social media marketing. Our stylized model investigates the strate gic effects of social media marketing spending (SMM spending) with the presence of exogenous quality revelation through sources over which <sup>fi</sup>rms have no direct control. Unlike traditional advertising, social media marketing has two roles: awareness enhancement and information revelation. Consumers are heterogeneous in their awareness of the product (e.g., whether they know the existence of the product). Our analysis yields three main <sup>fi</sup>ndings. First, our results show the existence of a separating equilibrium and a partial-pooling equilibrium where the high-/low-quality <sup>fi</sup>rms pool together and spend less on social media marketing than the mid-quality <sup>fi</sup>rm. Intuitively, the high-quality <sup>fi</sup>rm gets enough quality transparency from background user-generated discussions, and the cost of maintaining a social presence outweighs the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. The low-quality <sup>fi</sup>rm avoids social media marketing because quality transparency is broadly detrimental, whereas the midtier <sup>fi</sup>rm is just right to bene<sup>fi</sup>t from social media discussions they encourage. Second, we also <sup>fi</sup>nd that in the partial-pooling equilibrium, as the fraction of aware consumers increases, the mid-quality <sup>fi</sup>rm spends less on social media marketing, whereas its optimal spending on social media marketing <sup>fi</sup>rst decreases, and then increases with the precision of the external information signal. Third, by comparing these two equilibria, we show that if the partial-pooling equilibrium survives the intuitive criterion, it Pareto-dominates the separating equilibrium.

## 1. Introduction

Social media comes in many forms, ranging from blogs, microblogs, social networks, forums, wikis, media-sharing sites, and more. The ubiquitousness of social media and channels present great opportunities and challenges for <sup>fi</sup>rms to manage their brands. Social media marketing, the process of marketing using social networks, online communities, electronic wordof-mouth, and more (Sajid 2016), has attracted many marketers’ attention. Results from the February 2018 Chief Marketing Of<sup>fi</sup>cer (CMO) survey indicate U.S. companies currently spend 12% of their marketing budgets on social media, and the social media marketing spending (SMM spending) is expected to reach 20.5% of marketing budgets in the next <sup>fi</sup>ve years (Moorman 2018). Social media marketing, de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the use of social media applications and its related technologies and features to help achieve marketing objectives (Dahnil et al. 2014), is quite different from traditional of<sup>fl</sup>ine and online media. Key differences between social media marketing and traditional advertising channels include two-way conversation versus one-way conversation, transparent versus opaque, authentic content versus polished content, active involvement versus passive involvement, real-time creation versus preproduced, and unstructured communication versus controlled communication (Hausman 2012). In contrast to other media, social media rather resembles living, interconnected, egalitarian, and interactive organisms beyond the control of any organization (Peters et al. 2013). On social media platforms, users’ opinion about a <sup>fi</sup>rm is no longer in<sup>fl</sup>uenced primarily by a message that is carefully orchestrated by the company’s marketing machine. Rather, conversations about experiences with <sup>fi</sup>rms are initiated on social media platforms (Avery et al. 1999, Dellarocas 2003), which in turn could shift the role of a <sup>fi</sup>rm from broadcasting its message to mediating the social conversation.

Social media marketing typically centers on brand building, brand awareness, consumer acquisition, and engagement. The SMM spending could be on, but not limited to, managing the online presence, and engaging consumers through creative content, paid advertising, campaigns, and promotions. We examined Facebook “brand pages,” pages set up on Facebook, and Twitter accounts run by 40 brands. We observe social media being used to (1) engage with fans and followers, (2) promote products and brands by amplifying the message to the friends of their fans, (3) recruit prospects who share great interest in the brand, and (4) listen to customers’ feedback and respond to concerns (some examples are provided in Figure 2 in Online Appendix F).<sup>1</sup> One of the main differences between social media marketing and traditional advertising is in the information revelation that is inherent in the former. Traditional advertising serves mainly two purposes: (1) to raise awareness of the existence of the product among the uninformed consumers and generate demand and (2) to persuade consumers into buying the product. Firms usually carefully design and deliver their advertising messages in traditional advertising, whereas with social media marketing, <sup>fi</sup>rms actually create a platform to facilitate information disclosure and transmission about their products among prospective consumers. Although many <sup>fi</sup>rms try to monitor the “sentiment” and impact of social media conversations and to identify opportunities for shaping them, non–<sup>fi</sup>rm-controlled sources of chatter will always exist.

Although many <sup>fi</sup>rms have come to recognize the importance of social media and the changes it has brought to the media landscape, we also observe that <sup>fi</sup>rms’ spending on social media marketing ranges from millions of dollars to zero. For instance, Apple’s ad spending still largely focuses on TV ads (i.e., NBA Basketball, the 2018 FIFA World Cup). Among the digital advertising spend in Q2 2018, desktop video accounted for 78% percent of its digital advertising spend, mobile video accounted for 13%, and almost nothing went to social media marketing (Gasbara 2018). Besides Apple, Tesla spends nothing on social media marketing, yet it has one of the highest social media pro<sup>fi</sup>les among all the automobile brands because of its organic engagements on social media (Hanley 2019).

Intuitively, one might expect high-quality <sup>fi</sup>rms to try to facilitate consumer-generated information transmission about product quality by spending more on social media marketing, whereas low-quality <sup>fi</sup>rms might want to take steps to deter information disclosure and constrain information dissemination online, if possible. However, this intuition runs counter to some of what we see in practice. Take the restaurant industry for an exampleP: high-end restaurants such as Per Se in New York are as much less likely to advertise on Yelp as a street-corner pizzeria compared with some midtier restaurants. This inverted U-shaped relationship between quality and advertising spending in the restaurant industry has been empirically documented in Horstmann and Moorthy (2003). Because their work only examined restaurants’ ad spending in three traditional media outlets, we conducted a preliminary empirical analysis on the association between quality and advertising on Yelp, which yielded a similar conclusion.<sup>2</sup> Similarly, Hollenbeck et al. (2019) also found an inverted U-shaped relationship between hotels’ average ratings on TripAdvisor and ad spending, both in traditional media and in online advertising. As many industry practitioners have explicitly stated, “businesses with poor or mediocre reviews should avoid considering Yelp advertising until their review score is higher. So, if your business currently has two stars on Yelp, advertising your page at this time will shine a light on your <sup>fl</sup>aws instead of highlighting your strengths” (Nate 2018).

The preceding discussion frames a number of interesting questions. Should a high-quality <sup>fi</sup>rm simply let information about its product spread organically or should it invest in SMM spending to facilitate a greater level of user-generated information? Given that the observation of such a spending might have some signaling value in itself, should a low-quality <sup>fi</sup>rm try to mimic a high-quality <sup>fi</sup>rm’s level of spending on social media marketing, in order to masquerade as a highquality <sup>fi</sup>rm, while running the risk of greater transparency into its product? How about the mid-quality <sup>fi</sup>rm? Can a <sup>fi</sup>rm try to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the social media con versation by adding to it or simply moderating it smarter?

Our paper suggests an alternative hypothesis: It may be strategically sensible for high-quality <sup>fi</sup>rms to proactively choose lower levels of SMM spending. Our explanation involves three aspects whose combination is unique to social media marketing. (1) Like many forms of advertising, it is a potential signal of quality, and at the same time, it could increase consumer awareness of the product (the awarenessenhancement effect). (2) Simultaneously, it is also a source of informative but noisy information that could increase quality transparency (the informationrevelation effect). (3) Product-quality information will always come from other sources that a <sup>fi</sup>rm cannot deter or control. As a consequence of this combination, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that SMM spending is not always monotonic in <sup>fi</sup>rm quality. Rather, in contrast to traditional “money-burning” models of advertising, an inverted U-shaped relationship often exists between SMM spending and quality.

We consider a stylized model in which a monopoly <sup>fi</sup>rm whose quality is either high, midtier, or low decides the optimal level of SMM spending. Social media marketing has two roles: increasing awareness and revealing the true quality.<sup>3</sup> Consumers are heterogeneous in product awareness (e.g., whether they know the product exists). Firms have private information about their product quality, which is partially revealed by social media activity. The extent of quality revelation is governed by the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s own spending on social media marketing and the external information signal that comes from social interactions and usergenerated content. Consumers make strategic inferences about the quality of the <sup>fi</sup>rm based on its level of SMM spending, as well as the information revealed by social media activities. Our results show the existence of a separating equilibrium and a partialpooling equilibrium where the high-/low-quality <sup>fi</sup>rms pool together and spend less on social media marketing than the mid-quality <sup>fi</sup>rm. We also <sup>fi</sup>nd that in the partial-pooling equilibrium, as the fraction of aware consumers increases, the mid-quality <sup>fi</sup>rm spends less on social media marketing, whereas the optimal level of SMM spending <sup>fi</sup>rst decreases and then increases with the precision of the external information signal. By comparing these two equilibria, we show that if the partial-pooling equilibrium survives the intuitive criterion, it Pareto-dominates the separating equilibrium.

Our paper contributes to the literature in three aspects. First, we add to the quality-signaling literature by examining that SMM spending can be used to signal quality in addition to other signaling mechanisms such as price (Desai and Srinivasan 1995), advertising (Milgrom and Roberts 1986), brand extension (Wernerfelt 1988, Moorthy 2012), money-back guarantees (Moorthy and Srinivasan 1995, Soberman 2003), warranties (Lutz 1989), message precision (Anand and Shachar 2007), and performance-based advertising (Feng and Xie 2012). Second, we contribute to the literature on traditional advertising as a signaling device. A unique feature of social media marketing is that, unlike the traditional advertising which broadcasts information in a controlled way, social media marketing facilitates information revelation that improves the quality transparency. Our study is also related to the literature on online word of mouth as a new product information channel (Chen and Xie 2008). Third, our study speaks to social media marketing strategies, an area that continues to receive more attention (Shaw 2018), and shows that <sup>fi</sup>rms should consider both the strategic effects and the informational effects when conducting social media marketing.

## 2. Related Literature

More generally, our paper lies mainly at the nexus of two streams of study: the literature on advertising as a quality signaling device and the literature on informative advertising. We now discuss the essence of each stream of research and how our model contrib utes to the existing research.

## 2.1. Advertising as Signals

One of the dominant paradigms in both economics and marketing literature on the relationship between advertising spending and quality is the Nelson (1974) signaling theory. The theory proposes that consumers cannot verify the quality of experience goods before actual consumption, and thereby the claims in the advertisement for experiential qualities cannot be infor mative to the consumers. However, the fact that <sup>fi</sup>rms with experiential qualities advertise provides the mos important information. Consumers will reason that only high-quality <sup>fi</sup>rms will advertise, because they gain more in the long term than the low-quality <sup>fi</sup>rms. Several papers including Milgrom and Roberts (1986), Kihlstrom and Riordan (1984), and Bagwell and Riordan (1991) formalized Nelson’s signaling theory in various ways. In Kihlstrom and Riordan (1984), the interaction between cost structures and repeated purchases creates an asymmetry in the returns to advertising, generates an equilibrium in which advertising signals quality, and predicts a positive relationship between advertising spending and quality. In thei paper, even without repeated purchases, as long as consumers are well informed about the <sup>fi</sup>rms’ cost structures, such as high-quality <sup>fi</sup>rms requiring a high <sup>fi</sup>xed cost in production, whereas low-quality <sup>fi</sup>rms do not, advertising remains an effective signal of quality as consumers reason that only high-quality <sup>fi</sup>rms could pro<sup>fi</sup>t from advertising. Milgrom and Roberts (1986) allowed both price and noninformative adver tising to serve as signals of quality. Repeated pur chases play a crucial role in this paper. As quality information is revealed after consumption, high-quality <sup>fi</sup>rms will attract more repeated purchases than lowquality <sup>fi</sup>rms. Therefore, although advertising produces a symmetric effect in the introductory phase, high quality <sup>fi</sup>rms are the ones that could recover the costs of advertising from repeated purchases. Bagwell and Riordan (1991) considered the long-run trend of price as a quality signal and demonstrated that as the number of informed consumers increases, the price-signaling dis tortion decreases, which results in a declining price pro<sup>fi</sup>le.

In contrast to these papers, the spending on social media marketing in our study is not purely money burning or dissipative but has a role that goes beyond pure strategic signaling. As in Zhao (2000), it extends the advertising signaling literature by exploring the awareness-raising function of advertising rather than a purely dissipative expense. His paper derives a separating equilibrium in which high-quality <sup>fi</sup>rms actually spend less on advertising than low-quality <sup>fi</sup>rms to make low-quality <sup>fi</sup>rms less willing to mimic.

Conversely, high-quality <sup>fi</sup>rms should raise prices to signal quality, because low-quality <sup>fi</sup>rms have higher pro<sup>fi</sup>t margins. Raising prices would hurt low-quality <sup>fi</sup>rms more and hence is not appealing for them to mimic. Although both Zhao (2000) and our paper explicitly incorporate the awareness-enhancement effect, our paper also takes into account the information disclosure effect from social media marketing, which is the key to render the nonlinear relationship between quality and social media marketing spending.

Horstmann and Moorthy (2003) considered the relationship between advertising spending and quality under the assumption that advertising will improve capacity utilization. Although high-quality <sup>fi</sup>rms have higher pro<sup>fi</sup>t margins, they may not have more excess capacity, which eventually leads to the nonmonotonic returns to advertising. Thus, the relationship between advertising spending and quality could be either decreasing or an inverted U-shaped, depending on the extent of capacity utilization. In our paper, the mechanism of producing such an inverted U-shaped relationship is different, which is because of a combination of the awareness-enhancement and the information-revelation effects.

Besides the extensive research on advertising as signals of quality, a large body of literature investigates other signaling devices, such as brand extension (Wernerfelt 1988, Moorthy 2012), money-back guarantees (Moorthy and Srinivasan 1995, Soberman 2003), warranties (Lutz 1989), message precision (Anand and Shachar 2007), and performance-based advertising (Feng and Xie 2012). Other studies also suggest horizontal product information could be signaled by targeting (Anand and Shachar 2009), whereas products price image could be signaled by the selling cost (Shin 2005). Some of these papers use multivariate signaling (Wilson 1985), in which consumers condition their belief on two observables, such as price and advertising (Milgrom and Roberts 1986), or price and warranty (Lutz 1989).

Our work also contributes to the nascent literature on integrating quality disclosure and signaling, such as Daughety and Reinganum (2008). They analyzed a continuum-of-types model and showed that in the separating equilibrium, <sup>fi</sup>rms with the lowest-quality product will not disclose for any positive disclosure cost, whereas a higher-quality type will need to distort its price in order to signal its true quality. Janssen and Roy (2015) extended this question to a duopoly setting and studied the choice between signaling and disclosure of quality information by competing <sup>fi</sup>rms that have private information about their types. Our study adds to this stream of research, as we study the signaling role of SMM spending in the presence of information revelation through social media content.

## 2.2. Firm Choices and Quality-Information Revelation

Another stream of research looks at informative ad vertising and, more generally, direct information transmission between <sup>fi</sup>rms and consumers or among consumers, for instance, online word of mouth.

Informative advertising literature argues that <sup>fi</sup>rms can inform consumers of the existence and price of their products (Butters 1977, Grossman and Shapiro 1984), or directly communicate the matching information (Anand and Shachar 2007, 2009). Anand and Shachar (2009) argued that <sup>fi</sup>rms prefer to provide partial product match information rather than full match information. Kuksov and Lin (2010) found that high-quality <sup>fi</sup>rms should always provide information resolving quality uncertainty, and low-quality <sup>fi</sup>rms have more incentive to provide information resolving consumers’ quality-preference uncertainty. Mayzlin and Shin (2011) considered a model in which <sup>fi</sup>rms strategically withhold product attribute information in order to encourage consumers to search for information on their own. Although both models have strategic signals and information investment, their paper mainly focuses on endogenizing consumers search decisions, whereas our model attempts to explain social media’s signaling ability in light of its information-revelation effect.

Conversely, consumer-created information such as online word of mouth emerges as an important and more credible source for resolving consumers’ uncertainty about product quality and other product attributes. Chen and Xie (2008) considered word of mouth as an alternative marketing communication channel and argued that seller-created information and consumer-created information could serve as both complements and substitutes to each other. When the consumer base consists of suf<sup>fi</sup>cient novice consumers, these two types of information become substitutes and <sup>fi</sup>rms should reduce the amount of attribute information conveyed by their marketing channels. Kwark et al. (2014, 2017) focused on the differential effects that the information in the quality dimension and <sup>fi</sup>t dimension embedded in the online product reviews has on the competition between two products in a two-level channel structure, and whether the retailer could use the upstream pricing scheme, wholesale or platform, as a strategic tool to bene<sup>fi</sup>t from consumer reviews. Zhang et al. (2018) explicitly modeled the retailer’s decision to induce consumer reviews and found that the retailer’s and the manufacturers’ preferences regarding number of reviews are not always aligned. Our model differs from these models in that we do not directly investigate the dynamics between consumer reviews and seller-created reviews or examine the effects of consumer reviews have competition and welfare in a channel structure but rather focus on the strategic decisions on SMM spending as a signaling device, with the presence of external information sources such as consumer reviews.

A vast number of studies have documented some empirical evidence of the impact of a variety of online user-generated content. For instance, previous studies suggested that online word of mouth, such as product reviews, plays an important role in revealing productrelated information and further in<sup>fl</sup>uences other consumers’ purchase decisions. Consumer reviews have been shown to have an impact on book sales (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006, Forman et al. 2008), music sales (Dhar and Chang 2009), movie box of<sup>fi</sup>ce revenue (Liu 2006, Duan et al. 2008), video game sales (Zhu and Zhang 2010), TV ratings (Godes and Mayzlin 2004), and stock performance (Tirunillai and Tellis 2012). However, most of this empirical evidence falls short of showing the pro<sup>fi</sup>tability of social media marketing, with only a few recent studies starting to capture the returns of SMM spending. Villanueva et al. (2008) compared two channels of customers acquisition, marketing investment and the word-of-mouth (WOM) process, and found that consumers acquired through word of mouth generate more long-term value to the <sup>fi</sup>rm than those acquired through traditional marketing channels. Trusov et al. (2009) also demonstrated a longer carryover effect for WOM referrals compared with traditional marketing vehicles. Another study by Goh et al. (2013) assessed the impact of both user-generated content and marketer-generated content on consumers’ purchase decisions, and further suggested that social media content affects consumer purchase behavior through embedded information and persuasion.

In summary, our model contributes to the literature of signaling for quality by proposing that SMM spending serves as a new signaling device, highlighting the existence of exogenous information and considering two additional roles of social media: awareness enhancement and information revelation.

In our case, social media marketing can serve the purpose of raising awareness of the product among unaware consumers. Following the information asymmetry literature (Akerlof 1978), consumers are not fully informed about the product’s quality before the purchase. In our model, <sup>fi</sup>rms communicate product quality to consumers through two channels: (i) quality information revelation through social media activities or (ii) signaling of quality via <sup>fi</sup>rms’ actions on SMM spending and pricing that in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumers’ beliefs about quality. In the separating equilibrium, consumers can infer the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s quality based on the SMM spending and prices that the <sup>fi</sup>rm chooses. In the partial-pooling equilibrium, consumers can only infer the quality of the <sup>fi</sup>rm that separates itself from the other two types. For those types that pool together, consumers’ posterior beliefs will be updated based on the external information signals. In the pooling equilibrium, because the high-quality, the mid-quality, and the low-quality <sup>fi</sup>rms will make the same actions on social media marketing spending and pricing, consumers are not able to update their earlier beliefs about the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s quality based on <sup>fi</sup>rms’ SMM spending and pricing. Consumers’ posterior beliefs again rely on the information revealed through social media.

## 5.2. Two-Period Model

In this section, we extend our model to a two-period one. In the two-period model, a unit mass of customers enters the market at the beginning of period one and lives for two periods. As discussed earlier, a monopoly <sup>fi</sup>rm carries a product of quality unknown to the consumers. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst period, the <sup>fi</sup>rm <sup>fi</sup>rst learns about its own type and makes decisions on the SMM spending and pricing that will remain the same in the second period. Among the aware consumers, they receive an external information signal $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , online WOM), the precision of which will be affected by the amount of SMM spending. Consumers also observe the <sup>fi</sup>rms’ spending in SMM and pricing, and in turn they form an expectation of the product quality. The best price that the <sup>fi</sup>rm can set is equal to consumers’ reservation value. What’s different in the two-period model is that in period two, the true quality is revealed to consumers who purchased the product in the <sup>fi</sup>rst period. Therefore, if the price is higher than their willingness to pay after the true quality is revealed, some consumers will choose not to buy anymore in period two. Each customer consumes one product at most in each period, and we assume all <sup>fi</sup>rms’ decisions are made in period one. The separating equilibrium remains the same in that the true quality can be correctly inferred from the SMM spending in period 1, hich leads to no discrepancy between the expected quality and the true quality. We also obtain a partial-pooling equilibrium similar to that in Proposition 4 (detailed proof as shown in Online Appendix E).

## 5.3. Noisy Signaling

Although the notion that consumers can perfectly observe quality signals such as advertising spending has been widely accepted in the signaling literature, such an assumption seems somewhat strong in most real-world applications and in particular in our setting. The reasons are multifold. First, SMM spending is typically part of a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s overall marketing expenses, which can be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to clearly observe or estimate from the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s disclosed <sup>fi</sup>nancial information. Consumers may then turn to social media activities to infer how much the <sup>fi</sup>rm spends on SMM. Second, much social media marketing takes place over various social media platforms, and not everyone is “tuned $\mathrm { i n } ^ { \prime \prime }$ to those marketing activities. Third, different <sup>fi</sup>rms may have varying preferences toward those social media platforms, resulting in differences in their allocation of resources. It may also cost differently to conduct marketing activities on those social media platforms. In light of those reasons, it seems reasonable to assume that consumers cannot tell how much <sup>fi</sup>rms spend on SMM exactly. Prior literature on noisy signaling has been relatively underexplored. Some works suggest that high levels of noise force high-quality <sup>fi</sup>rms to choose high signal cost, and the separating equilibrium may cease to exist when the noise is suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently large, whereas the pooling equilibrium always exists (de Haan et al. 2011). ## 6. Discussion and Conclusion

Firms increasingly use social media marketing to establish a social presence, engage with brands’ fans, help shape their customers’ experiences, and even leverage their voices for greater marketing impact (Lipsman et al. 2012). Although social media marketing has increasingly become a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s most important strategic marketing investment, its corporate evaluation is still measurement heavy rather than strategic. Online views, likes, retweets, and other measures of engagement make up the building blocks of social media marketing’s return on investment (ROI). However, these measurements provide little or no strategic guidance for the broader decisions about whether to spend on social media marketing and encourage social media activity proactively. Understanding the strategic impact of SMM spending is essential in this fast-growing trilliondollar industry.

Our model provides a <sup>fi</sup>rst step toward framing SMM spending as a strategic investment. We recognize that social media marketing, although capable of increasing consumer awareness and improving the realized perceptions of a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s true quality, also has strategic signaling effects. To make our model realistic, we included features that re<sup>fl</sup>ect the ground reality: Firms do not have complete control over social media activities about their brands but perpetually face wildcard information revelation through sites such as TripAdvisor and Yelp.

The results that we highlight are stark: The midquality <sup>fi</sup>rm makes the largest investment in SMM spending, whereas the high- and low-quality <sup>fi</sup>rms pool at lower spending levels. The explanation is that the high-quality <sup>fi</sup>rm receives suf<sup>fi</sup>cient quality transparency from background social media activity and, given this higher prior, obtains a suf<sup>fi</sup>cient payoff from pooling with the low-quality <sup>fi</sup>rm. The lowquality <sup>fi</sup>rm avoids actively embracing social media marketing because the quality transparency is broadly detrimental. The mid-quality <sup>fi</sup>rm, on the other hand, is “just right” (much like Goldilocks’ eventual choices); the tradeoff balances in a way that causes the <sup>fi</sup>rm to invest heavily in SMM spending, which separates it from the lower-quality <sup>fi</sup>rm. Our results may provide the <sup>fi</sup>rst theoretical explanation for why the lower SMM spending of high-quality brands such as Apple compared with their midtier competitors may be strategically sensible, and we also seek to provide a framework for a more informed social media strategy.

We contribute to the literature on the signaling role of traditional advertising. Typically, this literature assumes advertising is either purely dissipative or can only increase consumers’ awareness of the existence of the product. Other studies, such as Kwark et al. (2014, 2017) and Zhang et al. (2018), focused on the informational value of online WOM. Our study uni<sup>fi</sup>es these two streams of research by incorporating both functionalities, raising awareness and revealing information, into one marketing instrument. As a result, we <sup>fi</sup>nd a partial-pooling equilibrium in which a nonlinear relationship between quality and SMM spending exists, and it dominates the separating equilibrium. We also document that in such a partial-pooling equilibrium, the mid-quality <sup>fi</sup>rm’s optimal SMM spending decreases as more consumers are aware of the product at the beginning. In addition, it <sup>fi</sup>rst decreases and then increases as the precision of the external information signal increases.

As discussed earlier, the higher precision of the external information signal implies that with a higher likelihood, consumers are certain about the true quality, which could be a result of a relatively low cost in information search because of the characteristics of the product or the information environment. An example of the former is search goods (Nelson 1970), which are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as those goods dominated by search attributes for which full information can be acquired prior to purchase. By contrast, experience goods are goods for which information search is more costly/dif<sup>fi</sup>cult than direct product experience. On average, consumers are more likely to be certain about the product quality of a search good than about that of an experience good, based on product descriptions, consumer reviews, and so on. Moreover, the information environment is another determinant of the level of informativeness among consumers. Some external information sources could reveal the quality information (i.e., third-party expert reviews) to various degrees. A more precise information signal or an information signal that could reach more consumers means a higher level of λ. For instance, a mainstream product may have more information about the product quality out there, compared with a niche product. Similarly, consumers may have varying amount of product information for products at different development stages (e.g., mature versus new products). As the precision of the external information signal varies across products and different information environments, we have the different directions of $\partial s _ { M } ^ { * } / \partial \lambda$

Our model captures key characteristics unique to social media in a uni<sup>fi</sup>ed economic model. It differs from previous work explaining the nonmonotonic relationship between advertising and product quality, such as Horstmann and Moorthy (2003). Horstmann and Moorthy (2003) found that the advertising spending of mid-quality restaurants in New York is more than the spending of both the high-quality and lowquality restaurants. Kirmani (1990) demonstrated that advertising expenditures had a nonmonotonic relationship with perceived brand quality in an experiment of a hypothetical shoe advertisement. Homer (1995) replicated the <sup>fi</sup>ndings that extremely high levels of costs were associated with lower quality. Kirmani (1997) proposed that consumers may perceive advertising expenditures as excessive and doubt the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s con<sup>fi</sup>dence in its product quality when the level of advertising repetition for an unfamiliar brand is too high. A similar inverted U-shaped relationship was also found in Feltovich et al. (2002), in which a job seek er decides whether to reveal his grades to the interviewer who also receives additional recommendation letters for this job seeker. We also investigate three types of message senders, which in our case are the <sup>fi</sup>rms.

Our study has several limitations. First, for certain product categories, the information contained in existing social media sources could reduce consumers uncertainty in the way we discussed in this paper. However, for other product categories such as cre dence products,<sup>20</sup> social media may just add more noise to consumers’ decision-making process, in which case, <sup>fi</sup>rms’ spending on social media marketing may not necessarily bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the information-revelation effect. Second, the simplifying assumption that consumers have homogeneous preferences in quality can be relaxed in future studies. Allowing the possibility that some consumer segments may prefer the high quality product more than other consumer segments would be an interesting extension. Because consumers are partitioned, and social media is technically capable of identifying relevant segments, it may provide a strong impetus for <sup>fi</sup>rms to invest in social media marketing to improve performance through targeted advertising. Third, our analysis has not addressed issues such as competition. In practice, competition may in fact drive social media marketing efforts. For instance, on TripAdvisor, hotels ranging from major chain brands to small independent hotels have all designated personnel to respond to customer feedback and resolve consumers’ complaints. Last, we assume that consumers could observe <sup>fi</sup>rms’ social media marketing spending, which in reality might not always hold. Another possible scenario that we do not consider is that <sup>fi</sup>rms may disguise themselves and pose as consumers to promote their inferior products by providing fake reviews. We do not study these scenarios in our paper, but they would be interesting directions for our future work.
