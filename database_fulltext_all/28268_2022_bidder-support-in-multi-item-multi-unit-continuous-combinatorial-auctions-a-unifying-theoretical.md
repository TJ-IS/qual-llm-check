# Bidder Support in Multi-item Multi-unit Continuous Combinatorial Auctions: A Unifying Theoretical Framework

Abstract. Despite known advantages of combinatorial auctions, wide adoption of this allocation mechanism, especially in consumer-oriented marketplaces, is limited partially by the lack of effective bidder support information that can assist bidders to make bidding decisions. In this paper, we study the bidder support problem for general multi-item multiunit (MIMU) combinatorial auctions, where multiple heterogeneous items are being auctioned and multiple homogeneous units are available for each item. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we consider continuous MIMU auctions, which impose minimal restrictions on bidding activities, thereby reducing the complexity of participation. Two prevalent bidding languages: OR bidding and XOR bidding, are discussed separately. For MIMU auctions with XOR bids, we derive theoretical results to calculate important bidder support metrics. We further demonstrate that bidder support results for MIMU auctions with OR bids can be de rived directly from those with XOR bids, by viewing OR bids as XOR bids with each bid submitted by a unique bidder. Consequently, we establish MIMU auctions with XOR bids as the most general case, and unify the theoretical insights on bidder support problem for different bidding languages as well as different special cases of general MIMU auctions, namely single-item multi-unit (SIMU) auctions and multi-item single-unit (MISU) auctions. The derived theoretical results lead to algorithmic procedures that are capable of providing bidder support information ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently in practice, and that outperform the commonly used integer programming approach. Theoretical insights of the general MIMU auctions also extend to auctions with additional bidding constraints, including batch-based combinatorial auctions, hierarchical combinatorial auctions, and combinatorial reverse auctions.

## 1. Introduction

Combinatorial auctions, as a market mechanism to allocate multiple assets, have received increasing attention from both researchers and practitioners. In a combinatorial auction, bidders can place bids both on items individually and on bundles of items. It can lead to desirable allocative ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency when the values of items being auctioned exhibit synergies (Banks et al. 1989, de Vries and Vohra 2003) and create signi<sup>fi</sup>cant economic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (Olivares et al. 2012, Kim et al. 2014). Accordingly, there have been several applications of combinatorial auctions to solve real-world allocation problems, including the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) spectrum auctions (Cramton 2013), transportation resource allocations (Rassenti et al. 1982, Brewer and Plott 1996, Caplice 2007), and procurement auctions (Narahari and Dayama 2005, Bichler et al. 2006).

Despite the attractive advantages of the combinatorial auction mechanism, its application in large-scale consumer-oriented markets (e.g., e-commerce platforms such as eBay) is very limited (Sandholm 2002, Bichler et al. 2017), partly because it is highly complex to participate. At least two important factors contribute to the complexity of participation. First, the combinatorial nature of the auction makes it hard for bidders to keep track of auction status. For example, in a typical multi-item combinatorial auction, the number of possible bundles that bidders can bid on increases exponentially with respect to the number of heterogeneous items, and <sup>fi</sup>nding the winning bids is computationally intractable (Rothkopf et al. 1998, Sandholm 1999, Rothkopf 2007). As a result, unless informed by the auctioneer, a bidder may not even know if a particular bid the bidder has placed wins the auction.<sup>1</sup> Moreover, combinatorically combined bids give rise to intricate bidding dynamics. For instance, as an auction progresses and bidders place one or more bids, a bid currently not winning the auction might become winning later, simply by combining with other bids on a complementary set of items. As a result, it is generally dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for bidders to formulate their bids in an effective manner (Adomavicius and Gupta 2005, Bichler et al. 2017). Second, many realworld industry-oriented combinatorial auctions are conducted in an iterative format, with prede<sup>fi</sup>ned rounds of bidding and specialized activity rules (e.g., Ausubel and Milgrom 2002, Ausubel et al. 2005, Bichler et al. 2009, Scheffel et al. 2011, Day and Cramton 2012). These auction designs tend to be restrictive and elaborate, further adding to the participation complexity for regular consumers.

Recognizing the dif<sup>fi</sup>culties of participating in iterative combinatorial auctions, researchers have advocated for continuous combinatorial auctions as more suitable for consumer-oriented markets (Adomavicius et al. 2007, 2012). In a continuous auction, bidders are allowed to join, bid, and leave at any time during the course of the auction, without restrictions regarding the speci<sup>fi</sup>c auction rules (auction formats, activity or bidding rules, etc.). However, to mitigate the participation complexity in such auctions, useful information that supports bidders to make bidding decisions must be provided, without which the auction mechanism may not even be adopted, and the economic outcomes may suffer (Kwasnica et al. 2005; Leskela et al. 2007; Adomavicius et al. 2012, 2013a, b). Moreover, the bidder support information should be provided in a timely fashion (e.g., right after each and every bid, in real time). Otherwise, providing outdated feedback that does not represent current auction status to bidders can mislead their decisions and jeopardize the economic outcomes of the auction.

An important bidder support scheme, which depends only on information from actually submitted bids, is proposed by Adomavicius and Gupta (2005), where three key pieces of bidding-related information can be provided on demand in real time: (1) which bids are currently winning the auction (winner information); (2) how much one needs to bid on a particular bundle to have it winning immediately, that is, if the auction were to stop after this bid (winning level information); and (3) how much one needs to bid on a particular bundle to have a chance of winning it in the future (deadness level information). Previous research on this type of bidding support scheme has primarily focused on two basic and canonical types of combinatorial auctions: (1) multi-item single-unit (MISU) auctions, where multiple heterogeneous items are being auctioned, one unit for each (Adomavicius and Gupta 2005, Petrakis et al. 2013); and (2) single-item multiunit (SIMU) auctions, where multiple homogeneous units of one item are being auctioned (Adomavicius et al. 2019). A more general form of combinatorial auctions is the multi-item multi-unit (MIMU) auction, where multiple heterogeneous items are being auctioned, and multiple units are available for each item. Each bid can cover an arbitrary set of items and arbitrary available number of units for each item. Despite all the existing work on the two basic, canonical types of combinatorial auctions—MISU and SIMU—unifying the two and, thus, providing effective bidder support for the general MIMU auctions has remained a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant challenge, given the fundamental differences between MISU and SIMU auctions in terms of their sources of complexity as discussed in the next section.

In this paper, we study the problem of providing real-time bidder support for the general continuous MIMU auctions. We consider two canonical types of bidding languages, OR bidding and XOR bidding (Nisan 2000). Under OR bidding, multiple bids submitted by one bidder can potentially be winning the auction. Under XOR bidding, as a contrast, at most one bid from a particular bidder can possibly win the auction. The XOR bidding language is fully expressive of any bidder preference (Nisan 2000). We refer to MIMU auctions with OR and XOR bidding as MIMU OR and MIMU-XOR auctions, respectively.

Throughout the paper, we have three important considerations in mind. First, as a common requirement in combinatorial auction literature (Pekec and Rothkopf 2003), partial allocation (or partial ful<sup>fi</sup>llment) is not allowed, that is, bidders either win the entire set of items speci<sup>fi</sup>ed in their bid or nothing. Considering only all-or-nothing bids is what makes the auctions combinatorial (Pekec and Rothkopf 2003), because it enables bidders to truly express synergies in their preferences. Second, we focus on value maximization for the auctioneer, rather than selling all the items.<sup>2</sup> This assumes that the auction participants can dispose of assets freely, again consistent with the general literature (e.g., Parkes 1999, Sandholm et al. 2002). Third, our goal is to design a comprehensive bidder support system for general continuous MIMU auctions, and we make no speci<sup>fi</sup>c assumptions about bidder/auctioneer behaviors (e.g., bidding strategies, bidder/auctioneer private values, reserve prices, etc.). Although these assumptions are often used for understanding the game-theoretic properties (equilibrium bidding strategy, incentive compatibility, etc.) of specialized instances of MIMU auction mechanisms (often with additional restrictions on auction formats and activity rules), our goal is to provide objective bidder support information, that is, information that can be calculated based on actual submitted bids.

We summarize the key ideas of our paper in Figure 1. Providing bidder support in a continuous combinatorial auction involves bidders querying about information that helps them make bidding decisions and the auctioneer answering those queries. We decompose the task of bidder support into two parts: keeping track of subauction status as the auction progresses and calculating bidder support information based on up-to-date subauction status. First, using subauctions as the key building blocks of combinatorial auctions, we present a unifying theoretical framework of bidder support for arbitrary combinatorial auctions with OR and XOR bidding. We <sup>fi</sup>rst derive theoretical results of calculating various bidder support information for MIMU-XOR auctions, then show that the results of bidder support for MIMU-OR can be derived from those of MIMU-XOR, and that the results of bidder support for both MISU and SIMU auctions can be derived from the general results of MIMU auctions. Second, our theoretical results naturally give rise to the computational infrastructure (i.e., data structures and algorithmic procedures) that is capable of providing real-time bidder support for practically relevant auction sizes. Our proposed computational infrastructure tracks the status of all subauctions and updates them in a dynamic programming fashion as new bids arrive, after which arbitrary bidder support queries can be answered ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently. We also conduct benchmarking analyses that compare the computational performance of our implementation against an alternative strategy, where the values and winning bids of subauctions needed to answer bidder queries are computed on the <sup>fl</sup>y using integer programming (IP), a common approach for winner determination in combinatorial auctions. Our implementation demonstrates superior performance under realistic settings of bidder engagement (i.e., query frequency). In addition to these two main results, we discuss how the theoretical insights of the genera MIMU auctions extend to other variations, namely the batch-based MIMU auctions and the hierarchical MIMU auctions, both of which impose additional bidding constraints. Finally, we consider combinatorial reverse auctions, where multiple sellers bid for the contract to sell to a single buyer. Despite some key differences in the auction format, we show that our results on forward MIMU auctions also readily extend to reverse MIMU auctions.

The primary contribution of our work is solving the bidder support problem for continuous MIMU auctions, that is, deriving the theoretical results and designing ef<sup>fi</sup>cient computational infrastructure to compute the bidder support information. Based on our solutions, we are able to unify the theoretical insights of SIMU and MISU auctions under the general framework of MIMU auctions. In other words, our solutions uncover fundamental understanding of the bidder support problem in continuous MIMU auctions, from which the bidder support solutions for SIMU and MISU auctions can be readily derived. From a practical perspective, our proposed approach can support bidders in MIMU auctions to make more informed bidding decisions, and can contribute toward the design of automated bidding systems for complex combinatorial auction environments.

## 2. Related Literature

Our work is related to two streams of previous research on MIMU auctions: (1) the design of ef<sup>fi</sup>cient algorithms to solve the winner determination problem, and (2) the theoretical analyses of bidder support problem in basic cases of MIMU auctions, namely the MISU and SIMU auctions.

Winner determination in MIMU auctions is known to be NP-complete (Sandholm et al. 2002). Nonetheless, various search algorithms have been developed to <sup>fi</sup>nd winning bids in these auctions. One typical class of algorithms relies on branch-and-bound search, typically embedded as part of an integer programming formulation, which systematically examines feasible allocations to discover the optimal, valuemaximizing allocation (e.g., Gonen and Lehmann 2000, Leyton-Brown et al. 2000, Sandholm and Suri 2003, Sandholm et al. 2005, Xia et al. 2005). Although these algorithms have been initially designed for MIMU-OR auctions, they can be adapted to allow for XOR bids, by adding “dummy goods” (Leyton-Brown et al. 2000), that is, single-unit items that do not actually exist. Adding a dummy item to two bids can force only one of them to end up in the winning allocation, because the dummy item can only be allocated once. This is also known as the OR\* bidding language (Nisan 2000, 2006).

Although being computationally ef<sup>fi</sup>cient and useful, these winner determination algorithms generally do not provide suf<sup>fi</sup>cient insights regarding how to calculate bidder support metrics, such as winning levels and deadness levels. To do so, one needs to obtain a deeper theoretical understanding of the auction dynamics. As a primary objective of our paper, we provide comprehensive theoretical analyses of continuous MIMU auctions to solve this problem.

Meanwhile, previous research on the bidder support problem in MISU and SIMU auctions informs our theoretical analyses. Adomavicius and Gupta (2005) studied the bidder support problem in MISU auctions with OR bidding (MISU-OR) and formally proposed the winning levels and deadness levels as two bidder support metrics to guide bidder decisions. Petrakis et al. (2013) studied MISU auctions with XOR bidding (MISU-XOR). Although their primary goal was to discuss game-theoretic properties of such auctions rather than to provide real-time bidder support, they derived theoretical results to calculate winning levels and deadness levels. Notably, they showed that the calculation of deadness levels is even harder (Π<sup>P</sup>-complete) than the calculation of winning levels (NP-hard). Most recently, Adomavicius et al. (2019) examined SIMU auctions with both OR and XOR bidding (SIMU-OR and SIMU-XOR). Both winning and deadness levels represent natural and informative bounds for bidding, and deadness levels (as ask prices in ascending MISU-XOR auctions) can lead to an ex post equilibrium (Petrakis et al. 2013). The usefulness of these two metrics in supporting bidding decisions has also been validated in experimental studies (e.g., Adomavicius et al. 2012).

A central and recurrent theme underlying bidder support in MISU and SIMU auctions is the concept of subauctions (Adomavicius and Gupta 2005). As an example, consider a MISU auction of three distinct items $\{ A , { \bar { B } } , C \}$ . Here, a subauction is de<sup>fi</sup>ned by a unique set of items, say $\{ A , B \}$ , that includes all bids placed on bundle $\{ A , { \dot { B } } \}$ and all its subsets. Subauctions act as the building blocks of the entire auction, which can be used to characterize the status of bids (e.g., winning or dead) and to calculate the winning and deadness levels. The subauction concept has proven to be critical in understanding the dynamics of both MISU and SIMU auctions. Accordingly, the computational design to provide bidder support works by tracking subauction information. In this paper, we show that the subauction concept continues to be instrumental for understanding auction dynamics and bidder support in the general MIMU auctions as well.

Although this may appear easy at the outset, given that SIMU and MISU auctions are special cases of MIMU auctions, it is nontrivial to achieve and we are not aware of any prior work that explicitly discussed it. Integrating insights from SIMU and MISU auctions, we see both differences and similarities between them. On one hand, SIMU and MISU are fundamentally different types of auctions. In particular, a bundle in SIMU auctions is an integer quantity, whereas a bundle in MISU auctions is a set of distinct items, which leads to complex combinatorial problems of very different kinds. For example, whereas winner determination in SIMU-OR auctions is equivalent to knapsack, in MISU-OR it is equivalent to set-packing, both NP-hard problems (Vazirani 2003). On the other hand, MISU and SIMU auctions also share similarities at an abstract level. In particular, leveraging concepts such as subauction, we realize that the principles for identifying winning bids and calculating winning levels for speci<sup>fi</sup>c bundles are closely related for SIMU and MISU auctions. Therefore, deriving a unifying theoretical framework requires conceptual reexamination of these auctions.

In general, every existing bid at a given auction state belongs to one of two disjoint sets: the set of bids that can win the auction and the set of bids that cannot possibly win the auction. ## 3.3. Calculating Winning and Deadness Levels of Bids in MIMU-XOR Auctions

In this section, we discuss the relationships between subauction value and bid status (winning, live, or dead) in order to calculate winning and deadness levels and provide bidder support information.

## 4.1. OR Bids as a Type of XOR Bids

We use the same notations from MIMU-XOR auctions to represent the auction size and arbitrary bids in MIMU-OR auctions. ## 4.3. Unifying Theoretical Results of MIMU Auctions

Based on the previous theoretical results, we summarize a few important similarities and differences between MIMU-XOR and MIMU-OR auctions as the following observations.

Observation 1. In both MIMU-XOR and MIMU-OR auctions, a new bid wins a subauction if and only if it combines with winning from the complementary subauction to outbid (subject to tie breaking) the pre vious winning allocation of the focal subauction.

Observation 2. In both MIMU-XOR and MIMU-OR auctions, a bid being live is equivalent to winning at least one special subauction, determined by both the bid’s span and the viable coalition set. In a MIMU-OR auction, a live bid with span $\vec { x }$ has to be winning at least one subauction of span $\vec { u } .$ , where ${ \vec { u } } \geq { \vec { x } }$ . In a MIMU-XOR auction, a live bid with span $\vec { x }$ and bidder $i ^ { * }$ has to be winning at least one subauction of $\vec { x }$ items and $| \vec { U } - \vec { x } |$ other bidders removed.

Observation 3. Given a speci<sup>fi</sup>c span, the winning level and deadness level in a MIMU-XOR auction can be different for different bidders, whereas in a MIMU-OR auction they are the same for all bidders.

The results for MIMU-OR and MIMU-XOR auctions enable us to provide a unifying theoretical framework, connecting MIMU auctions with the other two canonical types of combinatorial auctions, namely SIMU auctions (Adomavicius et al. 2019) and MISU auctions (Adomavicius and Gupta 2005, Petrakis et al. 2013). The key connection is the fact that SIMU/MISU auctions are simpli<sup>fi</sup>ed MIMU auctions. A MIMU auction of size $\vec { U } = \mathsf { \hat { ( } } u _ { 1 } , u _ { 2 } , \ldots , u _ { M } )$ reduces to a SIMU auction with the constraint $M = 1 ~ ( { \mathrm { i . e . } }$ , there is a single item with $u _ { 1 }$ available units), and reduces to a MISU auction with the constraint $\forall j \in \{ 1 , 2 , \ldots , M \} , u _ { j } = 1$ $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ there is a single unit available for each distinct item). Accordingly, based on these transformations in auction representation, one can map the theoretical results regarding winner determination and bidder support from MIMU auctions to SIMU and MISU auctions. Furthermore, because theoretical results of MIMU-OR auctions can be derived from those of MIMU-XOR auctions, the latter is established as a more general form of combinatorial auction. We visualize the mapping from MIMU auctions to SIMU/ MISU auctions for OR and XOR bidding in Figure $^ { 2 , }$ and summarize the key theoretical results of all auc tion types in online Appendix C.

Finally, we note that there can be other ways to unify the theoretical results of MIMU auctions. For example, prior literature on bidding languages shows that XOR bids can also be viewed as a special case of OR bids with added dummy items to enforce the XOR bidding constraints, known as the OR\* bids (Nisan 2000, 2006). However, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the theoretical results of bidder support (especially deadness level calculation) in MIMU-OR auctions are not easily applicable to MIMU-XOR implementation with $\mathrm { O R ^ { * } }$ bids. To demonstrate this, we walk through an illustration in online Appendix J. Instead, by treating OR bids as a special case of XOR bids, we show that one can derive bidder support solutions, including not only winner determination but also winning and deadness level calculations, for MIMU-OR auctions directly from those of MIMU-XOR auctions.

## 5. Implementing Bidder Support in MIMU Auctions

The implementation of bidder support for MIMU auctions with both OR and XOR bidding languages is informed directly by the theoretical results in Sections 3 and 4. Importantly, our implementation has three key design objectives in mind: (1) Incremental winner/value update, that is, the implementation should keep track of subauction values and winners at any auction state, and update such information after the arrival of a new bid. This is necessary because subauction winners are needed to determine winners of the entire auction (as well as live bids), and subauction values are needed to calculate winning and deadness level information for any span. (2) Winner determination means that the implementation should be able to retrieve the winning bids (subject to tie breaking) of any subauction (including the entire auction) at any state. (3) Finally, on-demand winning/deadness level calculation, which means the implementation should be able to calculate arbitrary winning and deadness levels at the demand of bidders.

In this section, we outline the data structures and algorithms that can achieve these design objectives, and conduct simulation experiments to demonstrate the computational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency performance of our implementation. For expositional simplicity, we start by discussing the implementation for MIMU-OR auctions, followed by the more complex case of MIMU-XOR auctions.

## 5.3. Performance Benchmarking

A key characteristic of our bidder support implementation strategy is that it tracks the values and winning bids of all subauctions and updates them in a dynamic programming fashion as the auction progresses. We solved the integer programs using IBM CPLEX in the same hardware environment as the previous simulation experiments.<sup>8</sup>

Given our goal to provide bidder support information in continuous MIMU auctions, we conducted benchmarking analyses to compare the running time of our approach against that of IP to calculate all the winning levels and deadness levels that can be queried by auction participants. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, these include the winning and deadness levels of all possible spans in a MIMU-OR auction, or all possible spans for each individual bidder in a MIMU-XOR auction. Having all winning and deadness levels calculated allows provision of bidder support for arbitrary queries from bidders and can also facilitate possible development of automated bidding systems that select advantageous (e.g., surplus-maximizing) bundles for bidders.

For our approach, incremental update of subauctions is a prerequisite for calculating any bidder support information, and we therefore include the time of incremental update as a part of providing bidder support. The $\mathrm { I P }$ approach, in contrast, computes each winning or deadness level by solving for the values of needed subauctions on the <sup>fl</sup>y, without storing all of those values in order to avoid incurring exponential storage. For instance, to compute the winning level of x<sup>→</sup> in a MIMU-OR auction, the IP approach would follow our theoretical results and compute the values of subauctions $\vec { U }$ and $\vec { U } - \vec { x }$ . Note that we store the value of U $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ the entire auction) after it is <sup>fi</sup>rst computed, because it is used in every winning level calculation. We simulated a total of 30 live bids that arrive sequentially and reported the average running time across these bids.<sup>9</sup> As in the previous experiments, we simulated both an averagecase scenario with random live bids and a worst-case scenario with singleton live bids. IP is especially slow in calculating deadness levels, as the values of many subauctions are needed. The IP approach again takes substantially longer than our approach to calculate all bidder support information. In general, performance of the IP approach greatly depends on the frequency of bidder support queries, that is, on the informational needs of bidders during the auction. Importantly, even if only a smal fraction of all winning or deadness levels are of interest to bidders, solving a large (often exponential) number of subauctions inevitably renders impractica running time for IP. In other words, the IP approach can potentially outperform our approach when the auction size is relatively large and query frequency is very low. For instance, in a MIMU-OR auction with four items, 50 units per item, and 30 existing bids, the incremental update of all subauctions after a new singleton live bid takes about 260 milliseconds on average with our approach. In contrast, the IP approach on the same computer takes around 30 milliseconds to solve for the value of one subauction. It can therefore be faster than our approach if (i) the bidders care only about winner determination of the entire auction, or (ii) there are only a very small number of queries (e.g., no more than seven winning level queries among al bidders and not even one deadness level query for a singleton bid). As the number of queries grows, our approach quickly outperforms the IP approach. In addition, as the auction progresses, although the computational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of our approach is minimally affected (as it only has to deal with the latest bid for its incremental update), the IP approach would become increasingly slower because all historical bids need to be accounted for in each integer program. The IP approach could certainly be improved by storing the values of subauctions and, as a new bid arrives, incrementally update only the affected subauctions. However, this would again require exponential storage space. It might be possible to design a caching mechanism that strategically stores the values of a small number of frequently accessed subauctions. In addition, it may also be possible to design speci<sup>fi</sup>c formulations of IP that directly and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently compute the bidder support information. Both design problems represent interesting directions for future research. Overall, by tracking and updating all subauctions as auctions progress, our implementation is uniquely suitable for the purpose of providing comprehensive bidder support in continuous MIMU auctions.

## 6. Two Special Cases of General MIMU Auctions

MIMU auction in its full generality can be very complex. For a MIMU auction of size $\dot { U } = ( u _ { 1 } , u _ { 2 } , \dots , u _ { M } )$ the total possible number of bundles that bidders can bid on is <sup>S</sup> 1, which is $\left( u + 1 \right) ^ { M } - 1$ when $u _ { j } = u$ for all $j .$ In reality, bidders may have specialized preference structures and may not be interested in bidding on all possible bundles (Rothkopf et al. 1998). For example, in auctions of real estate properties, the assets may be geographically clustered, and bidders generally do not place bids on combinations involving assets across different clusters. In this section, we discuss two such special cases, namely the batch-based MIMU auctions and hierarchical MIMU auctions. We demonstrate that our solutions to the bidder support problems of general MIMU auctions can be straightforwardly extended to these special cases, and readily give rise to ef<sup>fi</sup>cient implementations. For illustration purposes and expositional simplicity, we consider the special cases under OR bids and focus on highlighting their unique characteristics, though the results under XOR bids can be analogously derived.

## 6.1. Batch-Based MIMU Auctions

Rothkopf et al. (1998) discussed a type of MISU auction with cardinality constraints, where the permitted bids only include singletons and bundles whose cardinality exceed a certain threshold. The cardinality threshold is often large (e.g., half of the cardinality of the entire auction), and as a result, few large bids can be winning, making the winner determination problem computationally tractable. Here, we consider a different variation of the cardinality constraint, particularly relevant in industrial procurement contexts, which yields interesting theoretical results and practical insights.

Consider a manufacturer that makes several different types of construction materials. These materials are the same in size and weight, but different in functionalities. Many identical units are produced for each type of material. However, these materials can only be shipped via containers of a <sup>fi</sup>xed capacity. In other words, say each container has a capacity of 500, then it can hold either 500 units of one type of material, or a mixture of multiple types of materials adding up to 500 units. Suppose the manufacturer wants to use a combinatorial auction mechanism to sell these con struction materials. Because of this batch-based ful<sup>fi</sup>llment constraint, each permitted bid must cover 500 units of materials (of one or more types), or multiples of 500 units. We refer to such an auction with size constraints on permitted bids as a batch-based MIMU auction, denoted as $\mathrm { { M M U } _ { B } . }$ Formally, a ${ \mathrm { M M U } } _ { \mathrm { B } }$ auction with OR bidding $( \mathrm { M I M U _ { B } \mathrm { - O R } ) }$ is characterized by two parameters, $\vec { U } = \left( u _ { 1 } , u _ { 2 } , \ldots , u _ { M } \right)$ and Ω, where $\vec { U }$ is auction size and Ω is a <sup>fi</sup>xed constant representing the batch constraint. Any permitted bid with span vector $\vec { x } ( \vec { 0 } < \vec { x } \le \vec { U } )$ must satisfy $\begin{array} { r } { | \vec { x } | = \sum _ { j = 1 } ^ { M } x _ { j } = r \Omega } \end{array}$ with $r \in \mathbb { N } ^ { + }$ . In other words, a permitted bid must have cardinality that is equal to multiples of Ω. We set $\vert \vec { U } \vert \equiv 0 \ ( \mathrm { m o d } \ \Omega )$ , that is, $\vec { U }$ is permitted itself, as a natural choice of auction size.

Denote the set of all permitted spans that bidders can bid on as S. The key characteristic of such MIM-$\mathrm { U } _ { \mathrm { B } ^ { - } } \mathrm { O R }$ auction is that the total number of permitted spans is roughly $1 / \Omega$ of all possible spans. We formalize it in Theorem F.1 and Corollary F.1 in online $\mathrm { A p \mathrm { - } }$ pendix F. Moreover, the set of all permitted spans, $S ,$ de<sup>fi</sup>nes all subauctions that we need to keep track of in order to provide real-time bidder support. This is because incremental value update and winning/deadness level calculations for a given permitted span all involve the value of the complementary span, which must also be permitted. More formally, if $\vec { x } , \vec { y } \in S ,$ , and $\vec { x } \le \vec { y }$ , then the complementary span $\dot { \vec { y } } - \vec { x } \in S$ . In other words, all subauctions that are relevant in providing bidder support are already included in S.

This property of $\mathrm { M I M U _ { B } \mathrm { - } \dot { O } R }$ auctions can signi<sup>fi</sup>- cantly reduce the computational burden of providing real-time bidder support, especially when the <sup>fi</sup>xed cardinality constraint Ω is large. Bidder support for $\mathrm { M I M U _ { B } \mathrm { - } \dot { O R } }$ auctions can be readily implemented based on the data structures and algorithms of MIMU-OR auctions. We discuss the implementation details and report a simulation experiment in online Appendix F. In general, because the cardinality constraints reduced the number of subauctions involved in incremental value update, we are able to handle much larger auction sizes than the ones simulated for general MIMU-OR auctions. In addition, increasing Ω can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly reduce the total number of permitted spans and, consequently, result in much faster incremental updates.

## 6.2. Hierarchical MIMU Auctions

Another important special type of combinatorial auction is the hierarchical combinatorial auction (Rothkopf et al. 1998, Goeree and Holt 2010), where permitted bundles form a hierarchical structure $( \mathrm { e . g . , }$ a tree structure). Hierarchical combinatorial auctions have straightforward practical relevance. For example, this mechanism has been used by the U.S. FCC to sell the 700 MHz spectrum licenses, where the permitted bundles included individual licenses, regional licenses, and national licenses (Goeree and Holt 2010). These permitted bundles form a hierarchy, with individual licenses in the bottom layer, regional licenses in the middle layer, and national licenses in the top layer. Because of the hierarchical structure, winner determination can be solved in polynomial time (Rothkopf et al. 1998).

We consider hierarchical MIMU (denoted as MIM-$\mathrm { U _ { H } } )$ auctions with the following setup. First, there is a hierarchy of permitted packages that bidders can bid on, which form a tree structure. The bottom layer consists of singletons, that is, packages of single distinct item, and the top layer consists of the package of all items. Second, any permitted bid can involve one or more copies of a permitted package. Equivalently, one can think of a nonsingleton permitted package as a combined item, and bidders can bid for one or more units of it. Third, we restrict our discussion to OR bidding $( \mathrm { M I M U _ { H } \mathrm { - } O R ) }$ , although the results can be extended to XOR bidding as well.

Formally, suppose a $\bar { \mathrm { M I M U } } _ { \mathrm { H } } – \mathrm { O R }$ auction has size $\dot { U } = \left( u _ { 1 } , u _ { 2 } , \dots , u _ { M } \right)$ and $\forall j \in \{ 1 , \ldots , M \} , \ u _ { j } = u _ { \prime }$ , that is, there is the same number of available units for each item. All permitted packages form a tree of H layers, with layer 1 containing all singletons and layer H containing package $\{ 1 , \ldots , M \}$ . The set of permitted pack ages at layer $h \in \{ 1 , \ldots , H \}$ is denoted as $P ^ { h } = \{ P _ { 1 } ^ { h } , \ldots ,$ $P _ { M _ { h } } ^ { h } \}$ . By de<sup>fi</sup>nition, $M _ { 1 } = M , M _ { H } = 1 , \forall h \in \{ 1 , \dots , H \}$ $\cup _ { j = 1 } ^ { M _ { h } } P _ { j } ^ { h } = \{ 1 , \dots , M \}$ , and $\forall P _ { j _ { 1 } } ^ { h } , P _ { j _ { 2 } } ^ { h } \in P ^ { h } , P _ { j _ { 1 } } ^ { h } \cap P _ { j _ { 2 } } ^ { h } = \emptyset$ . Each package in layer h is always fully contained in one package at layer $h + 1$ . We represent a permitted span vector $\vec { x } = s ( P _ { j } ^ { h } , x )$ as vector with value $x ( 0 < x \leq u )$ on positions speci<sup>fi</sup>ed by $P _ { j } ^ { h }$ and 0 everywhere else. As a result, the value of the complementary span is not readily available. Nonetheless, it turns out that such value can be obtained by decomposing the nonpermitted span into the sum of several permitted spans. We describe the details in online Appendix $G .$ We also report a simulation experiment in online Appendix G to demonstrate the performance. We are again able to handle much larger auctions than in the general MIMU-OR auctions, mainly because the number of permitted spans is much smaller, and there are substantially fewer subauctions to consider, as permitted spans need to cover the same number of units for each item. For any permitted span, only the parent spans in the tree structure need to be considered in an incremental update.

## 7. Reverse MIMU Auctions

So far we have focused on forward auctions, a common auction format where buyers compete with each other to bid for items from a seller. An alternative combinatorial auction format is known as the reverse auction, where multiple sellers compete with each other for the right to sell items to a buyer (Sandholm et al. 2002). For example, in industrial procurement auctions, several suppliers can bid for the contract to sell certain goods (e.g., commodities) or services (e.g., software development capabilities) to the buyer (Moreno and Terwiesch 2014). A key distinction between the two auction formats is that, whereas buyers generally have to raise their bid values to win a forward auction, sellers have to lower their bid values to win a reverse auction. In terms of winner determination, whereas a forward auction can be formulated as a set-packing problem, a reverse auction can be formulated as a set-covering problem (Sandholm et al. 2002). Despite these differences in auction formats, we demonstrate in this section that our results on forward MIMU auctions can be readily extended to reverse MIMU auctions, with several important modi<sup>fi</sup>cations. In the following discussions, we focus on the reverse MIMU-OR (denoted as R-MIMU-OR) auctions as the example to illustrate both the similarities and differences in bidder support between these two auction formats. Note that the XOR bidding constraint can be accounted for by incorporating bidder identity information, in the same way as in forward MIMU-XOR auctions.

Because the auctioneer in a reverse auction is typically the buyer of certain assets, the individual cares about cost minimization (in contrast to value maximization in forward auctions). Accordingly, the value of a bid represents the cost of procuring the items speci-<sup>fi</sup>ed in the bid. In a reverse MIMU-OR auction of size $\vec { U }$ , we adopt the same notation of an arbitrary bid as in a forward MIMU-OR auction, $b = ( \vec { s } , v , t )$ , where $\vec { s }$ and t retain the same meanings of span vector and time as in the forward auctions, but v represents the cost of this bid to the auctioneer. The same concept of auction state that we have discussed for forward auctions is directly applicable here. In particular, we use $B _ { k } = \{ b _ { 1 } , b _ { 2 } , \ldots , { \overset { } { b _ { k } } } \}$ to represent the collection of all bids that have been submitted up to auction state k.

However, the concept of feasible allocation needs to be adjusted to re<sup>fl</sup>ect the procurement aspect of reverse auctions. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, let $\bar { C _ { k } = \{ C \subseteq B _ { k } \} }$ $\begin{array} { r } { \sum _ { b \in C } \vec { s } \left( b \right) \geq \vec { U } \} } \end{array}$ represent the set of all feasible allocations. In other words, a feasible allocation must contain bids that, in combination, can cover all items in the auction. Intuitively, this is because the auctioneer needs to procure at least all needed items.<sup>11</sup>

What is the proper de<sup>fi</sup>nition of subauction in reverse combinatorial auctions? Because of the certain symmetry between the reverse and forward auctions, it might seem natural to de<sup>fi</sup>ne a subauction $[ { \vec { x } } ] ( { \vec { 0 } } < { \vec { x } } \leq { \vec { U } } )$ ) simply as $B _ { k } [ \vec { x } ] = \{ b \in B _ { k } \mid \vec { s } ( b ) \geq \vec { x } \}$ However, this de<sup>fi</sup>nition turns out to be incomplete, as we show in Illustration 6.

In summary, a reverse MIMU-OR auction is different from a forward MIMU-OR auction on two critical aspects. First, whereas the auctioneer aims for value maximization in a forward auction, the individual aims for cost minimization in a reverse auction. Accordingly, all bidder support metrics need to be calculated based on costs of corresponding subauctions. Second, given subauction x<sup>→</sup> , only bids with smaller or equal spans can affect its value in a forward auction. This is not true in a reverse auction, and all bids that have spans with nonempty intersections with $\vec { x }$ can potentially affect its cost. However, after taking these two caveats into account, the comprehensive bidder support results that we have derived for forward MIMU-OR auctions are analogous to the ones for R-MIMU-OR auctions, and their theoretical results share many similarities. For example, for a new bid to win a subauction, it always needs to combine with winning bids from the complementary subauction. Finally, extending the bidder support results from forward MIMU-XOR auctions to reverse MIMU-XOR auctions can be done in an analogous fashion, by accounting for these two aspects, that is, cost minimization and the adjusted de<sup>fi</sup>nition of subauctions.

## 8. Conclusions and Future Work

In this paper, we address the problem of providing realtime bidder support in the general form of continuous multi-item multi-unit (MIMU) auctions, where multiple heterogeneous items are being auctioned and multiple indistinguishable units are available for each item. We consider MIMU auctions with both OR and XOR bid ding languages. By exploiting the concept of subauctions, we derive how relevant subauctions should be updated with respect to a newly submitted bid, and how winning levels and deadness levels should be cal culated based on subauction values. Importantly, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that, for the purpose of proving theoretical results of bidder support, OR bids can be viewed as a type of XOR bids where each bid is submitted by a distinct bid der. Thus, theoretical results for MIMU-OR auctions can be derived from those for MIMU-XOR auctions. Despite such a relationship, we also note a few unique aspects of MIMU-OR auctions. As an example, whereas the maximum possible number of live bids for a MIMU-OR auction equals the number of subauctions, the maximum possible number of live bids is smaller than the number of subauctions in a MIMU-XOR auction.

The theoretical insights also allow us to connect MIMU auctions with two canonical basic cases, that is, single-item multi-unit (SIMU) auctions and multiitem single-unit (MISU) auctions. Based on a particular transformation in auction representation, one can map major results of the bidder support problem for SIMU and MISU auctions directly from those for MIMU auctions. Therefore, this paper presents a unifying framework to understand the bidder support problem in arbitrary combinatorial auctions, with MIMU-XOR being the most general case that can be mapped to MIMU-OR, as well as reduced to two basic cases (SIMU and MISU) with both OR and XOR bidding languages. Given the fundamental differences between SIMU and MISU auctions and between OR and XOR biddings, the mapping relationships we have discovered further advance the understanding of these different types of combinatorial auctions.

The theoretical results also naturally give rise to our computational design. We propose data structures and algorithms to keep track of values and winning bids for all subauctions, in order to determine winners, winning levels, and deadness levels ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently at any state of an auction. We conduct simulation experiments to demonstrate that, even in the computationally worst case where only singleton bids are submitted, our implementation can support real-time bidder feedback for practically relevant auction sizes. Furthermore, by systematically tracking and updating the status of all subauctions, our implementation substantially outperforms the alternative, traditional integer-programming-based approach for winner determination (which computes subauction values on the <sup>fl</sup>y), under realistic settings of bidder engagement (i.e., query frequency). In other words, our approach is uniquely suitable for providing comprehensive bidder support ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently.

The state space of a general MIMU auction can be very large, and not every possible bundle may be of interest to bidders. Accordingly, we discuss two variations of MIMU auctions with package constraints, that is, the batch-based MIMU auctions and the hierarchical MIMU auctions. The theoretical insights we obtained for general MIMU auctions are readily applicable to understand these auctions. Because the number of permitted bundles can be substantially smaller in these auctions than in the general case, they can potentially be conducted at a much larger scale. We believe that, in addition to the general MIMU auctions, these constrained MIMU auctions may comprise promising mechanisms to be applied in large-scale online marketplaces. Finally, we consider the generalization of our results to reverse MIMU auctions, where bidders are bidding for the contracts to sell certain items to the auctioneer. Despite some important distinctions, we show that our main results in forward MIMU auctions can be naturally extended to provide comprehensive bidder support in reverse MIMU auctions. Given the wide application of reverse auction mechanism in industry procurement activities, our results can bene<sup>fi</sup>t the time and economic outcomes of auctions in this scenario.

Several directions are open for future research. First, whereas this study has developed bidder support for the general continuous MIMU auctions without any restrictions on auction formats or activity rules, future work can reexamine our theoretical results for specialized MIMU auction mechanisms, where bidding restrictions may be necessary or advantageous from a game-theoretic perspective. Intuitively, the current winning and deadness levels would become lower bounds for the actual winning and deadness levels in these specialized mechanisms. Second, whereas we have demonstrated the computational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of our implementation over a straightforward, winner-determination-based IP implementation, future research can investigate possibilities for specialized IP formulations that directly and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently compute the bidder support information. Third, one can further generalize the current bidder support framework to be used in combinatorial exchanges, where multiple buyers and multiple sellers coexist in one market (Sandholm et al. 2002). Finally, informed by our work, researchers can work toward building automated bidding systems for MIMU auctions. This likely involves scaling bidder support algorithms to even larger-size auctions as well as accounting for various human factors (e.g., bidder behaviors, strategies, and preferences).
