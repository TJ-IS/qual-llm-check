# Sprint Zeal or Sprint Fatigue? The Benefits and Burdens of Agile ISD Practices Use for Developer Well-Being

Abstract. Despite the predominant view in the agile information systems development (ISD) literature that agile information systems development practices use (AISDPU) has positive rami<sup>fi</sup>cations for ISD teams and projects, previous research has paid considerably less attention to whether and how these practices can be simultaneously beneficial and burden some to individual developers. In addition, much of our knowledge on AISDPU and its effects relies on research into the differences between developers over an extended period of time. Yet, agile ISD practices are typically used on a day-to-day basis and can thus be viewed as an inherently within-person phenomenon, pointing to the need for daily investigations that provide a closer look at the immediate, lived experiences of developers. Drawing on the holistic stress process model, we develop the idea that daily AISDPU can be a source of stressful situational demands with both energizing and depleting effects that may thus help or harm developer well-being. In an experience-sampling study of 131 de velopers who responded to daily surveys spread over 10 workdays, we show that AISDPU can indeed be a double-edged sword: instrumental to developers’ energy resources (by in creasing work engagement) on some days yet detrimental to their energy resources (by increasing depletion) on others—two forces with opposing effects on developer well-being. Importantly, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the divergent effects of AISDPU critically hinge on whether developers appraise it as a challenge or a threat. As a potential antidote, we investigate the moderating role of information technology (IT) mindfulness in developers’ appraisal processes. Our results show that IT mindfulness serves as a facilitator of challenge appraisals and as a buffer against threat appraisals. More broadly, this study challenges the predomi nant view of AISDPU as an inherently good thing and offers meaningful insights into why and when AISDPU improves versus impairs developer well-being.

## Introduction

Over the last few years, information systems development (ISD) projects have increasingly shifted from traditional to agile approaches emphasizing the use of agile ISD practices included in methods such as scrum, extreme programming (XP), and lean development. Underscoring the widespread adoption of agile ISD practices in organizations, recent industry reports <sup>fi</sup>nd that agile methods and practices are pervasively used across industries and throughout the world (Collabnet 2019). According to a global study by the Project Management Institute in 2018, 71% of enterprises now claim to use agile approaches (PMI 2018), and as reported in the Stack Over<sup>fl</sup>ow (2018) developer survey, 85.9% of 101,592 surveyed software developers used agile in their daily work. Consequently, in organizations, agile ISD practices are a highly prevalent phenomenon that substantially shapes developers’ everyday experiences.

The predominant view in information systems (IS) research is that agile information systems development practices use (AISDPU) is largely bene<sup>fi</sup>cial and conducive to project- and team-level outcomes. For example, previous research has shown that AISDPU has a positive effect on software project quality by increasing development speed and decreasing code defect density (Maruping et al. 2009). When compared with nonagile projects, successful agile programs can increase productivity per developer by 27%, reduce launch delays by 30%, and decrease defects in production by a factor of three (Chandrasekaran et al. 2016). In addition, it facilitates effective team processes by fostering team motivation and transparency about customer requirements (Rosenkranz et al. 2013). A few empirical studies conducted at the individual level have also shown that AISDPU increases developers job satisfaction and decreases turnover (Tripp et al. 2016).

However, as our opening quotations illustrate, portraying AISDPU as largely bene<sup>fi</sup>cial may be an oversimpli<sup>fi</sup>cation. Industry reports and recent empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that agile ISD practices can be experienced by developers as a bene<sup>fi</sup>t and burden and that such experiences may <sup>fl</sup>uctuate over short time intervals (Balbes 2017, Tuomivaara et al. 2017). As agile ISD methods and their associated practices signi<sup>fi</sup>- cantly change the structure and environment of software developers’ daily work because of demanding features that emphasize fast turnaround times, responsiveness to change, and individual performance transparency (Baham and Hirschheim 2021), they may have important consequences for developers daily appraisals and stress responses. On one day, agile ISD practices may be experienced as a challenge and “<sup>fi</sup>re developers up.” On another, they may be appraised as a threat and “burn developers out.” If left unchecked, when individuals fail to respond adequately after stressful experiences, the detrimental effects of AISDPU might thus undermine the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and even lead to severe health consequences, such as impaired well-being, burnout, or even coronary heart disease (Wang et al. 2016, Salvagioni et al. 2017), all of which may feed back into developers’ work productivity. Given that agile ISD practices represent a significant organizational investment in developers’ human capital, the consequences of having depleted developers may therefore be profound and far reaching. In light of this impact, examining the potential burdens of AISDPU in conjunction with its bene<sup>fi</sup>ts represents a theoretically intriguing and practically important research endeavor.

Despite the prevalence and complex nature of AISDPU, we highlight two salient, underexplored issues identi<sup>fi</sup>ed from our review of the IS literature (see Table A.1 in Online Appendix A). First and as alluded to, previous IS research has primarily focused its investigations on the bright side of AISDPU for ISD teams and projects. However, although we know much about the instrumental consequences of AISDPU, we know comparatively little about its detrimental effects and potential trade-offs for individual developers, even though recent studies have emphasized the need for deeper insights into the effects of AISDPU on developer well-being (Windeler et al. 2017, Venkatesh et al. 2020). Indeed, although prior work has shown that AISDPU can reduce developers work exhaustion through more even project workload allocations over time (Tuomivaara et al. 2017) and via the promotion of clear and unambiguous role perceptions (Windeler et al. 2017, Venkatesh et al. 2020), we still lack a more comprehensive understanding of how and why AISDPU can have both positive and negative implications for developer well-being. This failure to capture a balanced view of developers AISDPU stress experience may therefore trivialize the potentially adverse health effects on developers.

Second, despite the advances made by recent research, much of our knowledge of AISDPU effects builds from nomothetic studies examining differences among developers in their role demands or their workload perceptions over an extended period of time. Although valuable, such a between-person perspective neither accounts for how AISDPU shapes developers’ momentary experiences in their natural settings nor explains why their stress responses may <sup>fl</sup>uctuate from one day to the other, even though IS scholars have called for research into the episodic nature of AISDPU (Tripp et al. 2016). Against this backdrop, there is a need for longitudinal, idiographic AISDPU inquiries that take a dynamic, within-person view of how such practices affect developer wellbeing over shorter time spans.

Given that the theorizing in agile ISD research is likely incomplete in terms of explaining the doubleedged and episodic nature of AISDPU, the purpose of the current research is to develop a comprehensive theoretical lens that may explain the divergent daily effects of AISDPU on developer well-being. Drawing on the holistic stress process model (HSPM; Nelson and Simmons 2003), we argue that AISDPU and its distinct features place situational demands on software developers that represent a unique source of work stress. The daily experiences of AISDPU, as well as how it is appraised, can vary, which explains why functional and dysfunctional effects emerge. In particular, <sup>fl</sup>uctuations in how AISDPU is appraised—as a threat or a challenge—differentially affect developers resource-based stress responses that may impact their well-being. On a daily basis, AISDPU can be appraised as a challenge, wherein the potential bene-<sup>fi</sup>ts enhance developers’ energy resources (a state called work engagement; Crawford et al. 2010), thereby potentially producing functional effects (i.e., improved well-being). Similarly, on a daily basis, AISDPU can be appraised as a threat, wherein the potential harm depletes developers’ energy resources (a state called resource depletion; Baumeister 2002), thereby potentially producing dysfunctional effects (e.g., impaired well-being). In short, AISDPU is predicted to be associated with both challenge and threat appraisals, and these appraisals explain the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and burdens of AISDPU for developer well-being.

As a broad construct referring to how individuals experience the quality of their lives by evaluating their positive and negative affect as well as their happiness (Diener 1984), well-being is an important outcome because it has rami<sup>fi</sup>cations for work performance, absenteeism, and turnover (Armstrong et al. 2015). Moreover, it is particularly suited to capturing the daily proximal resource implications and health consequences of demanding activities (Oishi et al. 2007). Thus, understanding why and under what circumstances AISDPU helps or harms developer well-being is paramount for researchers and practitioners interested in improving a variety of organizationally relevant outcomes. As the appraisal process of demanding daily activities is critically shaped by how individuals cognitively <sup>fi</sup>lter information about and orient themselves to ongoing events and experiences, our research model also accounts for an essentia moderator that is implicated in the HSPM and is particularly relevant in AISDPU appraisal processes. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we examine the role of information technology (IT) mindfulness (Thatcher et al. 2018)—a dynamic IT-speci<sup>fi</sup>c trait that captures the mindful usage of IT—in shaping the appraisal of AISDPU and its downstream effects on developer resources and well-being.

We tested our research model with a total of 131 agile developers participating in an experience-sampling study in which they were asked to respond to daily surveys for two consecutive workweeks. The results supported our model and advance agile ISD research in several important ways. First, although previous research has portrayed AISDPU as largely bene<sup>fi</sup>cial by viewing it as a means to control job demands and reduce developer stress (Windeler et al. 2017, Venkatesh et al. 2020), we draw a more balanced picture by showing that AISDPU can be a stressor in and of itself with both well-being–enhancing and well-being–eroding effects. In doing so, we expand the theoretical purview and our way of thinking about AISDPU consequences by revealing its trade-offs for developers. Second, complementing between-person (i.e., role demandand workload-centric) accounts of AISDPU effects on developer well-being (Tuomivaara et al. 2017, Venkatesh et al. 2020), our study takes an important <sup>fi</sup>rst step in theorizing the intrapersonal appraisal and stress response mechanisms by which AISDPU unfolds its dy namic well-being effects in developers. Finally, by shedding light on how IT mindfulness can enhance the likelihood of functional outcomes and diminish the likelihood of dysfunctional outcomes from AISDPU, we not only integrate developer personality into the study of the intrapersonal effects of AISDPU but also, identify a potent antidote to the detrimental side effects of AISDPU. Overall, our model answers why and under what conditions AISDPU’s bright and dark side effects on developers are likely to appear.

## Theoretical Development

We <sup>fi</sup>rst review the literature related to AISDPU and then present an overview of the HSPM that forms the backdrop for our research model and sets the stage for hypothesizing, through a stress lens, on the two opposing effects of AISDPU on developers.

## Agile ISD Practices Use and Its Effects

Since the turn of the millennium, growing frustration with the poor success rates of software projects has fueled the evolution of agile ISD methodologies (Ågerfalk et al. 2009), which include scrum, XP, lean development, kanban, and others, and re<sup>fl</sup>ects the principles articulated in the Agile Manifesto (Agile Alliance 2001). Through their emphasis on people and face-to-face communication, on high-quality working software, on active customer involvement in the development process, and on accepting change to create value, these methodologies address the imperative for <sup>fl</sup>exibility and agility de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the ability to create and respond to change (Cockburn 2006). Agile development teams typically work in short cycles in which they deliver working software incrementally and iteratively and focus on emergently understanding the problem space during development (Harris et al. 2009).

Although agile-method advocates claim a common philosophy as described in the Agile Manifesto, each method emerged out of different circumstances and has a different focus. For example, scrum is primarily a project management method, whereas XP focuses more on software development concerns. Consistent with previous research (Sarker et al. 2018), we apply the term “practices” to speci<sup>fi</sup>c agile techniques, such as pair programming and continuous integration. In contrast, “methods” refers to any of a number of de-<sup>fi</sup>ned, interdependent sets of practices developed by software practitioners, such as scrum, XP, and kanban. In addition, although agile practitioners claim to adopt methods, few adopt all of the practices in a particular method, or they only use some single isolated practices; additionally, many adopt practices from multiple methods (Collabnet 2019). Given the reality that agile developers most often apply a mix of various practices on a daily basis that collectively shape their proximal experiences and psychological responses, our focus in this research is on an aggregate view of agile practices across different methods. That is, we refer to AISDPU as the use of a collection of agile project management, software development, and process improvement practices (Tripp et al. 2016).

Early IS research investigating agile ISD methods and practices has focused on examining the (contingent) nature of AISDPU in organizations and its effectiveness vis-a-vis traditional, plan-based ISD approaches (e.g., \` Fitzgerald et al. 2006, Austin and Devin 2009). With the proliferation and intensi<sup>fi</sup>cation of AISDPU, IS scholars have gradually shifted their investigation to the effects of AISDPU on team- and project-level outcome criteria. For example, although Maruping et al. (2009) looked at the impact of AISDPU on software project quality (i.e., bug severity and software complexity), Ramesh et al. (2012) examined how projects that use agile distributed ISD methods developed contextual ambidexterity: that is, the ability to pursue con<sup>fl</sup>icting demands simultaneously. Only recently have scholars begun to shed light on the effects of AISDPU on individual outcomes, including job satisfaction, work exhaustion, and developer stress (Tripp et al. 2016, Windeler et al. 2017, Venkatesh et al. 2020).

Collectively, previous research on the effects of AISDPU has predominantly highlighted their bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, relegating the potential burdens for developers to the background. Despite the emphasis on promoting sustainable development to maintain a constant pace, the reality in agile ISD projects often tells a different story. By striving to enact agile values and principles, such as rapid time-boxed iterations to frequently deliver working software, permanent responsiveness to change and feedback, and open, collaborative interactions with multiple stakeholders (Maruping and Matook 2020, Baham and Hirschheim 2021), AISDPU can place speci<sup>fi</sup>c situational (e.g., time-, change-, and transparencyrelated) demands on developers that may carry unintended consequences. To the best of our knowledge and based on our review of the AISDPU literature (see Online Appendix A), we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed only three relevant studies that looked into the individual-level implications of AISDPU for developer stress and work exhaustion. While Tuomivaara et al. (2017) showed that AISDPU may reduce developers’ work exhaustion by leveling out their workload across the entire duration of a project compared with plan-driven methods and practices, Windeler et al. (2017) found that technical ISD risk factors (i.e., project size, complexity, and target volatility) can heighten developer stress levels in agile software teams by increasing role ambiguity and role con<sup>fl</sup>ict. Moreover, Venkatesh et al. (2020) showed how AISDPU helps developers alleviate work exhaustion by achieving clear and unambiguous role perceptions, especially if the developers have strong organizational skills to effectively interact with customers and stakeholders within their organization.

Although all three studies have provided valuable initial insights into the effects of AISDPU on developer well-being by taking a job-demand-control perspective, their main theoretical thrust was toward explaining how AISDPU could be a means to control and reduce developer stress. However, industry reports and recent empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that AISDPU can be experienced as instrumental on some days yet detrimental on others (Balbes 2017, Fortmann-Muller¨ 2018). Consequently, AISDPU can be a stressor in and of itself with both bene<sup>fi</sup>cial and burdensome effects that have to be accounted for to fully capture its individual-level implications for developers. In addition, most prior studies on AISDPU effects have looked into the differences among developer perceptions of role or workload demands over longer time intervals and far removed from immediate stress reactions. As illustrated in our opening quotations and as suggested in previous research (Tripp et al. 2016), however, AISDPU is an erratic phenomenon that affects developers’ work experiences on a daily basis and thus, may also trigger short-term processes with more proximal effects. As such, to examine its effects at a level closer to how the immediate experiences of developers unfold naturally, a more <sup>fi</sup>ne-grained portrayal of AISDPU is essential. An appropriate empirical test of this process requires an intrapersonal lens that allows researchers to consider how AISDPU and subsequent within-person reactions vary daily. In doing so, existing role demand- and workload-centric accounts of AISDPU effects on developer stress and well-being can be complemented by explanations fo cusing on intrapersonal processes.

Against this backdrop and given that developers’ daily experiences of AISDPU play a crucial role in maintaining a healthy and sustainable software development rhythm, it is reasonable to view AISDPU as a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant source of stress that can stimulate divergent psychological responses in developers. Indeed, we contend that AISDPU’s potentially contrasting effects become clearer when it is examined through a “holistic stress lens.”

## The Holistic Stress Process Model

The HSPM was developed by Nelson and Simmons (2003) to depict an overarching representation of how occupational stress unfolds from the perspective of an individual interacting with his or her workplace environment. Extending previous stress models, such as the cognitive appraisal theory by Lazarus and Folkman (1984), which almost exclusively focused on individuals’ negative responses and coping mechanisms intended to alleviate them, the HSPM explicitly incorporates both positive and negative psychological responses to demands. It thus re<sup>fl</sup>ects a more balanced view of the ongoing stress process, a view that has also found its way into the IS literature (Tarafdar et al. 2019, Benlian 2020, Califf et al. 2020). The gist of the HSPM is that stress should be conceptually framed in terms of two equally important subprocesses embedded in a holistic stress process: the eustress and the distress subprocesses. The eustress subprocess is related to positive stress or “good stress” (Selye 1978), and it involves understanding stressors that are appraised as challenges and their association with positive psychological responses and in turn, positive outcomes (Hargrove et al. 2013). The subprocess of distress is related to negative stress or “bad stress” (Selye 1978), and it involves understanding stressors appraised as threats or hindrances and their association with negative psychological responses and in turn, negative outcomes.

The holistic stress process is triggered by environmental conditions (i.e., stressors or demands) and comprises three intrapersonal evaluation processes (see the lower part of Figure 1): the appraisal process, the response process, and the performance process (McGrath 1976, Califf et al. 2020). The appraisal process links the environmental stressors to the appraisals, the response process connects appraisals and the psychological responses, and the performance process links the psychological responses to the outcomes. According to the HSPM, an individual’s behavior in an organization is shaped based on the interaction of three systems: the physical-technological environment in which the behavior takes place (e.g., technology and other material items); the social environment or patterns of interpersonal relations in which behavior occurs (e.g., value systems, organizational structures, and social interactions); and the “self-system” of the focal person (e.g., perceptions, habits, and personality) (McGrath 1976). The intersection of the three systems embodies the conditions of the holistic stress process that represent several potential sources of stressful situations (or stressors) in the workplace, such as technological/role/task demands, demands from interpersonal and behavioral expectations, and job conditions, among others (Hargrove et al. 2013).

Stressors are considered inherently neutral (Nelson and Simmons 2003). That is, a stressor is de<sup>fi</sup>ned by how an individual appraises the stressor in the context in which he or she works (Hargrove et al. 2013). In this sense, individuals can appraise the same stressor differently, and the same stressor can vary across contexts. As such, when individuals begin to experience a stressor, they evaluate the stressor through a cognitive appraisal process in which they determine whether the stressor is bene<sup>fi</sup>cial (i.e., appraised as a challenge) or harmful (i.e., appraised as a threat) to his or her job tasks. Supporting this dual-appraisal notion, recent research in new product development teams has shown that team members can appraise time pressure as a challenge stressor (by experiencing it as a motivating and stimulating force), such that it promotes goal achievement, or as a hindrance stressor (by constraining attentional resources, thus increasing the chance of misjudgment), such that it thwarts goal achievement (Chong et al. 2011).

After the individual appraises the stressor as bene-<sup>fi</sup>cial or harmful, the individual enters a response process during which she or he decides how to respond to the stressor (McGrath 1976). According to the HSPM, the individual can decide to respond positively or negatively to the stressor,<sup>1</sup> which is manifested in the form of positive or negative psychological states, depending on whether the stressor is appraised as bene<sup>fi</sup>cial or as harmful, respectively. A positive psychological response is indicated, for example, by the presence of hope, positive affect, vigor, or engagement, whereas a negative psychological response includes the presence of anxiety, negative affect, depletion, or exhaustion (Nelson and Simmons 2003).

After the individual experiences a psychological response, the individual enters the performance process during which he or she decides how to act or perform in the workplace, based on the psychological response. The effect of the performance process is an individual or organizational (workplace) outcome, which is the consequence or action of an individual in reaction to the psychological response (Nelson and Simmons 2003). Although the HSPM includes outcomes typical for organizational stress research such as job satisfaction and work performance, it places great emphasis on individual health or well-being effects such as mental or physical health (Branson et al. 2019).

It should be noted that scholars have suggested that challenge and threat appraisals may not be mutually exclusive—the same stressor may be appraised as a threat and a challenge (Lazarus and Folkman 1984). Accordingly, positive and negative appraisals and subsequent responses can occur simultaneously, as a result of the same demand or stressor, and should be considered distinct but related constructs. Thus, for any given demand, an individual can have both a degree of positive and negative appraisal. It is the response to demands that has positive and/or negative valence based on the degree of attraction and/or aversion the individual experiences toward the stressor (Simmons and Nelson 2007). In this regard, Nelson and Simmons (2003) contend that most work stressors elicit a mixed bag of both positive and negative responses in individuals that alter daily. Indeed, they emphasize that the full range of the stress response cannot be appreciated without an approach to assess eustress and distress concurrently.

## Agile ISD Practices Use as a Trigger of Divergent Stress Processes

Given that prior research has indicated that AISDPU can elicit both functional and dysfunctional reactions in developers, we argue that these seemingly opposing reactions can be explained by daily <sup>fl</sup>uctuations in how AISDPU is appraised. Agile practices place situational demands on developers through incorporating speci<sup>fi</sup>c values, principles, and work rules that need to be enacted by developers on a daily basis. The extent to which these agile practices are used re<sup>fl</sup>ects how developers are exposed to and engage with these practices. Although not fully consistent with HSPM’s main focus on exogenous, environmental demands, we conceptualize AISDPU as a stressor that is not completely imposed on developers but also includes some personal discretion expressed in how developers engage in agile practices. Yet, this engagement is constrained by the low controllability of situational demands and is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the decisions of the management and project team. Such a view of AISDPU is also in line with the notion that stressors are demanding situations that emerge through an interaction between external (i.e., the physicaltechnological and social environment) and internal (i.e., self-system) conditions (Cooper et al. 2001).

Accordingly, we propose that the daily experience and handling of work procedures (e.g., daily standups, refactoring), social interactions (e.g., pair programming, client meetings), and artifacts (e.g., sprint backlog, lightweight documentation) embraced by AISDPU create situational (e.g., time-, change-, and transparency-related) demands that may trigger divergent appraisal and stress response processes. For example, because it creates opportunities for mutual learning, pair programming may be appraised as exciting and energizing on some days, creating eustress. Yet, on other days, when ambivalent feelings of “being watched” prevail, developers may appraise it as daunting and depleting, and thus, they may experience distress. These <sup>fl</sup>uctuations in the appraisal may shape how AISDPU differentially affects psychological states and explain why AISDPU can have both well-being–enhancing and well-being–eroding effects.

In our study, we focus on stress responses that relate to developers’ energy resources being affected during the psychological response process. As one of the primary stress response indicators of the HSPM (Nelson and Simmons 2003), individuals’ energy resources are a representation of an individual’s internal energetic capacities, comprising cognitive, affective, and physical energy (Kahn 1990, Quinn et al. 2012). While physical energy is evident by a state of exertion and active behavioral ability, cognitive energy is captured by a heightened sense of awareness and mental dexterity. Emotional energy is re<sup>fl</sup>ected in empathic connections with others and excitement for work (Kahn 1992). The three sources of energy combine to occur simultaneously, re<sup>fl</sup>ecting the extent to which an individual feels a sense of vitality, of being psychologically vigorous and alert (Ryan and Frederick 1997). Individuals typically have a limited pool of resources available that they need to sustain attention and block out distracting information to perform properly in the roles they assume in work and private life (Muraven and Baumeister 2000). Appraisals of stressors have been found to be key factors affecting psychological resource availability, with positive appraisals having enhancing effects and negative appraisals having depleting effects (Hobfoll 1989). Focusing on energy resources as key stress indicators in the psychological response process thus aids in explaining how challenge and threat appraisals can result in individuals’ resources being in an enriched or depleted state, which may be responsible for functional or dysfunctional health consequences. Moreover, individuals’ energy resources have not only been found to be an important intrapersonal mechanism explaining psychological health and well-being effects, but they also capture the imminent, short-term effects of daily work stressors via appraisals (Lana et al. 2014, Trougakos et al. 2015).

In accordance with the two divergent stress subprocesses in the HSPM, we examine work engagement and resource depletion as crucial resource-based stress responses in a dual-pathway model that foregrounds the equality and simultaneity of eustress and distress subprocesses triggered by AISDPU. Work engagement is typically treated as an indicator of resource gain in the eustress response and has frequently been used to represent the direct opposite of resource depletion (Nelson and Simmons 2003), which is an important marker of resource loss in the distress response (Mitchell et al. 2019). As such, from the HSPM perspective, both work engagement and resource depletion represent positive and negative psychological stress responses to challenge appraisals and threat appraisals, respectively. Table B.1 in Online Appendix B depicts sample quotes collected in our main study to illustrate the divergent, daily <sup>fl</sup>uctuating appraisals and opposing resource-level effects of speci<sup>fi</sup>c agile practices. They throw the duality of pathways emanating from AISDPU into sharp relief while showing how AISDPU can simultaneously have positive and negative implications for developers’ appraisals and energy resources.

Taken together, owing to its balanced approach and particular emphasis on health effects, the HSPM provides a useful theoretical lens that allows us to examine the dual effects of AISDPU on developer wellbeing over the course of their daily work. Its focus on intrapersonal processes also lends itself to examining the within-person variability and episodic nature of dynamic phenomena. In sum, this holistic theoretical perspective enables us to identify and tie together the main elements of our research model (see the upper part of Figure 1) that we develop next.

## Model Development

Building upon the HSPM, we argue that AISDPU serves as an activator of both positive and negative stress subprocesses. The gist of our theorizing is that AISDPU appraisal is the linchpin to determining the nature and valence of the relationship between AISDPU and well-being. On some days, when AISDPU is appraised as a challenge, it triggers a eustress response by enhancing work engagement, thus increasing developer well-being. Yet, on other days, when AISDPU is experi enced as a threat, it activates a distress response by depleting energy resources, thus impairing developer well-being.

## The Effect of Agile ISD Practices Use Appraised as a Challenge on Work Engagement

As mentioned, AISDPU has a set of inherent situational features related to time, change, and transparency, which are prevalent in the daily work of developers. In combination, they represent potential sources of stressful demands that, we argue, may be appraised by developers as a challenge.

First, developers experience agile practices through constant time pressure and work progress via rapid, incremental cycles of software development and daily intensive feedback (e.g., stand-up meetings, pair programming sessions) from various stakeholders (e.g., codevelopers, customer representatives, scrum masters).

Such temporal aspects lend themselves to stimulating developers in their daily pursuit of learning and thriving in the workplace. Indeed, time pressure experienced as motivating has been shown to be associated with ful<sup>fi</sup>llment and a strong proclivity to succeed (Prem et al. 2017). Second, high responsiveness to change and the emphasis on lightweight documentation in AISDPU push developers to stay adaptive and <sup>fl</sup>exible in their daily decision making (Cram and Marabelli 2018). In this way, developers are encouraged to remain “mentally <sup>fl</sup>uid” on a daily basis to avoid getting locked into obsolete knowledge and beliefs. To the extent that developers feel nimble and unencumbered through these features, they are likely to appreciate AISDPU as a vehicle to accomplish their goals more seamlessly. Third, a high level of individual work-performance transparency experienced through AISDPU, such as pair programming or collective code monitoring, places developers in the spotlight (Darino et al. 2019). Through this attention to their individual contributions to team outcomes, developers are stimulated into leaving their comfort zones and taking ownership of their daily work projects, which is likely to incite developers to strive for ongoing personal growth. Collectively, we suggest that it is in these ways that daily AISDPU may be experienced as an opportunity for achieving desirable goals. Following this reasoning, experiencing AISDPU as promoting daily thriving and progress toward task accomplishment would result in AISDPU being appraised as a challenge.

According to the HSPM, stressors appraised as a challenge instigate a eustress process that promotes positive psychological states (Nelson and Simmons 2003). Speci<sup>fi</sup>- cally, daily challenge appraisal has been shown to be invigorating and increase personal resources, bringing individuals into an enriched psychological state (Mitchell et al. 2019). It stands to reason, then, that on days when developers appraise AISDPU as challenging, they may become aroused by the stimulating and conducive aspects of AISDPU, which are likely to enhance their energetic resources. Such an enhanced state of energy resources is often referred to as engagement or in the work domain, as work engagement (Crawford et al. 2010). Previous research has shown that individuals exhibit higher levels of engagement when they are psychologically activated and have suf<sup>fi</sup>cient energy resources available to employ and express themselves in their work roles (Kahn 1990, Lanaj et al. 2019). Accordingly, developers who feel spurred on by daily AISDPU helping them advance toward their individual goals and thus, who appraise it as a challenge are likely to mobilize their personal energies and drive them into their daily work activities. Putting these arguments together, we propose that daily AISDPU, to the extent that it leads developers to appraise AISDPU as a challenge and a motivating factor, will trigger a positive psychological state in the form of increased work engagement.

Hypothesis 1. On a daily basis, there is a positive indirect effect of AISDPU on work engagement through challenge appraisal.

## The Effect of Agile ISD Practices Use Appraised as a Threat on Resource Depletion

Acknowledging the possibility that individuals’ experience of the same stressor can be simultaneously positive and negative (Nelson and Simmons 2003, Hargrove et al. 2013), we contend that AISDPU may also be appraised as threatening. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, developers may appraise AISDPU through its time-, change-, and transparency-related situational features as hindering their daily task progress and goal achievement.

First, the fast-paced, largely time-boxed delivery of new product increments in agile development entails increased work cadences and puts developers under constant strain, which may alert developers to threats or dangers (e.g., possible failure or overload) they must navigate on a daily basis (Hemon-Hildgen et al. 2020). The intensity of the temporal pace being imposed on agile developers and its appraisal is suc cinctly expressed in the following statement of one developer re<sup>fl</sup>ecting on how “SCRUM was evil perhaps due to being always under pressure, the need to meet at odd times frequently, and because the week before the end of the sprint, people were working 90-hour weeks just to catch up” (Sarker et al. 2018, p. 112). Moreover, the emphasis on daily time-consuming face-to-face meetings with various stakeholders en tails signi<sup>fi</sup>cant time con<sup>fl</sup>icts and opportunity costs detracting from the resources developers could otherwise dedicate to work they value more (Cram and Marabelli 2018). This may also create a sense of time famine when there are other, more valued activities to perform. Second, permanent change and high target volatility inherent in AISDPU have the potential to in: crease developers’ daily mental strain because low predictability in core work features (e.g., workload, shifting priorities) and a constant readiness to change direction have been shown to promote discomfort and behavioral uncertainty (Dikert et al. 2016). Likely com pounding this is the reliance in AISDPU on tacit knowledge, trial and error, and informal knowledge repositories at the detriment of explicit, more formal knowledge documentation. This may heighten developers’ feelings of insecurity regarding the lack of a stable direction and <sup>fi</sup>xed anchor points. Finally, high individual visibility and daily exposure to perfor mance feedback in daily stand-ups or pair programming are likely to unsettle and intimidate developers because they may feel put on the spot and pushed toward incessantly proving themselves. Such constant exposure and scrutiny have also been shown to create feelings of insecurity, anxiety, and paralysis (Carver and White 1994). Overall, experiencing AISDPU as overwhelming or unsettling, hindering developers from making daily progress toward goal attainment and task accomplishment, would result in AISDPU being appraised as a threat.

Following the HSPM, stressors appraised as threatening trigger a distress process that creates negative psychological states. In particular, threat appraisal has been shown to be effortful and to consume energy resources, leaving individuals in a depleted psychological state (Mitchell et al. 2019). In this light, on days when developers appraise AISDPU as threatening, they can become overwhelmed by the unpleasant and cumbersome aspects of AISDPU, which likely tax developers and drain their energy resources. This drained state is also referred to as resource depletion or a de<sup>fi</sup>ciency of energy resources, often manifested in intensi<sup>fi</sup>ed feelings of fatigue (Baumeister 2002). Research has shown that threat appraisals reduce blood glucose levels, impair concentration, and increase subjective fatigue, all of which are indicators of resource depletion (Hagger et al. 2010). Correspondingly, threat appraisal stemming from AISDPU is likely to put a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant burden on developers’ energy resources and deplete their energy reservoirs. Combining our arguments, we thus propose that daily AISDPU, to the extent that it leads developers to appraise AISDPU as a threat and a disturbing factor, will drain developers energy and leave them in a negative psychological state of resource depletion.

Hypothesis 2. On a daily basis, there is a positive indirect effect of AISDPU on resource depletion through threat appraisal.

## The Opposing Indirect Effects of Agile ISD Practices Use on Developer Well-Being

Given that the performance process in the HSPM places great emphasis on individual health effects induced by potentially divergent psychological responses, we investigate the opposing indirect effects of AISDPU (through stress appraisals and energy resources) on developers’ subjective well-being. As a broad construct referring to individuals’ cognitive and affective evaluations of their lives, including emotional reactions to events as well as judgments of satisfaction and ful<sup>fi</sup>llment (Diener et al. 1999), it is particularly suited as a mental health-related outcome to capturing the daily proximate resource implications of stressful demands. Subjective well-being is also consequential because it has more far-reaching rami<sup>fi</sup>cations for work performance, absenteeism, and turnover (e.g., Armstrong et al. 2015).

We theorize that AISDPU appraised as a challenge will enhance developers’ work engagement, which in turn, will promote their well-being. Appraising AISDPU as a challenge increases the energy resources required for developers’ daily functioning and performance. Heightened energetic resources are captured by work engagement, encompassing enhanced physical energy, sharpened cognitive thinking, and elevated emotional resources (Kahn 1992). Engagement broadens “the reper toire of thoughts and actions that a person has available to them” (Quinn et al. 2012, p. 363) and can thereby enhance task pro<sup>fi</sup>ciency and nurture bene<sup>fi</sup>cial social interactions that increase the likelihood of a positive, ful<sup>fi</sup>lling state of mind in the workplace (Bakker and Demerouti 2008). Heightened energy resources re<sup>fl</sup>ected in higher work engagement are thus likely to enhance the positive feelings and emotions inherent in subjective well-being. Taken together, because AISDPU appraised as a challenge should foster work engagement and because work engagement is likely to improve assessments of wellbeing, we expect work engagement to positively mediate the relationship between AISDPU, challenge appraisal, and developer well-being.

Hypothesis 3. On a daily basis, there is a positive indirect effect of AISDPU on developer well-being through chal lenge appraisal and work engagement.

Although we argue that appraising AISDPU as a challenge has bene<sup>fi</sup>cial downstream effects on developer well-being, we expect an opposite effect pattern for appraising AISDPU as a threat. That is, we suggest that the latter will have a burdening effect on developer well-being because these developers will have de pleted energy resources. Subjective well-being theory (Diener 1984) posits that well-being deteriorates when individuals’ energies are taxed and they perceive that they are not progressing toward their goals. In a similar vein, developers have a <sup>fi</sup>nite amount of energy resources, the depletion of which will result in undesirable consequences for their well-being. AISDPU appraised as a threat takes away from the energies and abilities to make progress toward work goals, thereby increasing the likelihood of developers feeling worn out, tired, or exhausted. Exhaustion and fatigue stemming from job stressors have been shown to undermine employee well-being in general (Lennard et al. 2019) and IT professionals’ well-being in particular (Moore 2000, Armstrong et al. 2015). Given that, when appraised as a threat, AISDPU depletes limited developer resources and thwarts goal progress, we expect that it will impair developers’ cognitive and affective evaluations of their life—that is, their well being. On the whole, therefore, resource depletion is expected to negatively mediate the relationship between AISDPU, threat appraisal, and developer wellbeing.

Hypothesis 4. On a daily basis, there is a negative indirect effect of AISDPU on developer well-being through threat appraisal and resource depletion.

## The Moderating Influence of IT Mindfulness

According to the HSPM, person factors (or traits) may in<sup>fl</sup>uence cognitive appraisals in the stress process because they alter how individuals cognitively process and make sense of situational demands. Integrating this argument with our theoretical foundation suggests that personal traits may shape developers’ mental models and attentional capacity in the cognitive appraisal of AISDPU, thereby affecting the overall AISDPU stress experience.

Recent IS research has identi<sup>fi</sup>ed IT mindfulness as a particularly revelatory individual trait for navigating technology adoption and use in ambiguous and stressful situations (Sun et al. 2016). IT mindfulness refers to “a dynamic IT-speci<sup>fi</sup>c trait, evident when working with IT, whereby the user focuses on the present, pays attention to detail, exhibits a willingness to consider other uses, and expresses genuine interest in investigating IT features and failures” (Thatcher et al. 2018, p. 832–834). In this respect, IT mindfulness is viewed as an overarching mental mindset driven by awareness of the larger context and openness to new and effective ways to accomplish tasks by using IT. This speaks directly to the vigilant and attentive usage of IT to <sup>fi</sup>lter and process information. IT mindfulness is rooted in the broader mindfulness concept that depicts how individuals interact with their environment (Langer 1989), and it helps people to “distinguish between wholesome and unwholesome, bene<sup>fi</sup>cial and unbene<sup>fi</sup>cial tendencies” (Wallace 2006, p. 61). While the broader concept of mindfulness has been repeatedly embraced in IS research to shed light on digital operations, IT innovation, IT security (e.g., Butler and Gray 2006, Jensen et al. 2017, Salovaara et al. 2019), and also, agile ISD (McAvoy and Butler 2009, Vidgen and Wang 2009), the primary focus of these studies was at the organizational or project level. This neglects the possibility that mindfulness and especially mindfulness in IT use are also central to and consequential for developers’ cognitive appraisals and stress responses.

Previous mindfulness research has shown that through attentional broadening, dynamic awareness, openness to change, and psychological <sup>fl</sup>exibility, mindful attention promotes more adaptive appraisals and involves a greater willingness or ability to receptively process internal and external stimuli as they occur (i.e., an “up-regulation” or ampli<sup>fi</sup>cation of positive appraisals of stressors). Mindfulness has also been found to foster more objectively informed responses; situations, then, can potentially be viewed in more benign or at least neutral terms (Garland et al. 2015). Conversely, recent scholarly work has demonstrated that mindfulness promotes desensitization in the moment and a reduction in emotional reactivity to potentially threatening stimuli (i.e., a “turning down” or attenuation of negative appraisals of stressors), allowing for a mitigated strain response in the face of stressors (e.g., Hulsheger et al.¨ 2013).

In line with this reasoning, we argue that developers mindfully using IT (i.e., agile ISD tools) in their projects are more (less) likely to appraise AISDPU as an opportunity (threat). Agile ISD tools, including applications for project management and strategic planning (e.g., Jira, Trello); for coding, planning, and deployment (e.g., Github, Jenkins); and for communication and collaboration (e.g., Slack, Con<sup>fl</sup>uence), are widely used to facilitate developers’ daily work by structuring and aligning tasks, priorities, and schedules (Ciancarini et al. 2019). In doing so, agile ISD tools are an essential contributor to organizing and shaping agile developers’ mental models through which they make sense of their (of<sup>fl</sup>ine and online) reality and daily experiences (Baskerville et al. 2020). Given the capacity of mindfully using agile ISD tools to adjust developers’ mode of information processing (Sun et al. 2016, Kudesia 2019), we thus expect that in creating a more functional trajectory for the downstream effects of the stress experience, developers IT mindfulness should moderate the effects of AISDPU on stress appraisals.

First, developers high in IT mindfulness have a greater orientation to the present through their deeper and more attentive use of agile ISD tools (e.g., of digital backlogs, sprint plans, and kanban boards), which makes them more likely to attend to the bigger picture and better understand their role in the broade scheme of things (Baham et al. 2017, Kudesia 2019). This broadened attention to the overall context makes developers less likely to experience daily AISDPU as overwhelming and threatening because they can mentally step back, detach from the narrow processing of their local situation, and put situational demands from AISDPU into perspective. By genuinely attending to the features and affordances of agile ISD tools, highly IT mindful developers should be better equipped to encounter the daily time pressures and extensive meetings in agile projects with greater ease and more willingness to understand the overall pur pose and the speci<sup>fi</sup>c connections to personal thriving and improvement.

Second, developers high in IT mindfulness are aware of multiple perspectives and are open to creating new and effective ways to accomplish tasks through their thoughtful use of agile ISD tools (Thatcher et al. 2018). This openness to reacting <sup>fl</sup>exibly to new task demands makes developers less likely to feel overburdened by abrupt changes in user requirements or by high volatility in their daily workloads. On the contrary, high IT mindfulness should make developers more adept at recognizing, understanding, and addressing the dynamic situational demands from AISDPU, which should foster appraising unpredictable changes as an opportunity to promote individual progress and personal growth (Sun et al. 2016).

Finally, IT mindfulness should enable developers to be more psychologically <sup>fl</sup>exible in the moment to mentally <sup>fi</sup>lter information about stressors. The broader mindfulness research has demonstrated that high mindfulness allows negative emotions to become less potent by defusing and separating them from selfinvolvement, which should make disregarding or rejecting harmful stressor information more likely (Blanke et al. 2020). Similarly, developers who have a greater contextual understanding of the work priorities and schedules in agile projects through their heedful usage of agile ISD tools should be more likely to focus on the wholesome aspects when being exposed to the daily demands of AISDPU and more protecting from dwelling on the unwholesome aspects. For example, developers high in IT mindfulness should be well prepared to view the high visibility inherent in daily pair programming or stand-up meetings as an opportunity because they are more likely to integrate situational information (e.g., performance feedback) into a broader, more positive appraisal of how such information can lead to growth or meaning. Conversely, they should be more able to attenuate or stave off immediate negative assessments by deemphasizing adverse stressor information and detaching themselves from self-recrimination and avoidance (Garland et al. 2015), making threat appraisal less probable.

By shaping the likelihood of challenge and threat appraisals, IT mindfulness should also affect how developers respond to stress from AISDPU. More specifically, we argue that when developers are high in IT mindfulness—through their conscious and attentive use of agile ISD tools—and thus, tend to appraise daily AISDPU as challenging, they are more likely to become energized and get into a positive, vigorous psychological state of engagement. The higher likelihood of challenge appraisal through IT mindful developers attending to the immediate world and broader contextual understanding of interdependencies should thus stimulate an accumulation of energetic resources and facilitate work engagement. Indeed, mindfulness has been conceived of as the essence of engagement, as individuals in a heightened state of activation are more likely to detect changes in the environment and possibilities for wholesome actions (Langer 1989). Conversely, developers high in IT mindfulness should be less likely to suffer from negative psychological states and to become resource depleted because of their enhanced capacity to de<sup>fl</sup>ect threatening aspects of situational stressors. By redirecting their mind with the help of agile ISD tools toward present moment experiences and larger contexts and away from potentially harmful stressor information, IT mindful developers should bene<sup>fi</sup>t more from freeing up energy resources that would otherwise be consumed by elaborating on negative and undesirable outcomes (i.e., by threat appraisal).

Collectively, we theorize that IT mindfulness will moderate the effects of daily AISDPU on cognitive stress appraisals. On a daily basis, developers low in IT mindfulness should have less ability to fend off ruminating about potentially negative demands from AISDPU, making them more likely to appraise daily AISDPU as threatening. By foregrounding the threatening aspects, these less IT mindful developers are likely to suffer from resource depletion, which will impair their well-being. Conversely, developers high in IT mindfulness should possess a greater ability to focus on the potentially positive aspects of AISDPU, making them more likely to appraise daily AISDPU as challenging. By emphasizing the opportunities of daily AISDPU, these highly IT mindful developers are likely to experience an enhanced state of work engagement that will increase their well-being. Taken together, we thus hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 5. On a daily basis, the positive indirect effect of AISDPU on (a) work engagement (through challenge appraisal) will be stronger when IT mindfulness is higher rather than lower and (b) resource depletion (through threat appraisal) will be stronger when IT mindfulness is lower rather than higher.

Hypothesis 6. On a daily basis, the (a) positive indirect effect of AISDPU on developer well-being (through challenge appraisal and work engagement) will be stronger when IT mindfulness is higher rather than lower and the (b) negative indirect effect of AISDPU on developer wellbeing (through threat appraisal and resource depletion) will be stronger when IT mindfulness is lower rather than higher.

## Methods

## Participants

To assess our research model, we collected survey data from 131 respondents who worked as software development professionals in the United States. We recruited our sample by using Empanel, a data collection company that specializes in recruiting internetbased survey panels. Several previous IS studies have successfully collected and used data in this manner (e.g., Tripp et al. 2016). We obtained 1,045 potential respondents from Empanel and asked potential respondents whether they were currently working in a software development team that was using agile ISD practices and what role they played on the team. Moreover, to ensure that our respondents had suf<sup>fi</sup>- cient background and experience, we asked them to report on their total IT experience and how much experience they had at their current organization. Consistent with previous AISDPU research (Tripp et al. 2016), the respondents had to play a role as a member of an agile ISD team, have at least one year of total experience, and have worked for their current organization for at least six months. Based on the responses to these <sup>fi</sup>lter questions, we screened out 475 potential respondents and invited the other 570 respondents to participate in a daily study about their experiences with agile ISD practices. The participants were incentivized with a raf<sup>fl</sup>e, in which each completed survey resulted in an entry toward 1 of 20 \$100 prizes. The email included a link containing a description of the purpose and requirements of the voluntary study and the informed consent. We also assured the con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality and anonymity of the responses to all surveys.

Individuals who consented and were eligible for our study (working full time and having regular access to their email through a work computer or smartphone) were then directed to a baseline survey containing measures of the between-person variables and demographics. Following the baseline survey, the participants were enrolled in the experience-sampling portion of the study and were sent four surveys a day for 10 consecutive workdays. A total of 178 participants signed up to take part in the study, of which 131 provided complete data for the baseline survey and for at least 3 days (of a maximum of 10 days) of the matched daily surveys (Singer and Willett 2003). The completed surveys resulted in a <sup>fi</sup>nal response rate of 23%, which is comparable with other experience-sampling studies (Gabriel et al. 2019).

The developers had an average age of 38.1 years, 64.1% were male and 41.3% held a university degree. Their average organizational tenure was 10.1 years, and their average experience with agile ISD practices was 6.5 years. Similar to previous studies on agile ISD developers (e.g., Tripp et al. 2016), in our study, developers’ roles covered tasks related to software development, project management, quality assurance and tests, and software architecture. We performed t tests to compare the developer demographics included in the data analysis with those of the invited developers who did not participate in the study. We found no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences in any demographic variables captured in this study.

## Experience-Sampling Procedures and Measures

As described, over three weeks, we conducted an experience-sampling study including two major parts. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst part of the study, the developers completed a baseline survey that assessed demographics and the between-person variables (i.e., IT mindfulness, betweenperson controls). In the second part of the study, which began one week after the completion of part 1, data were collected by using an experience-sampling methodology (ESM). The ESM featured repeated measurements of the same subjects with a focus on assessing variables that exhibit substantial within-person variation over the short term that would usually go unnoticed with crosssectional surveys (Fisher and To 2012). The ESM also allowed us to access developers in their everyday environments and to measure constructs where they were experienced, thus increasing realism and minimizing recall biases (Bolger et al. 2003). Thus, for the work of agile de velopers, this multiple measurement design helped to paint a more accurate picture of daily AISDPU than would be obtained via a single retrospective survey. Not assessing the dynamic relations between AISDPU and its downstream effects (i.e., taking dynamic processes and assessing them with static, “in general” assessments of constructs) would also misalign the theoretical underpinnings of the constructs and the level of analysis used to assess their relationships (Klein and Kozlowski 2000).

In our study, the developers were asked to complete four short surveys each workday over two weeks (see Figure B.1 in Online Appendix B). Based on the recommendation of Reis and Wheeler (1991, p. 287), who contended that a “2-week record-keeping period is assumed to represent a stable and generalizable estimate of social life,” we chose 10 days for the daily surveys. In line with previous ESM studies, we used a <sup>fi</sup>xed time schedule for daily data collection. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, on each morning of the study, the participants were sent the link to the <sup>fi</sup>rst daily survey at 9:30 a.m. To control for possible serial effects, the participants were asked to report their baseline levels of resource depletion and work engagement in the morning. The link to the second daily survey was sent at 3:00 p.m. to ask participants to report about the extent of AISDPU and their challenge and threat appraisals. At 5:30 p.m., in a third daily survey, the participants were asked to report their feelings of depletion and engagement again. Then, each evening at 8:30 p.m., the participants received the link to the last daily survey that assessed their well-being. The time lags between the morning, afternoon/late afternoon, and evening surveys allowed us to examine changes in resource levels and to establish temporal separation and causal precedence between the predictor, mediator, and outcome variables. Following the convention of daily-level ESM studies (Bolger et al. 2003), all items in the daily surveys referred strictly to daily responses. All surveys involved were implemented by using an online survey platform, and the links to the surveys were sent through emails (see data collection procedures in Online Appendix B).

We adopted measurement items from validated instruments for all constructs in our study and adapted them to the daily level (i.e., to daily experiences at work) where necessary. Online Appendix B presents the measurement items of all constructs, their psychometric properties, and the results of a con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory factor analysis that demonstrates the convergent and discriminant validity of our constructs. In terms of within-person controls, we controlled for developer morning work engagement and resource depletion to account for possible autoregressive effects and to better establish our presumed causal ordering. To account for alternative explanations, we also controlled for late afternoon positive and negative affect because affect states not only assist with eliminating issues tied to common method variance but also, in<sup>fl</sup>uence reactions to stressful job demands (Gabriel et al. 2019). Moreover, we included the day of the study (i.e., days 1–10) to control for spurious effects due to time variation because it has been found to be a suitable proxy to account for potential general linear time trends in work/project environments (Beal and Weiss 2003, Gabriel et al. 2019). In terms of between-person controls, following recent AISDPU studies (Tripp et al. 2016, Kude et al. 2019, Venkatesh et al. 2020), we included age, gender, agile development experience, total work experience, team size, project size, project complexity, developer stress (job related), and global life satisfaction as between-person controls in the baseline survey. Accounting for the between- and within-person controls allowed us to examine the incremental validity of our predictions over these factors. Following suggested procedures and for the sake of parsimony, we did not include study day and within-person positive and negative state affect as well as the between-person controls in our <sup>fi</sup>nal analyses (see Table 2) because regardless of these controls, the direction, magnitude, and form of our hypothesized effects held (Becker 2005, Carlson and Wu 2012).<sup>2</sup> However, to ensure that the analyses involving the energy resource constructs represented change in the level of these constructs (Gabriel et al. 2019), we kept morning work engagement and resource depletion as within-person controls.

## Analytic Approach

As our data have a multilevel structure (days nested within individual developers), we analyzed our model by following the procedures for multilevel path analysis using Mplus 8.6 (Muthen and Muth´ en´ 1998) and R 4.1.1 (Bliese 2016). These procedures remedy many of the threats for type I and type II errors by explicitly accounting for and modeling the nonindependence of observations in the data. Following best practices in modeling ESM data (e.g., Beal 2015), we used random slopes to model the level 1 within-person relationships between AISDPU, threat appraisal, challenge appraisal, resource depletion, work engagement, and developer well-being. IT mindfulness was modeled as a between-person level 2 construct. We handled missing data using the robust full information maximum likelihood estimation, which is a preferred method in multilevel models for dealing with missing data (Enders 2010). According to the recommendations of Hofmann and Gavin (1998), we group-mean centered the level 1 predictors and grand-mean centered IT mindfulness. Group-mean centering provides a pure estimate of the within-person relationship by removing betweenperson variance. This means that the relationships among the level 1 variables are not confounded by individual differences, such as response tendencies, personality traits, and demographics (Gabriel et al. 2019). As mentioned earlier, we also followed best practice recommendations from Beal (2015) to control for lagged criteria (i.e., resource depletion and work engagement), allowing us to account for a possible autocorrelation in the depletion and engagement scores and to diminish concerns of reverse causality. To test mediation and crosslevel-moderated mediation, according to the recommendation by Preacher et al. (2010), we applied a parametric bootstrapping procedure. We ran a Monte Carlo simulation with 20,000 resamples to create 95% bias-corrected con<sup>fi</sup>dence intervals (95% CIs) around the indirect and serial indirect effect estimates to test the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of these effects. For moderated mediation, we also tested the difference in conditional indirect effects following the procedures of Hayes (2015).

## Discussion

Prior work examining the consequences of AISDPU largely focused their investigations on project- and team-level outcomes to shed light on the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of AISDPU. More recently, research has begun to look at individual-level, between-person implications for developers and has mainly focused on how AISDPU can reduce developer stress and work exhaustion. Taking a within-person perspective, our study viewed the effects of AISDPU through the lens of the HSPM to develop a dual-pathway model emphasizing the opposing and dynamic effects of daily AISDPU on developer well-being.

Our study demonstrated that the appraisal of AISDPU quali<sup>fi</sup>es the nature of the relationship between AISDPU and well-being via two divergent stress processes. On the one hand, daily AISDPU was associated with higher work engagement when appraised as a challenge, thus increasing developer well-being. On the other hand, daily AISDPU depleted energy resources when appraised as a threat impairing developer well-being. Moreover, we found that IT mindfulness is an in<sup>fl</sup>uential IT-speci<sup>fi</sup>c trait in this dynamic stress experience because it creates a lens through which developers <sup>fi</sup>lter and make sense of the situational demands inherent in AISDPU. Less IT mindful developers were more likely to appraise daily AISDPU as threatening, thereby promoting a state of resource depletion that eventuates in reduced wellbeing at the end of the workday. In contrast, more IT mindful developers were more likely to appraise daily AISDPU as challenging, thereby fostering a state of work engagement that increased end of day wellbeing. Taken together, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from our dualpathway model show why and under what conditions AISDPU has both bene<sup>fi</sup>cial and burdensome effects on developer well-being.

## Implications for Theory and Research

We believe that our study adds to agile ISD research in three speci<sup>fi</sup>c areas. First, the core theoretical contribution of this study involves the idea that AISDPU can have both well-being–enhancing and well-being–eroding effects such that it can simultaneously bring bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and burdens to developers. Assuming a job-demand-control perspective (Windeler et al. 2017, Venkatesh et al. 2020), previous scholarship has largely treated AISDPU as a means to reduce developer stress by controlling job demands, highlighting its role as a remedy. By viewing AISDPU through a holistic stress lens, our study extends prior research and shows that AISDPU can be a stressor in and of itself, with both bene<sup>fi</sup>cial (eustress) and burdensome (distress) effects that have to be considered together to paint a more complete picture of its developer-level implications. Ignoring either side may result in an omission of an essential part of the story on AISDPU implications and lead to lopsided assessments of the success or failure in contemporary software development projects. Taken together, this study expands our thinking about AISDPU by emphasizing a balanced and dialectical view of AISDPU that can produce both bright and dark side effects for developers.

Second, complementing between-person (i.e., role demand- and workload-centric) accounts of AISDPU effects on developer well-being (Tuomivaara et al. 2017, Venkatesh et al. 2020), our study takes an important step in identifying and theorizing the intrapersonal stress response processes by which AISDPU unfolds its dynamic well-being effects in developers. Most prior research on AISDPU effects has studied interpersonal differences in developer perceptions over longer time intervals—far removed from immediate stress reactions. However, our study <sup>fi</sup>nds that AISDPU is an erratic phenomenon that differentially affects developers’ appraisals and stress responses on a daily basis and thus, also triggers more proximal, short-term processes. By seizing AISDPU effects at a level closer to how momentary developer experiences and reactions unfold naturally, our study opens up a window into novel intrapersonal mechanisms that treat short-term <sup>fl</sup>uctuations as a substantive signal rather than transient noise. As such, in echoing previous calls for increased attention to the episodic nature and effects of AISDPU on developers (Tripp et al. 2016), our study advances previous research by deepening our understanding of within-person processes associated with everyday AISDPU dynamics.

Finally, we extend theory and research by demonstrating that the intrapersonal consequences of AISDPU do not occur in a vacuum but are shaped by developer traits. While prior research has found that organizational skills facilitate interpersonal interactions and help agile developers better cope with role demands, thereby reducing work exhaustion (Venkatesh et al. 2020), we demonstrate that IT mindfulness, a dynamic IT-speci<sup>fi</sup>c trait, can enhance the likelihood of functional outcomes and diminish the likelihood of dysfunctional outcomes from AISDPU. Incorporating developer personality traits into the inquiry of AISDPU consequences, our work thus adds to prior skill-focused, interpersonal explanations and shows how IT mindfulness can substantially alter intrapersonal responses to AISDPU.

## Practical Implications

Because of its unique situational pressures and demands, AISDPU can serve as a source of daily stress for developers, and failure to account for it may have long-term detrimental effects on developer health.

Given that agile ISD project success is only as good as its developers’ productiveness, taking care of developer well-being is of utmost importance to organizations. In contrast to well-intended recommendations for managers to encourage AISDPU per se, our study sounds a cautionary note about more consciously dealing with its up- and downsides. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, our results suggest that AISDPU can be a double-edged sword, triggering both functional and dysfunctional well-being outcomes. On a daily basis, developers may appraise AISDPU as a challenge and become “<sup>fi</sup>red up,” which enhances their resources and subsequent well-being. Also, on a daily basis, developers may appraise it as a threat, which depletes their resources and makes them feel “burned out,” ultimately impairing their developer well-being. A key takeaway is in <sup>fi</sup>nding ways to in<sup>fl</sup>uence developers to appraise AISDPU and its key features as a challenge rather than a threat. Managers should take care in how they relay AISDPU to developers to reduce the likelihood that it is negatively construed (i.e., avoiding threat appraisals). For example, organizations could use awareness programs or workshops to explain and emphasize

AISDPU’s opportunities and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to developers while pointing to its potential burdens and risks. Within-person <sup>fl</sup>uctuations in AISDPU also suggest that organizations would bene<sup>fi</sup>t from time-sensitive recovery interventions aimed at replenishing developer resources when they are depleted, such as microbreaks for relaxation or nutritional intake.

Another implication for managers is the need to understand the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts afforded by IT mindfulness and its role as an antidote. We found that more IT mindful developers paid increased attention to the challenging aspects of AISDPU while seemingly defusing its threatening aspects. More IT mindful developers were also more likely to experience work engagement from AISDPU, which is needed to enhance instrumental outcomes such as well-being. Notably, less mindful developers were more likely to appraise AISDPU as a threat, which created a draining effect, resulting in decreased well-being. Because IT mindfulness has been shown to be malleable, mindfulness training interventions could be established, akin to an administered vaccine to regularly refresh agile developers’ mindful usage of IT to shape their appraisal processes and energy resources. Organizations are also increasingly using personality factors to assess important attributes in hiring and screening processes because traits can in<sup>fl</sup>uence positive motivational states and behaviors. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest there is value in screening for IT mindfulness (e.g., in job interviews or assessment centers), especially when putting together agile ISD teams. In sum, our research is important because it highlights a malleable factor by which organizations can foster constructive challenge appraisals and work engagement in agile ISD teams while mitigating counterproductive threat appraisals and resource depletion.

## Strengths, Limitations, and Future Research Directions

Our research has several strengths, including the following: the collection of intensive longitudinal data that allowed us to tap into ongoing experiences of AISDPU in developers’ natural, everyday context; the “unpacking” of opposing intrapersonal stress processes; and the separation in time and space of our focal constructs. Despite these strengths, as in any study, our work has some limitations that can be leveraged to inform future research.

First, our theoretical focus was on an aggregate-level perspective of daily AISDPU, and its conceptualization was theoretically driven, consistent with previous AISDPU research (Sarker et al. 2018). Notwithstanding this focus, because of the increased likelihood of response fatigue through intense surveying typical of ESM studies, we were constrained in our ability to collect more <sup>fi</sup>ne-grained data about speci<sup>fi</sup>c agile practices. As such, we only measured the extent of daily

AISDPU. However, the frequency, intensity, or duration of daily AISDPU may provide additional insights into its bene<sup>fi</sup>cial and burdensome effects. Moreover, we recognize that our conceptualization of AISDPU as a stressor is not fully faithful to HSPM’s primary focus on environmental demands. Although developers have some personal discretion in choosing the extent of AISDPU, albeit limited in light of their dependency on managerial decisions about the collective use of agile practices in project teams, we nevertheless believe that AISDPU can trigger consequential appraisal and stress response processes. We invite future research to decompose aggregate conceptualizations of AISDPU and focus on discrete agile methods (e.g., XP) and practices (e.g., pair programming). Together with the use of richer and more <sup>fi</sup>ne-grained measures, this will enable research at a more granular level to better understand which speci<sup>fi</sup>c daily agile practices create more or less eustress and distress. Future research may also espouse more exogenous conceptualizations of speci<sup>fi</sup>c situational (e.g., time-, change-, or transparency-related) demands inscribed in agile practices to be more consistent with HSPM’s traditional focus on environmental stressors.

Second, although the time-lagged, four-point daily measurement approach provided robust results in our longitudinal study, a range of experiences can occur in the time gaps between the measurement points. Other than developers’ resource levels and affect, we did not consider any further within-person factors that may have allowed us to examine additional short-term <sup>fl</sup>uctuations in environmental conditions. For example, how would the daily ups and downs in developers’ perceived workload (e.g., from demands unrelated to AISDPU) alter the appraisal and stress response processes induced by AISDPU in the afternoon and evening? Even though it is impossible to consider all potentially relevant controls in one study, we encourage future research to include other within- and between-person control variables (e.g., workload dynamics, task complexity) to account for or rule out alternative explanations.

A third limitation relates to potential reverse causality. Consistent with the hypothesized causal direction of relationships grounded in the HSPM, our study examined AISDPU effects on developer wellbeing in one direction. Thus, it focused on the health effects of AISDPU to the exclusion of potential health effects on AISDPU. Although we can empirically rule out some reverse causal effects via lagged measurement and robustness checks assessing potential endogeneity bias (see Online Appendix D), in reality, it is impossible to rule out all alternative temporal explanations without an interventional or (quasi-)experimental design. Future research may attempt to overcome this limitation by using such designs that allow for stronger causal inferences. Finally, our study context (e.g., all participants of the focal study were sampled from organizations in the United States) may limit the generalizability of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Future research is warranted to validate our research model across different cultures, a boundary condition that has been shown to signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly shape AISDPU’s effects (Ramesh et al. 2012).

Despite these limitations, our study provides severa directions for future research. First, consistent with the theoretical underpinnings of the HSPM, we depicted cognitive appraisals and responses to challenge and threat appraisals as parallel and independent stress processes. However, we acknowledge the possibility of reappraisals (e.g., an initial threat appraisal may be cognitively reframed as a challenge appraisal) and crossover effects (e.g., threat appraisal may discourage developers from engaging with their work). Further re search is warranted to examine these reappraisal pro cesses and crossover effects in more depth. Second, ou research focused on psychological stress responses in the HSPM that focused on developers’ energy resources to explain the opposing effects of AISDPU on thei well-being. This focus was driven by theoretical consid erations regarding the resource-intensive nature of dai ly AISDPU. Future research could also look into the physiological concomitants of AISDPU in conjunction with the psychological effects to arrive at more holistic preventive care programs and treatment strategies. Alternatively, future studies could hone in on other psychological stress indicators, such as transient and <sup>fl</sup>uctuating emotions (e.g., anxiety, hope) or cognitions (e.g., rumination), and could thus delve deeper into dif ferent kinds and severities (e.g., fatigue, burnout, de pression) of the distress subprocess. Third, we chose to focus on IT mindfulness as a potent moderator in the HSPM because it strikes at the heart of and shapes de velopers’ sensemaking process. A next compelling fron tier for moving this work forward would be to identify within-person level moderators and moderators tha are less stable than personal traits. What factors decide each day whether AISDPU is good or bad for a speci<sup>fi</sup>c person? Which conditions do we need to create on a daily basis that amplify the bene<sup>fi</sup>cial and attenuate the burdensome stress effects? Tackling such questions would pave the way for a stream of research with enormous management implications. Finally, we encourage future research to examine the wider implications of AISDPU. In particular, it would be interesting to look at how AISDPU and its well-being–enhancing and well-being–eroding effects on developers in<sup>fl</sup>uence oth er team members at work and how these effects may spill over to affect family members at home (Benlian 2020). These future efforts may shed light on the knock on effects and more far-reaching consequences of AISDPU at the work-life interface.

## Conclusion

AISDPU can be experienced as a bene<sup>fi</sup>t on one day and a burden on another, as indicated by our opening quotations. On one day, AISDPU may be appraised as a challenge, fueling work engagement and improving well-being. On another day, AISDPU may be appraised as a threat, depleting developers and impairing their well-being. By demonstrating that daily <sup>fl</sup>uctuations in their cognitive appraisals in<sup>fl</sup>uence how developers react to AISDPU, our study helps to unravel the seeming paradox that AISDPU can help and hurt developer well-being. We also showed that IT mindfulness can shape this appraisal process, such that developers with high IT mindfulness are more likely to appraise AISD-PU as a challenge and are less likely to appraise it as a threat, increasing the possibility of bene<sup>fi</sup>cial well-being effects. Together, our study paints a more holistic picture by viewing AISDPU as a stressor with both bright and dark side effects, extends AISDPU research to account for intrapersonal developer experiences, and suggests several promising areas for future research that should help render AISDPU more sustainable over time.
