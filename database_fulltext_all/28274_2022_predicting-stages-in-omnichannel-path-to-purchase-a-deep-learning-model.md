# Predicting Stages in Omnichannel Path to Purchase: A Deep Learning Model

Abstract. The proliferation of omnichannel practices and emerging technologies opens up new opportunities for companies to collect voluminous data across multiple channels. This study examines whether leveraging omnichannel data can lead to, statistically and economically, signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly better predictions on consumers’ online path-to-purchase journeys, given the intrinsic <sup>fl</sup>uidity in and heterogeneity brought forth by the digital transformation of traditional marketing. Using an omnichannel data set that captures consumers’ online behavior in terms of their website browsing trajectories and their of<sup>fl</sup>ine behavior in terms of physical location trajectories, we predict consumers’ future path-to-purchase journeys based on their historical omnichannel behaviors. Using a state-of-the-art deep-learning algorithm, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that using omnichannel data can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly improve our model’s predictive power. The lift curve analysis reveals that the omnichannel model outperforms the corresponding single-channel model by 7.38%. This enhanced predictive power bene<sup>fi</sup>ts various heterogeneous online <sup>fi</sup>rms, regardless of their size, of<sup>fl</sup>ine presence, mobile app availability, or whether they are selling single- or multicategory products. Using an illustrative example of targeted marketing, we further quantify the economic value of the improved predictive power using a cost-revenue analysis. Our paper contributes to the emerging literature on omnichannel marketing and sheds light on the inherent dynamics and <sup>fl</sup>uidity in consumers’ online path-to-purchase journeys.

## 1. Introduction

Merging the physical and digital worlds to improve our understanding of consumer behavior is piquing many companies’ interest. For instance, Amazon and Alibaba, which have successfully tracked customers online behavior for almost two decades, have recently started setting up various kinds of of<sup>fl</sup>ine touchpoints, which, in turn, help them track consumer behavior of<sup>fl</sup>ine. Many marketers, however, have traditionally viewed online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine data sources as two separate entities. Yet, they increasingly realize that connecting the two sources of data—what consumers browse/purchase online and where consumers travel of<sup>fl</sup>ine—can reveal a wealth of information. Aside from adopting omnichannel strategies, the proliferation of connected technologies, such as smartphones, sensors, and Internet of Things (IoTs), has also enabled various companies to collect granular and large-scale data across multiple channels (Ghose 2017, Wang et al. 2017,

Adamopoulos et al. 2021b). In addition, third-party data vendors can further help <sup>fi</sup>rms to easily and affordably purchase and synthesize omnichannel data (Neumann et al. 2019, Ray et al. 2020). Such advancements and developments create opportunities for novel insights for the industry and academia alike by tapping into a more holistic view of consumers’ omnichannel behaviors.

The enhanced omnichannel data collection capabili ties raise several essential questions for <sup>fi</sup>rms, with an overarching one being whether and how these companies can use omnichannel data (online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine combined) to better predict customers’ online journey toward a purchase. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, how much is the of<sup>fl</sup>ine data worth, if anything, in addition to the existing online data? What analytical tools should be leveraged to fully realize the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of omnichannel data? To answer these questions, we leverage a data set that captures consumers’ online behavior in terms of their website browsing trajectories and, at the same time, their offline behavior in terms of physical location trajectories. Using this data set and a sophisticated deep-learning-based method, extreme deep factorization machines (xDeepFM) (Lian et al. 2018), we predict the consumers future purchase journeys. In particular, following the related literature on path-to-purchase journey (Ding et al. 2015, Liaukonyte et al. 2015, Ghose et al. 2017, Ursu 2018, Ursu et al. 2020, Gopalakrishnan and Park 2021), we extract a rich set of predictors from the online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels and learn latent behavioral patterns from the data enabling us to accurately predict consumers’ purchase journey in the online world. To assess the incremental value of using omnichannel data (as opposed to using single-channel data), we compare the omnichannel model with several baseline models. In addition, to determine whether we might bene<sup>fi</sup>t from using state-of-the-art deep-learning methods, we also compare our analytical approach with several benchmark algorithms. Similarly, we further examine the predictive performance across different businesses.

We obtain several interesting <sup>fi</sup>ndings. First, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that using omnichannel data can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly improve our consumer journey models’ predictive power. This conclusion is derived by comparing a collection of predictive metrics across the model leveraging omnichannel features and the models leveraging only single-channel features. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we conduct statistical signi<sup>fi</sup>cance tests, comparative analyses, and a lift curve analysis to show the outperformance achieved by the omnichannel model. For instance, the lift curve analysis reveals that, on average, it outperforms the single-channel model by 7.38%. Moreover, the proposed model can bene<sup>fi</sup>t heterogeneous online <sup>fi</sup>rms, regardless of their size, of<sup>fl</sup>ine presence, mobile app availability, or whether they are selling single- or multicategory products. Besides, we also illustrate how this improved predictive power can be translated into economic value, discussing an illustrative example of targeted marketing.

This work contributes to the growing body of literature on omnichannel marketing and consumers’ pathto-purchase journeys. Prior literature on omnichannel marketing has documented that <sup>fi</sup>rms can bene<sup>fi</sup>t from omnichannel presence via creating more awareness (Bell et al. 2015), improving ad effectiveness (Luo et al. 2020), and boosting sales via cross-channel spillover effects (Zhang et al. 2019). However, prior research has not examined whether the integration of data from different channels could be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial for predicting each consumer’s journey, despite the managerial relevance of this question (Cui et al. 2021). Hence, the incremental value that could potentially be gained from using such data remains unknown. This work looks into this new opportunity arising from the enhanced omnichannel data collection capabilities and shows that using omnichannel data can lead to better predictions on consumers’ online path-to-purchase journey. Although prior literature on consumers’ online path-to-purchase journey has indeed discussed the case of using granular online clickstream data to make predictions, this study also examines data from another channel; that is, of<sup>fl</sup>ine geo-trajectory data. Compared with the extant literature, it is novel to observe user behavior for the same individuals across both channels. This richness of our omnichannel data set has enabled us to better predict the path-to-purchase journey, which prior studies have not investigated. Although using more granular data from the same channel is expected to increase predictive power, using data from different channels could hurt the models’ predictive performance if such features are redundant, irrelevant, or introduce too much noise. We empirically show we achieve a statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant increase in predictive performance leveraging our omnichannel data and state-of-the-art deep learning methods. Importantly, we also empirically quantify the economic impact of data by examining the extra value of omnichannel data above and beyond single-channel data. To the best of our knowledge, our study is the <sup>fi</sup>rst to show that leveraging omnichannel data can lead to, statistically and economically, signi<sup>fi</sup> cantly better predictions on consumers’ online path-topurchase journey and create better monetization potential beyond leveraging single-channel data, thereby contributing to the omnichannel streams of research.

Finally, our research yields several relevant managerial implications. Propelled by the increasing capacity to analyze data collected in an omnichannel environ ment, <sup>fi</sup>rms nowadays increasingly realize the importance of leveraging voluminous data to better under stand the customer journey. Our study offers the valuable insight that <sup>fi</sup>rms can tap into omnichannel data to generate more precise predictions and therefore make improved business decisions in various areas, such as online ad targeting, digital attribution, advertising budget allocation, and web-based personalization. Furthermore, the predictive framework in this work provides an actionable strategy for managers to identify which customers to target based on the path to purchase journey predictions. For instance, the boost in predictive power can enable them to target customers more precisely, bring in more leads, and eventually convert these leads into sales. Similarly, managers can use our framework to personalize marketing promotions based on the predicted path to purchase journey. This, in turn, can improve for instance <sup>fi</sup>rms’ multi channel attribution and advertising budget allocation decisions. Nevertheless, the cost-revenue analysis attached to the proposed prediction framework can also help companies decide whether they should invest in such data at all. We further discuss additional implications in the discussion section of this manuscript.

## 2. Literature Review

This section discusses the related literature and how this study builds on and extends various streams of research in information systems (IS) and marketing.

## 2.1. Omnichannel Marketing

First, this work is related to the stream of literature on omnichannel marketing. With increased digitization, consumers today interact with <sup>fi</sup>rms across various channels (Adamopoulos and Todri 2015, Ghose 2017), which, in turn, has led to a shift toward “omnichannel” marketing that emphasizes a uni<sup>fi</sup>ed consumer experience (Verhoef et al. 2015, Cui et al. 2021). Existing research on omnichannel marketing has indeed emphasized that <sup>fi</sup>rms could bene<sup>fi</sup>t from their omnichannel presence (Bell et al. 2015; Zhang et al. 2019; Kumar et al. 2019; Luo et al. 2020).

The integration of customer experience across channels also opens up new opportunities for data-driven value creation. A <sup>fi</sup>rm running a business in a single channel can only track the consumer within that channel. By contrast, in an omnichannel environment, a consumer probably interacts with a <sup>fi</sup>rm in multiple channels, enabling the <sup>fi</sup>rm to track customer-speci<sup>fi</sup>c experiences across different channels. Hence, online <sup>fi</sup>rms adding of<sup>fl</sup>ine channels expand their footprints in the of<sup>fl</sup>ine channel, resulting in increased data-collection touchpoints (Cui et al. 2021). Such opportunities include tracking consumers across these increased touchpoints, collecting more voluminous and diverse data of consumers in the of<sup>fl</sup>ine channel, and creating extra value using an additional data source.

One valuable data resource in the of<sup>fl</sup>ine world made available to practitioners and researchers in recent years is individual-level geo-trajectory data. Unlocking the value of of<sup>fl</sup>ine location data to empower innovation is drawing signi<sup>fi</sup>cant attention from practitioners, as indicated by the wide adoption of location-based services (Luo et al. 2013, Andrews et al. 2016, Ghose 2017, Adamopoulos et al. 2021a), and many startups such as SafeGraph<sup>1</sup> and X-mode Social.<sup>2</sup> It also has gained ground in academic research as there has been a surging strand of literature studying location data-enabled applications in the past decade (Ghose et al. 2012, 2019, 2020; Hui et al. 2013; Luo et al. 2013; Molitor et al. 2020). Despite its growing importance in practice and research, relatively few studies have investigated how much value can be added by investment in analyzing data collected in a different channel. The proliferation of omnichanne practices and new technologies makes it possible to collect granular and large-scale data across multiple channels, amplifying the chances at hand for research. To sum, in this paper, we look into a new opportunity arising from the enhanced omnichannel collection capability and show that making good use of data collected across channels can indeed create extra business value.

## 2.2. Path-to-Purchase Journey

Another related stream of literature is the work examining the consumer path-to-purchase journey. Researchers have long sought a better understanding of how consumers arrive at their purchase decisions. Prior literature suggested that consumers usually get through several discrete stages before purchasing a product, also known as the path-to-purchase journey (Strong 1925, Lavidge and Steiner 1961, Barry and Howard 1990, Tellis 2003). The traditional marketing framework that was used to capture the <sup>fi</sup>xed and sequential stages of a typical consumer’s decision path toward the <sup>fi</sup>nal purchase is the attention-to-interestto-desire-to-action (AIDA) framework (Strong 1925). Although the original AIDA framework has been vastly modi<sup>fi</sup>ed and expanded by later work (Lavidge and Steiner 1961), this stream of literature has adhered to the theme that consumers <sup>fi</sup>rst become aware of and interested in the <sup>fi</sup>rm/product and then they search, feel, and take actions (Chandukala et al. 2011). In the past, because of lack of granular and precise data and limitations in measurement of ad effectiveness, advertisers used the traditional AIDA marketing framework. However, it is important to acknowledge that things have dramatically changed in recent years with the digital transformation of marketing and advertising and that the path to purchase journey is not necessarily <sup>fi</sup>xed or sequential for consumers.

Understanding how consumers move through the path-to-purchase journey and the inherent dynamics, heterogeneity, and <sup>fl</sup>uidity in that journey is an important area of research. Lemon and Verhoef (2016) have highlighted that understanding the customer journey and experience over time is critical for <sup>fi</sup>rms. For instance, prior studies have shown that it can be vital for designing competitive online advertising strategies (Sayedi et al. 2014), managing word-of-mouth systems (Huang et al. 2019), avoiding annoyance in consumers (Todri et al. 2020), and overall optimizing marketing efforts (Gopalakrishnan and Park 2021, Lee and Hosanagar 2021). These studies primarily rely on consumers’ behavioral data (e.g., website browsing data) to model how consumers interact with the environment and make decisions (Montgomery et al. 2004, Hui et al. 2009, Todri et al. 2020, Gopalakrishnan and Park 2021). Previous literature has focused on merely using single-channel data to analyze consumers’ online behavior (Park and Park 2016, Shukla et al. 2021) or to predict consumers’ online journey (Moe 2003, Montgomery et al. 2004, Lee et al. 2018), whereas no prior work has used omnichannel data to predict the journey. Nevertheless, the increasingly available off line location data nudges us to think about the chan ces of augmenting the prior studies that merely exploited data collected in a single channel.

## 2.3. Omnichannel Data for Purchase Journey Stage Predictions

This work extends the relevant streams of literature by illustrating whether the use of omnichannel data for matched individuals can, in practice, reduce prediction errors and improve the predictive performance of path-to-purchase journey models. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to use omnichannel data to predict various pieces of the consumers journey. Although more granular data from the same channel is expected to increase predictive power, using data from different channels could hurt the models’ predictive performance if such features are redundant, irrelevant, or introduce too much noise (Kelleher et al. 2020). Moreover, we also contribute to the literature quantifying the economic impact of the omnichannel data by empirically deriving the extra monetary value generated compared with singlechannel data. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the <sup>fi</sup>rst to assess the economic signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of omnichannel data above and beyond single-channel data. Thus, our work contributes to several streams of work in the omnichannel marketing literature, such as incorporating new data and analytics and predicting consumer behavior (Lemon and Verhoef 2016, Fisher et al. 2019, Cui et al. 2021, Verhoef et al. 2021).

## 3. Data and Features

In this section, we discuss in detail our data set and the main variables of interest.

## 3.1. Data Overview

In particular, our omnichannel data set comes from a major telecommunications provider. This data set includes a random sample of 1,397 customers from a metropolis area for 10 weeks, from early-July to mid-September 2017. The raw data set has two parts, accounting for consumer behaviors across both the online (digital) and of<sup>fl</sup>ine (physical) worlds.

The online part of the data set records customers online browsing trajectories in terms of uniform resource locators (URLs) visits with timestamps. For example, we may observe the following browsing record for a consumer in our data set (user ID: F4X1-6M7q-3LXp, URL: www.amazon.com/dp/B07ZZVXdF2/, time: 10:00:00 a.m. on Aug 2nd, 2017). In total, our data set contains approximately 7 million visit records to 5,318 distinct website domains. It is worth noting that our data differ from cookie data, which are widely used in previous studies but have certain limitations (Montes et al. 2019).

In addition to collecting online browsing data, the <sup>fi</sup>rm also collects of<sup>fl</sup>ine geolocation data via mobile tracking. The of<sup>fl</sup>ine part of the data set records customers’ of<sup>fl</sup>ine traveling trajectories in terms of geolocations with timestamps. For example, we may observe the following geolocation record for the same customer as above (user ID: F4X1-6M7q-3LXp, longitude: 121.499128, latitude: 31.235378, time: 11:00:00 a.m. on Aug 2nd, 2017). The of<sup>fl</sup>ine part of the data set contains approximately 16 million geolocation records and more than 396,000 unique locations.

The online and of<sup>fl</sup>ine parts of the data set are linked by the encrypted user IDs, based on which we can merge customers’ omnichannel behaviors, and do not include any consumer names or demographics.<sup>3</sup>

## 3.2. Online Trajectory Data and Path-to-Purchase Consumer Journey

Prior work notes that consumers usually get through several discrete stages in the path-to-purchase journey (Strong 1925, Lavidge and Steiner 1961, Chandukala et al. 2011, Gopalakrishnan and Park 2021). This sec tion demonstrates how we derived from the online data set the corresponding variables related to the different stages in the online path-to-purchase journey. In particular, we started with parsing the domains (namely, the website names) from URLs. For example, after parsing, the domain name amazon.com will be extracted from the full URL www.amazon.com/dp/ B07ZZVXdF2/. We then queried an IBM API (api. xforce.ibmcloud.com) to categorize each website domain into different categories of interest (e.g., “Shopping”). By doing so, we can translate the raw URL data into meaningful online activities, which enables us to determine, for instance, if a customer was shopping online or sur<sup>fi</sup>ng on social media. This step yielded 50 distinct categories for the 5,318 domains, among which 381 domains belong to the Shopping category; nonshopping domains are still used in the model as described in the next section. With this, we next describe how we extract from the data the consumer path-to-purchase path decisions. We followed the recent literature (Gopalakrishnan and Park 2021) to de<sup>fi</sup>ne three distinct journey stages: the visit stage, followed by the consideration stage, followed by the purchase stage, as described next. This framework does not mean that every consumer necessarily goes through this <sup>fi</sup>xed number of structured stages nor does it imply that they go through it in a sequential manner.

3.2.1. Stage 1: Visit. The <sup>fi</sup>rst stage of the corresponding consumer decision-making is deciding whether to visit the e-commerce website (Gopalakrishnan and Park 2021). To nudge the consumer into this stage, the advertisers usually need to create brand awareness or af<sup>fi</sup>liation with their product or service. For example, advertisers can build awareness with display ads. Consumers seeing the display ads may become aware of the corresponding brand or product. If they are interested, they will click on the ads and move to visit the shopping website, which signals that this consumer enters into the interest stage in the AIDA framework. Operationalizing the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage of the journey as a customer visit is in accordance with prior literature on path-to-purchase journeys (Gopalakrishnan and Park 2021).

3.2.2. Stage 2: Consideration. Conditional on a visit, a customer may spend more time engaging in search activities, indicating that he/she is currently considering the corresponding products. This further signals that the focal shopping website has grabbed the customer’s attention and that the customer is developing a favorable disposition toward the brand or product (i.e., the customer has entered the desire stage in the AIDA framework). For instance, the literature on consumer search has documented that consumers usually spend a considerable amount of time searching, as the average search duration is 3.47 minutes, conditional on a click (Ursu et al. 2020). Following this extant literature, we can detect if a consumer enters the consideration stage by looking at whether s/he stayed on the same shopping website domain for more than <sup>fi</sup>ve minutes. One concern though is that we cannot determine whether each customer was truly spending time searching and acquiring information or whether s/he was staying put. Hence, we only consider customer search activities during this window if they browsed at least two web pages. This additional classi<sup>fi</sup>cation rule is supported by the prior literature showing that consumers usually browse more than one webpages (e.g., landing pages and gallery pages) to acquire adequate information (Ursu 2018) and that the number of web pages viewed can signal customers’ shopping intention (Ding et al. 2015).

3.2.3. Stage 3: Purchase. The <sup>fi</sup>nal stage of the journey where consumers decide whether to convert their visit(s) and subsequent consideration into a purchase typically has corresponded to the “action” stage in the AIDA framework (Lavidge and Steiner 1961, Gopalakrishnan and Park 2021). Using our data set, this stage can be inferred by examining whether there are any online payment or buying-related activities within 10 minutes of the latest online consideration activity at the corresponding shopping domain. The purchase activities can be recognized by analyzing the browsed URLs. For example, visiting paypal.com or amazon.com/gp/buy can serve as good evidence that this consumer has indeed arrived at the purchase stage.

Following these steps, we derived three path-topurchase journey variables for each customer and each shopping domain. We found that in total there are 437,000 shopping website visits, 35,000 consideration stage sessions, and 29,000 purchases across all customers and domains throughout the whole period of our data set; these correspond to an average of 31.28 shopping website visits, 2.49 consideration sessions, and 2.03 purchases per customer per week. Descriptive statistics of these variables across all 381 shopping domains are presented in Table 1.

Apart from the consumer journey variables, we also obtained the number of visits per customer per week for all 50 website categories to use as additional features in the predictive models. Descriptive statistics of all online features are given in Table A9 in the online appendix.

## 3.3. Offline Trajectory Data and POI-Empowered Features

To enable our model to capture valuable information from the of<sup>fl</sup>ine raw data, we also reverse geocoded each latitude and longitude pair, matching them with a POI in the physical world. To conduct this matching, we used a POI data set purchased from an online map provider. This POI data set includes almost all public locations and businesses in the focal city and categorizes these locations into 11 POI categories (e.g., business, park, hotel).<sup>4</sup> We matched all the geolocations in our data set with the nearest POI in terms of Haversian distance. For example, the location (121.499128, 31.235378) was matched with the “Shangri-La Hotel” under the “Hotel” category. Next, we counted the number of visits to these location categories by each customer during each week to use as additional features in the predictive models. We used 5 minutes as the threshold to distinguish a visit (e.g., spending 30 minutes in a restaurant) from merely passing by the corresponding business. Descriptive statistics of these of<sup>fl</sup>ine features are given in Table A9. The data points were aggregated at the user-week level, yielding 13,970 ( 1,397 10) observations for all the variables.

## 4. Model Development

We considered a state-of-the-art deep-learning method as the main predictive tool for our empirical analysis. In addition to the deep-learning model, to examine whether companies should use such advanced state-of-the-art algorithms to fully realize the bene<sup>fi</sup>t from using omnichannel data and to ensure the robustness of the results, we also conducted the same predictive tasks with alternative widely-used predictive methods, as discussed in the following subsections.

## 4.1. Deep Learning–Based Predictive Modeling Approach

4.1.1. Deep-Learning Method. In particular, we leveraged the method of eXtreme Deep Factorization Machines (xDeepFM; Lian et al. 2018), a cutting-edge deep-learning-based predictive algorithm, to conduct our predictive analysis. In the next paragraphs, we provide an overview of its key characteristics, major advancements over previous methods, and how it can <sup>fi</sup>t well our research context. We use xDeepFM as our main predictive algorithm as it allows us to use very atomic data features while the model can learn and take advantage of the latent behavioral characteristics of consumers.

At a high-level, Figure 1 shows the architecture of xDeepFM, in which we can see that xDeepFM combines three modules: (1) a plain deep neural network (DNN) that learns high-order feature interactions at the bit-wise level; (2) a compressed interaction network (CIN) that learns high-order feature interactions at the vector-wise level; and (3) a linear part that learns the linear relationship. The <sup>fi</sup>nal output of this model is the combination of the outputs of these three modules and corresponds to the predicted score of the corresponding target variable.

A notable advancement of xDeepFM over DNNs is its ability to learn vector-wise feature interactions empowered by the CIN module. Traditionally, DNNs take the embeddings of all features concatenated together as input and learn the feature interaction at the bit-wise level. To illustrate this nuance intuitively, suppose we use a vector to represent the embedding of a feature as $\boldsymbol { v } _ { 1 } = \left[ v _ { 1 1 } , v _ { 1 2 } \right]$ and another vector to represent the embedding of another feature as $v _ { 2 } = \left[ v _ { 2 1 } , v _ { 2 2 } \right]$ . A DNN usually takes the concatenated embedding $\boldsymbol { v } = [ v _ { 1 1 } , v _ { 1 2 } , v _ { 2 1 } , v _ { 2 2 } ]$ as input and learns the interactions of the elements within an embedding vector $( \mathrm { e . g . } , \ v _ { 1 1 } \cdot v _ { 1 2 } )$ . In contrast, the CIN module learns the interactions via the product of two vectors (namely, $v _ { 1 } \cdot v _ { 2 } )$ . Through combining these modules, xDeepFM gains several advantages over previous methods because it can accommodate and learn richer sets of feature interactions. Hence, the xDeepFM provides relevant advantages that could lead to a higher predictive performance in our omnichannel setting.

The xDeepFM method can be trained ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently by minimizing the objective function $\mathcal { T } ,$ , which is the log loss plus the regularization term:

$$
\mathcal {J} = - \frac {1}{N} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} y _ {i} \log \widehat {y _ {i}} + (1 - y _ {i}) (1 - \log \widehat {y _ {i}}) + \lambda_ {*} \Theta ,\tag{1}
$$

where N is the number of training instances, $y _ { i }$ is the true outcome, $\widehat { y _ { i } }$ is the predicted score, λ denotes the regularization term, and Θ denotes the set of model parameters of all three modules. Python packages Keras<sup>5</sup> and Tensor<sup>fl</sup>ow<sup>6</sup> were used to build and train the model. We trained the model using the standard architecture (e.g., DNN hidden units 256, CIN layer size 128, and activation function relu) <sup>fi</sup>rst and then tuned the hyperparameters (i.e., DNN hidden units, CIN layer size, activation function, dropout, and learning rate) by grid-searching on the validation set, following Lian et al. (2018). We also avoid potential over<sup>fi</sup>tting issues with early stopping (Rudin and Carlson 2019) and tuning the number of epochs following Malik and Singh (2019).

4.1.2. Target Variables. Our goal is to predict who will visit, consider a product, and make a purchase at which domain, as previously discussed. Therefore, three response (target) variables are constructed for every user-domain pair: 1(user ID, domain, visit), 1(user ID, domain, consideration), and 1(user ID, domain, purchase), where 1( ) represents the indicator function. For example, if the customer with the anony mized user ID F4X1-6M7q-3LXp visits amazon.com during the prediction period, then 1(F4X1-6M7q 3LXp, amazon.com, visit) would be true, and false otherwise. Hence, the output of the model is the numeric (continuous) predicted score of the corresponding (binary) target variable. Regarding the input to the model, each row in our training sample has the format: (user ID, domain ID, response variables, and predictors), and the model takes all the available predictors as the input. That is, all features listed in Table A9, along with the user ID and domain ID, are used as predictors; we further describe in the next paragraph the exact predictors that are available for each model. To fully leverage the architecture of our model, we used one-hot encoding for user IDs and domain IDs. Besides, all the numerical predictors were normalized following best practices for deep-learning methods (Goodfellow et al. 2016).

## 4.2. Evaluation Protocols

Following the extant literature, to rigorously train and evaluate our models, we use the time-based nested cross-validation approach (Tashman 2000, Bergmeir and Ben´ıtez 2012, Geva et al. 2017); all our results are robust to using simple holdout evaluation. Figure 2 provides a visual representation of the time-based nested cross-validation approach showing how exactly the data were partitioned to each fold’s training and test set. In particular, starting from the <sup>fi</sup>rst week, we generated a sample in which the predictors are constructed by averaging the data taken from the <sup>fi</sup>rst <sup>fi</sup>ve weeks, and the target variables are constructed using the data captured from the following two weeks.<sup>8</sup> This setting enables us to leverage historical data to forecast who will visit, consider, and purchase a product at which domain in the future, avoiding any data leakage. For example, given that we know the visits to online websites (namely, the online features) and the visits to of<sup>fl</sup>ine POIs (namely, the offline features) for a customer in the past <sup>fi</sup>ve weeks, this setting enables us to predict the likelihood of this customer visiting, considering a purchase, and <sup>fi</sup>nally purchasing at each shopping domains (e.g., amazon.- com) in the coming two weeks; we also conduct additional robustness checks varying the time windows.<sup>9</sup> As such, each sample makes use of data of seven weeks. We repeated such a sample generation process until all 10-week data were fully used, which forms a type of “moving window” approach (Geva et al. 2017, Song et al. 2019), yielding four samples, as Figure 2 illustrates. For each sample, we trained the model using 10-fold cross-validation to <sup>fi</sup>nd the optimal hyperparameter using the training set and evaluate the model on the hold out (test) set. We computed the mean and variance of the evaluation metrics to conduct statistical tests later on and further examine any differences in performance across the different models and algorithms.

The current research contributes to the growing body of literature on omnichannel marketing and consumers’ online path-to-purchase journeys while also providing actionable implications for academic researchers and industrial practitioners.

Our study yields several implications for the emerging IS and marketing research on omnichannel marketing and consumer journey predictions. Omnichannel marketing has been perceived as a promising area for both research and practice. This perception is based on conventional wisdom in industry and existing research acknowledging that using additional data/features can bring a wealth of information (Ghose 2017, Adamopoulos et al. 2018). Despite its growing importance in business, relatively few studies have investigated the value of leveraging omnichannel data over single-channel data. Nevertheless, the proliferation of mobile and tracking technologies makes it possible to collect granular and large-scale data across multiple channels, amplifying the chances at hand for research. Thus, our work focuses on how consumers’ behavioral data collected across multiple channels can be used to make more precise predictions about consumers’ purchase journeys.

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study also provide important managerial implications for various players in the industry. First, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings have direct implications for online retailers, who increasingly realize nowadays that they would bene<sup>fi</sup>t from combining multiple data sources. Our study offers a crucial insight that online retailers can tap into omnichannel data for better predictions about consumers’ path-to-purchase journeys and illustrate that various online retailers can bene<sup>fi</sup>t from it. The corresponding boost in predictive power can enable them to target customers more precisely, bring in more leads, and eventually convert these leads into sales. In addition, our illustrative application with pro<sup>fi</sup>t curves provides an actionable strategy for managers to identify which customers to target based on the predictions. Managers can, thus, use our framework to personalize marketing promotions and incentives based on the predictions of the consumer path to purchase journey.

Furthermore, consumers’ path to purchasing journey has signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly evolved as digital advertising has expanded the opportunities for advertisers to reach potential purchasers, making different digital advertising channels effective substitutes. Because of lack of granular user level data and limitations in measurement of ad effectiveness, advertisers in the past used the traditional (AIDA) marketing funnel to create separation in advertising budgets. However, we should keep in mind that the ease of measurement of ad effectiveness has led to the growing substitutability of various digital advertising channels, the unraveling of the traditional marketing funnel from a static one to a more dynamic one, and to advertisers increasingly willing to reallocate dollars in their advertising budg ets in a <sup>fl</sup>uid manner across channels. Hence, implica tions from our paper should be used carefully by brands and advertisers keeping in mind these changes.

Finally, the economic signi<sup>fi</sup>cance analyses provided in this work may justify why it would be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial for such companies to adopt an omnichannel strategy, by quantifying the monetary value that <sup>fi</sup>rms might obtain based on their omnichannel view into customer behaviors. Companies can use such an estimate of the added monetary value to make decisions about data investment. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings also have managerial implications for third-party data vendors. With an increasing number of industrial practices and academic research into synthesizing, utilizing, and monetizing omnichannel data, we start to observe new business models enabling retailers to quickly and affordably purchase such data from third-party providers. For example, a major telecommunications provider in the United States has recently announced that (unless consumers opt-out) it will share customers’ web and mobile app data with advertisers.<sup>17</sup> Similarly, we observe more and more consumer data available for sale, and tracking such of<sup>fl</sup>ine behavior becomes standard practice. For instance, companies like X-mode Social and SafeGraph exemplify the new business model of “location intelligence,” which allows advertisers to create more accurate and ef<sup>fi</sup>cient marketing solutions based on consumers’ location data. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings justify that this kind of data exchange business models can bene<sup>fi</sup>t both the sellers and buyers of such data, should they comply with relevant laws and regulations.

This paper has several limitations that could be explored in future research. First, we were not able to observe the journey in terms of speci<sup>fi</sup>c products or additional journey stages (e.g., adding to the cart) merely from analyzing the URLs. Second, we did not have access to any data on of<sup>fl</sup>ine purchases. Third, although the conventional wisdom suggests that consumers have a structured or static path-to-purchase journey, in practice, consumers actually move back and forth between different phases in their path-to-purchase journey (e.g., a customer who reached the consideration stage may go back to the visit stage). As our paper focuses on predicting stages in the consumer journey separately, we do not impose any structure on the stage transitions. Future work might want to examine the transition patterns between different stages and the nonsequential and <sup>fl</sup>uid nature of consumer journey across stages. Notwithstanding these limitations, we hope that our work can spur more research toward understanding how to harness the full potential of omnichannel data to better understand the consumer journey.
