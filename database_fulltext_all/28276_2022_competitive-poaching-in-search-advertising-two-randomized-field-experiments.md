# Competitive Poaching in Search Advertising: Two Randomized Field Experiments

Abstract. Keyword searches with brand names occur very commonly on search engines, enabling <sup>fi</sup>rms to infer not only when consumers are searching for them but also when consumers are searching for their competitors. This enables <sup>fi</sup>rms to generate traf<sup>fi</sup>c from search advertising by bidding not only on their own keywords but also on competitors’ keywords. The strategy of bidding on competitors’ keywords, known as competitive poaching, present unique opportunities for academic research. In this research, we examine the factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence the effectiveness of competitive poaching, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally the role of different ad copies and the type of competitor (poached brand) from which a brand is poaching. We also examine how the presence of sponsored ads from the poached brand affects competitive poaching. We collected data from two randomized <sup>fi</sup>eld experiments, one with a business school in the Northeastern United States and the other one with a leading automobile dealership company, where these <sup>fi</sup>rms bid on keywords of competing brands and randomly display different types of ad copies in the sponsored search listings. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that, when poaching on keywords of high-quality brands, ad copies that feature vertical differentiation are more effective than the control ad copies, which do not convey any differentiation or prescriptive messages. We also <sup>fi</sup>nd that when poaching from low-quality brands, ad copies featuring horizontal differentiation perform better than the control ad copies. Finally, we show that the presence of the poached brand’s own ad has a positive association with the ad effectiveness of the poaching brand when that poached brand is high quality and a negative association when the poached brand is low quality. The academic and managerial contributions are also discussed.

## 1. Introduction

Online advertising is one of the fastest growing areas in advertising with online ad spending reaching \$124.6 billion in 2019, up 15.9% from 2018. In particular, online search advertising constitutes a dominant and fast-growing fraction, contributing 43.9% of all online ad spending, up 12.9% from 2018 (Interactive Advertising Bureau 2019). In search advertising, consumers search for speci<sup>fi</sup>c keywords on search engines, such as Google and Bing, and advertisers submit bids to have their ads (also known as sponsored search results or sponsored ads) appear alongside the search results (known as organic search results). Search engines use proprietary algorithms that consider the bid amount, the ads’ click potential, and advertiser characteristics to rank these ads in the sponsored search results (Agarwal et al.

2015). Previous research mainly examines how a <sup>fi</sup>rm can improve the effectiveness of search advertising when consumers search for keywords related to its products or services. These studies have fo cused on the importance of ad position (e.g., Ghose and Yang 2009, Agarwal et al. 2011), keyword characteristics (e.g., Ghose and Yang 2009, Agarwal et al. 2011, Gong et al. 2018), and the effect of competition from other sponsored ads (e.g., Agarwal et al. 2011, Agarwal and Mukhopadhyay 2016). However, little prior work focuses on how <sup>fi</sup>rms can increase the effectiveness of search advertising when consumers search for competitors’ keywords.

The use of brand-speci<sup>fi</sup>c keywords, such as “Apple” or “BMW,” as search keywords by consumers is very common. In fact, keywords related to brands are among the most commonly used keywords

on search engines; and 9 of the top 10 keywords on Google are related to a brand.<sup>1</sup> Although consumers reveal their preferences through any keywords they search for, brand search is unique in that information about preferences is conveyed through the type of brand that they search for, giving opportunities for marketers to appeal to those preferences through their advertising strategies. By typing the name of a brand, a consumer signals awareness of and interest in the brand and a preference for the attributes associated with that brand. This gives competing <sup>fi</sup>rms a unique opportunity to provide sponsored ads in a way that shows themselves as alternatives to the focal brand, thus expanding consumers’ consideration set (Animesh et al. 2011, Narayanan and Kalyanam 2015). As a result, many <sup>fi</sup>rms bid for placements in sponsored search results not only when consumers search for keywords related to their own brands but also for keywords related to competitors’ brands, a strategy known as competitive poaching (Sayedi et al. 2014). Figure 1 shows an example of competitive poaching in the context of higher education. In this case, a keyword search for “Carlson MS in Business Analytics” shows sponsored ads from other universities, such as Crieghton University, College of William and Mary, and Full Sail University, in addition to the sponsored ad from the Carlson School of Man agement, University of Minnesota itself.

Competitive poaching is a widespread practice across many industries. A quick online search for common brands reveals that competitive poaching is practiced in industries such as automobile, software, food delivery, grocery, solar, and banking and <sup>fi</sup>nancial services, among many others. Anecdotal evidence suggests that <sup>fi</sup>rms are increasingly using competitive poaching as a strategy to drive additional competitor traf<sup>fi</sup>c to thei own websites as well as to increase consumer awareness and brand recall (Erksine 2018, Smith 2018, Bond 2020). Prior academic literature—both analytical (Sayedi et al. 2014) and empirical (Simonov et al. 2018)—suggests that competitive poaching is bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to <sup>fi</sup>rms for various reasons. For example, poaching from larger brands can be an effective search advertising strategy, especially for smaller brands (Simonov et al. 2018, Simonov and Hill 2020). Simonov et al. (2018) show that competitors can steal substantial traf<sup>fi</sup>c from poaching a brand, especially if the poached brand has no ad listed on the sponsored listing. Further, bidding on brand keywords is less expensive than bidding on generic keywords.<sup>2</sup> Consumers who search for a brand are also at a more advanced stage in the purchase process (than consumers who search for generic keywords) and are more likely to convert (Rutz and Bucklin 2011).<sup>3</sup> Therefore, competitive poaching can be a cost-effective way of generating leads.

Even though it is illegal for advertisers to directly use a competitor’s brand name or trademark in advertising (Smith 2018, Powley 2020), bidding on a competitor’s keyword is considered legal and in fact encouraged by search engines (Simonov et al. 2018). Google started to allow competitive poaching in 2004 and has been a vocal defender of this practice by <sup>fi</sup>ghting and prevailing in several court cases brought on by brand trademark owners against competitive poaching. Prior literature has shown that, from search engines’ perspective, poaching can be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial as competition drives up bid amounts in the keyword auction and thus leads to the additional pro<sup>fi</sup>t being garnered by the search engine (Desai et al. 2014, Sayedi et al. 2014, Simonov et al. 2018). For example, Sayedi et al. (2014), using an analytica model, <sup>fi</sup>nd that poaching can bene<sup>fi</sup>t the search engine when budget asymmetry is small between the poaching and the poached <sup>fi</sup>rms; when budget asymmetry is large, the search engine bene<sup>fi</sup>ts by allowing poaching but controlling the extent of poaching. Desai et al. (2014) <sup>fi</sup>nd that poaching may create a prisoner’s dilemma scenario and the search engine captures the lost pro<sup>fi</sup>ts.

Despite the growing interest in competitive poaching in industry and early analytical and empirical work demonstrating its usefulness, there has been little empirical investigation as to how to make poaching more effective. Researchers, such as Sayedi et al. (2014), Desai et al. (2014), Du et al. (2017), and Simonov et al. (2018), have examined various aspects of competitive poaching and its underlying mechanisms but do not investigate factors that can increase the effectiveness of competitive poaching. With marketers across industries increasingly spending their advertising budgets on competitive poaching, effective strategies while poaching from competitors is crucial and is the objective of our study.

Brand search presents a context wherein a consumer reveals their preference for the type of product through the brand keyword for which they search. A key attribute associated with brand equity is perceived quality (Aaker 1996). Prior theoretical work in search advertising has focused on quality as a main differentiator of vertically differentiated brands (e.g., Jerath et al. 2011, Desai et al. 2014). Prior literature in search advertising has also shown “consumers have brand bias in clicking on search engine results (Leong et al. 2012) and brands can serve to signal product or service quality (Jacoby et al. 1971, Wernerfelt 1988, Rao and Monroe 1989)” (Agarwal and Mukhopadhyay 2016, p. 540). This gives <sup>fi</sup>rms the opportunity to appeal to those preferences through appropriate differentiation strategies.

In the context of search advertising where the length and format of the ad are strictly dictated by the search engines and consumers have low search intensity, effective ad copy design is arguably one of the most essential aspects for marketers to position themselves favorably with consumers as well as differentiate themselves against competitors (Animesh et al. 2011). In competitive poaching where <sup>fi</sup>rms target consumers to make them switch from their initial choices, the <sup>fi</sup>rms’ positioning strategy must be tailored according to the type of product the consumer is interested in as inferred from the kind of brand that he or she is searching for. Descriptive and normative models underlying consumer search posit that consumers differ in terms of their needs and product attribute preferences (Bell and Lattin 1998). These preferences may be driven by quality (consumers seeking quality products) or nonquality at tributes, such as <sup>fi</sup>t (consumers seeking products that match their preferences), the two most important attributes of any product (Wolinsky 1983, Levin and Johnson 1984). Thus, the type of information sought and acquired by consumers depends on their product attribute preferences. Although qualityseeking consumers have a higher willingness to pay for higher-quality products (Wolinsky 1983, Desai et al. 2001), for consumers seeking <sup>fi</sup>t, their willingness to pay is commensurate with their preferences being met by the speci<sup>fi</sup>c product’s attributes. This motivates us to choose quality as one of the key attributes to investigate in this study.

The main objective of our research is to examine the effectiveness of ad copy variations in driving the effectiveness of competitive poaching, where the focal advertiser (hereafter referred to as the “poaching brand” or “focal brand”) bids on the keywords of competitors (hereafter referred to as the “poached brand” or “target brand”). We speci<sup>fi</sup>cally explore how the quality of the poached brand plays a role in the relationship between the type of ad copy and the effectiveness of competitive poaching. That is, we examine whether the poaching brand should provide different ad copies when poaching consumers from a high-quality competitor versus a low-quality competitor. Few studies have examined how ad copy variations may drive the effectiveness of search advertising, and the primary focus of such studies has been on the effect of ad messages on the performance of the focal brand’s ad (e.g., Animesh et al. 2011). There is a lack of understanding of how ad copies in<sup>fl</sup>uence the effectiveness of competitive poaching. Furthermore, although prior literature suggests that the quality of the poached brand plays an important role in the effectiveness of competitive poaching (Desai et al. 2014), it is unclear how advertisers should design ad copies when poaching from competitors with varied quality levels. Our <sup>fi</sup>rst research question is as follows:

How does the effectiveness of different ad copies in competitive poaching vary by the quality of the poached brand?

Following advertising literature, we consider two broad categories of ad messages: informative ad messages, which contain veri<sup>fi</sup>able information about product attributes (Resnik and Stern 1977), and noninformative ad messages (Porter and Golan 2006, Berger and Milkman 2012). Drawing from the product differentiation literature (Gabszwicz and Thisse 1986), we further consider two types of informative ad copies: vertical differentiation ad copies that highlight quality related attributes and horizontal differentiation ad copies that highlight non-quality-related attributes that are rooted in consumer tastes (Tremblay and Polasky 2002). For noninformative ad copies, we consider prescriptive ad copies that contain messages that prescribe consumers to use the product through various aspects of the consumer’s personality, such as emotion, humor, and small talk, which have been shown to increase message sharing and overall engagement (Porter and Golan 2006, Berger and Milkman 2012, Lee et al. 2018).

Further, we seek to investigate the role of the presence of the poached brand’s own ad. Consumer preferences for brands may be driven by the consideration set of ads that they see on the search engine (Narayanan and Kalyanam 2015). The presence of competing ads provides more options to consumers to choose from and can modulate the consumers consideration set, which in turn in<sup>fl</sup>uences their click decisions. Simonov et al. (2018) show that although competitors steal 1%–4% of the traf<sup>fi</sup>c if the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm has its own ad in the sponsored search page, this can be as high as 18%–42% if the focal brand has no ad listed in the sponsored listing, which suggests that the presence of the poached brand’s own ad is likely to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the ad performance of the poaching brand. However, it is unclear how the effect of the presence of the poached brand’s own ad may vary by the quality of the poached brand. Prior literature on competition in regular search advertising has shown that competing high- (low-) quality ads appearing above the focal ad has a positive (negative) effect on the click-through of the focal ad (Jeziorski and Segal 2015, Agarwal and Mukhopadhyay 2016), suggesting that the effect of competing ads varies depending on the quality of these competing ads. However, whether these effects hold when bidding on competitors is an open question. Thus, our second research question is as follows:

What is the association between the presence of poached brand’s own ad and the effectiveness of competitive poaching, and how does this association vary by the quality of the poached brand?

To examine these research questions, we collected data from two randomized <sup>fi</sup>eld experiments, both of which ran search advertising campaigns on Google. In Study 1, we collaborated with a business school located in the Northeastern United States, one of whose graduate programs was ranked among the top 20 according to U.S. News & World Report during the study period. For con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality, we refer to this school as XYZ University. We ran a three-month search advertising campaign and bid on a list of competitor keywords. A user who searched one of the competitors keywords was randomly exposed to one of four ad copies of XYZ University, namely, (1) an ad copy featuring vertical differentiation, (2) an ad copy featuring horizontal differentiation, (3) an ad copy featuring a prescriptive message, and (4) an ad copy without any differentiation or prescriptive messages serving as the control ad copy. By randomly varying the ad copy, we test our hypothesis on which type of ad copy im proves ad performance. In Study 2, we similarly collaborated with a leading automobile dealership company in the Northeastern United States and collected data from a search advertising campaign as in Study 1, with two variants of each type of ad copy.

Our fully randomized <sup>fi</sup>eld experiments help eliminate many sources of endogeneity that commonly arise in observational studies as discussed in prior studies (e.g., Agarwal et al. 2011, Agarwal et al. 2015, Simonov et al. 2018). First, in an observational study, there is often no control over the number of impressions that individuals in different treatment groups are exposed to. With randomized controlled experiments, the randomization of the treatments ensures that individuals in different groups are exposed to a similar number of impressions. Second, in an observational study, the individual manipulations (in our context, the ad copies) cannot be controlled. For example, each treatment might have multiple elements that are different, such as a different design as well as a different length. Having multiple different elements thereby makes the comparison among treatments dif<sup>fi</sup>cult. In a randomized <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment, on the other hand, the experimenter has control over the treatments, thus eliminating any noise. Third, observational studies are prone to selection biases that may plague the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of the treatment (Agarwal et al. 2015). For example, individuals in different treatment groups may differ in observable characteristics. Such differences can be eliminated because of randomization in experimental studies. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, in our context, without randomization, the search engine would optimize which ads to show to which customers to maximize click-through rate (CTR), thereby making it impossible to compare different treatment groups. Running a randomized <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment leads to the elimination of such biases as the randomization ensures that the ad copy received by each individual is not correlated with the individual’s propensity to click on the ad. Thus, the randomized <sup>fi</sup>eld experiments allow us to randomly display different types of ad copies for each brand keyword in our sample, thereby ensuring that the display of ad copies is exogenous to the poached brand’s quality.

Our results from both studies are consistent. First, we show that when poaching from high-quality competitors, ad copies featuring vertical differentiation messages have signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly higher click performance than the control ad copies, suggesting that consumers who search for high-quality brands tend to be attracted to quality signals featured in vertical differentiation messages by the poaching brand (Animesh et al. 2011). Second, when poaching from low-quality competitors, ad copies featuring horizontal differentiation messages have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly higher click performance compared with the control ad copies, suggesting that consumers not seeking quality get attracted to messages about nonquality attributes shown by poaching brands. Additionally, we show that the presence of the poached competitor’s own ad has a positive association with the click-through performance of the poaching brand when poaching from high-quality competitors and a negative association when poaching from low-quality competitors. This suggests that the presence of highquality poached brands' own ad has a positive halo effect (Nisbett and Wilson 1977) leading to higher click performance of the poaching brand, whereas the presence of low-quality poached brands' own ad has a negative halo effect leading to lower click performance of the poaching brand. The results are consistent after controlling for various control variables and <sup>fi</sup>xed effects at keyword and time levels and are robust to alternate model speci<sup>fi</sup>cations.

This research makes several contributions to the growing body of Information Systems (IS) literature on search advertising. By combining theoretical perspectives from product differentiation, advertising, and brand equity, we investigate the effectiveness of competitive poaching in the presence of heterogeneous competitor quality and the presence of the poached competitor’s ad. Although the advertising literature helps motivate the importance of investigating different ad copy variations, the product differentiation literature helps motivate the use of quality as a moderating factor on the effect of ad copy variation on competitive poaching. We add to the nascent but growing body of work on competitive poaching, which has mainly investigated this phenomenon using analytical modeling (Desai et al. 2014, Sayedi et al. 2014). To our knowledge, this is one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst empirical examinations of this phenomenon, especially how optimal ad copy design can increase the success of poaching. Our work also contributes to the understanding of ad positioning in search advertising and the interplay between the competitive position espoused in an ad copy and the brand equity of the keyword being searched. Although prior research suggests that both horizontal and vertical differentiation are feasible advertising strategies in general, optimal ad copy design in the poaching context should incorporate information about the competitor the <sup>fi</sup>rm is trying to poach from. Only a handful of IS researchers have examined ad copy design in online marketing (e.g., Animesh et al. 2011, Lee et al. 2018). However, their focus has been solely on strategies that work for the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm’s own advertising and not on competitive advertising. For example, although Animesh et al. (2011) mainly examine different ad copies, we also consider the interaction between the types of ad copies and the types of keywords searched by the users. Unlike regular search advertising, competitive poaching is unique in that <sup>fi</sup>rms know the competitor they are poaching from and can infer the type of the consumer (quality seeking versus those seeking attributes other than quality). As a result, the differentiation strategy that <sup>fi</sup>rms follow can be targeted accordingly. Although both vertical and horizontal differentiation can be viable positioning strategies in advertising, we show that in competitive poaching <sup>fi</sup>rms should use ad copies for each positioning type based on the competitor whose keyword is searched by the consumer.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we review the relevant literature. We propose our hypotheses in Section 3 and introduce and present two <sup>fi</sup>eld experiments in Sections 4, 5, and 6. The manipulation checks for ad copy variations and other robustness checks are presented in Section 7. Finally, we discuss the implications for theory and practice as well as highlight the limitations of our work and propose areas for future research in Section 8.

## 2. Literature Review

Our study draws on the prior literature on search advertising and the role of message framing and product differentiation in advertising.

## 2.1. Search Advertising

Prior literature on search advertising has mainly focused on the position effect, competition, and keyword selection of such ads. Research has shown that the click-through rate of search ads decreases with their position on the sponsored listing (e.g., Ghose and Yang 2009, Agarwal et al. 2011). Jeziorski and Moorthy (2018) show, however, that advertiser prominence can act as a substitute for ad position. Sun et al. (2020) ex amine the relative effectiveness of search versus social media advertisements on traf<sup>fi</sup>c and sales and how seller reputation moderates this relationship.

The second stream of literature examines competition in search advertising and, particularly, the effect of competing ads on the performance of the focal ad. Agarwal and Mukhopadhyay (2016) show that a competing high-quality ad appearing above the focal ad has a lower negative effect than a competing lowquality ad. Furthermore, they show that the effect of the competing ad varies with the position and the type of keyword. Jeziorski and Segal (2015) show that users choose to click on ads sequentially in order to maximize their expected utility. However, because of the presence of competing ads, there is a strong substitution effect, resulting in fewer clicks. Chiou and Tucker (2012) show that when a parent <sup>fi</sup>rm allows af-<sup>fi</sup>liates to use its trademark, it leads to a decrease in clicks on the parent <sup>fi</sup>rm’s paid search links; this effect, however, is outweighed by the increase in clicks on the parent <sup>fi</sup>rm’s sponsored links.

The third stream of literature examines the role of keyword characteristics. For example, Agarwal et al. (2011) use a hierarchical model to analyze the impact of keyword characteristics (such as keyword specificity) on advertising effectiveness. Ghose and Yang (2009) <sup>fi</sup>nd that keyword length (as a proxy for speci<sup>fi</sup>city) is negatively associated with CTR. Gong et al. (2018) show that ambiguous keywords are associated with higher baseline CTR but faster decay with ad position.

Finally, the fourth stream of literature examines competitive poaching where <sup>fi</sup>rms bid on competitors’ keywords in search advertising. Prior work has shown that bidding on generic keywords is more expensive than bidding on brand keywords and that consumers who search for a brand are at a more advanced stage in the purchase process (than consumers who search for generic keywords) and are more likely to convert (Rutz and Bucklin 2011). Therefore, bidding on keywords with competitor brand names can be a low-cost way of generating leads. Early work on competitive poaching mainly employed analytical modeling to provide insights into this phenomenon. For example, Sayedi et al. (2014) analyze budget constraint and <sup>fi</sup>rms’ strategic behavior to show that for <sup>fi</sup>rms under budget constraints, smaller <sup>fi</sup>rms are more likely to bid on competitors keywords than bigger <sup>fi</sup>rms; this may result in information asymmetry, which leads larger <sup>fi</sup>rms to retarget their ad budgets toward traditional forms of of<sup>fl</sup>ine advertising. Desai et al. (2014) analyze the strategic behavior of <sup>fi</sup>rms in competitive poaching and show that <sup>fi</sup>rms may decide to buy their own keywords to prevent competitors from bidding on their keywords. They show, however, that ultimately competitive poaching is an equilibrium strategy under certain market conditions and <sup>fi</sup>rms <sup>fi</sup>nd it optimal to adopt it even though they may be worse off than a scenario where competitive poaching was not allowed. Du et al. (2017) provide an empirical investigation into the importance of differentiating among various bidding strategies for different keyword categories. Simonov et al. (2018) show that competitive poaching impacts the poached brand and <sup>fi</sup>nd that competitors “steal” less traf<sup>fi</sup>c in the presence of the poached brand’s own ad at the top position.

## 2.2. Message Framing and Positioning in Advertising

Most prior work on message framing has been conducted in the context of traditional (of<sup>fl</sup>ine) advertising (e.g., Pechmann and Stewart 1990, Putrevu and Lord 1994). Traditionally, the literature on advertising has considered two broad categories of ad messages: informative and noninformative. Informative ad messages contain information such as brand mentions, price, and product information, which are objective and can be veri<sup>fi</sup>ed easily (Resnik and Stern 1977). One way <sup>fi</sup>rms disseminate such information in advertising is using differentiation (Grossman and Shapiro 1984). The product differentiation literature suggests that product attributes can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into two types—vertical differentiation and horizontal differentiation (Gabszwicz and Thisse 1986). Tremblay and Polasky (2002) suggest that ad copies can be used to create product differentiation. Differentiation ad copies contain signaling words or phrases to highlight the unique attributes of the advertised product or brand. Vertical differentiation ad copies highlight some quality-related attribute where all consumers can agree on the unanimous ranking of that attribute. Horizontal differentiation ad copies highlight non-qualityrelated attributes that are rooted in consumer tastes, and consumers can have heterogeneous preferences for each attribute. Irrespective of the type of differentiation, however, these messages are informative as they highlight raw facts describing various attributes of the product, which can be easily veri<sup>fi</sup>ed before purchase similar to search claims (Nelson 1970, 1974).

Noninformative messages, on the other hand, do not convey information about product attributes but rathe contain personality-related content, such as emotion, humor, or philanthropy (Porter and Golan 2006, Berger and Milkman 2012), which are subjective and cannot be veri<sup>fi</sup>ed prior to purchase. Prior literature in message framing has labeled these messages as “subjective message” (Edell and Staelin 1983), “evaluative claims” (Holbrook 1978), and “emotional appeal” (Atkin 1979). Although previous studies in marketing suggest that inclusion of personality-related content in messages can increase message sharing and overall engagement (Porter and Golan 2006, Berger and Milkman 2012), theoretical concepts developed by Nelson (1970, 1974) posit that these messages are akin to experience claims that cannot be determined prior to purchase and thus leads to higher skepticism from consumers.

The Animesh et al. (2011) study is one of the few studies in online settings that examines the ways in which <sup>fi</sup>rms can differentiate themselves using ad copies to drive CTR. However, they only examine two variants of ad copies: a price copy (which contains messages highlighting price, e.g., “50% discount”) and a quality copy (which contains quality-related messages, e.g., “secure and con<sup>fi</sup>dential”). Moreover, they focus on the effect of these ad copies while bidding on advertisers’ own keywords rather than competitor’s keywords. Lee et al. (2018) use Facebook data to classify ads according to whether ad copies are informative and/or personality related and then examine their effect on ad performance vis-a-vis the number of likes and the number of shares. Todri et al. (2020) investigate how different ad creatives moderate the relation between ad repetition and consumer annoyance across the different stages of the purchase funnel.

Whereas the prior studies mainly focus on ad copies for generic ads, we are not aware of any work in competitive poaching that examines how <sup>fi</sup>rms can effectively strategize to bid on competitors’ keywords. In regular advertising (where an ad promotes the focal product), message framing is important and vertical and horizontal differentiation can be viable positioning strategies. Although abundant research has examined the role of ad copies in driving click-throughs (Animesh et al. 2011, Lee et al. 2018) and how differentiation can act as a viable advertising strategy (Rossiter 2008), the importance of effective ad copy design is compounded in the competitive poaching context where brands are trying to lure consumers to switch from their initial choice to a competing product. By providing appropriately designed ads, <sup>fi</sup>rms give consumers an alternative to the focal brand and expand their consideration set (Animesh et al. 2011, Narayanan and Kalyanam 2015). Further, unlike regular advertising, competitive poaching is unique in that <sup>fi</sup>rms know the competitor they are poaching from and can infer the type of consumers that search for the competitor in advance. As a result, the differentiation strategy that <sup>fi</sup>rms follow can be targeted accordingly. In other words, a <sup>fi</sup>rm considering either vertical or horizontal differentiation ad copies in competitive poaching can target consumers using the optimal ad copy based on the competitor whose keyword is searched by the consumer. This is different from the case where the consumer is searching for a generic keyword when the advertiser has no information about their preference for different brand attributes.

Ad copies in search advertising also draw a parallel with prior literature on online markets that studies how textual cues can aid user decision making. For example, in prior literature on crowdfunding and online labor platforms, project descriptions are likely to in<sup>fl</sup>uence the likelihood of success (Mollick 2014, Guo et al. 2017). Other work in IS also explores how information cues can be effectively used to improve information processing (McNab 2009) or how verbal directional cues can orient visual spatial attention (Ho and Spence 2006). Because ad copy is the only cue that advertisers in the online search advertising context can play with to increase their ad’s click-through, it is critically important to understand how to design ad copies effectively. ## 3. Hypotheses Development

Consumers have varied preferences for product attributes (Bell and Lattin 1998), and they search for prod ucts that meet their needs. This enables businesses to target and segment consumers based on the keywords they search for (Ghose and Yang 2009, Animesh et al. 2011). Brand search provides a scenario where consumers reveal their preference through the brand key words they use on the search engine, thereby giving <sup>fi</sup>rms the opportunity to poach strategically on competing brands. Because a key dimension of brand equity is quality (Aaker 1996), the type of brand searched by consumers can give insights into their preference fo quality, enabling the poaching <sup>fi</sup>rms to segment consumers based on their preference. Quality-seeking consumers have a higher willingness to pay for quality than consumers who seek attributes other than quality (Wolinsky 1983, Desai et al. 2001). When using search engines for product search, quality-seeking consumers are more likely to search for high-quality brands than low quality brands (Animesh et al. 2011). Therefore, when poaching from competitors with different quality levels, the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm may need to tailor its advertising to appeal to consumers with different valuations for quality.

A primary objective of advertising by a <sup>fi</sup>rm is to position itself by choosing differentiating attributes as a “unique selling proposition” (Smith 1956, Dickson and Ginter 1987). Firms utilize advertising to provide unique and positive messages that affect perceptions of product differentiation (Boulding et al. 1994). In search advertising where consumers have low search intensity (Animesh et al. 2011), effective ad copy design is arguably one of the most essential aspects of marketers’ unique selling proposition strategy.

Drawing from the advertising literature, we consider two broad categories of ad messages: informative ad messages that contain information about veri<sup>fi</sup>able product attributes (Resnik and Stern 1977) and noninformative ad messages that often contain personality related content (Porter and Golan 2006, Berger and Milkman 2012). ## 3.1. Vertical Differentiation Ad Copies and High-Quality Competitors

Consumers differ in their valuations for quality (Wolinsky 1983, Levin and Johnson 1984). Quality seeking consumers have a higher willingness to pay for quality than other consumers who are looking for attributes other than quality (Wolinsky 1983, Desai et al. 2001). When using search engines for product search, quality-seeking consumers are more likely to search for high-quality brands than low-quality brands (Animesh et al. 2011). Therefore, when poach ing on keywords of high-quality competitors, the poaching brand needs to design its ad copies to appeal to quality-seeking consumers.

Vertical differentiation ad copies provide signals for quality-related attributes and thus appeal to qualityseeking consumers, who are more willing to pay for high-quality products (Tremblay and Martins-Filho 2001, Animesh et al. 2011). These messages signal quality using phrases such as “top rank” and “No 1 brand.” Recent research in economics (Shapiro 1983), IS (Animesh et al. 2011), and marketing (Wolinsky 1983, Desai et al. 2001) have shown that qualityseeking consumers are more attracted toward ads that signal high quality and have a higher willingness to pay for high-quality products.

Therefore, we expect that when poaching from high-quality competitors, vertical differentiation ad copies with quality signals would be more effective in comparison with ad copies that do not signal any attribute (in our case the control ad copies). Our <sup>fi</sup>rst hypothesis is as follows:

Hypothesis 1. When the poached competitor is high quality, vertical differentiation ad copies are more effective than control ad copies.

## 3.2. Horizontal Differentiation Ad Copies and Low-Quality Competitors

Consumers searching for low-quality brands are less likely to be quality seeking than those searching for high-quality brands. Although quality-seeking consumers are more likely to click on ads that signal high quality, consumers seeking attributes other than quality have their willingness to pay commensurate with the product’s attributes matching their preferences. Thus, such consumers get attracted to ad messages that highlight options matching their preferences (Wolinsky 1983, Levin and Johnson 1984, Desai et al. 2001). This makes these consumers a more natural target for horizontal differentiation, wherein two products differ across product attributes that do not necessarily signal any speci<sup>fi</sup>c quality but rather appeal to varied preferences. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, research in economics de<sup>fi</sup>nes horizontal differentiation as a case where consumers have different preference ordering over product attributes and these preferences dictate consumers’ purchase decisions (d'Aspremont et al. 1979, Tremblay and Martins-Filho 2001). Because different product attributes appeal to different consumers with different preferences (Gabszwicz and Thisse 1986), it is not quality but different consumer preferences that drive click and purchase decisions. Knowing that quality attributes may not be the major factor for decision making for such consumers, brands usually give a wide range of options to consumers to appeal to consumers’ varied preferences.

As consumers seeking attributes other than quality are more likely to search for brands that signal such attributes (matching consumers’ preferences), we expect that, when poaching on keywords of low-quality competitors, horizontal differentiation ad copies perform better than ad copies that do not signal any attribute (in our case, the control ad copies). Thus, we hypothesize that

Hypothesis 2. When the poached competitor is low quali ty, horizontal differentiation ad copies are more effective than control ad copies.

## 3.3. Prescriptive Ad Copies

Prescriptive ad copies contain messages that appea to consumers through various aspects of consumers personality, such as emotion, humor, and small talk. The economics of information theory (Stigler 1961) suggests that advertising claims will most often be true when consumers can easily verify the truthful ness of advertising. Consumers will be most skepti cal of advertising claims they can never verify and least skeptical of claims they can easily verify prior to purchase. Researchers in this area have also heavi ly relied on theoretical concepts developed by Nelson (1970, 1974), which classi<sup>fi</sup>es attributes of goods as having search or experience properties. Ford et al. (1990) adapted the Search-Experience-Credence framework for categorizing advertising messages. In particular, messages used in prescriptive ad copies are akin to experience claims (Ford et al. 1990). Consumers are more skeptical about experience claims (more subjective and not easily veri<sup>fi</sup>able) than search claims (more objective and can be veri<sup>fi</sup>ed easily). Moreover, in brand search advertising where consumers are much closer to making a purchase and have a reasonable expectation of the attributes they are looking for (i.e., they are in the “action” stage of the purchase funnel (Vakratsas and Ambler (1999)), consumers have high skepticism regarding such messages as these cannot be evaluated prior to purchase.

However, the level of skepticism for experience claims decreases if the advertiser can signal higher quality (Nelson 1970, 1974). Prior studies on comparative advertising<sup>5</sup> suggest that associating with a competitor may facilitate perceptions of similarity between the two brands (Wilkie and Farris 1975, Gorn and Weinberg 1984, Pechmann and Ratnesh war 1991). In competitive poaching, poaching on keywords from high-quality competitors may engender positive quality perceptions of the poaching brand, reducing the level of skepticism for experience claims. In contrast, poaching on keywords from low-quality competitors may result in negative quality perceptions of the poaching brand. Thus, we hypothesize that

Hypothesis 3. When the poached competitor is high quality (low quality), prescriptive ad copies are more effective (less effective) than control ad copies.

## 3.4. The Role of the Poached Competitor’s Own Ad

A brand engaging in competitive poaching may face competition from the poached brand itself. In other words, the poached brand may also advertise on keywords speci<sup>fi</sup>c to its own brand name. In such cases, the ad of the poaching brand would be considered along with the ad of the poached competitor by the consumer. Prior literature on search advertising has shown that competing high- (low-) quality ads appearing above the focal ad has a positive (negative) effect on the click-through of the focal ad (Jeziorski and Segal 2015, Agarwal and Mukhopadhyay 2016), suggesting that the effect of competing ads varies depending on the quality of these competing ads. The comparative advertising literature suggests associating with a competitor may result in less psychological distance between the two brands (Wilkie and Farris 1975, Gorn and Weinberg 1984, Pechmann and Ratneshwar 1991). Therefore, consumers may use the presence of a highquality competitor’s own ad as a positive signal of the poaching brand’s quality. This has a positive halo effect (Nisbett and Wilson 1977) on higher click performance of the poaching brand when poaching on keywords for high-quality competitors. By contrast, when a brand poaches from low-quality competitors, the presence of the poached competitor’s own ad gives a negative signal to consumers (negative halo effect), which leads to lower click performance of the poaching brand. Thus, we hypothesize

Hypothesis 4. When the poached competitor is high quality (low quality), the presence of the poached competitor’s own ad has a positive (negative) association with the ad effectiveness of the poaching brand.

## 4. Data and Context

We test our hypotheses using data from two studies. In Study 1, we collaborate with a business school located in the Northeastern United States, one of whose graduate programs was ranked among the top 20 during the study period as per the U.S. News & World Report. During the <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment, we bid on competitor keywords focusing on the focal graduate program. We classify competitor keywords based on the quality of each competitor as proxied by rankings from the 2017 U.S. News & World Report for the focal graduate program. We elaborate on the details of this study in Section 5.

In Study 2, we collaborated with a <sup>fi</sup>rm that owns and operates automobile dealerships in the Northeastern United States selling Audi cars. As in Study 1, we consider both high-ranked and low-ranked brands, among which the high-ranked brands have consistently appeared at the top of multiple well-established car rankings for consecutive years. The details for this study follow in Section 6.

In both studies, our operationalization of quality is in an absolute sense. In our context, poaching competitors based on their absolute quality is more relevant as the goal of our study is to design ad copies that appeal to consumers who are searching for keywords related to competitors’ brands. A consumer searching for a brand is likely to convey his or her preference for attributes related to the brand (such as quality). Theoretically, vertical differentiation ad copies would appeal to consumers searching keywords of high-quality competitors. When implementing the vertical differentiation ad copies, we use phrases such as “Top Ranked” (in Study 1) and “Ranked #1” (in Study 2). It is expected that ad copies with such phrases would be more appealing to consumers who are searching for competitors that are top ranked (in an absolute sense).

Each <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment is run on Google AdWords where a consumer who searched for one of the competitors’ keywords is randomly exposed to one of the four types of ad copies, namely, vertical differentiation (DiffVertical), horizontal differentiation (DiffHori zontal), prescriptive (Prescriptive), and control ad copies (Control). Such randomization allows us to identify the causal effect of ad copies on click-throughs and address endogeneity concerns that may arise with secondary data. Furthermore, in comparison with controlled laboratory experiments, conducting <sup>fi</sup>eld experiments by collaborating with real-world brands in different industries increases the generalizability and ecological validity of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

## 5. Study 1

We collected our data from a three-month randomized <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment by running a search advertising campaign on Google in collaboration with a business school located in the Northeastern United States, one of whose graduate programs was ranked among the top 20 during the study period as per U.S. News & World Report. The higher education market provides an ideal context for our research because search advertising is a common marketing tool and schools frequently poach on each other’s keywords, as seen in Figure 1. To preserve the anonymity of the school, we label the focal university as XYZ University and we label the focal graduate program as ABC program in the rest of the paper.

During the experiment, we bid on 11 competitor keywords focusing on the ABC program. We used rankings from the 2017 U.S. News & World Report for the ABC program as proxies for the quality of each competitor. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we categorized competitors into two tiers: high-ranked programs (i.e., ranked in the top 20 in U.S. News Rankings) and low-ranked programs (i.e., ranked 40 and below). We further checked the 2016 U.S. News & World Report rankings to make sure that these chosen programs consistently fell into the same tier during the two consecutive years (2016–2017).

In the <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment, a consumer who searched for one of the competitors’ keywords (i.e., a poached competitor’s keyword related to the ABC program) was randomly exposed to one of the four ad copies for XYZ University, namely, vertical differentiation (DiffVertical), horizontal differentiation (DiffHorizontal), prescriptive (Prescriptive), and control (Control) ad copies.

In addition to the <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment, we also collected data on competing ads for each keyword to determine whether the poached competitor’s ad appeared in the sponsored search results during the time period. We scraped data from the Google search page every day for the duration of the experiment. This process simulates the information that a user sees when he or she searches for a speci<sup>fi</sup>c keyword, including information about competing ads in addition to the focal ad of XYZ University.<sup>6</sup>

## 5.1. Experimental Design

To examine how advertising effectiveness varies with ad copy design, we designed the following four ad copies: vertical differentiation, horizontal differentiation, prescriptive, and control. At the time we ran the experiment, Google required that each ad copy had two headings with up to 30 characters, a description line with up to 80 characters, and a URL with up to 15 characters.<sup>7</sup> Adhering to these guidelines, we proposed the ad copies listed in Table 2.

Each time a consumer searched for a speci<sup>fi</sup>c competitor keyword using Google’s search engine, one of the four ad copies was randomly displayed to the consumer using Google AdWords, which provided an option to rotate all ad copies equally. Thus, when a user searched for a competitor’s keyword, he or she was exposed to one of the ad copies with equal probability.

In accordance with the guidelines in Lambrecht and Tucker (2018) about randomization in <sup>fi</sup>eld experiments, we checked the number of impressions for each ad copy for the duration of our experiment. Prior research suggests that the click-through performance of search advertising increases if the ad appears higher on the sponsored list (Ghose and Yang 2009, Agarwal et al. 2011). We control for the ad to appear mainly between positions 3 and 5 to control for position effects. Table B4 in Online Appendix B shows that the difference in average position between ad copies for the same keyword is statistically insigni<sup>fi</sup>- cant. Google AdWords enables <sup>fi</sup>rms to control for position by suggesting a target budget for a certain level of advertising effectiveness, which is a proxy for bid amount. One can argue that setting different bid amounts for different ad copies can bias the results if we are comparing the effectiveness of different ad copies.<sup>9</sup> However, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that although the bid amount varies across different keywords, it does not vary much across different ad copies for the same keyword (Table B5 in Online Appendix B).

Moreover, in branded search advertising where consumers are much closer to making a purchase and have a reasonable expectation of the attributes they are looking for (i.e., they are in the “action” stage of the purchase funnel), consumers have high skepticism regarding such messages as these cannot be evaluated prior to purchase (Vakrat sas and Ambler 1999).

With respect to the control variables, as expected, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the number of impressions is positively associated with the number of clicks. The coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of log(Distance) is negative and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, which sug gests that as the physical distance between the two universities (poached and poaching) increases, the effectiveness of poaching decreases. This also shows that home bias, which has been studied in various contexts (McPherson et al. 2001, Disdier and Head 2008, Lin and Viswanathan 2015), exists in search advertising as well. The results of the Poisson model after the inclusion of keyword and week <sup>fi</sup>xed effects are shown in columns (3) and (4) in Table 4 and remain consistent.

To test Hypothesis 4, we use the data scraped from the Google search page on competing ads for each keyword to determine whether the poached brand’s ad appeared in the sponsored search results. From these data, we constructed the variable, Ad\_Presence, which measures the presence of sponsored ads from the poached competitor, and Countofads, which controls for the average number of ads displayed on the sponsored search results page in a given week when a user is searching for a speci<sup>fi</sup>c keyword. The results of the Poisson model are shown in Table 5. In columns (1) and (2), we control for Countofads in our model, whereas in columns 3 and 4 we introduce Ad\_Presence.

From Table 5, we observe that the main effects of the ad copies remain qualitatively similar to those of Table 4. Further, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient for Ad\_Presence is positive for the high-ranked competitors and negative for the low-ranked competitors (columns (3) and (4), respectively). This suggests that when poaching from high-ranked competitors, the presence of the poached competitor’s own ad has a positive association with the click-through of the poaching brand, whereas for low-ranked competitors there is a negative association. Thus, all else equal, for highranked competitors, the presence of a poached competitor’s own ad is associated with an increase in the number of clicks by 95% ( exp(0.668) 1). For lowranked competitors, the presence of the poached competitor’s own ad is associated with a decrease in the number of clicks by 49% ( exp( 0.719) 1). Thus, Hy pothesis 4 is supported. These results suggest that the presence of high-quality poached brands' own ad has a positive halo effect (Nisbett and Wilson 1977) leading to higher click performance of the poaching brand, whereas the presence of low-quality poached brands own ad has a negative halo effect leading to lower click performance of the poaching brand.

To further examine the effect of competition among sponsored search ads in competitive poaching, we perform content analysis to analyze textual data from ad copies of all sponsored search ads that appear when a consumer searches for a given keyword of a poached competitor, using data scraped from Google on all the sponsored search results. The main results remain consistent after controlling the types of ad copies used by other competing ads (Online Appendix E).

Summary of Findings from Study 1 and Study 2 In summary, for both studies, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that three of the four hypotheses are supported (i.e., Hypothesis 1, Hypothesis 2, and Hypothesis 4). ## 7. Manipulation Checks for Ad Copy Variations and Other Robustness Checks

7.1. Manipulation Checks for Ad Copy Variations We conducted manipulation checks for both studies to check the agreeability of raters to our ad copy classi<sup>fi</sup>cation. This was done using two separate studies conducted on Amazon Mechanical Turk (AMT) to check the reliability and consistency of our ad copy classi<sup>fi</sup>cation.

7.1.1. Reliability Study. We designed a survey hosted on AMT to check the intercoder reliability of our ad copy classi<sup>fi</sup>cations. From Study 1, we have four ad copies (as shown in Table 2) and from Study 2 we have eight ad copies (as shown in Table 6). Thus, in total, we have 12 ad copies. The details of the reliability study are provided in Online Appendix F. At the end of the study, we analyzed the responses of those coders who correctly attempted the study. Because this is a multiuser agreement context, we employ Fleiss-kappa, a variation of Kohen’s kappa coef<sup>fi</sup>cient but for more than two users (Fleiss et al. 1979). According to Fleiss et al. (1979), a reliability statistic of more than 0.4 indicates a fair to good level of reliability. As seen in Table F1 in Online Appendix F, the kappa values suggest that we have fair to good levels of reli ability across our ad copies.

7.1.2. Consistency Study. We further conducted a consistency study on AMT to check agreeability among raters on a seven-point Likert scale on our ad copy classi<sup>fi</sup>cations. Similar to the reliability study, the raters were <sup>fi</sup>rst shown the de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of each of the ad categories followed by questions where they rate on a seven-point scale on how much they think a phrase aligns with (i) vertical differentiation, (ii) horizontal differentiation, (iii) prescriptive, or (iv) control. This re<sup>fl</sup>ects what the literature has documented about measuring agreement among raters (Ordabayeva and Fernandes 2018). The details of this study are provided in Online Appendix F. This study leads to further justi<sup>fi</sup>cation for proper ad copy categorization.

## 7.2. Robustness Checks

To test the robustness of our results, we replicated our analyses using several alternate models. First, we considered the negative binomial model, with the dependent variable as the number of clicks. For both Study 1 and Study 2, the results of the negative binomial model concur with the results from the Poisson model. Second, we considered generalized linear models (GLM), assuming that the number of clicks (Clicks) generated from a certain number of impressions (Impressions) follows a binomial distribution, with either a probit or logit link. Our dependent variable for the GLM speci<sup>fi</sup>cation is whether there was a click or not (click/no click). For both studies, the results are qualitatively similar. Third, we considered an alternate dependent variable: CTR, instead of clicks. Because CTR takes fractional values between zero and one, we ran fractional logit and probit models. The results remain qualitatively consistent. All the results of the robustness checks are provided in online Appendix C.

## 8. Conclusion and Implications

This study seeks to understand how <sup>fi</sup>rms can use differentiation strategies in their ad copies to poach consumers from their competitors using search advertising. To our knowledge, this is one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst empirical examinations of competitive poaching on how <sup>fi</sup>rms can highlight different types of product differentiation strategies in ad copies while bidding on keywords of different types of competitors (high quality versus low quality). We also explore the role of the presence of the poached brand’s own ads in determining the effectiveness of competitive poaching. Our empirical analysis, consisting of two randomized <sup>fi</sup>eld experiments, provides a rich context for studying how consumers respond to competitive poaching and how this response varies by the type of poached competitors.

Our main <sup>fi</sup>ndings are as follows. First, when poaching on keywords of high-quality brands, only vertical differentiation ad copies are more effective compared with ad copies that do not communicate any information about product attributes. Second, when poaching from low-quality brands, horizontal differentiation ad copies are more effective compared with ad copies that do not communicate any information about prod uct attributes. Third, the presence of the poached brand’s own ad has a positive (negative) association with the click performance of the poaching brand when the poached brand is high quality (low quality). Finally, the effectiveness of competitive poaching increases as the distance between the location of the poached <sup>fi</sup>rm and the poaching <sup>fi</sup>rm decreases.

The research makes several contributions. First, this study contributes to the literature on competition in search advertising. Although prior research has focused on the effect of competing ads on the focal brand itself (e.g., Agarwal et al. 2011, Jeziorski and Sega 2015, Agarwal and Mukhopadhyay 2016), we focus on competitive poaching where a <sup>fi</sup>rm bids on competi tors’ keywords. Second, we contribute to literature on competitive poaching (e.g., Desai et al. 2014, Sayedi et al. 2014, Du et al. 2017) by investigating ways in which <sup>fi</sup>rms can design ad copies to increase the effectiveness of poaching based on competitors’ quality. Third, this study contributes to the stream of research that examines how keyword characteristics in<sup>fl</sup>uence search advertising effectiveness. Although prior studies mainly focus on either generic keywords or keywords of the focal brand (e.g., Ghose and Yang 2009, Agarwal et al. 2011, Gong et al. 2018), we focus on branded keywords of competitors. We show that the brand names searched by consumers communicate unique cues about their preferences, which can beused to tailor appropriate messages to different customers. Fourth, effective ad copy design in search advertising remains a nascent <sup>fi</sup>eld; only a handful of IS studies have examined message design in the sponsored search space (e.g., Animesh et al. 2011). Moreover, they investigate only a small list of ad copies while focusing solely on strategies that work for the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm’s own keywords and not those for competitors keywords. In comparison, we consider the interaction between the types of ad copies and the types of keywords being searched (i.e., keywords of different types of competitors). Finally, we extend the message framing literature by investigating how message framing has differential effects across consumers as they search for high-quality versus low-quality brands.

Our work also has implications for managers regarding how to optimize strategies when bidding on competitors’ keywords. Firms across industries are increasingly bidding on competitors’ keywords; a key question is how to make competitive poaching more effective, considering that consumers reveal their preference through the keywords they search for. This gives opportunity for <sup>fi</sup>rms to poach on competing brands. Our study takes the <sup>fi</sup>rst step in that direction by prescribing that <sup>fi</sup>rms should show different ad copies to consumers who are looking for a high-quality brand versus a low-quality brand. For a search advertising manager, this can be operationalized by creating different campaigns for different brand keywords. Table 10 summarizes the overall strategies that managers should follow while poaching from competitors of different quality levels based on <sup>fi</sup>ndings from our study. Because it is an intriguing conundrum where <sup>fi</sup>rms, on the one hand, need to counter poaching on their own keywords while, on the other hand, effectively designing strategies to poach on others’ keywords, this calls for the involvement of policymakers to formulate search engine policies accordingly.

Our study has a few limitations. First, because of our inability to manipulate the presence of the poached competitor’s own ad, we cannot unequivocally ascertain the causal effect for that construct. We thus cautiously interpret our <sup>fi</sup>ndings as associations rather than causal effects. Second, we focus mostly on clicks due to the limited number of conversions in the time frame of our data. Third, our data are aggregated at the weekly level as provided by Google AdWords. Data at a more granular level (e.g., the request level) could increase estimation ef-<sup>fi</sup>ciency. Fourth, we investigate effective strategies from the poaching <sup>fi</sup>rms’ perspective (not the poached <sup>fi</sup>rms). Future research could carry out experiments with a dyad of <sup>fi</sup>rms (poaching and poached), which may lead to interesting insights about how market shares of <sup>fi</sup>rms change because of competitive poaching. Fifth, in both studies, the product is one that can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as a high-quality product; it would be interesting to conduct a similar experiment with a low-quality <sup>fi</sup>rm as the focal <sup>fi</sup>rm. Sixth, our results hold for products where various brands are clearly differentiated in terms of quality. They would not necessarily hold for markets where a majority of the products do not have a clearly de-<sup>fi</sup>ned difference on the quality dimension. These limitations notwithstanding, this paper takes one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst steps in understanding how optimal ad-copy design in<sup>fl</sup>uences the success of competitive poaching. Future research can build on our <sup>fi</sup>ndings by examining other microeconomic issues related to this interesting phenomenon, such as the effect of other competing ads; poaching by a low-quality <sup>fi</sup>rm; ob serving alternate outcomes, such as conversion rates; and the effect of competitive poaching on consumers at different stages of the conversion funnel. Future research could examine other strategic tools, such as bid adjustment and quality of landing page, which could also be manipulated to the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s advantage. Finally, our work may also initiate interesting discussions not only in marketing but also in other areas, such as charitable contributions and elections where poaching can be strategically used by <sup>fi</sup>rms to gain competitive advantage.
