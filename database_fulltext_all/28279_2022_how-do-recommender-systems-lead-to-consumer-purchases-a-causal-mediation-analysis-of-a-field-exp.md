# How Do Recommender Systems Lead to Consumer Purchases? A Causal Mediation Analysis of a Field Experiment

Abstract. How do recommender systems induce consumers to buy? Extant research neglects to examine the causal paths through which the use of recommender systems leads to consumer purchases. In this study, we conduct a randomized controlled <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment on the website of an online book retailer and explore the causal paths by employing the recently developed causal mediation approach. Not surprisingly, the results show that the presence of personalized recommendations increases consumers’ propensity to buy by 12.4% and basket value by 1.7%. More importantly, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that these positive economic effects are largely mediated through affecting the consumers’ consideration sets. Speci<sup>fi</sup> cally, the presence of personalized recommendations increases both the size of consumers consideration sets (breadth) and how intensively they are involved with each alternative in consideration (depth). It is the two changes that go on to increase consumers’ propensity to buy and basket value. Furthermore, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the proportion of the total effects mediated through the breadth of consideration set is much larger and more signi<sup>fi</sup>cant than that mediated through the depth.

## 1. Introduction

Recommender systems are “software agents that elicit the interests or preferences of individual users for products, either explicitly or implicitly, and make recommendations accordingly” (Xiao and Benbasat 2007, p. 137–138). Whereas recommendations can be as simple as lists of “bestselling” or “most popular” items, in this paper we focus on personalized recommender systems that recommend different products to different consumers based on the analysis of their preferences (Tam and Ho 2006; Zhang et al. 2011; Adomavicius et al. 2018, 2019; Dzyabura and Hauser 2019). Recommender systems are pervasive in e-commerce, and the most well-known example is perhaps the one used by Amazon, recommending products indicated as “Customers who bought this item also bought.”

Recommender systems are designed to reduce consumers’ search costs and help them <sup>fi</sup>nd the most relevant products for purchases (Xiao and Benbasat 2007). They are expected to have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impacts on e-commerce practice, and thus have stimulated academic research since the early 2000s (Xiao and Benbasat 2007). Perhaps due to the limited availability of <sup>fi</sup>eld data, earlier studies were largely conducted in laboratory settings (Haubl and¨ Trifts 2000, Swaminathan 2003, Diehl 2005, Zhang et al. 2011), and the recommender systems examined in those early studies often used algorithms that were different from the current mainstream implementations, like collaborative <sup>fi</sup>ltering (CF) (Adomavicius and Tuzhilin 2005).<sup>1</sup> It is thus unclear how well the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of those laboratory studies could be generalizable to business practices.

Recent studies have investigated mainstream CF-based recommender systems and used <sup>fi</sup>eld data to examine how recommenders affect product sales and sales diversity. There appears to be a consensus that recommender systems can increase consumers’ propensity to buy and generate more sales (De et al. 2010, Pathak et al. 2010, Adamopoulos et al. 2021, Adomavicius et al. 2018, Lee and Hosanagar 2019). Yet, prior studies largely assume that recommender systems reduce search costs (Xiao and Benbasat 2007) and lower search costs enable consumers to <sup>fi</sup>nd products that <sup>fi</sup>t their preferences more easily (Bakos 1997), which ultimately induces consumers to buy. But the prior studies do not test these assumptions with <sup>fi</sup>eld data. Particularly, earlier research does not explore the causal paths through which the use of recommender systems affects the downstream economic outcomes (e.g., conversion rate and sales) or the mediating factors on the causal paths. To this end, they provide a limited understanding of the mechanisms that underlie the economic effects of recommender systems.

This study aims to <sup>fi</sup>ll this research gap and proposes that a consumer’s consideration set plays an important role on the causal paths through which recommender systems in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumer purchases and product sales (Hauser and Wernerfelt 1990, Shapiro et al. 1997, Zhang et al. 2011, Hauser 2014). To the best of our knowledge, the extant studies on recommender systems have not yet explored the role that consideration sets play in the effects of recommender systems on consumed purchases with <sup>fi</sup>eld data (Pathak et al. 2010, Hinz et al. 2011, Lee et al. 2016, Adamopoulos et al. 2017, Lee and Hosanagar 2019). Notably, De et al. (2010, p. 1934) speculate that consumers with the use of recommender systems “conduct more external search, discover a larger number of desirable products, and eventually make more purchases,” but they do not directly examine the role of consumers’ consideration set.

Since recommender systems reduce consumers search costs (De et al. 2010, Brynjolfsson et al. 2011), it is likely that the use of recommender systems could affect the size of consumers’ consideration sets. In addition, because recommender systems are expected to recommend products that <sup>fi</sup>t consumers’ preferences better (Adomavicius and Tuzhilin 2005, Xiao and Benbasat 2007), consumers’ involvement with an alternative in the consideration set could be different from a situation without recommender systems. Therefore, we develop a research model in which consumers consideration sets mediate the effects of recommender systems on consumer purchases. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we conjecture that the use of recommender systems could affect two facets of consumers’ consideration sets: consideration set size (the breadth of the consideration set) and the involvement per alternative (the depth of the consideration set), both of which in turn can affect the consumers’ propensity to buy (Shapiro et al. 1997).

To test this mediation model empirically with <sup>fi</sup>eld data, we collaborate with a large online book retailer in Europe and conduct a randomized controlled <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment. To inform the design choices of the <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment, we <sup>fi</sup>rst conduct two pilot laboratory experiments. The results of the <sup>fi</sup>rst pilot experiment show that CF recommenders lead to increased consumer purchases, relative to a situation with no recommendations and also a condition of merely showing random products. The results of the second pilot experiment show that the effects of CF recommenders, relative to the condition with no recommendations, appear to have an inverse U-shape relationship with the number of recommendations, and displaying three recommended products appears to be effective. Therefore, we use the situation with no recommendations as the control/baseline condition and displaying three recommendations in the treated condition of the <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, for the <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment, we deploy a recommender system that uses a CF-based algorithm and randomly assign one half of visitor sessions to the so-called “treatment” group in which visitors are exposed to the recommendations. The recommended titles are placed below a focal book, following the business practice of many e-commerce websites like Amazon.com. The recommender system is turned off in the control sessions and thus the recommendations are hidden for visitors in the control group. All other elements of the website are identical between treatment and control sessions.

The results support our research model. The use of the recommender system on average increases the odds of placing a purchase order by 12.4% and the revenues per session by 1.7%. Meanwhile, it on average increases the consideration set size by 3.2% and the involvement per alternative by 0.9%. All these estimates are statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at the 95% level. Further analyses show that the effects of recommender systems are mostly driven by consumers who click on and view at least one recommended product, whereas recommender systems have little effect on consumers who are presented with recommended products but do not click on or view recommended products. More importantly, we employ the recently developed causal mediation analysis method (Valeri and VanderWeele 2013, VanderWeele 2015, VanderWeele 2016) and <sup>fi</sup>nd that consideration set size and the involvement per alternative both mediate the effects of recommender systems on consumer purchases, such as conversion rate and the average order value. Statistically speak ing, the mediation effect is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, but the direct effect is insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant, suggesting that it is a full mediation (Baron and Kenny 1986) or so called “indirectonly mediation” (Zhao et al. 2010). According to the point estimates, the mediation effects through consideration set size and the involvement per alternative account for 25.7% and 9.9% of the total effect on conversion rate, respectively. It appears that consideration set size (the breadth) mediates a much larger and more signi<sup>fi</sup>cant part than the involvement per alternative (the depth). Together, the two facets of consideration set mediate 39.2% of the total effect.

Our study contributes to the information systems (IS) literature in several important aspects. First, although prior studies with <sup>fi</sup>eld data <sup>fi</sup>nd the positive effects of recommender systems on conversion rate, the understanding of the underlying mechanisms from the mediation perspective is still lacking. To the best of our knowledge, we are among the <sup>fi</sup>rst to uncover the causal paths from presenting recommendations to consumer purchases from the mediation perspective. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that both the breadth and depth of consideration set mediate the effects of recommender systems on consumer purchases. Second, prior studies have produced competing predictions about whether recommender systems increase or decrease the size of consumers’ consideration sets (Haubl and Trifts¨ 2000, Pereira 2001, Zhang et al. 2011). As the prior studies are largely conducted in laboratory settings, we provide the <sup>fi</sup>rst <sup>fi</sup>eld experimental evidence supporting that recommender systems increase consideration set size, which in turn leads to increased conversion rate. Third, little is known about the effects of recommender systems on the depth of consideration sets. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that the involvement per alternative in consideration mediates the positive effect of recommenders on consumer purchases. Finally and methodologically, we employ the recently developed causal mediation approach (Imai et al. 2010a, 2010b; Valeri and VanderWeele 2013; VanderWeele 2015; VanderWeele 2016), which allows for a clear causal interpretation of the mediation results. To the best of our knowledge, the causal mediation framework has not drawn much attention in the IS literature yet (Peng 2019).

## 2. Related Literature

Collaborative-<sup>fi</sup>ltering (CF) recommenders recommend products that similar consumers have purchased or liked before, where similar consumers are identi<sup>fi</sup>ed based on their prior co-purchases or other co-behaviors (Adomavicius and Tuzhilin 2005). Due to the technical feasibility and maturity, CF algorithms and hybrid variants with some content-based (CB) <sup>fl</sup>avor dominate in the current business practice (Adomavicius and Tuzhilin 2005).<sup>2</sup> Accordingly, recent studies that use <sup>fi</sup>eld data often examine recommender systems built upon CF algorithms (Lee and Hosanagar 2019, 2021). Extant studies with <sup>fi</sup>eld data mostly explore the effects of recommender systems on two important outcomes: product sales and sales diversity.

## 2.1. Effects on Product Sales

The <sup>fi</sup>rst research stream investigates the effects on product sales. De et al. (2010) <sup>fi</sup>nd that the use of a recommender system has a positive effect on the sales of promoted and non-promoted products. Pathak et al. (2010) support this claim and further <sup>fi</sup>nd that the effect is moderated by the recency of the recommended products. Lee and Hosanagar (2019) <sup>fi</sup>nd that purchase-based CF increases product views by 25% and sales volume by 35%, whereas view-based CF increases product views only by 3% and sales volume by 9%. Lee and Hosanagar (2021) examine the moderating effect of product attributes and <sup>fi</sup>nd that the impact of a recommender system on conversion rate is greater for hedonic goods than util itarian goods. They also <sup>fi</sup>nd that the positive impact on conversion rate is greater for products with lower average review ratings, but the impact does not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly differ for experience goods versus search goods.

In practice, recommender systems often present the recommended products on the web pages where consumers view a focal product. Accordingly, the effects of recommender systems can be examined from the perspective that considers a product recommendation network consisting of such co-presence or co-purchase relationships between products. There is an emerging literature that examines the effects of recommender systems from this perspective (Goldenberg et al. 2012, Kumar and Tan 2015, Kumar and Hosanagar 2019). For example, Oestreicher-Singer and Sundararajan (2012b) <sup>fi</sup>nd that the explicit visibility of a co-purchase relationship can lead to up to an average threefold ampli<sup>fi</sup>cation of the in<sup>fl</sup>uence that complementary products have on each other’s demands. Kumar and Tan (2015) <sup>fi</sup>nd that joint product display cannot only increase the sales of the focal products (direct effect) but lead to an increase in the sales of complementary prod ucts (spillover effect). Whereas the jointly displayed products are often complementary in the study (e.g., apparels and accessories; Kumar and Tan 2015), Kumar and Hosanagar (2019) examine a case where the focal product and the co-presented products are large ly substitutes. Interestingly, they continue to <sup>fi</sup>nd that the recommendation links of a product on average leads to an increase in the total sales of the focal prod uct and the recommended substitute products. Their detailed analysis reveals that it is because a recommendation link increases the daily number of page views on a focal product by 7% but reduces its sales given the page views by 8.5%, whereas it increases the sales of the recommended substitute products by 24.5%. Lin et al. (2017) explore the effects of the diversity of the product recommendation network and <sup>fi</sup>nd that a 1% increase in the category diversity of the incoming (outgoing) co-purchase network of a product is associated with 0.011% increase (decrease) in product sales.

Despite the considerable literature and the consensus on the positive effects of recommender systems on product sales, extant studies with <sup>fi</sup>eld data largely neglect to uncover the role that consumers’ consideration sets play in the causal relation between the use of recommender systems and consumers’ propensity to purchase. Our study will <sup>fi</sup>ll the literature gap.

## 2.2. Effects on Sales Diversity

The second research stream explores the effects of recommender systems on sales diversity. Because recommender systems aim to help consumers discover products that could <sup>fi</sup>t their preferences better (Dzyabura and Hauser 2019), they are believed to diversify the purchases of consumers with heterogeneous preferences, contributing to the “long tail” phenomenon in online retailing (Brynjolfsson et al. 2011). Researchers have empirically tested this conjecture (Fleder and Hosanagar 2009, Oestreicher-Singer and Sundararajan 2012a). For example, Hinz et al. (2011) <sup>fi</sup>nd that depending on the type of recommender systems, the recommender systems can shift toward either niche products or blockbuster products. Hosanagar et al. (2014) show that recommender systems can decrease aggregate-level sales diversity but increase individual-level sales diversity, because recommender systems can help consumers <sup>fi</sup>nd “niche” products that may <sup>fi</sup>t their preferences better. In a recent follow-up study, Lee and Hosanagar (2019) continue to <sup>fi</sup>nd supportive evidence and show that recommender systems are more effective in aiding individual consumers to discover popular products than uniformly aiding the discovery of less popular products. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings that the use of recommender systems increases consumers consideration set size (a larger number of different products in the consideration set) may partially explain how recommender systems increase the diversity of consumers’ individual-level purchases.

## 3. Theory Development

To uncover the mechanisms that underlie the effects of recommender systems, we propose that a consideration set, a well-established construct in the marketing and IS literature (Hauser and Wernerfelt 1990, Shapiro et al. 1997, Zhang et al. 2011, Hauser 2014), plays an important role in mediating the pathway through which the use of recommender systems in<sup>fl</sup>uences consumer purchases and product sales. A considerable body of literature documents that consumers, when they face many products from which to buy, simplify their purchase decisions using a “consider-then-choose” decision process in which they <sup>fi</sup>rst identify a consideration set (i.e., a set of alternative products for further evaluation) and then choose to buy from the consideration set (Hauser and Wernerfelt 1990, Nedungadi 1990, Shocker et al. 1991, Shapiro et al. 1997, Zhang et al. 2011, Hauser 2014, Shukla et al. 2021). Note that, in the “consider-then-choose” decision process, the formation of a consideration set and the involvement of alternative items in consideration can occur in parallel. That is, consumers search alternative items and, in the meantime, evaluate them. They identify the consideration set, and visit and revisit the alternative items in the consideration set before making the purchase decision. We build upon the “consider-then-choose” decision process and distinguish the mediating role of consideration set at two facets: breadth (consideration set size) and depth (involvement per alternative in consid eration). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we propose that the two facets mediate the effects of recommender systems on consumer purchases. Figure 1 illustrates the mediation model. We discuss the possible mediating roles of each of the two facets in the following sections.

## 3.1. Size (Breadth) of Consideration Set

Prior studies provide theoretical predictions, though contradictory, about whether the use of recommender systems could decrease or increase consideration set size. On the one hand, because recommender systems recommend products based on the analysis of consumers’ preferences and the predicted propensity to buy (Adomavicius and Tuzhilin 2005), consumers with the help of a recommender system are likely to <sup>fi</sup>nd the most relevant products more easily and quickly. Consequently, they do not need to add as many alternatives to their consideration sets as they would have to do without a recommender. Therefore, the use of recommender systems is expected to reduce the number of alternatives in consideration (Xiao and

Benbasat 2007). Haubl and his colleagues (H¨ aubl¨ and Trifts 2000, Haubl and Murray¨ 2003, Dellaert and Haubl¨ 2012) <sup>fi</sup>nd supportive evidence for this prediction in a series of laboratory experiments.

On the other hand, the use of recommender systems could increase the size of the consideration set. The literature suggests that consumers typically consider a trade-off between the marginal bene<sup>fi</sup>t and cost of searching an additional alternative before adding it to the consideration set (Hauser and Wernerfelt 1990, Shapiro et al. 1997, Ghose et al. 2019). From the bene<sup>fi</sup>t perspective, an alternative recommended by recommender systems based on the analysis of a consumer’s preferences (Adomavicius and Tuzhilin 2005, Xiao and Benbasat 2007) has a higher expected utility, because it is expected to match the consumer’s preferences better than a product screened without the help of a recommender system. From the cost perspective, recommender systems can substantially reduce a consumer’s search cost while screening a large set of alternative products online (Adomavicius and Tuzhilin 2005, Xiao and Benbasat 2007). Considering a higher expected bene<sup>fi</sup>t of an additional alternative and lower marginal search cost, the consumer would search more alternatives and add them to the consideration set in the presence of recommended products. Therefore, the use of recommender systems would increase the size of consumers’ consideration sets. Pereira (2001) supports this prediction by showing that consumers have more items in their consideration sets when interactive decision aids are used. Zhang et al. (2011) <sup>fi</sup>nd that higher quality personalized product recommendations are associated with a higher evaluation cost by increasing the size of consumers’ consideration sets. Supporting this logic in a different context, Mitra and Lynch (1995) <sup>fi</sup>nd that informative advertising increases the size of consideration sets by making consumers aware of more viable alternatives. Therefore, whether the use of recommender systems would decrease or increase consideration set size is an empirical question.

Furthermore, whether a larger consideration set would increase consumers’ propensity to buy also remains an empirical question. On the one hand, including more alternatives in the consideration set raises the chances that those alternatives as a whole are more likely to “hit” what consumers intend to buy (Xiao and Benbasat 2007, Zhang et al. 2011), thereby leading to a higher propensity to buy and ultimately more sales. On the other hand, when there are too many alternatives in the consideration set, the problem of “information overload” may occur (Eppler and Mengis 2004, Jones et al. 2004). In this case, consumers may <sup>fi</sup>nd that the comparison and evaluation of the many alternatives require too much cognitive resource and effort, thus leading to a lower propensity to buy. In this study, we will answer these empirical questions by analyzing data from a <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment.

## 3.2. Depth of Consideration Set

On the one hand, recommendations generated from the analysis of consumer preferences should be interesting to consumers and more relevant for purchases, thereby attracting more attention. Because of the greater attention and interest, consumers are likely to look at these alternatives for a longer time and more often. Also, since the recommended alternatives are close to what a consumer intends to buy (Adomavicius and Tuzhilin 2005), the consumer may need to spend more effort and time in inspecting product attributes and comparing across the alternatives before making a purchase decision (Zhang et al. 2011). Thus, in the presence of a recommender system, consumers would have a higher level of involvement or engagement with an average alternative. On the other hand, as the alternatives recommended by the recommender systems are more pertinent to consumers’ preferences, the comparison across them may be straightforward and thus could be conducted in a more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient and faster matter. Accordingly, consumers may spend less effort and time in assessing the <sup>fi</sup>t of the alternatives in consideration (Xiao and Benbasat 2007), thus leading to a lower level of the involvement with an average alternative. Therefore, whether the use of recommender systems would result in a higher or lower level of involvement with an average alternative is ultimately an empirical question.

Finally, the consumer psychology literature suggests that when consumers involve and engage with products, affective connection with the products could build up (Traylor and Joseph 1984). This would increase the consumers’ commitments to the products, inducing them to buy (Traylor 1984, Beatty et al. 1988). Prior studies document empirical evidence for the link between consumer involvement and commitment at both the product and brand levels (Quester and Lim 2003, Michaelidou and Dibb 2008). Even in the of<sup>fl</sup>ine setting, Hui et al. (2013) <sup>fi</sup>nd that a higher level of involvement with the assortment of a supermarket (i.e., walking and time spent in the supermarket) leads to more purchases. Therefore, we expect a higher level of involvement per alternative in consideration to be associated with a higher propensity to buy and an increase in product sales.

4.1.1. Pilot Study 1: Type of Recommenders. For the control/baseline condition, a natural design choice is to make recommendations invisible completely. The extant studies exploring the effect of recommender systems largely use this design choice of control condition (Adomavicius et al. 2013; Kumar and Hosanagar 2019; Lee and Hosanagar 2019, 2021). However, the estimated effect of recommender systems based on this control condition can be interpreted as a combined effect, including the saliency effect of merely showing (possibly irrelevant) products and the effect of showing relevant products. An alternative control condition is to recommend random (possibly irrelevant) products so that the saliency effect can be teased out from the effect of recommender systems. Although this alternative control condition (random recommendations) is unappealing in real-life business practices, as discussed by the recent literature (Lee and Hosanagar 2021), we examine it in Pilot Study 1 to better understand the saliency effect and the effect of showing relevant recommendations. In addition, we include a non-personalized recommender, bestselling, which recommends the most popular products, in Pilot Study 1, in comparison with the personalized CF-based recommender. Therefore, in Pilot Study 1, we compare the effects of CF recommender systems relative to showing no recommendations and two additional baseline conditions: random recommenders and bestselling recommenders.

We conducted a controlled laboratory experiment using a between-subject design in a university laboratory that is used for economics experiments. The university laboratory is equipped with Internet-connected personal computers that are placed in cabinets to separate the participants. We recruited participants with the help of a database that lists students who are willing to participate in laboratory experiments (show-up fee is 10 EUR). We randomly assigned the participants to one of four different conditions: CF recommendations, no recommendations, random recommendations, and bestselling recommendations.

For the laboratory experiment, we created an experimental online shop selling 100 different backpacks. Participants were asked to imagine that they had a shopping budget of 100 EUR. We modeled the online shop’s functionalities and features similar to Amazon, as well as extracted the product information from the Amazon shop, such as title, brand, description, price, and image. For the CF recommender system, we used an open-source implementation of the “Slope-One” algorithm. This algorithm implements the item-based collaborative <sup>fi</sup>ltering (IBCF), which is commonly used in business practice and academic research (Adomavicius et al. 2013; Lee and Hosanagar 2019, 2021). To circumvent the cold-start problem, we collected 4,795 ratings about the products from college students prior to the experiment. We fed the product ratings to the IBCF as the training data for the algorithm. Three alternative products recommended by the IBCF algorithm are presented on the product page (see Online Appendix A).

To ensure that participants only added products to the shopping cart that they really wanted to buy, we implemented an incentive alignment mechanism (Haubl and Trifts¨ 2000). Prior to entering the shopping environment, participants were informed that a winner, determined by a random lottery, would receive an extra remuneration of 100 EUR value in addition to the show-up fee. The remuneration consisted of the products that the participant put into the shopping basket, plus the difference of 100 EUR minus the total value of those products bought. We also showed the participants a concrete example to ensure that they understood the incentive alignment mechanism.

In total, 149 subjects participated in Pilot Study 1. Before they entered the experimental online shop for shopping, we asked each participant to rate <sup>fi</sup>ve products (from one to <sup>fi</sup>ve stars) that were randomly selected from the product pool, so that we could use the revealed preferences of the <sup>fi</sup>ve products to generate the recommended products using the IBCF algorithm and the prior product rating data. If a participant added certain products into the shopping cart during the experiment, then we also fed the revealed preference of the added products to the algorithm for further recommendations. Because only <sup>fi</sup>ve participants bought nothing at all, there was not enough variation in conversion rate, and thus we used basket value as the dependent variable in the analysis. The average basket value was 39.71 EUR (standard deviation [SD] 30.45), which means that the participants spent about one third of their budget on average. On average, they browsed 4.81 different products (SD 3.32, min 0, max 17). We analyzed the effects of different recommender systems on basket value using ordinary least squares (OLS) regression. Because we wanted to compare the effects of the CF recommendations with no recommendations, random recommendations, and bestselling recommendations, we used CF recommendations as the baseline condition in the analysis.

Notably, the positive effect of CF recommendations existed even when compared with the condition of merely showing some (random) products. This means that the positive effect of CF recommendations is not driven by a saliency effect alone. In fact, the estimated coef<sup>fi</sup>cients of no recommendations and random recommendations were very similar, suggesting that random recommendations do not perform better than no recommendations, and that merely showing some (random) products cannot increase consumer purchases. Therefore, we <sup>fi</sup>nd some preliminary evidence showing that no recommendations can be used as a reasonable control/baseline condition fo the <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment.

The estimated coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of bestselling recommendations was negative (albeit insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant), suggesting that personalized CF recommendations, might outperform non-personalized bestselling recommendations. Granted, we did not <sup>fi</sup>nd a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference between CF recommendations and bestselling recommendations in the experiment, which might be an artifact of the small sample size or due to limited personalized data about participants’ preferences.

## 4.2. Design of the Field Experimen

We collaborated with an online bookstore operating in the Netherlands to conduct a randomized controlled <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment. It is a pure online retailer without a physical presence. Accordingly, the visitors of the online bookstore do not necessarily come from a speci<sup>fi</sup>c neighborhood. The online bookstore only sells digital books (PDFs and e-pub format). The online bookstore sells books not only in mainstream popular categories but also in niche categories, similar to typical online bookstores like Amazon. The online bookstore has a web interface similar to most other online bookstores. A product detail page is available for each book title, providing the information about the title, author(s), ISBN, prices, shipping options, and other metadata. The online store had never used any recommender system prior to our experiment.

The goal of the <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment is to empirically test the mediation model proposed in Section 3. For the experiment, we followed the industry practice and deployed a recommender system based on the design principles of memory-based collaborative <sup>fi</sup>ltering (Adomavicius and Tuzhilin 2005, Su and Khoshgoftaa 2009). The recommender was able to suggest book titles that similar customers had bought but the focal customer did not yet buy; we present the details about the design factors and characteristics of the recommender system in Online Appendix B. The experimental design was as follows. The experimental units were all visitors to the focal store during the experiment pe riod (from April 2 to July 1, 2015). The treatment was assigned at the level of sessions opened on the web server, while taking great care that returning visitors were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as such and the assignment of treatment to the visitors remained stable during the experiment. We exogenously manipulated the elements of the book detail pages in the experiment and randomly assigned all visitors into one of the two experimental conditions (control and treatment). The experimental treatment was to show a box of recommended book ti tles on the detail page of a focal book. Visitors under the “treatment” condition were in the treatment group and could see three recommendations (in line with the results from Pilot Study 2) that the recommender system generated in a box below the basic information of the focal book.<sup>3</sup> Visitors assigned to the “control” condition were in the “control group” and unable to see any recommendations. All the other elements of the book detail pages between the two experiment conditions were identical. Figure 2 illustrates the screenshots of the two experimental conditions. We note that the manipulation of showing versus not showing recommendations in our experiment follows our results from Pilot Study 1 and is also in line with the experimental design of the extant studies exploring the effect of recommender systems (Adomavicius et al. 2013; Kumar and Hosanagar 2019; Lee and Hosanagar 2019, 2021).

## 5.3. Causal Mediation Analysis

Mediation analysis is a nontrivial empirical task and has become progressively popular in the IS and marketing literature (Mitra and Lynch 1995, Tsai and Bagozzi 2014, Krasnova et al. 2015, Zhou and Duan 2016). The methodology for mediation analysis was <sup>fi</sup>rst proposed by Baron and Kenny (1986) and then improved by Zhao et al. (2010) at several aspects. Scholars have recently reinvented it using the causal counterfactual framework (Imai et al. 2010a, b; Valeri and VanderWeele 2013; VanderWeele 2015; VanderWeele 2016), which allows for a clear causal interpretation of the mediation results based on counterfactuals. To the best of our knowledge, the application of the recent causal mediation approach in the IS literature is still lacking, and we are perhaps among the <sup>fi</sup>rst to employ the causal mediation analysis in the IS research (Peng 2019). To establish that an independent variable or exposure A (in our case: the use of a recommender system) affects an outcome Y (for example: the propensity to buy) through a mediating variable M, we estimate two equations:

Mediator Equation 1 $E [ M \mid a , c ] ~ = \beta _ { 0 } + \beta _ { 1 } a + \beta _ { 2 } ^ { ' } c$

Outcome Equation <sub>(</sub>2<sub>)</sub> E<sub>[</sub>Y <sub>|</sub> a, M, c<sub>]</sub>

$$
= \theta_ {0} + \theta_ {1} * a + \theta_ {2} * m + \theta_ {3} * a * m + \theta_ {4} ^ {\prime} * c
$$

An important difference from the classic Baron and Kenny approach is that we include an exposuremediator interaction in the outcome equation. This allows the mediator to have different in<sup>fl</sup>uences on the outcome for different values of the treatment (capturing the idea of A in<sup>fl</sup>uencing M, which goes on to affect Y). Using (1) and (2), we can give counterfactual de<sup>fi</sup>- nitions of direct and indirect effects. We are interested in the natural direct effect (NDE) and the natural indirect effect (NIE). The natural direct effect measures how much the outcome would change if the treatment condition would change from a situation without a recommender system, to a situation where the recommender system is available, but values for the mediator remain <sup>fi</sup>xed at their “natural” level. The natural level for the mediator is the level that it would take in the absence of the exposure (no recommender system). The natural indirect effect measures how much the outcome would change on average if the recommender system is there but the mediator is changed from the level it would have without a recommender system to the level it would have with a recommender system. NDE and NIE can be computed as linear combinations of the coef<sup>fi</sup>cients from the mediator and outcome equations. The total effect of the recommender system decomposes into NIE and NDE, allowing us to compute how much of the total effect of the recommender system is actually being mediated. We provide more details and explanations in Online Appendix D.

5.3.1. Single Mediator: Consideration Set Size. We report results of the mediation analysis using Order as the outcome variable,<sup>6</sup> and, accordingly, we estimate the effects on conversion rate. The recent study by Lee and Hosanagar (2021) also uses conversion rate as the primary dependent variable. We <sup>fi</sup>rst use consideration set size (lnSetSize) as the single mediator. As it is unclear if our experimental treatment and lnSetSize interact in their effects on the outcome ex ante, we include an exposure–mediator interaction in the estimation of step 2, following the best practice of causal mediation analysis (Valeri and VanderWeele 2013, VanderWeele 2015, VanderWeele 2016). Columns (1) and (2) of Table 8 report the results of the two-step regression. All coef<sup>fi</sup>- cients are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant except for RS lnSetSize, suggesting that there is no exposure–mediator interaction. Since the outcome Order is dichotomous, the natural indirect and direct effects are reported as odds ratios.

The <sup>fi</sup>rst row of Table 9 reports the estimates and 95%-level con<sup>fi</sup>dence intervals. All con<sup>fi</sup>dence levels are bootstrapped with bias correction. The estimated natural indirect effect odds ratio is 1.029 and statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at the 95% level (the 95% con<sup>fi</sup>dence interval lies entirely above 1.0). The natural direct effect odds ratio is 1.092 but not statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at the 95% level (the 95% con<sup>fi</sup>dence interval covers 1.0). Therefore, we <sup>fi</sup>nd a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant indirect effect mediated through the size of consideration set. According to the decision tree and terminology by Zhao et al. (2010), this is an indirect-only mediation (or so-called “full mediation”) since the direct effect is insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant. Yet, point estimates suggest that only 25.7% of the total effect is mediated through consideration set size. This is because the natural direct effect odds ratio is 1.092, though not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different from 1.0 at the 95% level.

If we want to interpret the detected mediation effect as causal, we need to assume that there are no mediator-outcome confounders, even if the treatment is randomly assigned. This is the so-called “sequential ignorability assumption” (Imai et al. 2010a, b). It is inherently dif<sup>fi</sup>cult, if not impossible, to manipulate the mediator M while manipulating the exposure $T$ (Bullock and Ha 2011), so it is advised to conduct sensitivity analysis examining the extent to which the detected mediation effect would have disappeared or changed its sign if there is indeed some unobserved mediator-outcome confounder (Imai et al. 2010a, b; Valeri and VanderWeele 2013; VanderWeele 2015). If an unobserved mediator-outcome confounder exists, the correlation between the residuals from the two es timation steps (i.e., correlation between $e _ { 1 }$ and $e _ { 2 } )$ would not be zero. Thus, one way of conducting sensitivity analysis is to show how the average causal mediation effect changes with the correlation parameter $\rho$ (VanderWeele 2015, VanderWeele 2016).

We use the mediation package in STATA to conduct the sensitivity analysis (Hicks and Tingley 2011). Figure 3 illustrates the average causal mediation effect as a function of $\rho$ and the 95% con<sup>fi</sup>dence interval. It shows that a value of $\rho$ greater than 0.30 would make the detected mediation effect disappear. In other words, the mediation effect would still be positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant as long as the correlation parameter $\boldsymbol { \mathrm { \ell } }$ is less than 0.30. Alternatively, the product of the $R ^ { ' 2 }$ values of the mediator and outcome models is 0.09, suggesting that the omitted confounder has to explain, for example, 30% of the remaining variance in the mediator and 30% of the remaining variance in the outcome $( 3 0 \% \times 3 0 \% = 0 . 0 9 )$ in order for the average causal mediation effect to disappear.

The estimated natural direct effect odds ratio is 1.111 and also signi<sup>fi</sup> cant at the 95% level (the bootstrapped 95% con<sup>fi</sup>- dence interval: [1.011, 1.229]). Therefore, we <sup>fi</sup>nd a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant indirect effect which is mediated through the involvement per alternative. The mediation effect is complementary with the direct effect, because they have the same sign (Zhao et al. 2010). The point estimates suggest that 10.2% of the total effect is mediated through lnViewsPerItem.

## 6. Discussion

## 6.1. Implications for Research

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings yield several important implications for research. First, prior studies that use <sup>fi</sup>eld data to estimate the effects of recommender systems typically assume that recommender systems could reduce con sumers’ search costs and help them <sup>fi</sup>nd products that are more relevant for purchase. But they largely ne glect to examine this assumption in depth. In this paper, we theorize and propose that two facets of the consideration set—namely, the size of the consideration set (breadth) and the involvement per alternative in consideration (depth)—mediate the pathways through which recommender systems in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumers’ purchase decisions. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings support the proposed mediation relations and uncover the mechanisms underlying the positive effects of recommender systems on consumer purchases.

Second, there is an ongoing debate about how recommender systems affect the size of the consideration set. On the one hand, Xiao and Benbasat (2007) propose that the use of recommender systems reduces consideration set size, and Haubl and his colleagues¨ support this proposition in a series of laboratory experiments (Haubl and Trifts¨ 2000, Haubl and Murray¨ 2003, Dellaert and Haubl¨ 2012). On the other hand, however, Pereira (2001) and Zhang et al. (2011) <sup>fi</sup>nd that recommender systems can increase consideration set size. Contributing to this ongoing debate, we provide <sup>fi</sup>eld evidence that collaborative <sup>fi</sup>ltering increases the size of consideration sets, which in turn increases the propensity to purchase. The discrepancy of our results, compared with the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the prior studies by Haubl and his colleagues (e.g., H¨ aubl and¨ Trifts 2000, Haubl and Murray¨ 2003) could be possibly explained by the fact that the recommender systems in their laboratory studies are not CF recommenders but are based on consumers’ self-explicated “attribute importance weights” of recommendation alternatives. Besides that, the recommended products in a laboratory setting may have a priming effect on subjects, leading them to search fewer alternatives and stop their search processes sooner. Another possible explanation could lie in the accuracy of the recommender systems under investigation, because a perfect recommender system may present the best-<sup>fi</sup>t product to a consumer immediately, whereas recommendations that are close to, but do not perfectly match, a consumer’s preference, may induce the consumer to look at more recommended items.

Third, whereas the literature documents the effects of recommender systems on the breadth (size) of consideration sets, prior research largely neglects to examine the effects on the depth of consideration sets. Some prior studies consider the total involvement with all alternatives to understand the total effort of in-depth comparison when they examine the effects of recommender systems (Xiao and Benbasat 2007, Zhang et al. 2011), but there is a mechanical positive correlation between total involvement with all alternatives and the number of alternatives (i.e., consideration set size), making it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to disentangle the two constructs empirically. We propose to examine the effect of recommender systems on the involvement per alternative in consideration, which provides a <sup>fi</sup>ne granular view. Recommender systems increase both the consideration set size and the involvement per alternative in consideration, which makes it reasonable to deduce that they increase the total involvement with all alternatives. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings also reveal that the mediating roles of the two facets of the consideration set are not equal; the mediation effect through the breadth (size) of consideration set is substantially larger than that through the depth.

Last but not least, whereas consumers often use the “consider-then-choose” decision process for shopping, they do so with heuristic decision rules that are often latent and not directly observable (Hauser 2014). Heuristic decision rules can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into compensatory and noncompensatory, and consumers are likely to use noncompensatory heuristic rules to add alternative products in the consideration set but use compensatory (e.g., more effortful) heuristic rules to compare and evaluate the alternatives before making a purchase decision (Dippold and Dietrich 2009). Since our results show that the presence of a recommendation system leads consumers to view more products and engage more with an average alternative in the consideration set, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings reveal that presenting recommendations to consumers can promote them to use both noncompensatory and compensatory heuristics in their shopping processes.

## 6.2. Implications for Practice

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings generate important implications for practitioners and online retailers. First, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings largely support the research model that underscores the important role of consumers’ consideration sets in mediating the positive effects of recommender systems on con sumer purchases. Practical strategies can be developed to facilitate the formation of the consideration sets. For example, to reduce consumers’ search costs and cognitive efforts, online retailers can display the recommended products in a descending order according to the predicted closeness of consumers’ preferences. Online retailers can further indicate the predicted closeness scores of consumers’ preferences for the recommended products. Given such a placement arrangement, consumers can quickly screen the recommended products and add the most relevant alternatives to their consideration sets, which should facilitate consumers’ shopping process and increase the shopping satisfaction.

Second, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment reveal that consideration sets play an important role in mediating the positive effects of recommender systems on consumer purchases. Particularly, the results suggest that a larger consideration set due to the use of recommender systems could induce consumers to buy. Yet, it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for consumers to manage many alternatives when the consideration set is very large. To facilitate consumers’ shopping process, online retailers need to consider strategies and tools that help consumers manage the alternatives in the consideration set in a betterorganized manner and facilitate the comparison across the alternatives. For example, online retailers can develop a web-based service that allows consumers to restore the alternative products that are either recommended by recommender systems or that consumers want to come back to and compare in depth. Online retailers can also develop a web-based tool like a comparison matrix (Haubl and Trifts¨ 2000), another online shopping decision aid that can complement typical recommender systems. The comparison matrix tools organize the alternatives in consideration using a matrix, in which each column represents an alternative product, and each row indicates speci<sup>fi</sup>c product attributes across the alternatives. Interestingly, Amazon.com has introduced such a comparison tool along with its widely used recommender system. We expect that more online retailers pick up this emerging practice.

Finally, our results contribute to the discussions in the domain of online shop design, where the prevailing opinion is that the process for customers has to be as lean and convenient as possible. This claim is supported by research (e.g., Heimbach and Hinz 2018) and instantiated by designs like the “1-Click” ordering by Amazon. However, our results show that a worthwhile and meaningful prolongation of the customer journey may increase the customers’ probability to buy through increasing the size and depth of consideration sets. Combining the results from previous research and our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, we recommend making the processes that do not contribute to the shopping experience (e.g., payment and entering shipping address) as ef<sup>fi</sup>cient and fast as possible, but online retailers do not need to see ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and fast customer journeys as the ultimate goal, because a longer customer journey (which is probably related to a positive shopping experience) can lead to more sales.

## 6.3. Limitations and Future Research

Our research also has some limitations that shed light on future research directions. First, the extant studies exploring the effects of recommender systems mostly use the condition of no recommendations as the control condition, especially those that have conducted <sup>fi</sup>eld experiments (Adomavicius et al. 2013; Kumar and Hosanagar 2019; Lee and Hosanagar 2019, 2021). This is because it is unappealing, if not impossible, for a real online retailer to deploy a recommender system that recommends irrelevant or random products to customers in real-life business practice. To this end, the design of our <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment is consistent with the extant literature. Accordingly, the estimated effects of recommender systems examined in our research need to be interpreted as a combined effect, including the saliency effect of merely showing (possibly irrelevant) products and the effect of showing relevant products. Although our pilot laboratory experiment provides preliminary evidence that random recommendations may have a similar effect as no recommendations, we do not tease out the saliency effect from the estimated effects of recommender systems in the <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment. Future research can address this limitation in a real-life <sup>fi</sup>eld setting.

Second, none of the extant studies with <sup>fi</sup>eld experiments that we are aware of use the bestselling recommender as an alternative control condition and neither does our research. The CF-based recommender system that we implement for the <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment, like any typical CF-based recommender system, tends to recommend popular products, mainly because popular products are more likely to be copurchased (or coviewed) with other products. Consistent with the extent literature (Adomavicius et al. 2013; Kumar and Hosanagar 2019; Lee and Hosanagar 2019, 2021), our research examines the effects of personalized CF-based recommender systems, compared to the condition of no recommendations. Accordingly, future research can explore the extent to which our main <sup>fi</sup>ndings about the mediation role of consideration sets hold when we explore the effect of a personalized CF-based recommender system, compared with the nonpersonalized bestselling recommender.

Third, memory-based CF is quite commonly used in the current business practice and studied in the recent academic literature (Lin et al. 2017; Adomavicius et al. 2018, 2019; Kumar and Hosanagar 2019; Lee and Hosanagar 2019, 2021). Yet, there are other types of CF recommenders, such as model-based CF, content-based CF, hybrid recommenders (Adomavicius and Tuzhilin 2005, Su and Khoshgoftaar 2009), and the more advanced deep-learning based recommenders (He et al. 2017). The recommender systems used in our research fall in the category of memory-based CF. It is unclear to what extent the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of our research can be generalizable to other types of recommender systems. Thus, we admit this is a limitation and an avenue for future research.

Finally, a consumer’s consideration set could be affected by product types (e.g., utilitarian vs. hedonic products), and, accordingly, the impact of a recommendation system on consideration sets may vary, depending on product types. Unfortunately, the online retailer that we have collaborated with for the <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment only sells one product category (books), which limits our possibility to explore the heterogeneity across product categories. Future research can seek to examine if the mediation role of consideration sets in the effects of recommender systems differs across different product types, such as utilitarian versus hedonic products, and search versus experience products.
