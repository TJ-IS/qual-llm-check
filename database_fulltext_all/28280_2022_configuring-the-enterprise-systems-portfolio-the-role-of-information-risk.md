Abstract. We investigate how public <sup>fi</sup>rms con<sup>fi</sup>gure their enterprise systems (ES) portfolio when faced with information risk, which refers to the likelihood that corporate <sup>fi</sup>nancia information is of poor quality. We focus on <sup>fi</sup>rms’ con<sup>fi</sup>guration of their ES portfolio by introducing a novel construct: ES portfolio balance, or the relative proportion of two cate gories of ES modules, operational and functional. We draw on the theory of information processing to hypothesize the impact of information risk on ES portfolio balance and how this impact is affected by internal controls. We construct a multisource panel data set of 697 <sup>fi</sup>rms and 1,993 <sup>fi</sup>rm-year observations from 2005 to 2008 and use econometric and multivariate procedures to test our hypotheses. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that when faced with an increase in information risk, <sup>fi</sup>rms change their ES portfolio balance more toward operational modules. However, when such <sup>fi</sup>rms are also faced with materially weak internal controls, they change their ES portfolio balance more toward functional modules instead. These <sup>fi</sup>nding expand our understanding of how <sup>fi</sup>rms’ information processing needs drive the con<sup>fi</sup>gu ration of their ES portfolio and, more broadly, IT resources portfolio.

## 1. Introduction

Enterprise systems (ES) are a key class of information systems that help <sup>fi</sup>rms meet information processing needs by providing a uni<sup>fi</sup>ed information technology (IT) architecture that enhances data consistency and integrates modular applications. Although worldwide spending on ES was estimated to be in excess of USD 40 billion in 2020,<sup>1</sup> research has shown that merely investing in ES implementations is not suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to create value (e.g., Grabski et al. 2011). Implementing ES without proper planning and alignment with business needs can have heavy downside costs.<sup>2</sup> Firms typically operate under IT budget constraints, so they need to con<sup>fi</sup>gure their ES portfolios based on their information processing requirements (Keil and Tiwana 2006).

The focus of prior research on IT resources, particularly ES, has been on the determinants of the success or failure of system implementations and the organizational and economic impacts of the implementations (e.g., Grabski et al. 2011 and Ghosh 2012). The understanding of resource con<sup>fi</sup>guration in organizations in response to information processing needs is largely underdeveloped. To establish de<sup>fi</sup>nitional clarity up front, we use resource configuration to mean the balancing of a resource portfolio in favor of one or some resources over others (Sirmon and Hitt 2003, Sirmon et al. 2007). In conceptualizing ES portfolio con<sup>fi</sup>guration, we differentiate between types of ES resources based on the type of module. The ES literature conceptualizes ES as encompassing two categories of modules: operational (customer and sales, supply chain) and functional (accounting and <sup>fi</sup>nance, human resources) (Barki and Pinsonneault 2005, Tian and Xu 2015). Building on and furthering this line of research, we focus on ES portfolio con<sup>fi</sup>guration, or balancing the ES portfolio in favor of implementing operational or functional modules.

We examine the determinants of a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s ES portfolio con<sup>fi</sup>guration through the lens of information risk. A concept that originated from accounting and <sup>fi</sup>- nance literatures, information risk refers to the likelihood that corporate <sup>fi</sup>nancial information is of poor quality (Francis et al. 2005). To elaborate, poor-quality <sup>fi</sup>nancial information does not accurately represent <sup>fi</sup>rm performance, thus exposing stakeholders (top management, investors, etc.) to risk when they use such information to make business decisions (Dechow and Dichev 2002, Dechow et al. 2010). Contemporary management relies heavily on a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s <sup>fi</sup>nancial information, usually based on standard accounting principles, to record <sup>fi</sup>rm activities and produce summative performance indices (e.g., annual and quarterly earnings). When making strategic decisions, a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s executive management must correctly understand how well the <sup>fi</sup>rm is functioning. Information risk can seriously jeopardize the effectiveness of managerial decision making because <sup>fi</sup>nancial information of poor quality will misguide decision makers (Krishnan et al. 2008). Information risk is also a serious concern to investors because it suggests that the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s <sup>fi</sup>nancial information, which is vital to investors’ pricing decisions, is of poor quality. The capital market uses a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s <sup>fi</sup>nancial information (e.g., earnings) to price <sup>fi</sup>rms and to allocate <sup>fi</sup>nancial resources. If a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s <sup>fi</sup>- nancial information appears highly uncertain, then it is risky to use it for capital allocation decisions. As a result, the capital market will increase the cost of capital to compensate for the uncertainty (Gray et al. 2009). The negative impact of information risk also includes damaged <sup>fi</sup>rm reputation, limited fund-raising ability, and even litigation costs, among other things (Dechow et al. 2010). Coping with information risk is therefore a critical information processing need for <sup>fi</sup>rms.

We submit that information processing needs rendered by information risk will in<sup>fl</sup>uence <sup>fi</sup>rms to change the con<sup>fi</sup>guration of their ES portfolios. Enterprise systems are implemented to conduct a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s value-chain activities through a uni<sup>fi</sup>ed system and to collect, integrate, process, and aggregate information pertaining to the business processes (Davenport 2000). Within a speci<sup>fi</sup>ed IT budget, which is usually the standard business practice, <sup>fi</sup>rms must decide how to con<sup>fi</sup>gure their ES portfolios by balancing the modules that are implemented in favor of operational or functional modules. Yet, to the best of our knowledge, prior research about ES implementation has not examined how information risk in<sup>fl</sup>uences ES portfolio con<sup>fi</sup>guration, and we seek to bridge this gap. Furthermore, we contend that answers to the how question would hinge on where answers are found— speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, across <sup>fi</sup>rms differing in the strength of their internal controls. If one merely attributes the ES portfolio con<sup>fi</sup>guration to addressing information risk, it would be a technology-centric perspective in the sense that <sup>fi</sup>rms only consider that technology addresses their information processing needs. However, <sup>fi</sup>rms must also take into consideration their internal rules and procedures for <sup>fi</sup>nancial information reporting, referred to as internal controls (Doyle et al. 2007b). It has been widely noted that internal controls aim to ensure the integrity of <sup>fi</sup>rms’ <sup>fi</sup>nancial information, but in reality, <sup>fi</sup>rms vary in internal control strength. In particular, <sup>fi</sup>rms may suffer from internal control material weakness (Doyle et al. 2007a). A material weakness is a de<sup>fi</sup> ciency, or a combination of de<sup>fi</sup>ciencies, in internal controls over <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting such that there is a reasonable possibility that a material misstatement of the company’s annual or interim <sup>fi</sup>nancial statements will not be prevented or detected in a timely manner. When <sup>fi</sup>rms con<sup>fi</sup>gure their information processing tools, decisions must be embedded in the context of the interna control processes where the technology tools are to be utilized (Li et al. 2012). With these points in mind, we ask the following: How does information risk faced by <sup>fi</sup>rms in<sup>fl</sup>uence their decisions about ES portfolio con<sup>fi</sup>g uration, and how does this in<sup>fl</sup>uence, if any, change when a <sup>fi</sup>rm has material weaknesses in internal controls?

## 2. Theory Background

## 2.1. Gaps in Prior Literature on Enterprise Systems

Synthesizing the ES literature, Grabski et al. (2011) identi<sup>fi</sup>ed three overarching objectives on which past research has focused: (1) critical success factors for ES implementations (e.g., user acceptance, organizational change management), (2) the organiza tional impact of ES implementations (e.g., control strategies, risk management), and (3) the economic impact of ES implementations (e.g., return to shareholders, market valuation). Our synthesis of the most frequently cited papers on ES, up to the writing of this paper, shows that studies have continued to focus on these three research objectives (see Online Appendix A).

Although prior research has advanced our understanding of the adoption and impact of ES, we recognize two critical gaps in our understanding. First, the literature provides limited insight on why and how ES portfolio con<sup>fi</sup>guration decisions are made in <sup>fi</sup>rms. The dominant perspective of past work has been to identify speci<sup>fi</sup>c ES modules and assess the impact of these modules, or their combinations, on a variety of organizational and economic outcomes. For example, prior research examined how the scope of implemented ES modules in<sup>fl</sup>uences <sup>fi</sup>rm productivity (Hitt et al. 2002) and <sup>fi</sup>rm performance volatility (Tian and Xu 2015). Although the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of these studies imply how <sup>fi</sup>rms need to structure their ES portfolios to improve performance, we aim to advance a conceptualization of ES portfolio con<sup>fi</sup>guration with a focus on discerning the emphasis placed on implementing different categories of modules and examine how and why <sup>fi</sup>rms make their ES portfolio con<sup>fi</sup>guration decisions. In fact, to the best of our knowledge, no study until now has placed salience on and explicitly conceptualized constructs related to the con<sup>fi</sup>guration of the ES portfolio and formally incorporated them in theorization and empirical research.

Second, although prior research has recognized that ES can help <sup>fi</sup>rms address a variety of information processing needs, the impact of <sup>fi</sup>rms’ information processing needs emanating from their <sup>fi</sup>nancial context on ES portfolio con<sup>fi</sup>guration is unclear. Prior studies typically assume that <sup>fi</sup>rms implement ES because these systems provide distinctive information processing capabilities that can enable them to achieve goals such as developing business-process capabilities, reducing economic risks, and improving <sup>fi</sup>rm valuation. Although it is well understood that the <sup>fi</sup>nancial data and reporting context creates distinctive information processing needs (e.g., highvolume transactions, segregation of duties, <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting) (Gray et al. 2009, Li et al. 2012), how these needs in<sup>fl</sup>uence ES portfolio con<sup>fi</sup>guration is unclear. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, it is unclear how and why <sup>fi</sup>rms will change their ES portfolios to address these information processing needs while working within the ES implementation budget.

2.2. Conceptualizing ES Portfolio Configuration Enterprise systems generally fall into two module categories: operational and functional (Barki and Pinsonneault 2005, Tian and Xu 2015). Building on this distinction, we conceptualize <sup>fi</sup>rms’ con<sup>fi</sup>guration of their ES portfolios as determining their ES portfolio balance by setting the proportion of operational and functional modules relative to one another. Our idea of ES portfolio balance is similar to the notion of balance/imbalance among process capabilities, as discussed in the ambidexterity literature (e.g., He and Wong 2004 and Tang and Rai 2014), and differential value gains, as discussed in the strategy literature (e.g., Kumar 2010) (see Table 1). He and Wong (2004), for example, studied how an imbalance between exploration and exploitation strategy affects a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s performance. Similarly, Tang and Rai (2014) investigated how the balance between process alignment and partnering <sup>fl</sup>exibility (where a higher difference between process alignment and partnering <sup>fl</sup>exibility re<sup>fl</sup>ects a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s more dominant pursuit of either the alignment strategy or the <sup>fl</sup>exibility strategy, and a lower difference indicates a more balanced pursuit of the two strategies) affects a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s competitive performance. Drawing on this idea, we posit that in response to information processing needs, <sup>fi</sup>rms can change the balance of their ES portfolios—for example, having a greater increase of operational modules relative to functional modules in their ES portfolio—or vice versa.

## 2.3. Information Processing Needs to Cope with Information Risk

The perspective of information processing (Galbraith 1974) has been widely used in ES literature. It contends that developing information systems is a key mechanism <sup>fi</sup>rms leverage to meet their information processing needs—particularly to meet the need to address uncertainty in operations and <sup>fi</sup>nancial processes (Premkumar et al. 2005, Tian and Xu 2015). The ES literature has identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the capabilities of ES to meet <sup>fi</sup>rms’ information processing needs to respond to uncertainties emanating from different contexts, such as interdependence between manufacturing plants (Gattiker and Goodhue 2005), coordination among distribution centers (Cotteleer and Bendoly 2006), and the competitive environment in an industry (Tian and Xu 2015). In this work, we focus on <sup>fi</sup>rms’ information risk—an uncertainty measure developed in the accounting and <sup>fi</sup>nance literatures—that measures the level of uncertainty regarding whether a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s <sup>fi</sup>nancial information accurately describes how well the <sup>fi</sup>rm is functioning.

A <sup>fi</sup>rm’s <sup>fi</sup>nancial statement records cash <sup>fl</sup>ows, including in<sup>fl</sup>ows and out<sup>fl</sup>ows (i.e., revenue and expense) that accompany the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s economic activities. Furthermore, <sup>fi</sup>rms use the accrual accounting principle to adjust cash-<sup>fl</sup>ow-based <sup>fi</sup>nancial information. Accrual accounting recognizes sales (expenses) before cash is received (paid out). The purpose is to match the timing of the accounting recognition with the timing of economic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts/costs from selling/spending (Dechow et al. 2010). In other words, accrual accounting aims to record timely information to describe the economic consequences of a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s activities (e.g., sales, production). On the contrary, solely relying on cash <sup>fl</sup>ows may cause delayed recognition of the economic consequences; for example, a cash payment made by a customer happens after con<sup>fi</sup>rming the sale (i.e., when the contract was signed), which, however, is the time when the sale triggers responses in production and inventory management and, therefore, is the correct time to record the economic impact of the sale.

Accruals, however, can bring uncertainty into <sup>fi</sup>nancial information because they are frequently based on managers’ assumptions and estimates (Dechow and Dichev 2002). Estimating accruals requires managers to forecast future operations (e.g., customers’ ability to pay cash, potential obsolescence of inventory, and the useful life of equipment). Given that accruals are based on anticipation, information risk underscores the uncertainty that a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s (accrual-based) <sup>fi</sup>nancial information may not re<sup>fl</sup>ect the truth. For instance, to recognize revenue from credit sales, managers need to estimate how much cash customers can pay in the future. If managers do not have reliable information about the sales pipelines and the customer’s credit history, then accruals may suffer from large estimation errors (i.e., discrepancies between the current estimated revenue and the future paid cash). In another case, managers recognize equipment impairment loss when facing damage or obsolescence. However, if managers lack reliable information about any damage in production or the useful life of an asset, they may overestimate assets’ impairment losses, causing an unnecessary underestimation of earnings.

Information risk renders information processing needs for a <sup>fi</sup>rm in two ways: handling uncertainty in business operations and mitigating discretionary errors (Dechow and Dichev 2002, Francis et al. 2005). First, uncertainty in a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s <sup>fi</sup>nancial information may be attributed to volatility of the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s operations. For example, a <sup>fi</sup>rm could recognize credit sales, but later market volatility could bankrupt its customers. The <sup>fi</sup>rm would then not be able to receive the payment, and a correction—bad-debt expense—would occur. The overestimated credit sales, as well as the subsequent bad-debt expense, would add “arti<sup>fi</sup>cial” noise to the variability of the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s true performance. In this example, noise in the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s <sup>fi</sup>nancial information is caused by uncertainties in the business operations.

Second, when estimating <sup>fi</sup>rm performance (e.g., earnings), managers need to collect <sup>fi</sup>nancial information throughout the <sup>fi</sup>rm. Compared with management at headquarters, divisional managers whose departments carry out speci<sup>fi</sup>c operations have more timely and accurate information about their operations, but information <sup>fl</sup>ows—from the places where <sup>fi</sup>rm operations take place toward headquarters—can be delayed, distorted, and lost. Managers may then use their discretionary judgment to compensate for the incomplete or noisy <sup>fi</sup>nancial data. Managers’ discretional judgment may introduce errors, intentionally or unintentionally.<sup>3</sup> As a result, <sup>fi</sup>nancial information is likely to be of poor quality or high risk.

To sum up, an increase in information risk means that <sup>fi</sup>rms will need to cope with uncertainty and volatility in business operations and face a higher possibility of discretionary error introduced into the data by managers. In the next section, we theorize why and how such critical issues create the need for automated, standardized, and integrated information processing capabilities so that <sup>fi</sup>rms will want to appropriately con<sup>fi</sup>gure their ES portfolio and accordingly emphasize the implementation of operational or functional modules.

## 3.1. Influence of Information Risk on ES Portfolio Balance

We propose that, in general, <sup>fi</sup>rms facing high information risk will resort to ES operational modules as a solution. In addition to actuals (i.e., completed earnings and expenses), <sup>fi</sup>rms also report accruals (i.e., expected earnings and expenses)—the key information reported to the market—based on anticipation of future performance. However, their estimation of accruals—and in particular, the expected earnings portion of the accruals—may be of poor quality (i.e., high information risk). This can arise when the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s operations are subject to high uncertainty or when managers lack information about fundamentals of business operations (Francis et al. 2005). To address these problems, <sup>fi</sup>rms must develop capabilities that will help them cope with uncertainty in business operations and be well informed about fundamentals, for which we posit that <sup>fi</sup>rms will con<sup>fi</sup>gure their ES portfolios toward operational modules.

First, <sup>fi</sup>rms can resort to ES operational modules to cope with uncertainties in business operations. For example, a <sup>fi</sup>rm may increase production in anticipation of future sales, but the expected customers may not be able to make the payments, leading to a bad-debt expense for the <sup>fi</sup>rm. Such a situation will create a greater need for the <sup>fi</sup>rm to develop information-driven, agile, and <sup>fl</sup>exible operational capabilities for which operational modules will be desirable. To elaborate, when there are changes to payments or customer orders, the supply chain module triggers a series of actions that include changing production plans, inventory monitoring, sending purchase orders to suppliers, and adjusting work schedules. The customer and sales module, meanwhile, triggers a follow-up series of actions that include scheduling ful<sup>fi</sup>llment and updating customer purchases (Tian and Xu 2015). Collectively, the operational modules can help <sup>fi</sup>rms modify their product lines fo other customers to reduce the impact of bad debt. Therefore, <sup>fi</sup>rms are likely to consider increasing the implementation and use of operational modules that develop capabilities for addressing the information proc essing needs created by information risk caused by disruptions in operations.

Second, <sup>fi</sup>rms can implement ES operational modules to standardize and thus reduce variation in business operations. Uncertainty and disruptions in business op erations can result in managers estimating earnings inaccurately, relying more on conjecture than on data. Insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient standardization of processes and a lack of business protocols, both of which underlie such concerns, create a need for operational modules that can be implemented to enforce procedural protocols, which are typically based on best practices in the industry. In addition to feeding transaction data to functional modules, operational modules help eliminate idiosyncratic personal discretion of personnel responsible for managing operations at the business site. Operational modules also enable employees to comply with the es tablished rules of the system, thereby reducing variation in operations- and transaction-related activities (Jia et al. 2020). For example, prior studies (e.g., Cotteleer and Bendoly 2006) have documented <sup>fi</sup>rms’ ability to remove bottlenecks in business processes by making changes in supply availability and customer demands; they do this by standardizing order speci<sup>fi</sup>cations and quality assur ance by implementing operational modules where nearly all business transactions are recorded using uniform standards and where the corresponding transactionrelated information is shared through the value chain. Thus, we suggest that <sup>fi</sup>rms will implement ES operational modules to respond to information risk because business process standardization is lacking.

Third, to improve the quality of earnings estimates, <sup>fi</sup>rms need the ability to integrate data along the entire value chain as though it were one entity (Tian and Xu

2015). The task of integrating data across the entire value chain in a timely manner becomes even more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult and important in the presence of information risk, creating the need for superior IT capabilities. Operational modules can help <sup>fi</sup>rms integrate transaction information up and down the supply chain ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently and effectively. A well-known utility of operational modules is to alleviate the “bullwhip effect” (i.e., the ampli<sup>fi</sup>cation of forecasting errors and inef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies across the supply chain) by sharing information about customer orders or supply changes. To illustrate, when a <sup>fi</sup>rm estimates its earnings, it may overestimate its sales potential, leading to excess stocking of inventory of raw materials and parts and overinvestment in machines needed for production. Later, the <sup>fi</sup>rm may have to claim losses if the inventory is not needed or the machines become obsolete.<sup>4</sup> To mitigate such forecasting errors and inef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies, <sup>fi</sup>rms need the ability to accurately identify the correct amount of machinery and inventory needed and track inter<sup>fi</sup>rm transactions regarding purchased materials, logistics, and supplier information, all of which operational modules typically provide (Rai et al. 2006). Operational modules enhance the sharing of information and coordination of activities across the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s supply chain so that business units within the <sup>fi</sup>rm and suppliers outside the <sup>fi</sup>rm can respond to demand changes in an integrated and timely manner, thus reducing operational volatility. In summary, the quality of estimated earnings information (i.e., accruals) depends on the quality of information related to business operations. This dependency will lead <sup>fi</sup>rms facing high information risk to focus on implementing operational modules to generate more accurate earnings information. Therefore, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 1. Firms facing higher information risk will configure their ES portfolio more toward operational modules.

## 3.2. Moderating Role of Internal Controls

Hypothesis 1 suggests that, on average, across contingencies, <sup>fi</sup>rms will implement ES operational modules as a solution to information risk. We further propose that the strength of internal controls is a key contingency that moderates the relationship between information risk and ES portfolio balance (see Figure 1).

Internal controls govern <sup>fi</sup>rms’ internal processes that convert operational data to <sup>fi</sup>nancial reports. When these internal controls contain material weaknesses, <sup>fi</sup>rms are vulnerable to (a) invalid integration of data from different business operations (e.g., duplicate entries for a single transaction, incorrect translation of foreign currencies, miscalculation of assets), (b) failure to follow generally accepted accounting principles (e.g., inadequate account reconciliations), and (c) deliberate manipulation of <sup>fi</sup>nancial information (Doyle et al. 2007b). Hence, in the presence of material weaknesses in internal controls, <sup>fi</sup>rms’ internal processes of <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting are considered unreliable (Doyle et al. 2007a, Hammersley et al. 2008).

Functional modules are a viable solution to weak internal controls because they can be used to authenticate system access and enforce procedural controls related to the processing of <sup>fi</sup>nancial transactions, segregation of duties, authorization of payment releases, and prevention and detection of fraud (Jia et al. 2020). First, through resolving automation problems, functional modules help <sup>fi</sup>rms eliminate manual work and mitigate data incompatibilities that are common to legacy systems. Second, by improving the level of business-process standardization, functional modules ensure that accounting and <sup>fi</sup>nancial data are collected using a standard chart of accounts (Brazel and Agoglia 2007). Third, functional modules help with integrating and aggregating data to generate information for decision makers (Jia et al. 2020). Thus, func tional modules reduce human error, verify access rights, and improve the quality of the information that describes <sup>fi</sup>rms’ <sup>fi</sup>nancial fundamentals.

Because of the above-mentioned capabilities provided by functional modules, a Committee of Sponsoring Organizations of the Treadway Commission framework suggests (e.g., see guidance no. 295 on technology and control activities in PricewaterhouseCoopers (2011)) and academic research has shown (e.g., see Masli et al. (2010), Gorka (2012), and Dorantes et al. (2013)) that implementing functional modules can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly improve the quality of internal controls. Accordingly, we do not hypothesize a direct relationship between weak internal controls and ES portfolio con<sup>fi</sup>guration<sup>5</sup> but focus on how weak internal controls moderate the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of information risk on ES portfolio balance. As such, we conceptualize weak internal controls as a quasi-moderator—one that has a direct effect on the outcome and also moderates the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of an independent variable on the outcome (Sharma et al. 1981). In other words, the presence of weak internal controls has a direct effect on ES portfolio balance, as previously identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in past research, while also affecting the relationship between information risk and ES portfolio balance, as we will now discuss.

We propose that, depending on whether <sup>fi</sup>rms have weak internal controls, they will adopt different strategies to balance their ES portfolios—that is, more toward operational or functional modules.

In the absence of weak internal controls, <sup>fi</sup>rms may not suffer from quality concerns regarding their <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting processes. Managers may work faithfully in processing and reporting <sup>fi</sup>nancial information. However, as discussed earlier, uncertainty in business operations will weaken their ability to accurately estimate earnings. Such <sup>fi</sup>rms need to focus on con<sup>fi</sup>guring their ES portfolio more toward operational modules. That is, the relationship proposed in Hypothesis 1—in which <sup>fi</sup>rms respond to higher information risk by implementing more operational modules—will hold in the absence of weak internal controls.

In the presence of weak internal controls, information risk will lead <sup>fi</sup>rms instead to increasingly emphasize functional modules. First, material weaknesses in <sup>fi</sup>rms’ internal controls re<sup>fl</sup>ect their failure to establish reliable <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting rules and procedures (Doyle et al. 2007a, Lambert et al. 2007). The quality of the total reported earnings can be so ambiguous that it can be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for these <sup>fi</sup>rms to differentiate between accruals (estimated earnings) and actuals (completed earnings) (Doyle et al. 2007a). This ambiguity between accruals and actuals raises signi<sup>fi</sup>cant concerns regarding the trustworthiness of the <sup>fi</sup>nancial statement preparation process and ampli<sup>fi</sup>es quality-related concerns pertaining to the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s <sup>fi</sup>nancial information. These <sup>fi</sup>rms will have to prioritize strengthening their <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting processes. The reason is that, even if business operations are digitized and accurate operational data are available, <sup>fi</sup>rms still cannot convert the operational data into high-quality <sup>fi</sup>nancial information if they lack robust and trustworthy processes for preparing <sup>fi</sup>nancial statements.

Second, when <sup>fi</sup>rms already facing an increase in information risk are also hampered by weak internal controls, they are further pressured to assure investors that they are taking effective steps to strengthen rules and procedures for <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting, thus reducing future possibilities that discretionary errors, or deliberate manipulations of earnings, would occur in <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting. The need to strengthen rules and procedures for <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting will orient <sup>fi</sup>rms more toward functional modules that can help them comply with industry best practices for access control, segregation of duties, and standardization of <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting processes. Functional modules help automate and standardize the processes to integrate, validate, and aggregate data for decision makers (Jia et al. 2020). Functional modules thus can reduce unintentional errors because managers will not have to use their discretion and can instead rely on aggregated <sup>fi</sup>nancial data generated using accounting and <sup>fi</sup>nance modules. Firms can also rely on functional modules to reduce managers’ intentional manipulations of <sup>fi</sup>nancial data. For example, human resource modules help <sup>fi</sup>rms determine the authority level of employees and their access rights when they are updating or <sup>fi</sup>nalizing transactions and payments in accounting and <sup>fi</sup>nance modules. Through automation of data processing and transmission, accounting and <sup>fi</sup>nance modules then assist in detecting data manipulations.

Third, in addition to enabling the automation of data processing and transmission and the standardization of rules and procedures for <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting, functional modules help <sup>fi</sup>rms aggregate data in their value chain from the bottom up, thereby generating performance reports and facilitating decision making for top management (e.g., forecasting for future performance). Top management of a <sup>fi</sup>rm are held personally responsible by the regulatory authorities under the Sarbanes–Oxley (SOX) Act of 2002<sup>6</sup> and can face punitive action for failing to maintain strong internal controls and establish the reliability of their <sup>fi</sup>nancial information. This may further pressure top management to prioritize the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s IT spending on building the credibility of its basic internal <sup>fi</sup>nancial information.

To sum up, without concerns on material weakness in internal controls, <sup>fi</sup>rms addressing information risk can focus primarily on implementing operational modules to digitize operations, whose inputs will then be used by <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting processes that are consid ered reliable. By contrast, when internal controls are materially weak, addressing information risk will require <sup>fi</sup>rms to solve problems embedded in <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting processes caused by weak internal controls, thus motivating <sup>fi</sup>rms to con<sup>fi</sup>gure their ES portfolio more toward functional modules. We hypothesize the moderating role of internal controls as follows.

Hypothesis 2. Firms without weak internal controls facing higher information risk will configure their ES portfolio more toward operational modules, whereas firms with weak internal controls facing higher information risk will config ure their ES portfolio more toward functional modules.

## 4. Data Collection, Analysis, and Results 4.1. Panel Data Set Construction

We constructed a panel data set using data from four sources: (a) the Computer Intelligence (CI) Technology Database, which provides data on ES implementations and other IT variables; (b) Audit Analytics (AA), which provides data for internal controls; (c) Compustat (CS), which provides data about <sup>fi</sup>rms’ <sup>fi</sup>nancial variables; and (d) Institutional Brokers’ Estimate Systems (I/B/E/ S), which provides data about analysts’ coverage of earnings forecasts. We <sup>fi</sup>rst collected ES module implementation and IT capital data for 1,275 <sup>fi</sup>rms, with 3,955 <sup>fi</sup>rm-year observations between the years 2005–2008 from CI. We then matched these data with Sarbanes–Oxley Act Section 404 (SOX 404) reports on internal controls from AA and with <sup>fi</sup>nancial metrics and other <sup>fi</sup>rm characteristics data from CS. This exercise produced 2,572 usable <sup>fi</sup>rm-year observations for 876 <sup>fi</sup>rms reporting between 2005 and 2008, resulting in a four-year panel data set. Depending on the vendor used, a <sup>fi</sup>rm conceivably may not have had the choice to implement a module because the ES vendor did not offer the module.<sup>7</sup> Likewise, some <sup>fi</sup>rms may not have the need for a particular ES module and therefore did not implement the module at all. To address these issues, we restricted our sample only to those <sup>fi</sup>rms where both modules were implemented in at least one site of the <sup>fi</sup>rm. This resulted in our <sup>fi</sup>nal sample of 1,993 <sup>fi</sup>rm-year observations for 697 <sup>fi</sup>rms.

To calculate total accruals quality, a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s total current accruals were regressed on cash <sup>fl</sup>ow; changes in revenues; and changes in property, plant, and equipment. The more the unknown factors <sup>fl</sup>uctuate, the more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult it is for investors to rely on a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s estimated accruals. The standard deviation of the residuals over the last <sup>fi</sup>ve years was used as a measure for total accruals quality. The higher the standard deviation, the lower the total accruals quality and the higher the information risk.

Following the accounting literature, we measured weak internal controls based on material weaknesses in internal controls over <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting disclosed by <sup>fi</sup>rms under SOX 404 regulations (Masli et al. 2010, Li et al. 2012). We classi<sup>fi</sup>ed a <sup>fi</sup>rm as either having weak internal controls (WeakControls 1) if it had a material weakness or not having weak internal controls (WeakControls <sub>-</sub> 0).

We controlled for changes in return on assets, sales growth, and <sup>fi</sup>rm risk, and <sup>fi</sup>nancial restatements. Therefore, we controlled for muni<sup>fi</sup>cence resulting from sales and income growth, dynamism resulting from sales and income volatility, and complexity resulting from market competition. Furthermore, it is possible that <sup>fi</sup>rms follow a pattern where they con<sup>fi</sup>gure their ES portfolio in a similar manner each year. To counter this, we included year dummies as controls. Finally, ES portfolio con<sup>fi</sup>guration is very likely to vary across industries. Some industries may not have a use for one or more ES modules. For example, banking services <sup>fi</sup>rms may not focus as much on supply chain ES as manufacturing <sup>fi</sup>rms, and such differences could occur at the most granular levels. We used four-digit Standard Industrial Classi<sup>fi</sup>cation (SIC) codes to control for industry-<sup>fi</sup>xed effects. Next, because our model may suffer from endoge neity issues that can bias the estimates (Greene 2003, Wooldridge 2009), we examined and corrected for endogeneity from one or more of the following causes: First, <sup>fi</sup>rms that implement ES are likely not random because certain <sup>fi</sup>rm characteristics may in<sup>fl</sup>uence ES implementations and lead to self-selection bias. Second, it is possible that other unobserved factors in<sup>fl</sup>uenced ES portfolio balance and information risk and that our model suffers from omitted variable bias issues. Third, ES portfolio balance in<sup>fl</sup>uences information risk, and our model suffers from reverse causality. To address these issues, we performed the following procedures: (1) two-stage least squares (2SLS), both random and <sup>fi</sup>xed effects, and (2) two-stage Heckman. The two procedures are discussed next.

4.3.1. 2SLS Procedure. Prior studies (e.g., Bharadwaj et al. 2007) have suggested that when panel data are available, instrument variable estimation or 2SLS procedures are effective in addressing endogeneityrelated concerns. Several prior studies have taken a similar approach where either ordinary least squares (OLS) or FGLS was employed for random effects and/or <sup>fi</sup>xed effects estimations using instruments. For example, Han and Mithas (2013) used the FGLS procedure and then reestimated their model using the <sup>fi</sup>xed effects 2SLS procedure. Similarly, Rai et al. (2015) used pooled OLS as well as both random effects and <sup>fi</sup>xed effects 2SLS procedures for robustness checks. Following these studies, we performed both random effects and <sup>fi</sup>xed effects 2SLS estimations.

Information risk is a key measure of the quality of the <sup>fi</sup>nancial information within a <sup>fi</sup>rm (Ewert and Wagenhofer 2011). Although we included several key factors related to a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s <sup>fi</sup>nancial fundamentals as controls in our model, there may be other factors correlated with information risk that cause our model to suffer from endogeneity. The literature on <sup>fi</sup>nancial reporting has considered two key factors—internal controls and <sup>fi</sup>rm risk—that also correspond closely to <sup>fi</sup>nancial information quality. Additionally, prior studies, such as Dorantes et al. (2013) and Tian and Xu (2015), have shown the impact of ES implementations on internal controls and <sup>fi</sup>rm risk. A key criterion of an instrument variable is that although it should be correlated with the endogenous variable, it should not have a direct in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the dependent variable (Wooldridge 2009). If it is likely that a variable could in<sup>fl</sup>uence the dependent variable, it should instead be included in the model. We had already included internal controls and <sup>fi</sup>rm risk in our model and ruled them out as potential instruments for information risk. Drawing on the prior literature, we used two instruments for information risk: current accruals quality (CAQ) and analyst coverage. We discuss the rationale for both instruments next.

4.3.1.1. Current Accruals Quality as an Instrument Variable. Ecker et al. (2005) showed that current accruals quality, de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the volatility of current earnings, is a reliable instrument for information risk. Unlike information risk, which is a measure of total accruals quality (TAQ) and is computed based on a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s cash <sup>fl</sup>ows from operations; revenue; and property, plant, and equipment over a period of <sup>fi</sup>ve years, the CAQ is measured based only on the cash <sup>fl</sup>ow from operations. The CAQ is computed by obtaining the standard deviation of a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s residuals from a re gression of working capital accruals on the prior year, current year, and following year’s projected cash <sup>fl</sup>ow from operations (Ecker et al. 2005). Although the measures of information risk and CAQ are conceptually similar (both re<sup>fl</sup>ect the standard deviation of the residuals from the mapping of accruals into lead and lag cash <sup>fl</sup>ows), information risk is a more comprehensive and powerful measure for detecting a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s earnings management and <sup>fi</sup>nancial environment. The CAQ serves as an effective instrument for information risk because (1) the concepts and measures of TAQ and CAQ are similar, but CAQ is smaller in magni tude than information risk; (2) unlike TAQ, CAQ is not a public indicator of <sup>fi</sup>rms’ <sup>fi</sup>nancial information and does not rely on <sup>fi</sup>rms’ cash <sup>fl</sup>ows from prior years (Ecker et al. 2005); and (3) CAQ is relatively less of a concern and is unlikely to in<sup>fl</sup>uence <sup>fi</sup>rms’ investment decisions, such as ES portfolio con<sup>fi</sup>guration.

4.3.1.2. Analyst Coverage as an Instrument Variable. Analyst coverage refers to the number of analysts issuing earnings forecasts for a <sup>fi</sup>rm. Analysts credibility and reputation, and consequently their income, depend on the accuracy of their earnings forecasts. Prior studies have found a negative relationship between the risks associated with <sup>fi</sup>rms and analyst coverage (Barth et al. 2001, Kim et al. 2019), citing that analysts tend to be wary of <sup>fi</sup>rms with greater earnings unpredictability. Therefore, the correlation between information risk and analyst coverage is plausible. It is important to establish that analyst coverage is not correlated with ES portfolio decisions, except through information risk. Analysts, as third-party experts offering recommendations to investors in the <sup>fi</sup>- nancial market, are not allowed by law to participate in <sup>fi</sup>rm investment decisions (e.g., ES, which is considered to be a con<sup>fl</sup>ict of interest) (Bushman et al. 2005). It is also arguable that <sup>fi</sup>rm decisions for ES portfolio con<sup>fi</sup>g uration may be correlated with analyst coverage only through consideration of improving <sup>fi</sup>rm information. Thus, analyst coverage is an effective candidate instrument. We collected data for the number of analysts who cover the <sup>fi</sup>rm from the I/B/E/S database. The measure of analyst coverage, drawn from prior literature, is the function of the number of analysts who issue earnings forecasts for a <sup>fi</sup>rm. We measured analyst coverage as the natural log of the count of analysts covering a <sup>fi</sup>rm.

## 5. Discussion

Our study opens the black box on how <sup>fi</sup>rms con<sup>fi</sup>g ure ES portfolios and uncovers how information processing requirements rendered by <sup>fi</sup>rms’ <sup>fi</sup>nancial context—speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, information risk and weak in ternal controls—affect the ES portfolio con<sup>fi</sup>guration choices. Our study has important implications for both research and practice.

First, we advance a novel conceptualization of ES portfolio con<sup>fi</sup>guration to understand ES implementation decisions. This conceptualization has three build ing blocks: (1) it moves the lens of ES implementation from the level of the overall system to the level of modules, (2) it differentiates among ES modules based on value-chain activities, and (3) it captures the “balance/imbalance” in the implementation of modules in the ES portfolio. We differentiate between functional and operational modules of ES because they enable <sup>fi</sup>rms to achieve different objectives with respect to the value-chain activities that they support and are thereby solutions to different information processing needs, and we use this distinction in conceptualizing ES portfolio con<sup>fi</sup>guration. Building on past work that has differentiated between operational and functional modules, our conceptualization of ES portfolio con<sup>fi</sup>guration captures the balance/imbalance in the implementation of the two modules in the ES portfolio. This conceptualization, as well as the corresponding operationalization, enables us to discern how <sup>fi</sup>rms change the balance between the two ES modules based on the information processing requirements rendered by the <sup>fi</sup>nancial environment. Although technologies such as cloud architecture and in-memory databases are leading to innovations in ES, the building blocks for our conceptualization of ES portfolio con<sup>fi</sup>guration are enduring. Future research can appropriate these building blocks to develop contextualized constructs for portfolio con<sup>fi</sup>guration of newer generations of ES and to examine how the con-<sup>fi</sup>guration is affected by information processing requirements and other factors.

Second, our study advances a needs-based perspective to theorize how information processing needs emanating from the <sup>fi</sup>nancial environment context affect ES portfolio con<sup>fi</sup>guration. The focus of prior studies on ES implementations has generally been on studying the impact of ES on process-level or <sup>fi</sup>rm-level outcomes (e.g., Karimi et al. 2007 and Dorantes et al. 2013). Our study shifts the focus to a novel, needs-based perspective, driving the con<sup>fi</sup>guration of the ES portfolio. Future researchers can use our perspective to develop contextualized constructs for information processing needs in their speci<sup>fi</sup>c research contexts as drivers of ES con<sup>fi</sup>guration and, more broadly, IT portfolio con<sup>fi</sup>guration.

Third, our study shows how information processing needs emanating from information risk in<sup>fl</sup>uence ES portfolio con<sup>fi</sup>guration and how this in<sup>fl</sup>uence could reverse when <sup>fi</sup>rms are faced with material weaknesses in their internal controls. Examination of information risk with ES portfolio con<sup>fi</sup>guration is novel because prior studies have been limited to examining the impact of information risk on a variety of <sup>fi</sup>nancial outcomes, such as the cost of capital and stock prices (e.g., Gray et al. 2009). We theorize and show that over and beyond <sup>fi</sup>nancial fundamentals, information risk in<sup>fl</sup>uences how <sup>fi</sup>rms change the con<sup>fi</sup>guration of their IT resources—speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, their ES portfolio balance. We found that, indeed, information risk creates the need for better information processing capabilities that motivate <sup>fi</sup>rms to change their ES portfolio balance more toward operational modules. However, when faced with information processing requirements to address information risk while having material weakness in internal controls, <sup>fi</sup>rms change the balance of their ES portfolios more toward functional modules. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings show how the information processing needs of the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s <sup>fi</sup>nancial environment in<sup>fl</sup>uence ES portfolio con<sup>fi</sup>guration decisions. Future research can build on these insights to evaluate how the information processing requirements of the <sup>fi</sup>nancial environment affect the con<sup>fi</sup>guration of other IT (e.g., business intelligence) and non-IT (e.g., audit) portfolios of resources.

## 5.1. Practical Implications

Our study has important implications for organizations. First, IT managers should adopt a portfolio-con<sup>fi</sup>guration perspective to manage ES implementations. This requires moving the focus from overall ES implementation levels to a modular perspective of ES that corresponds to different value-chain activities and their associated information processing requirements. Although technological advances such as cloud computing continue to drive innovations in the implementation and use of ES, the categorization of ES modules is based on a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s valuechain activities and appears to be enduring in practice. A modular stance can enable both IT and business managers to better understand the mapping between information processing needs and necessary actions to increase or decrease emphasis on a particular module. Given the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant difference in the types of value-chain activities supported by operational and functional modules, an ES portfolio con<sup>fi</sup>guration dashboard that makes transparent the implementation levels of each, as well as their balance, can yield shared understanding of the status of ES portfolio con<sup>fi</sup>guration.

Second, IT managers should evaluate the link between ES portfolio con<sup>fi</sup>guration decisions and the information processing requirements in the context of <sup>fi</sup>nancial processes. Other dimensions of <sup>fi</sup>rms’ information processing needs, such as market volatility, are already on IT managers’ radar, and improving operational performance in response to market changes is widely accepted as a motive for ES implementations. Our work highlights that these current practices to decide on ES implementations are inadequate, as they miss the information processing needs embedded in <sup>fi</sup>- nancial reporting. We recommend that senior IT managers who are responsible for providing the strategic direction for ES implementations incorporate information risk into their assessment on how to con<sup>fi</sup>gure the ES portfolio and allocate IT budgets accordingly.

Third, we recommend a routine of “sharing and solving” be established as part of the corporate governance process to coordinate the resolution of issues related to information risk and internal controls. As signi<sup>fi</sup>cant changes in information risk and weak internal controls are reported to the audit and risk management committees and the corporate board, we suggest that this information is also shared in a proactive manner with senior IT managers and business managers who are responsible for ES implementations. In addition, we suggest that the senior IT managers and business managers recommend solutions to the audit and risk management committees and the corporate board on the sequence and schedule of implementing operational and functional ES modules to address the issues. Such a sharing-and-solving approach that is integrated with the corporate governance process will establish the mechanisms needed to evaluate and authorize the recon<sup>fi</sup>guration of the ES portfolio that might otherwise not be prioritized or even be hamstrung in political negotiations within an organizational bureaucracy.

Overall, we recommend an integrated approach for identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of issues pertaining to information risk and internal controls, as well as the resolution of the issues through ES portfolio con<sup>fi</sup>guration. Our recommendations correspond to the broader need for <sup>fi</sup>rms to establish corporate governance mechanisms to coordinate expertise and governance related to <sup>fi</sup>nancial information with those related to IT capabilities.

## 6. Conclusion

We advance a novel conceptualization of ES portfolio con<sup>fi</sup>guration that differentiates between the two major modules supporting different value-chain activities and re<sup>fl</sup>ects the balance of these modules in the ES portfolio. Our study shows that <sup>fi</sup>rms respond to the information processing needs emanating from the <sup>fi</sup>- nancial information standpoint—speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, those presented by information risk and weak controls—by adjusting their ES portfolio balance toward operational or functional modules. Our study builds a foundation for future research to further investigate how <sup>fi</sup>rms dynamically con<sup>fi</sup>gure ES portfolios and, more broadly, IT resources to meet information processing needs under different <sup>fi</sup>rm-related and market-related contingencies.
