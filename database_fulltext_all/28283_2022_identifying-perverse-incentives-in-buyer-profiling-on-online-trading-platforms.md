# Identifying Perverse Incentives in Buyer Profiling on Online Trading Platforms

Abstract. Consumer pro<sup>fi</sup>ling has become one of the most common practices on online trading platforms. Many platforms strive to obtain and implement technological innovations that allow them to understand and identify consumers’ needs, and, thereafter, monetize this capability by charging sellers to present and/or sell their products or services based on consumers’ interests. However, an interesting and relevant question arises in this context: Does the platform have an incentive to pro<sup>fi</sup>le its buyers as accurately as possible? This paper develops and analyzes a parsimonious game-theoretic model to answer this re search question. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that, surprisingly, platforms that charge sellers for discoveries have a perverse incentive to deviate from accurate buyer pro<sup>fi</sup>ling. However, such a perverse incentive does not exist for platforms that charge sellers for transactions. As a result, with such a perverse incentive, social welfare under discovery-based pricing is lower than that under transaction-based pricing.

## 1. Introduction

Online trading platforms have increasingly become a primary shopping channel for many (Choi and Mela 2019). The online shopping market has observed, in recent years, a rapid growth worldwide, with an estimated value of 4.28 trillion U.S. dollars in 2020 and a projected increase to 5.4 trillion U.S. dollars by 2022 (Statista 2021). To compete in this space, many platforms have invested in technological innovations that allow them to identify and understand consumers needs. Through these technologies, online platforms can present products and/or services that increasingly match consumer interests (Neumann et al. 2019). For example, technologies such as semantic search (Bast et al. 2016) and contextual search (Lytvyn et al. 2017) are being implemented so that platforms can better infer consumer preferences. Many platforms have also monetized such a “buyer pro<sup>fi</sup>ling” practice by charging sellers to present and/or sell their products and/or services based on the consumers’ inferred preferences. Not surprisingly, recent advances in computer science, machine learning, and arti<sup>fi</sup>cial intelligence have focused on developing algorithms that can pro<sup>fi</sup>le consumers with a reasonable level of accuracy (Asha and Rajkumar 2019). Even then, buyer pro<sup>fi</sup>ling is not “perfect”; that is, it remains possible for some consumers to be improperly pro<sup>fi</sup>led such that their needs are not accurately portrayed by the algorithm. Nevertheless, most, if not all, of the literature on online marketplaces (e.g., Edelman et al. 2007, Balachander et al. 2009) assumes that such a pro<sup>fi</sup>ling is perfect. More importantly, there is virtually no prior work that examines whether the platform will be better off when such a buyer pro<sup>fi</sup>ling becomes more accurate. The primary objective of this paper is to study the economic implications of such a consumer pro<sup>fi</sup>ling practice. Particularly, we propose the following research question: Does an online trading platform have an incentive to profile its buyers as accurately as possible?

We answer this research question by developing and analyzing a simplistic and parsimonious gametheoretic model. The model setup is quite standard, with an addition of the buyer pro<sup>fi</sup>ling component that we aim to study. In line with the literature (Chen et al. 2016), we consider two types of online trading platforms that currently dominate the market: the “payments-for-discoveries” platforms (i.e., those that charge sellers when they are discovered by buyers on the platform) and the “payments-for-transactions” platforms (i.e., those that charge sellers a fee for completing the transactions on the platform). As noted in Chen et al. (2016), the payments-for-discoveries policy is similar to a search auction. Therefore, we model the payments-for-discoveries policy as implementing a second-price auction. In such a case, the sellers submit bids to be discovered by the buyers and, conditional on discovery, pay the intermediary in a manner that resembles the pay-per-click mechanism. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that, surprisingly, the platform does not necessarily have an incentive to accurately pro<sup>fi</sup>le the buyers. In fact, there may be some incentives to pro<sup>fi</sup>le buyers inaccurately. However, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that such a perverse incentive only exists among platforms that charge for discoveries and not for platforms that charge for transactions. As a result, because of such a potential strategic mispro<sup>fi</sup>ling by the platform, the payments-for-discoveries policy can lead to lower social welfare outcomes when compared with the payments-for-transactions policy. This revelation provides another fresh perspective to the literature on online trading platforms.

In the next section, we review the prior literature that is related to this study and establish the unique contribution of our work among these prior studies. Following the literature review, we describe the model setup in Section 3. Then, we present the equilibrium analyses of the two types of trading platforms, while also accounting for the intermediary’s choice with regards to buyer pro<sup>fi</sup>ling, in Section 4. Following that, we compare the two policies in Section 5 and generalize some of our results while relaxing a few assumptions in Section 6. Lastly, we conclude the paper in Section 7.

## 2. Survey of the Existing Literature

Our study relates primarily to two streams of literature: online platforms and auction mechanisms in the context of online platforms. In that regard, we <sup>fi</sup>rst survey prior works on online platforms. More speci<sup>fi</sup>- cally, we focus on the revenue models of the platforms in these markets. Second, we review prior literature that studies auction mechanisms that are prevalent on online platforms.

## 2.1. Online Platforms

For decades, electronic commerce has been one of the primary topics for information systems research. Our study relates to the online perspective of e-commerce. Thus, we limit our review of the literature to the online aspects of e-commerce only.<sup>1</sup>

Numerous issues regarding online platforms exist, such as security (e.g., Kalvenes and Basu 2006), technology (e.g., Dukes and Liu 2015), and services (e.g., De et al. 2013). One of the primary issues is revenue generation, in which the platform operator usually facilitates the transactions and, in return, charges the agents for the services provided on the platform. A platform can choose from several revenue models, and our study also relates to the choice of revenue model for online platforms. The prominent revenue models extensively studied in the previous literature are the membership-based model, the advertisingbased model, and the commission-based (or transac tion-based) model. For example, Schiff (2003) reports that the membership-based and commission-based models are equivalent from a pro<sup>fi</sup>t perspective in a Cournot duopoly. The two models are also equivalent if consumers are homogeneous with respect to the number of transactions and if there is no uncertainty related to this number. However, the choice between the two depends on the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty of monitoring usage and the nature of the externality between the buyers and the sellers (Evans and Schmalensee 2007). Meanwhile, the advertising-based model has become one of the most pro<sup>fi</sup>table revenue models among online businesses (Rappaport 2007). Our study further advances understandings of the trade-offs between transaction- and advertising-based revenue models.

In addition, a substream of the literature that stud ies strategies of online trading platforms is also closely related to our study. In particular, the strategies concerning the choice of an online publisher between tar geted and traditional advertising covered in Chen and Stallaert (2014) may resemble the choice of buyer pro-<sup>fi</sup>ling capabilities under the payments for discoveries (i.e., advertising model) by the e-commerce platforms studied herein. For example, the choice to not always pro<sup>fi</sup>le buyers accurately in an advertising model—as revealed in one of our results—may seem equivalent to the choice of traditional advertising (i.e., deviation from targeted advertising) of Chen and Stallaert (2014). However, their result only holds true for a small number of advertisers (equivalent to sellers in our model). Meanwhile, in our case, the results apply to any number of sellers. In addition, the similarities of the results for a small number of sellers is merely a coincidence, since the intuitions behind their result and ours are completely different—in their case, the preference for traditional advertising for a small number of buyers arises from the negative effect of relaxed competition in behavioral targeting; in our case, the choice arises as a consequence of the platform’s ability to increase the value of the second-highest bidder and extract some of this value through an appropriate pricing mechanism. Relatedly, a work by Chen et al. (2016) compares the advertising (i.e., payments-fordiscoveries) and brokerage (i.e., payments-for-transactions) models for online trading platforms. However, they focus on how manipulating the relative exposure of advertised and unadvertised sellers through changing the proportion of space dedicated to advertising impacts platform revenue and social welfare. On the other hand, our work examines platforms’ incentive to pro<sup>fi</sup>le buyers accurately and compares such an incentive in two different types of online trading platforms. Evidently, our work demonstrates that, because inaccurate buyer pro<sup>fi</sup>ling could be pertinent in the payments-for-discoveries platform, the social welfare under such a platform could be lower than that under a payments-for-transactions platform—a perspective that is not covered in Chen et al. (2016) or any other related works. In addition, our <sup>fi</sup>nding regarding the perverse incentive of the platform to not always pro<sup>fi</sup>le buyers accurately may also appear to coincide with the <sup>fi</sup>ndings in Choudhary and Zhang (2014), which studies platform strategies in the context of recommender systems. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the authors demonstrate that <sup>fi</sup>rms may have an incentive to deliberately mislead consumers by not displaying ideal products. However, their result is driven by the tradeoff between consumers’ search cost and mis<sup>fi</sup>t cost. The underlying mechanism in our study is vastly different from theirs. Our result is a consequence of the platform’s revenue mechanism, resulting in economic implications that arise from buyer mispro<sup>fi</sup>ling.

## 2.2. Auctions on Online Platforms

Many online platforms that adopt an advertising-based revenue model sell ad slots using different auction mechanisms. For example, search ads use a generalized second-price (GSP) auction, display ad exchanges usually adopt a Vickrey (second-price) auction, and Facebook uses the Vickrey-Clarke-Groves (VCG) mechanism for its social network ads (Varian 2009, Varian and Harris 2014). One of the revenue models that we study for e-commerce platforms, which we label as a “payments-for-discoveries” model, resembles the second-price auction. One of the seminal articles on this topic is that of Edelman et al. (2007). They <sup>fi</sup>rst characterize the equilibrium in the context of search ads, as with Google and Yahoo, and show various properties of the GSP auction. Varian (2007) also characterizes the equilibrium and provides empirical evidence that Google’s ad auction outcome reasonably resembles the properties of the equilibrium. In such auctions, advertisers act as bidders and compete against each other for the opportunity to reach out to buyers. One of their discoveries is that, unlike a standard second-price auction (Vickrey 1961) or the VCG mechanism, which is the more generalized form of the Vickrey auction (Vickrey 1961, Clarke 1971, Groves 1973), the GSP auction does not lead to a truth-telling equilibrium in general. However, GSP with a single-item case is similar to that of a second-price auction and truth-telling is a dominant strategy equilibrium. Variants of the GSP auction exist in various contexts. Prior information systems research has revealed the implications of these variations. Asdemir et al. (2012) compare the cost per click (CPC) and cost per thousand impressions (CPM) models for pricing online ads. Similarly, Hu et al. (2016) consider the relative attractiveness of basing an advertiser’s payment for ads on clicks versus impressions. Balachander et al. (2009)

compare the ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy of the highest-bid and highestpro<sup>fi</sup>t auction policies and consider the impact of product-market positioning on the pro<sup>fi</sup>t of both advertisers (i.e., bidders) and publishers (i.e., auctioneers). Feng et al. (2005) use simulations to compare search engine revenues from the highest bid and the highest prof it policies (along with others) to choose the winners and their payments. They conclude that the highest pro<sup>fi</sup>t policy weakly dominates the highest bid policy in terms of revenue to the search engine. In contrast, Balachander et al. (2009) use an analytical model to demonstrate that search engine revenues under the highest bid policy can equal or exceed those under the highest pro<sup>fi</sup>t policy. When developing our theory, we intend to use some of the modeling details from the previously men tioned articles.

Note that it is not our intention to provide an exhaustive coverage of studies related to GSP auctions in this subsection. Several other papers have considered problems that are variants of GSP but do not directly relate to our model. For example, from a mechanism design standpoint, Weber and Zheng (2007) consider the use of additional information about an advertiser’s product performance in ranking bidders. Similarly, Xu et al. (2011) study how to assess endogenously the value of a “superior” advertising position in the price competition. From an analytical perspective, Yao and Mela (2011) develop a dynamic structural model of consumer clicking behavior and advertiser bidding behavior and perform estimations based on the empirical data from a search engine.

A substream of the GSP literature may also appear to be directly related to the execution of our platform analysis. On mobile app platforms, pricing often involves sharing ad revenue. Hao et al. (2017) study the interplay of ad-sponsored and agency-pricing models. Casadesus-Masanell and Zhu (2010) study the optimal strategy of a high-quality incumbent that faces a low-quality ad-sponsored competitor. They show that the typical optimal response of an incumbent to an ad-sponsored rival is to compete exclusively through the subscription-based or the ad-sponsored model, rather than adopting both. Lin et al. (2012) show that offering both ad-free and ad-supported services is the optimal strategy for a monopolist, because ad revenues compensate for the cannibalistic effect of vertical differentiation. However, in a duopoly equilibrium, only one <sup>fi</sup>rm offers both services when the ad revenue rate is suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently high. Even though this substream involves platforms and ad-based pricing, the main aspect that we consider in our model is not pertinent to this context. As mentioned earlier, we are focused on how “payments for discoveries” on a platform compare against “payments for transactions” with respect to the buyer pro<sup>fi</sup>ling practice and corresponding welfare.

## 3. Model Setup

This section presents a setup of our stylized model. The objective of this study is to highlight a core behavior that is counterintuitive and has not been demonstrated in prior works. For the sake of exposition and tractability, we make our main model extremely parsimonious and base it on models that have widely been used in the literature (e.g., Edelman et al. 2007, Balachander et al. 2009). The effect of the behavior may subside but will continue to exist when the assumptions are relaxed. Later in Section 6, we demonstrate how the key insights carry forward when several of the assumptions made here are relaxed.

In our main model, there exist one intermediary (interchangeably referred to as the platform) and two sellers {i, j}. We also have buyers, who are pro<sup>fi</sup>led into two types. To indicate that each buyer type is aligned with one of the sellers with regard to their preferences, we simply label them as buyer pro<sup>fi</sup>le types i and j.<sup>2</sup> We assume that the arrival of each buyer pro<sup>fi</sup>le type to the platform is equally likely.<sup>3</sup> The arrival of a buyer to a seller’s listing on the platform is expected to generate some pro<sup>fi</sup>ts. Suppose that vˆ <sub>mn</sub> corresponds to the valuation that seller m perceives from buyer pro<sup>fi</sup>le type n. We assume that seller valuations are private. Further, similar to Edelman et al. (2007), we assume that sellers are identical along the dimensions other than these valuations.<sup>4</sup>

We illustrate the model with an example similar to that in Balachander et al. (2009). Imagine a world where the buyers are one of two types: one that is interested in sports shoes and another that is interested in formal shoes. The two sellers in the market are selling only Nike or only Clarks shoes. Naturally, the Nike shoe seller would value the buyer interested in sports shoes more; likewise, the Clarks shoe seller would value the formal shoe buyer more. Corresponding to the model, we denote the buyers who are pro<sup>fi</sup>led as sports shoe enthusiasts as type i (correspondingly, potential buyers of formal shoes as j), and the Nike seller as seller i (correspondingly, Clarks seller as j). The intermediary pro<sup>fi</sup>les the buyer using various pieces of information (such as their web browsing habits, search keywords, etc.) to decide on whether the buyer is a sport or formal shoe enthusiast, and we expand on this point further.

In our model, the buyer arrives and is pro<sup>fi</sup>led by the platform (i.e., the platform infers the buyer’s preferences and classi<sup>fi</sup>es her into one of the pro<sup>fi</sup>le types). A buyer may be pro<sup>fi</sup>led based on her revealed preferences through search keywords. However, the intermediary may not require an explicit action (e.g., searching for products) by the buyers to pro<sup>fi</sup>le them. Many platforms in reality implicitly pro<sup>fi</sup>le buyers preferences (e.g., through tracking cookies, af<sup>fi</sup>liation networks, etc.) even before they start searching. In this section, for the ease of exposition, we treat the pro<sup>fi</sup>ling as a black box and assume that the intermediary adopts an algorithm that allows it to pro<sup>fi</sup>le the types of buyers as either buyer pro<sup>fi</sup>le type i or j. We formally present the actions performed by the players in our model setup as a game with four stages:

1. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage, the platform chooses its revenue mechanism.

2. In the second stage, the sellers make their participation decision. They know only about their valuation and are unaware of the competing seller or even the proportion of the different buyer pro<sup>fi</sup>le types.

3. The third stage, referred to as the discovery stage, begins with a buyer arriving at the platform. The platform pro<sup>fi</sup>les the buyer into one of the two types. Depending on the buyer pro<sup>fi</sup>le type, the platform decides which seller to display, allowing the seller and the buyer to discover each other.

4. In the fourth stage—the transaction stage—the buyer decides whether to complete the transaction with the seller who was displayed in the third stage.

Next, we expand on the platform’s two revenue mechanisms by discussing unique characteristics of both mechanisms with respect to our model setup.

## 3.1. Payments for Discoveries

Here, the intermediary adopts the payments-for-discoveries policy in stage 1. Generally, the product listings available to the buyers show both organic search results and inorganic listings. For the inorganic listings, Chen et al. (2016) have already shown that intermediaries, such as Taobao, list the sellers using a mechanism that is similar to Google search. We also focus only on inorganic listings in this study.<sup>5</sup> The intermediary solicits bids from the sellers a priori, uses the standard sealed-bid, second-price auction mechanism to sort the seller listing in stage 2, and then the seller listing is shown on the platform to be discovered by the buyers once they arrive in stage 3.

In stage 2, the intermediary convenes the auction for each buyer pro<sup>fi</sup>le type. Therefore, in our context, there is an auction for each buyer pro<sup>fi</sup>le type i and j. Naturally, each seller submits different bids to be shown to each of the buyer pro<sup>fi</sup>le types. There is only one slot where the platform displays the winning seller.<sup>6</sup>

Next, in stage 3, buyers arrive on the platform. The intermediary pro<sup>fi</sup>les buyers into one of the types. For each pro<sup>fi</sup>led buyer type, the intermediary considers the bids submitted by the sellers and chooses the highest bidder to be displayed on the speci<sup>fi</sup>ed slot. Upon discovery, for example, for each click by a buyer to the seller’s product listing, the seller pays a fee corresponding to the bid submitted by the second-highest bidder. Thereafter, in stage 4, a buyer may transact with a seller on the platform. Note that, under the payments-for-discoveries policy, neither the seller nor the buyer pays any additional fees to complete the transaction.

## 3.2. Payments for Transactions

Note that the payments-for-discoveries model will form the main focus in the next sections, as it is through this model that we will demonstrate the perverse incentives. This section on payments for transactions is presented only as a contrast, and so we make quite a few reasonable but simplifying assumptions for the purposes of exposition. If the intermediary adopts this payments-for-transactions policy in stage 1, then the intermediary only charges for the transactions that are completed on the platform in stage 4. Unlike in the case of payments for discoveries, the platform conducts no auction in stage 2 to decide on payments under this policy. In this revenue mechanism, the intermediary decides on what percentage of the value generated should be collected as transaction fees for each transaction completed on the platform.<sup>7</sup> We denote the platform’s choice regarding the commission rate as $f ,$ where $0 \leq f \leq 1$ . The decision on $f$ is made in stage 1. The sellers decide to participate in stage 2 unaware of the other seller. In stage 3, the intermediary pro<sup>fi</sup>les the buyer and displays a seller that it deems as the best <sup>fi</sup>t in the speci<sup>fi</sup>ed slot. In stage 4, if a buyer of type n transacts with seller m on the platform, then the intermediary gains a transaction fee of $\hat { v } _ { m n } f .$

Note that, typically, the marketplaces charge a transaction fee to the sellers for those transactions that are completed on the platform. However, the transaction fee introduces a trade-off with respect to the transaction. Although the platform implements rules to facilitate market transactions, some users take advantage of the platform’s search and communi cation infrastructure to exchange information privately, thereby evading marketplace fees by undertaking trans actions outside the platform (Kirchner and Schußler¨ 2019). This phenomenon is predominant across many online platforms and is popularly known as platform leakage (Hagiu and Wright 2021). As noted in Pham (2009), power sellers on eBay pay hundreds of thou sands of dollars per year as commission fees, and many <sup>fi</sup>nd ways to bypass the platform to save on these fees through multiple evasion techniques such as redirection and price shifting. Thus, when the intermediary charges at the transaction stage, not all transactions may take place on the platform. The decision to bypass the inter mediary has to be mutually agreed upon by both the buyer and the seller. How much of this saved transac tion fee is split between the seller and the buyer de pends on their relative bargaining power (Pham 2009), which is not a critical modeling detail that we intend to focus on given the scope of our interest. Considering its impact on both the seller’s and the buyer’s payoffs sepa rately will complicate and clutter the insights that we focus on. ## 4. Buyer (Mis)Profiling

As one might notice, Section 3 presents a simplistic model wherein much of the strategic interactions have been studied either by using a simplistic framework or from established results such as those in Edelman et al. (2007). In this section, we expand on certain modeling features to identify some counterintuitive results.

Notice that the platform pro<sup>fi</sup>les an incoming buyer. As described earlier, the platform does so by using various technologies (such as tracking cookies or pro-<sup>fi</sup>ling search keywords). Recent advances in semantic search (Bast et al. 2016) and contextual search (Lytvyn et al. 2017) have improved the accuracy of the pro<sup>fi</sup>ling procedure. Yet, the procedure remains far from perfect in terms of accuracy (Lungu 2017). Therefore, it is possible that not all buyers who are pro<sup>fi</sup>led as type i are truly type i. The results are usually a mix of true positives (real buyer type i) and false positives (the other buyer type, which, in our two-buyer-type model, corresponds to the real buyer type j). For our model, we introduce a new variable that measures the pro<sup>fi</sup>ling accuracy. In reality, the accuracy may be different for the pro<sup>fi</sup>le types. Suppose that $p _ { i }$ is the pro-<sup>fi</sup>ling accuracy of buyer type i. It simply means that a pro<sup>fi</sup>le of buyer type i consists of a buyer who is truly type i with $p _ { i }$ probability and a buyer who is truly type j with $( 1 - p _ { i } )$ probability. Technically, the accuracy of pro<sup>fi</sup>ling buyer type j may be a different $p _ { j }$ —but, in the two-buyer-type settings, it is related to $p _ { i } .$ . Our main results on perverse incentives are not dependent on the relationship between the pro<sup>fi</sup>le accuracy of the two types. With that intention and without loss of generality, we hereafter present the results from the perspective of buyer pro<sup>fi</sup>le type i, even though similar results can also be obtained for buyer pro<sup>fi</sup>le type $j .$ Because we deal with only i’s pro<sup>fi</sup>le accuracy hereafter, we simplify the notation and denote $p _ { i }$ as p.

Recall that we intentionally did not characterize the pro<sup>fi</sup>ling process in Section 3 but described it as a “black box.” As we introduce the pro<sup>fi</sup>ling accuracy, we will next unpack some of the variables that we had de<sup>fi</sup>ned earlier. Consider $\hat { v } _ { m n } ,$ which we had de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the valuation for seller m from engaging with a buyer pro<sup>fi</sup>led as type n. We want to distinguish it from $v _ { m n }$ (without the hat), which represents the value that seller m attains from a “true” type n buyer. With the pro<sup>fi</sup>ling accuracy of p, $\hat { v } _ { i i } = p v _ { i i } + ( 1 - p ) v _ { i j }$ . Thus, the valuation by seller i for a buyer pro<sup>fi</sup>led as type i is a weighted combination of the valuation by seller i for a true type i buyer $( \mathrm { i . e . , ~ } v _ { i i } )$ and the valuation by seller i for a true type j buyer $( \mathrm { i . e . , } v _ { i j } )$ . Further, in order to capture the preference of a seller for a buyer of its own type, we consider $\{ v _ { i i } , v _ { j j } \} > \{ v _ { i j } , v _ { j i } \}$ . This assumption simply states that the true valuation that seller i has for buyer type i dominates the same seller’s true valuation for buyer type j and also seller $j ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s }$ true valuation for buyer type i. Similarly, valuation that seller j has for buyer type j dominates the same seller’s true valuation for buyer type i and also seller i’s true valuation for buyer type j. Such a higher valuation can manifest through several avenues. It can be that buyers of the same type tend to have a higher conversion rate than buyers of the different type. It can also be that buyers of the same type have a higher willingness to pay than buyers of the different type, and so on.

Typically, the pro<sup>fi</sup>ling accuracy is known only to the intermediary and not to the sellers. As a result, one may wonder how a seller m in our model will realize the valuation $\hat { v } _ { m i }$ without any explicit knowledge of $p .$ However, when sellers can make repeated transactions, even a simple moving window average of the conditional payoffs should reveal this valuation information to the seller. Thus, this model that includes the pro<sup>fi</sup>ling accuracy continues to be consistent with the model speci<sup>fi</sup>ed earlier. For one, the knowledge of pro<sup>fi</sup>ling accuracy is private information to the intermediary. Even then, the seller realizes the valuation $\hat { v } _ { m i }$ that we continue to assume to be private.

Next, let us add some decision-making elements to the model presented in the previous section. In stage 1, we assume that the platform chooses $p ,$ in addition to choosing the revenue mechanism. Choosing p is equivalent to exerting effort to pro<sup>fi</sup>le the buyers. It is reasonable to assume that the random pro<sup>fi</sup>ling can be done effortlessly but that it is more costly to be more accurate. Setting a low $p$ also may impose a shadow cost, as the buyers and sellers may move to a compet ing platform.<sup>9</sup> Even so, for the sake of exposition, we intentionally assume that there is no cost to increase the buyer pro<sup>fi</sup>ling accuracy beyond what can be achieved through random pro<sup>fi</sup>ling. We make this admittedly strong assumption but note that this is an extremely conservative one considering the results that we demonstrate.

We next characterize the equilibrium that includes the choice of $p .$ Note that the equilibrium presented in the previous lemmas simply becomes a subgameperfect Nash equilibrium in this revised game. Thus, we do not have to reinvent the wheel and can simply focus on the choice of $p .$

## 4.1. Payments for Discoveries

We use backward induction to solve the game as described in Section 3. Note from Lemma 1 that the payoff for the platform, when it serves a buyer pro<sup>fi</sup>led as type i, is simply min $\{ \hat { v } _ { j i } , \hat { v } _ { i i } \}$ , and it is min $\{ \hat { v } _ { i j } , \hat { v } _ { j j } \}$ when the platform serves a buyer pro<sup>fi</sup>le type j. Additionally, corresponding to the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage, we solve for optimal p that maximizes the intermediary’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t. The proposition below characterizes the strategic decisions.

Proposition 3. When an intermediary adopts the payments-for-discoveries policy, the intermediary’s any choice of $p \in \left[ \hat { p } , 1 \right)$ dominates the choice of $p = 1$ , where $\hat { p } =$ $\frac { v _ { j j } - v _ { i j } } { v _ { j j } - v _ { i j } + v _ { i i } - v _ { j i } }$ and $0 < \hat { p } < 1$ . For $p \in \left[ \hat { p } , 1 \right)$ , corresponding to buyer type $i ,$ the intermediary’s profit under the paymentsfor-discoveries policy is $\pi _ { d } ( p ) = p v _ { j i } + ( 1 - p ) v _ { j j }$ . In a oneslot scenario with two sellers and two buyer types, the intermediary’s optimal choice $\boldsymbol { p } ^ { * } = \boldsymbol { \hat { p } } ,$ , and the corresponding equilibrium profit $\begin{array} { r } { \pi _ { d } ^ { * } = \frac { v _ { i i } v _ { j j } - v _ { i j } v _ { j i } } { v _ { i i } - v _ { j i } + v _ { j j } - v _ { i j } } . } \end{array}$

This result is counterintuitive, and it is contrary to how intermediaries are assumed to have incentives to pro<sup>fi</sup>le customers as accurately as possible. This result is driven by how the bidding outcomes are affected by the platform. In the one-slot setup, the platform’s payoff is directly based on how well it helps the second-best seller for a given buyer pro<sup>fi</sup>le. To increase the revenue generated from the slot, the intermediary has an incentive to increase the value for the second bidder through its pro<sup>fi</sup>ling strategy. For a buyer pro<sup>fi</sup>led as type $i ,$ when $p = 1$ , the intermediary makes only $v _ { j i \cdot }$ . When $p < 1$ , what the intermediary presents as buyer pro<sup>fi</sup>le type i is actually $p$ fraction of a real buyer type i and $( 1 - p )$ fraction of a real buyer type $j .$ Then, the expected valuation for seller $j$ for a buyer pro<sup>fi</sup>led as type i is $p v _ { j i } + ( 1 - p ) v _ { j j }$ . Therefore, when seller i wins, the second bidder, that is, seller $j ^ { \prime } \mathbf { s }$ expected valuation from what is perceived as buyer type $i ,$ increases, which aids the intermediary.

We wish to clarify a key aspect regarding p, which may seem confusing at the <sup>fi</sup>rst glance. The choice of $p$ only affects how the pro<sup>fi</sup>ling of the buyer types is done. The platform does not introduce anything directly related to p when it executes the auction (there is only an indirect effect, as bids submitted by the bidders are affected by p). In other words, the platform only shows the winning seller, but the losing seller is never displayed. For example, the platform does not randomly show the losing seller with $p$ probability. Therefore, in this regard, the sellers do not have an incentive to free-ride; this issue connects with another related observation that we will further explain.

One might wonder what the optimal bids are when the intermediary makes no effort to accurately pro<sup>fi</sup>le a buyer, and, therefore, it is equally likely for a buyer to be pro<sup>fi</sup>led as type i or type j. When the platform does not have any incentive at all to pro<sup>fi</sup>le buyers accurately, will the sellers drop their bids down to zero, thereby penalizing the platform? Suppose that the equilibrium involves both sellers submitting bids of zero for buyers pro<sup>fi</sup>led as type i. Such an equilibrium cannot be sustained, since seller i has an incentive to increase the bid by $\epsilon ,$ thereby winning and generating a higher payoff. Seller j can respond to the bids by in creasing its bids even further, and this tatonnementˆ continues.

One might also wonder if the intermediary needs to know the private information about seller valuations for this perverse incentive to occur. Based on the following reasoning, the answer is no. Assume that the world in which the intermediary operates is similar to our model, which comprises two sellers and two buyer types. Suppose that the intermediary optimally chooses a pro<sup>fi</sup>ling accuracy that we refer to as $" \mathrm { o l d } ^ { \prime \prime }$ $p .$ The sellers submit their bids, which we refer to as the $\ddot { \mathbf { \omega } } ^ { \prime \prime } \mathrm { o l d } ^ { \prime \prime } \hat { v } _ { m i }$ corresponding to buyer type i. Suppose that, without being aware of the real seller valuations, the intermediary increases the pro<sup>fi</sup>ling accuracy by $\delta > 0$ . After the moving average of their conditional payoff changes, the sellers will alter their payments to their $' \mathrm { n e w } ^ { \prime \prime } ~ \hat { v } _ { m i }$ . The intermediary will notice that its pro<sup>fi</sup>ts are lower than where they were with the $\mathrm { \Omega ^ { \prime \prime } o l d ^ { \prime \prime } }$ $p$ and will have an incentive to go back to the $\mathbf { \Delta } ^ { \prime \prime } \mathrm { o l d } \mathbf { \Gamma } ^ { \prime \prime } p .$ In such a case, the intermediary may still choose to forgo pro<sup>fi</sup>ling its buyers more accurately, even if the intermediary still does not know the actual valuations of the sellers.

Lastly, several other natural questions arise, and we address some of them elsewhere in the text as follows. The perverse incentive that we observe exists even in a multislot case, and we demonstrate this in Section 6.1. In addition, we show that our results continue to hold when our assumption on the symmetric arrival of buyers is relaxed in Section 6.2. As discussed earlier, adding a cost for the pro<sup>fi</sup>ling effort will not change the result either. Even though, in this parsimonious setup, the optimal choice of p for the intermediary is $\begin{array} { r } { \frac { v _ { j j } - v _ { i j } } { v _ { j j } - v _ { i j } + v _ { i i } - v _ { j i } } , } \end{array}$ , pinpointing the optimal value of $p$ is not the focus of this paper. The optimal value of p can be different in a more generalized setup. In Proposition $^ { 3 , }$ and in the generalizations of the results that follow, we intend to solely make a point that the intermediary has an incentive to choose a $p < 1$ . ## 4.2. Payments for Transactions

Next, we consider the choice of $p$ under the payments for transactions. Once again, we use backward induction to solve the game described in Section 3. The equilibrium presented in Lemma 2 is simply a subgame-perfect Nash equilibrium to this revised game, where we also solve for the intermediary’s <sup>fi</sup>rststage decisions regarding the choice of $p .$

Proposition 4. When an intermediary adopts the payments-for-transactions policy, the following strategies and payoffs constitute a dominant strategy equilibrium: the intermediary’s choice of p equals one, and it charges $\frac { 1 } { 2 \beta }$ fraction of the value generated as transaction fees for each transaction that are completed on the platform. At $\{ p ^ { * } = 1 , f ^ { * } = { \textstyle \frac { 1 } { 2 \beta } } \}$ , half of the transactions take place on the platform, and the platform makes profit of $\begin{array} { r } { \pi _ { t } ^ { * } = \frac { v _ { i i } } { 4 \beta } } \end{array}$ when serving buyer type i.

Since the intermediary charges only for transactions, when the intermediary sets the value of p at $^ { 1 , }$ it maximizes the value of the transaction. This result is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant deviation from that in the previous section on payments for discoveries. Here, if costs for pro<sup>fi</sup>ling are included, it will have an implication on the natural tendency to increase $p .$ Note also that the intermediary charges a positive fraction $( f > 0 )$ of the value of the transaction for completing transactions on the platform.

In the following section, we analyze the intermediary and the platform users’ choices across these two possible intermediary policies—payments for transactions and payments for discoveries. We focus on comparing the pro<sup>fi</sup>t and social welfare across these two policies as a consequence of the intermediary’s choice of buyer pro<sup>fi</sup>ling capabilities.

## 5. Policy Comparison: Payments for Discoveries vs. Transactions

In the previous section, we focused on the optimal choice of p by the intermediary, as well as the optimal actions of the buyers and sellers. In this section, we study how the intermediary chooses the monetization policy and the social welfare consequences that ensue from the selection. Note that the intermediary’s policy choice is related to how the mispro<sup>fi</sup>ling strategy is implemented. One way to execute this is to set up p as the same value across all pro<sup>fi</sup>ling efforts (i.e., independent of buyer pro<sup>fi</sup>les). In such an instance, the intermediary assesses the pro<sup>fi</sup>ts from all the pro<sup>fi</sup>ling efforts and then decides on the outcome, but setting up the same $p$ across all pro<sup>fi</sup>le types need not be the case. With advertising <sup>fi</sup>rms spending an extensive amount of compute cycles to classify the different buyer types, it may be possible to set the pro<sup>fi</sup>ling inaccuracy for a certain subset of the pro<sup>fi</sup>led buyer types if it is more pro<sup>fi</sup>table. As an aside, we do not have any evidence that the intermediaries are indeed doing so. Given that prior research has not revealed these perverse incentives, we believe that it is prudent to provide insights based on a simplistic representation, assuming that the mispro<sup>fi</sup>ling levels are set at the level of individual buyer types.

## 5.1. Intermediary Profits

We <sup>fi</sup>rst compare the pro<sup>fi</sup>ts generated by platforms with different revenue mechanisms when the potential strategic mispro<sup>fi</sup>ling is considered.

Proposition 5. An intermediary prefers the payments-for discoveries to the payments-for-transactions policy when, for buyer type $\begin{array} { r } { i , \ \frac { v _ { j i } } { v _ { i i } } > \frac { 1 } { 4 \beta p _ { d } ^ { * } } - \Big ( \frac { 1 - p _ { d } ^ { * } } { p _ { d } ^ { * } } \Big ) \Big ( \frac { v _ { j j } } { v _ { i i } } \Big ) } \end{array}$ or $\begin{array} { r } { \beta > \frac { v _ { i i } } { 4 \left( p _ { d } ^ { * } v _ { j i } + ( 1 - p _ { d } ^ { * } ) v _ { j j } \right) } , } \end{array}$ where $p _ { d } ^ { * }$ is the intermediary’s optimal choice of p under the payments-for-discoveries policy.

The proposition characterizes the condition when the intermediary chooses the revenue mechanism. Note that the ratio $\frac { v _ { j i } } { v _ { i i } }$ represents how closely the true buyer types are valued for buyer pro<sup>fi</sup>le type i. This ratio has a great impact on the choice of policy. When the two valuations are similar, this ratio is close to one. Corresponding to this condition, the platform prefers the payments for discoveries. Stated differently, when the valuations are naturally similar, the platform prefers the payments for discoveries. However, when they are dissimilar, the platform’s choice of $p$ under the payments-for-discoveries policy attempts to bring both seller valuations closer, at least for the pro<sup>fi</sup>led group. At the other extreme, the payments-for-transactions policy is preferred when the value realized from an accurate pro<sup>fi</sup>ling is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly higher $( v _ { i i } > > v _ { j i } )$

## 5.2. Social Welfare

Next, we consider the following question: How does mispro<sup>fi</sup>ling impact social welfare? In doing so, we investigate the social welfare implication of the two monetization policies.

Proposition 6. The social welfare with the payments-fordiscoveries policy is increasing in $p _ { d } ^ { * } ,$ , where $p _ { d } ^ { * }$ is the intermediary’s optimal choice of p under the payments-for-discoveries policy. In addition, the social welfare is always lower with the payments-for-discoveries policy compared with the payments-for-transactions policy.

Proposition 6 indicates that, when the platform adopts the payments-for-discoveries policy, the social welfare increases in $p .$ However, recall that when the platform charges for discoveries, it has an incentive to deviate from the $( p = 1 )$ strategy. As a result, the social welfare is suboptimal with the payments-for-discoveries policy, because the choice of p being less than 1 limits the value generated from an encounter between a buyer and a seller. Subsequently, the social welfare under the payments-for-discoveries policy is lower than that under the payments-for-transactions policy.

Although the result from this proposition is intuitive (i.e., that the inaccurate buyer pro<sup>fi</sup>ling results in lower social welfare), it highlights the incentive misalignment between the platform and its users. As a result, the moderating role of social planners or policy makers, whose role is to maximize collective welfare, is crucial in such a marketplace. In addition, our results on social welfare differences also provide new insights that contradict conventional wisdom in the literature, which consistently demonstrates that socia welfare tends to be lower under the payments-fortransactions policy (compared with that under the payments-for-discoveries policy). We <sup>fi</sup>nd that, in fact, the payments-for-transactions policy leads to a higher social welfare than the payments-for-discoveries policy, as the buyer pro<sup>fi</sup>ling can be inaccurate under the latter.

## 6.1. Multiple Buyers, Sellers, and Display Slots

We demonstrate how perverse incentives can also exist in a scenario involving multiple sellers. As before, we have real buyer types, profiled buyer types, and sellers. Let the focal buyer type be n˙ , and let there be a suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently large pool of buyers who belong to at least one other buyer type (at this stage, we are not distinguishing between real and pro<sup>fi</sup>led buyer types). We denote the set of buyer types as $\mathcal { N }$ and use n as an index to represent an individual buyer type. Suppose that there are M sellers on the platform interested in serving the buyers. Without loss of generality, we index the sellers in our model $m = 1 , 2 , \bar { 3 } , \ldots , M$ such that the <sup>fi</sup>rst seller generates the most payoff from interacting with the focal buyer, followed by the second seller, and so on. So, $v _ { 1 \dot { n } } > v _ { 2 \dot { n } } > . . . > v _ { M \dot { n } }$ . These sellers are competing to offer their products among the K slots/positions made available by the intermediary.

Previous studies (e.g., Grzywaczewski et al. 2010, Hof 2015) have empirically demonstrated that, when the same ad is shown in different positions, it tends to generate a different number of clicks based on its position. Consistent with such a notion, let the likelihood of position k being clicked be $\alpha _ { k } .$ . Without loss of generality, let $\alpha _ { 1 } > \alpha _ { 2 } > . . . > \alpha _ { K }$ . Furthermore, $\alpha _ { k } = 0$ for $k > K .$ Critically, note that the value of $\alpha _ { k }$ is not affected by a seller or buyer valuation. Essentially, $\alpha _ { k }$ captures the “position-speci<sup>fi</sup>c factor” denoted in Edelman et al. (2007). The ad slot with the highest positional factor is referred to as the first slot, the next one as the second slot, and so on. In any given situation, sellers prefer the <sup>fi</sup>rst slot to the second slot, the second slot to the third slot, and so on, for their advertisement to be displayed. This order of preference is independent of the seller, because it only relates to the position.

We consider that the platform adopts a generalized second-price (GSP) auction mechanism. The mechanism allocates the <sup>fi</sup>rst slot to the seller with the highest bid, the second slot with the second-highest bid, and so on. Under GSP, given a buyer type, a seller ranked k pays an amount equal to the bid by the seller ranked $k - 1$ . The equilibrium bidding strategy for sellers is consistent with the locally “envy-free” equilibri um, as presented in Edelman et al. (2007). As a result, our assumptions regarding each seller’s knowledge about the other’s bidding strategies are also consistent with Edelman et al. (2007). Furthermore, Varian (2007) empirically analyzes auction data from Google and reports that locally envy-free Nash equilibrium portrays Google’s ad auction bidding reasonably accurately. ## 7. Conclusion

With advanced technological innovations that allow online platforms to identify and understand user preferences, the world has observed an unprecedented degree of investments in and implementation of consumer pro<sup>fi</sup>ling technologies in recent years. However, although the pro<sup>fi</sup>ling of consumers is still far from perfect, we recognize that this imperfection in performance is usually overlooked in the current literature on online trading platforms. More importantly, the economic implications of pro<sup>fi</sup>ling capabilities have been left unexplored in the literature, revealing a critical gap. Our paper aims to <sup>fi</sup>ll this knowledge gap by developing and analyzing a simplistic and parsimonious game-theoretic model to examine whether online platforms have incentives to accurately pro<sup>fi</sup>le their consumers.

In our model, we consider two types of common online trading platforms: platforms that charge for discoveries and platforms that charge for transactions. Interestingly, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that discovery-based platforms do not always have incentives to accurately pro<sup>fi</sup>le their buyers. Indeed, their pro<sup>fi</sup>ts could be higher when the buyer pro<sup>fi</sup>ling is imperfect. However, such a perverse incentive does not exist for transactionbased platforms. In this case, a platform’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t is maximized when they pro<sup>fi</sup>le their buyers accurately. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings also carry important implications regarding social welfare. Unlike prior studies, which consistently show that social welfare tends to be higher under discovery-based platforms, we demonstrate the cases where social welfare is higher under the transaction-based platform, with the main driver of these social welfare differences being the potential strategic mispro<sup>fi</sup>ling from the payments-for-discoveries platform.

Our paper is subject to several limitations, which may also represent a great avenue for future research. For example, we do not explicitly model the case where an intermediary displays both organic and inorganic results in response to buyer searches, since we consider the case of inorganic search results only. Furthermore, we do not analyze any competitive markets in this paper. In a competitive environment, the choice of a revenue model and the buyer pro<sup>fi</sup>ling capability of an intermediary can be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the choice of other intermediaries in the same market space. Additionally, we do not incorporate the aspect of twosided markets, such as buyer or seller participation, in our model. Analyzing participation decisions by buyers and sellers may be considered as a future extension. In our model, we also assume the information on seller valuation to be private to the sellers. Future research can explore if sellers had imperfect information about the valuation by other sellers. Lastly, the intermediary platforms may attain ancillary revenue from other sources such as advertisements. Such factors are not explicitly modeled in this work but could affect a platform’s choice of buyer pro<sup>fi</sup>ling capability. Future empirical research could identify the salience of the perverse incentive identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in our work and/ or verify the moderating effect of other external factors.
