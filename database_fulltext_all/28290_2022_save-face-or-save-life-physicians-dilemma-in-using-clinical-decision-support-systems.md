# Save Face or Save Life: Physicians’ Dilemma in Using Clinical Decision Support Systems

Huigang Liang,<sup>a</sup> Yajiong Xue<sup>b,</sup>\*

<sup>a</sup> Department of Business Information and Technology, Fogelman College of Business and Economics, University of Memphis, Memphis, Tennessee 38152; <sup>b</sup> Department of Management Information Systems, College of Business, East Carolina University, Greenville, North Carolina 27858 \*Corresponding author

Abstract. Clinical decision support systems (CDSS) provide advice to physicians that could save patients’ lives, but they could also make physicians feel losing face. Considering the pivotal role of face in social interactions in China, this research attempts to understand Chinese physicians’ dilemma of choosing between saving face or saving patients and how this dilemma makes them reluctant to follow CDSS recommendations. Drawing from the dual process theory, we postulate that physicians’ resistance to CDSS recommendations is reduced by their rational perception of the system’s usefulness and increased by their experiential belief of face loss and that these effects are contingent on two contextual factors, professional autonomy, and time pressure. Based on a longitudinal <sup>fi</sup>eld survey with 182 physicians and follow-up interviews in a large Chinese hospital, we <sup>fi</sup>nd support for the opposing effects of face loss and perceived usefulness. Furthermore, we show that face loss has a stronger effect on CDSS resistance at a high level of professional autonomy. In addition, when time pressure is high, perceived usefulness more strongly reduces while face loss more strongly increases CDSS resistance, thus amplifying the dilemma. This research makes a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant contribution by integrating the concept of face loss into information technology resistance research and revealing the dark side of professional autonomy and the paradoxical moderating effect of time pressure in the healthcare context.

## Introduction

Healthcare organizations around the globe are under enormous pressures to create an ef<sup>fi</sup>cient and effective system by controlling costs while maintaining care quality. China is no exception. The Chinese healthcare market is the second largest in the world.<sup>1</sup> There are more than 1 million healthcare organizations and more than 34,000 hospitals serving 1.4 billion Chinese people. China’s overall healthcare expenditure was \$970 billion in 2019, which is less than one third of the healthcare expenditure of the United States.<sup>2</sup> However, there are 8.7 billion physician of<sup>fi</sup>ce visits in China every year,<sup>3</sup> which is about 10 times as many as those in the United States.<sup>4</sup> With so many patients to care for, it is a huge challenge to make healthcare accessible while maintaining high quality. The Chinese government always emphasizes care quality and patient safety and has issued a series of policies and regulations to actively promote the use of information technology (IT) by physicians to achieve these goals.<sup>5</sup> Health IT applications provide considerable promise for improving healthcare quality through managing information, reducing costs, and facilitating quality management programs (Chaudhry et al. 2006, Holroyd-Leduc et al. 2011, Romano and Stafford 2011). Clinical decision support systems (CDSSs), with the potential to standardize healthcare practices and improve patient care, have been advocated in the healthcare industry for many years. Many studies have shown that CDSSs can improve practitioner performance and patient outcomes (Garg et al. 2005, Bright et al. 2012). The recent advancement of data analytics and arti<sup>fi</sup>cial intelligence has greatly boosted the capabilities of CDSSs by offering data-driven insights that otherwise cannot be discovered by experts (Yang et al. 2019). However, widespread use of CDSSs has not become available because of technological, social, psychological, legal, <sup>fi</sup>nancial, and organizational barriers (Boonstra and Broekhuis 2010, Ali et al. 2016). Yang et al. (2019) report that almost all attempts to put arti<sup>fi</sup>cial intelligence (AI)- based CDSSs into clinical practice have failed during the past three decades. In China, much anecdotal evidence shows that it is a daunting task to promote electronic health records, particularly

The success of CDSS implementation relies on not only key technical features such as proper interface design, work<sup>fl</sup>ow compatibility, and suitable time and location, but also healthcare professionals’ acceptance (Kawamoto et al. 2005, Coiera 2015). Decades of experience with computer-based information systems suggests that the critical issues in the implementation of these systems are social and organizational and not solely technical (Kaplan et al. 2001). Among many social organizational factors, user resistance is considered one of the main causes for the failure of CDSSs (Coiera 2015). User resistance has consistently been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as a major obstacle to the success of implementations of information systems in general (Kim and Kankanhalli 2009, Lapointe and Beaudry 2014). In fact, it has been reported as the number one challenge to the implementation of large-scale systems and is widespread in the early stages of system implementation (Markus 2004). User resistance is particularly serious in healthcare settings in which healthcare professionals are characterized by high professiona autonomy and low propensity for being persuaded by people outside their profession (Lapointe and Rivard 2005, Bhattacherjee and Hikmet 2007, Liang et al. 2010, Mishra et al. 2012). Physicians have long been known for being cautiously reluctant to adopt IT innovations. Both qualitative and quantitative studies con-<sup>fi</sup>rm that physician resistance is one of the top barriers to implementing clinical information systems (Poon et al. 2004, Boonstra and Broekhuis 2010). In China, this problem is also prevalent among physicians. Media reports indicate that Chinese physicians are unwilling to use IT at work and one reason is the concern that IT will make their work transparent and reveal their weaknesses to others.

Despite the abundance of general IT resistance research and the importance of physician resistance, only a few studies in the information systems (IS) literature have explicitly investigated IT resistance among physicians (Lapointe and Rivard 2005, Bhattacherjee and Hikmet 2007, Xue et al. 2015). Much is left unknown regarding why physicians engage in IT resistance. Moreover, little research has examined CDSS resistance, de<sup>fi</sup>ned as physicians’ neglect or rejection of CDSS outputs that require their follow-up actions. CDSSs differ from other health information systems in that they are more than data entry, processing, and display. They actually act like a human expert to provide guidelines, recommendations, and alerts to physicians during clinical encounters to facilitate decision making (Osheroff et al. 2007). When physicians use CDSSs, it appears as if they are taking advice or complying with instructions from the system. The nature of this type of interaction is unique and intricate, which con<sup>fl</sup>icts with the autonomous and powerful status that characterizes clinicians (Wallace 1995). Although clinicians are used to telling patients what to do, in this case, they need to take recommendations from CDSSs. It is questionable that physicians will willingly accept this role change and be submissive to CDSSs.

Ostensibly, it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for physicians to assume an obedient role when using CDSS because of their longestablished dominant role in medical encounters. In the traditional patient-physician relationship characterized by paternalism (Emanuel and Emanuel 1992), there exists a power imbalance between patients and physicians. Physicians usually have more power because of their highly specialized education and expertise that enable them to conduct clinical tasks such as diagnosis and treatment. On the contrary, patients are powerless because they usually are not knowledgeable of their conditions and possible treatments and may not even have the intellectual capacity to understand such specialized knowledge. Thus, the knowledge disparity and information asymmetry give rise to the typical power imbalance between physicians and patients. Physicians are proud of their professional status and like to maintain their dominance in the physician-patient relationship (Grol 2001). Such pride is historically entrenched, and its origin can be traced back to as early as the fourth century (Metraux 1995). In modern societies, physicians continue to have superior social status and maintain a high leve of professional pride. They are used to taking the powerful role to give and to help, and it would appear awkward for them to be in the powerless role to receive and to be helped.

Against this backdrop, we draw on the cultural concept of face to explain why Chinese physicians pride can make them resist CDSSs. Face describes how a person’s image is constructed and reconstructed by social norms and through social interactions (Goffman 1955). Although face is recognized as a general concept applicable in both Eastern and Western countries (Goffman 1978), it is believed to play a more salient role in China in guiding social interactions because of its deep root in the Confucius tradition (Hwang 2012). Face is lost when a person fails to meet the expectations of her social position, which could lead to negative consequences such as psychological imbalance (Hwang 2012) and interference with normal social functioning (Ho 1976, Keil et al. 2007). In medical settings, physicians are expected to con<sup>fi</sup>dently, competently, and independently make clinical decisions, and they will lose face if they fail to meet these expectations. Thus, face is an important concern that physicians often implicitly take into account when deciding their behaviors that are visible to others.

Considering both the medical bene<sup>fi</sup>t and facelosing side effect of CDSS use, physicians confront a perplexing dilemma. Thinking re<sup>fl</sup>ectively, they focus on ful<sup>fi</sup>lling their professional duty to save patients and should follow advice from CDSSs to improve care quality and reduce medical errors. Yet, thinking heuristically, they focus on protecting their authoritative image to maintain face and are inclined to avoid embarrassment by resisting CDSS recommendations. It will be interesting to see how this dilemma plays out in clinical encounters, particularly in China where face is deep-rooted in culture and plays an essential role in social exchanges. Our interviews with Chinese physicians have con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that they indeed have ambivalent thoughts when deciding whether to take recommendations from CDSSs. Hence, in this research, we attempt to investigate the re<sup>fl</sup>ective and heuristic factors in<sup>fl</sup>uencing Chinese physicians’ resistance of CDSS in outpatient settings in opposite ways. Speci<sup>fi</sup>- cally, we posit that CDSS resistance is determined by two antecedents: a rational cognitive factor from the individual’s perspective (perceived usefulness of CDSS) and a heuristic experiential factor from the cultural perspective (perceived face loss). Moreover, we contextualize our theorization by considering two unique moderators: a factor related to the medical profession (professional autonomy) and a factor related to the clinical work environment (time pressure). Our research question is as follows: How is physicians CDSS resistance influenced by their perceptions of CDSS usefulness and face loss in opposing directions and how is this dilemma contingent on the medical context in China? By investigating the contradictions and paradoxes physicians experience, we delineate how CDSS resistance is shaped and the boundaries within which the <sup>fi</sup>ndings hold, thus making a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant contribution to both theory and practice in the realm of healthcare information technology.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. After reviewing the relevant literature and theories, we propose the research model and hypotheses. This is followed by the description of a longitudinal survey study conducted in a large Chinese hospital. Finally, we present results and discussions.

## Literature Review and Theoretical Development CDSS Resistance

The IS discipline has almost 40 years of history of investigating users’ IT resistance, dating back to the seminal work of Markus (1983). Resistance could exhibit itself as reluctance, nonuse, or low level of IT use and cannot be simpli<sup>fi</sup>ed as the mirror opposite of IT acceptance (Bhattacherjee and Hikmet 2007). A myriad of different forces could give rise to resistance. IS researcher have attempted to theoretically explain how IT resistance is shaped under different circumstances (Laumer and Eckhardt 2012). Most researchers have focused on studying the cognitive beliefs’ impact on resistance. Markus (1983) explains that the resistance behavior is a result of individuals perception on gain or loss of organizational power associated with the change. Joshi (1991) posits that users react to change by comparing their net gain based on the change in their inputs and outcomes. Cenfetelli (2004) develops a dual factor model that posits that users simultaneously examine the enabling and inhibiting factors before deciding whether to use a system. He de<sup>fi</sup>nes inhibitors as negative factors that discourage IT use when present, yet do not necessarily favor use when absent and calls for more attention to be paid to inhibitors in resistance research.

User resistance has also been explained as a result of status quo bias, suggesting that loss-aversive users tend to perceive switching to new technology as a loss because of their psychological commitment to the existing technology (Kim and Kankanhalli 2009, Polites and Karahanna 2012). Besides, social in<sup>fl</sup>uence plays a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in inducing user resistance (Eckhardt et al. 2009). In general, user resistance is recognized as a complex phenomenon shaped by factors related to people, technology, and environment, as well as their interactions (Ali et al. 2016). According to Markus (1983), “it is the situation-dependent interaction between characteristics related to the people and characteristics related to the system that determines resistance” (p. 431).

As a unique group of users, physicians and healthcare IT have attracted increasing attention from IS researchers (Kohli and Tan 2016). Several studies have particularly focused on physician resistance of IT. From three case studies, Lapointe and Rivard (2005) <sup>fi</sup>nd physicians’ group resistance of electronic medical records (EMRs) is caused by their perceived threat of the EMRs such as loss of status or power. Along this line of logic, Bhattacherjee and Hikmet (2007) con<sup>fi</sup>rm that the losses because of technology can lead to perceived threat that shifts physicians’ behavior toward resisting the Computerized Physician Order Entry technology. Xue et al. (2015) further reveal that the relationship between perceived threat and physician resistance of telemedicine can be reduced by environmental factors. The common theme of these studies is that physician resistance stems from the threat perception that their power is challenged by the technology. It is surprising that, to date, no studies have focused on physician resistance of CDSSs, which supposedly poses more serious threat to their power than other types of Health Information Technology (HIT). CDSSs are more threatening because they go beyond the affordance enabled by regular healthcare IT to offer knowledge-based recommendations to physicians, which directly challenge physicians’ authority in clinical decision making (Cabana et al. 1999). Despite the potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of CDSSs, physician resistance of CDSSs is a common problem. A study of 2,872 clinicians shows that they overrode most of medication safety alerts generated by CDSS, although it might have left patient safety at risk (Isaac et al. 2009). Hence, it is both theoretically interesting and practically important to study physician resistance of CDSSs.

As suggested by prior resistance research (Markus 1983, Ali et al. 2016), CDSS resistance is likely to be determined by the interaction of a multitude of factors at different levels. Following this wisdom, we draw on dual process theory to examine the effect of two opposing appraisals of possible consequences of CDSS use: one enabling factor based on individual cognition (perceived usefulness) and one inhibiting factor based on cultural norms (face loss). We also include two contextual factors, professional autonomy and time pressure, related to the medical profession and clinical work environment, respectively, to understand how they moderate the effects of perceived usefulness and face loss. The goal is not to provide a comprehensive account of how CDSS resistance is determined. Rather, we attempt to demonstrate the complexity of the shaping mechanisms of CDSS resistance by focusing on dilemma to shed light on the nuanced relationships among various factors.

## Face as a Cultural Concept

Face is a term originated in ancient China before 400 BC around the Zhou dynasty (Hwang 2012). Derived from Confucianism that has dominated the Chinese ideology for more than 2,000 years, it has fundamental in<sup>fl</sup>uences on individual behaviors in China. In the Chinese tradition, face has two meanings: mianzi and lian. The former denotes one’s prestige or reputation of personal success recognized by society, whereas the latter stands for the society’s respect of one’s morality (Hu 1944). In this article, we focus on the mianzi interpretation of face because of its relevance to our research context.

Leung and Chan (2003, p. 1575) de<sup>fi</sup>ne face as “the respect, pride and dignity of an individual as a consequence of his/her social achievement and the practice of it.” As a part of the Chinese norms, people pay great attention to caring for one’s face (Huang et al. 2008). A person can claim face for herself from others based on her position in the social network and the congruence between her actions and social expectations (Ho 1976). The claim to face may be based on situation and vary across groups with which a person is interacting (Hwang 2012). People maintain or gain face by winning recognition and respect from others via personal efforts such as hard work, contribution to the society, wealth, and achievement. People lose face when they fail to meet the requirements of their social status. In Chinese societies, the desire to maintain face and avoid losing face has been hardwired into people’s cognitive schemata to help them avert negative consequences such as stigma, negative emotions, and impairment in social functioning (Ho 1976). Thus, face is an important social consideration that have profound in<sup>fl</sup>uences on individuals’ social behaviors in China.

Although indigenous to China, face is recognized by Western scholars as a universal human need that transcends national and cultural boundaries (Brown et al. 1987, Tuncel et al. 2020). The seminal work of Goffman (1955, 1978) on face inspired much interest among Western anthropology, sociology, and psychology researchers. Similar to the Chinese concept of mianzi, face in the Western context is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as one’s positive self-image or self-worth associated with ability, achievement, or wealth, and face can be gained or lost when others recognize or reject one’s claim of these properties (Goffman 1978). Extensive empirical evidence has shown that face is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant concern for interpersonal activities such as communication, con<sup>fl</sup>ict, and negotiation in Western societies (Brown et al. 1987, Earley 1997, Tuncel et al. 2020).

Face has been applied by IS researchers to explain IS phenomena such as bad news reporting, smartphone repurchase, online aggression, and knowledge sharing. Keil et al. (2007) <sup>fi</sup>nd that losing face is a serious concern when reporting bad news on software projects and blame-shifting can mitigate this concern for Americans but not for South Koreans. Filieri et al. (2017) show that smartphone repurchase intention is determined by buyers’ face enhancing or maintaining considerations. Xu et al. (2016) indicate that face saving serves as an internal deterrent to reduce online aggression behaviors. Huang et al. (2011) discover that face saving and face gaining have opposite effects on knowledge sharing. Young et al. (2012) demonstrates that face saving motives could impede knowledge sharing because of fear of criticism when using knowledge-based systems. Ding et al. (2017) further reveal that face saving or gaining plays an important moderating role in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing employees’ knowledge sharing behavior. A consensus among these studies is that people’s desire to gain face or avoid losing face can have a large impact on their IT-related or knowledge-related behaviors.

In the healthcare context, face has been applied to understand healthcare providers’ behavior. For example, a qualitative study shows that the feedback culture in the residency program of Brigham and Women’s Hospital, a teaching hospital of Harvard Medical School, is based on face saving that may impede honest constructive feedback from medical faculty to residents (Ramani et al. 2018). A survey (Kirschbaum 2012) <sup>fi</sup>nds that face concern does exist among surgeons and anesthesiologists in an American hospital, and the level of face concern is positively related with their self-construal (or independence). Another survey of a national sample of nurse anesthetists further suggests that face concern is widely prevalent among nurses in the United States (Kirschbaumphd 2018). In addition, several studies conducted in China discover that nurses’ face-saving concern can strongly increase their fear of reporting medication errors (Chiang and Pepper 2006, Yang et al. 2020).

In summary, face is a universal cultural concept and is particularly salient in China. It has great potential to generate insights into human behaviors in the IS and healthcare contexts. However, limited attention has been paid to this unique concept. In this research, we try to integrate face into the dual process theory to explain physicians’ CDSS resistance behavior.

## Dual Process Theory

Dual process theory (DPT) refers to a group of theories in social and cognitive psychology positing that human beings have two fundamentally different modes of processing information when making judgments and decisions: System 1 and System 2 (Evans 2008, Kahneman 2011). Despite many ways of describing the two types of processes, scholars generally agree that System 1 processes are unconscious, rapid, automatic, effortless, intuitive, heuristic, and experiential, whereas System 2 processes are conscious, slow, deliberative, effortful, analytic, systematic, and rational (Evans 2008). The thoughts, attitudes, and behaviors of individuals are determined by how they process various information through these two systems. Numerous studies have supported the application of DPT in explaining a variety of human activities related to reasoning, judgement, decision, and social cognition (Kahneman 2011). Psychology theorists have different opinions regarding whether the two systems operate sequentially or in parallel (Evans and Stanovich 2013). Regardless of the temporal sequence, there is consensus that the two systems work in complementary or competitive manner in a given situation. The social cognitive neuroscience research provides evidence that System 1 and System 2 processing are associated with activations in completely different brain regions (Lieberman et al. 2002, Lieberman 2007). It strongly suggests that the two systems are structurally separate and could take place together.

Drawing from DPT, we propose that physicians engage in dual systems of information processing when they decide whether to accept or resist CDSSs. System 1 processing allows them to automatically form an opinion about CDSSs based on their intuitions and heuristics developed from incumbent work and social experiences (Evans 2008). Although many heuristic thoughts could be possibly processed under System 1, given the unique characteristics of physicians, we focus on face loss as a salient heuristic cue. We posit that face loss operates under System 1 based on two considerations. First, attention to face loss can become automatic or implicit after long-term exposure to Confucian values. As Evans (2008) explicate, explicit cog nitions can become implicit when the behavior turns habitual. For Chinese people whose personal experiences are socially shaped since childhood by the Confucian culture, face-saving responses are often automatically activated, and they are unaware they are trying to save face or prevent losing face (Hwang 2012). Second, because face is closely related to selfesteem (Goffman 1978), face loss inevitably leads to damaged self-esteem. Abundant evidence shows that human behavior is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by implicit self-esteem, making the link from self-attitude to the defensive behavior introspectively unidenti<sup>fi</sup>able (Greenwald and Banaji 1995, Koole et al. 2001). That is, the person cannot establish the causal linkage between her response and her sense of face loss, although she may be aware of both.

The operation of face loss as a System 1 process can be further illustrated by drawing from selfpresentation theory (Goffman 1978, Baumeister 1982). Self-presentation is an impression management behavior to communicate some information about oneself to others, which is in line with the concept of face. Human beings have a persistent and continuous concern with their self-presentation in public (Leary 2019). They prefer to be perceived by others in certain desirable ways rather than undesirable ways and thus are motivated to construct their public selves consistent with their ideal (Baumeister 1982). People’s selfpresentation concerns affect a wide array of social behavior, particularly those involving human interactions, by putting tacit constraints on their behavioral options (Leary 2019). In the context of CDSS use, physicians, just like any other people, are unwilling to make an impression of being incompetent, ignorant, or inexperienced (Alexander et al. 2010). Regardless of the correctness of this concern, the deeply entrenched cultural value of face saving could be automatically processed under System 1 to affect physicians’ CDSS resistance before they know it is happening. That is, the instinct of avoiding face loss becomes a heuristic cue for physicians when they try to decide whether to use CDSS, and this cue implicitly in<sup>fl</sup>uences their resistance behavior.

In contrast, System 2 processing enable physicians to deliberatively form an opinion based on their rational and analytical evaluation of the outcomes of CDSS use (Kahneman 2011). Given the purpose of CDSS, physicians would expect it to be helpful in facilitating their clinical decisions. To evaluate whether and how it is helpful, physicians would effortfully engage in re<sup>fl</sup>ective thinking by considering questions like what information CDSS can provide, whether the information is relevant and important, what bene<sup>fi</sup>ts can be gained from the information, and what losses will occur if the information is neglected, and so on. This leads to a perception of CDSS usefulness, which is one of the most important cognitive factors in<sup>fl</sup>uencing physician resistance. As Kim and Kankanhalli (2009) <sup>fi</sup>nd, perceived value derived from usefulness can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly reduce user resistance of an information system.

## Theory Contextualization

Although both System 1 and System 2 can operate in parallel, the relative strength of their effects on the subsequent behavior in a particular situation depends on the idiosyncratic context (Epstein et al. 1996). It is important to note that contextual factors are not necessarily, and to some extent may not be, prescribed by the theory they intend to contextualize because their purpose is to extend the theory. As Hong et al. (2013, p. 119) suggest, “a general model may not fully capture the characteristics of individuals and usage context. Thus, researchers can identify salient individual and contextual factors based on the context being studied.” Following the approach recommended by Hong et al. (2013), we select two contextual factors for our research: professional autonomy of the medical profession context and time pressure of the clinical work context to situate DPT in the CDSS resistance context.

The relevance of these two contextual factors can be inferred from prior DPT research related to involvement (Petty et al. 1983) and time pressure (Rand et al. 2012, Protzko et al. 2019). DPT posits that involvement, or personal relevance to the task at hand, moderates the dual processes because, when the task is more personally relevant, one would be more motivated to take efforts to engage in System 2 processing (Petty et al. 1983, Park et al. 2007). Based on this logic, it is arguable that professional autonomy can partially re<sup>fl</sup>ect physicians’ issue involvement given that using CDSS to assist clinical decision making is personally important to them. Regarding time pressure, as Kahneman’s classic work, “Thinking, fast and slow,” indicates, a critical distinction between System 1 and System 2 processing is speed. Not surprisingly, time pressure is widely studied by DPT researchers as a factor that affects the dual processes in various contexts (Rand et al. 2012, Protzko et al. 2019). The logic is that System 1 is more likely to be activated when time pressure is high. However, the moderating role of time pressure between the dual processes and outcome behaviors has been overlooked. Thus, the original DPT does not fully specify how professional autonomy and time pressure should be studied as a moderating variable.

Rather than being con<sup>fi</sup>ned by DPT, we deliberately identify professional autonomy and time pressure as the two most salient characteristics of our research context to create a potential to extend DPT. Both autonomy and time pressure <sup>fi</sup>t with our physician level investigation because they can be conceptualized as physicians’ perception of the broader context within which they practice medicine, thus providing a more proximal re<sup>fl</sup>ection of the distal context (Johns 2017). They are chosen from many potential contextual factors<sup>8</sup> because of their theoretical importance and practical relevance. More important, we propose their moderation effects that are not part of the prior DPT research, and such divergence presents exciting opportunities to generate novel theoretical insights in the given contexts for this research, as elaborated next.

First, the medical profession to which physicians belong values autonomy (Mirvis 1993). A study shows that autonomy is one of the most important determinants of physicians’ career satisfaction (Stoddard et al. 2001). Physicians’ professional autonomy can boost their self-esteem, which enhances their con<sup>fi</sup>dence in their knowledge and judgment (Deci and Ryan 2000). As a result, if physicians spend effort and time in deliberatively evaluating an object, they tend to have a <sup>fi</sup>rm belief in their conclusion. Thus, the resulting perceptions and judgments of their System 2 information processing tend to have a more signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on their decisions and behaviors. Moreover, professional autonomy is a signal of physicians’ occupational prestige. Typically, as their job tenure increases, physicians accumulate more social experiences and assimilate more culture-speci<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge through social interaction with other people in different roles. Consequently, the face-saving value is likely to habituate and become part of their implicit memory which directs their System 1 processing (Evans 2008). In the IT resistance literature, professional autonomy is mostly studied in the form of reduced autonomy as an antecedent of user resistance (Walter and Lopez 2008, Xue et al. 2015). To date, little research has treated professional autonomy as a moderator to enhance theoretical contextualization.

In addition, time pressure is a common feature of professional decision situations, and lack of time is typical for doctors in clinical settings (Faraj and Xiao 2006). Particularly, time pressure has long been a serious problem for Chinese physicians, especially in large hospitals. Physicians in these hospitals have only a few minutes for a patient during a medical encounter (Bai 2014, Jin and Chen 2016). Several factors might have exacerbated this problem. One is the exploding growth of the patient population. In 2018,

China’s total number of patient visits was more than 8.3 billion, an average of six visits per citizen, whereas there were only 2.59 practicing physicians per 1,000 population (NHC 2019). The other is that China’s uneven distribution of medical resources. Experienced doctors and advanced equipment are concentrated in large tertiary hospitals, which attracted most patients. In addition, without the gatekeeping of a referral system, patients can freely go to any hospital. As a result, most doctors in large hospitals are extremely overloaded. A study <sup>fi</sup>nds that the average daily workload for an outpatient doctor in a large Chinese hospital ranges from 72 to 125.75 patient visits (Jin and Chen 2016). That is, each patient has only around four to seven minutes to see the doctor. Although the government requires doctors to spend at least eight minutes for each patient, it is impossible to comply with this policy because of overwhelming patient demands (Jin and Chen 2016). High-quality decisions depend on activities that take time to complete, and time pressure has been found to lower decision quality (Svenson and Maule 1993). Given that time is a critical resource in human judgment and decision making, it will inevitably in<sup>fl</sup>uence physicians’ resistance of CDSSs. The literature seems to assume that physicians tend to resist the use of IT when under time pressure (Boonstra and Broekhuis 2010). However, no research has theoretically explained and empirically veri<sup>fi</sup>ed how time pressure impacts physicians’ technology resistance as a moderator.

## Research Model and Hypotheses

Under the overarching framework of DPT, we synthesize multiple literatures to develop a research model (Figure 1). We posit that physician resistance of CDSS is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by a pair of opposing perceptions: the rational perception of CDSS usefulness and the sociocultural perception of face loss. To contextualize this model, we include professional autonomy and time pressure as two moderating factors that are unique for physicians’ occupation and work contexts. Next, we discuss detailed theoretical rationales behind each hypothesis.

Face is important for individuals embedded in social interactions, especially for physicians who take a great deal of pride in what they do to save lives. Face saving pertains to dignity and pride that physicians would love to maintain, and losing face will be seen as a shame and disgrace (Leung and Chan 2003). The effect of face can be explained through the lens of self presentation. As the dramaturgical view (Goffman 1978) posits, humans’ social interactions are similar to theatrical plays and all individuals, including physicians, are actors who try to control or guide how others make an impression of them by engaging in self-presentation behaviors. Individuals will avoid embarrassment by using various impression management tactics, which is essentially a face-saving action (Leary 2019). The dramaturgical view has been applied to understand medical professionals’ behavior during encounters with patients (John 1996). In a similar vein, we suggest that the patient encounter can be seen as a play in which physicians perform the provision of medical services in front of a group of audience, possibly including the patient, nurses, physician assistants, residents, and medical students. To become a physician, one needs to undertake intensive education and training and pass dif<sup>fi</sup>cult board exams. Given that the elite position of being doctors is achieved through competitive selection and lengthy socialization, physicians try to maintain an ideal public impression that matches their social status. This implicit motivation is socially and culturally constructed and becomes deeply entrenched in physicians’ mind (Huang et al. 2008).

Motivated by face saving, physicians attempt to cast an image of professional con<sup>fi</sup>dence and authority in public. However, much prior research shows that physicians felt threat from HIT because of loss of power (Lapointe and Rivard 2005, Walter and Lopez 2008). Physicians may be worried that patient think they were incompetent and lack necessary medical knowledge if they used CDSS for reference when making clinical decisions. Thus, CDSSs could be implicitly associated with a reputational threat and physicians would prefer to make decisions independently without seeking help from CDSS so that face is saved.<sup>9</sup> This is consistent with prior research showing that physicians can develop threat perceptions toward IT and subsequently resist IT use (Bhattacherjee and Hikmet 2007, Xue et al. 2015). Some anecdotal evidence in media articles have also con<sup>fi</sup>rmed, from other perspectives, that in general face loss is a concern for physicians (Graham 2007, Witman 2014). As Graham (2007) observed, “doctors were too proud, … too worried about losing face” in terms of admitting medical errors, and Witman (2014) found that physicians are afraid of losing face to peer physicians. This general sensitivity to face loss is likely to play a role when physicians use CDSS. Therefore, we propose that face loss related to CDSS use will impel physicians to resist the use of CDSS.

## Hypothesis 1. Physicians’ perceived face loss is positively associated with their resistance to CDSS.

Although heuristic thinking (System 1) makes physicians concerned about their own face loss, re<sup>fl</sup>ective thinking (System 2) guides physicians to evaluate the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of CDSS for patients. Perceived usefulness refers to the extent to which users believe that CDSS use will enhance their job performance in helping patients (Davis 1989, Davis et al. 1989). Replete evidence repeatedly con<sup>fi</sup>rms that perceived usefulness is a salient cognitive determinant of IT use in the workplace. CDSS can provide relevant knowledge and personspeci<sup>fi</sup>c information, intelligently <sup>fi</sup>ltered and timely presented, to assist physicians and other health professionals with their clinical decision-making tasks for improved healthcare (Osheroff et al. 2007). A plethora of studies have shown that CDSS are effective in improving healthcare processes and clinical and economic outcomes (Bright et al. 2012). Although consistently strong evidence is yet to emerge, there is consensus that CDSS has great potential to enhance clinical decision quality, improve job ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, reduce medical errors, and improve patient outcomes (Garg et al. 2005, Bright et al. 2012). When physicians engage in deliberative cognitive process of evaluating the consequences of CDSSs, they can realize the bene-<sup>fi</sup>ts of using CDSSs to improve performance and care quality. As a result, they are likely to actively integrate CDSSs into their clinical practice and resistance becomes unlikely. Thus, we hypothesize the following.

## Hypothesis 2. Physicians’ perceived usefulness of CDSS is negatively associated with their resistance to CDSSs.

Because physicians possess specialized medical expertise and knowledge, one characteristic that distinguishes them from other professionals is the high autonomy in performing clinical tasks (Wallace 1995, Sharma 1997, Liang et al. 2010). Professional autonomy is the ability to independently make work-related decisions. It generally refers to professionals' control over the conditions, processes, procedures, or content of their work according to their individual or collective judgment in the application of their knowledge and expertise (Walter and Lopez 2008). Typically, a physician has a high level of authority to decide how to provide medical care to a patient without outside interference (Mirvis 1993). Although autonomy is a salient characteristic of the medical profession relative to other professions, within the medical profession itself physicians can have different levels of autonomy based on their individual attributes. Physicians with higher professional autonomy tend to have a stronger ego and are more likely to spontaneously protect their self-image when they sense a threat (Alexander et al. 2010). They work hard to reach the current professional status. It is understandable that they try to engage in self-presentation to match their status.

In the context of CDSSs, physicians’ roles as knowledge experts, controllers, and authority are challenged, because CDSS offers advice or points out their mistakes and they appear to be “coached” by CDSS. These changes brought forth by CDSS tend to reduce physicians’ autonomy and undermine their power and privileges (Bhattacherjee and Hikmet 2007, Xue et al. 2015). As a result, physicians’ feeling of face loss becomes a more salient concern when they use CDSSs, and physicians with higher autonomy will have more dif<sup>fi</sup>culty in adapting to these changes. This can be explained from two perspectives. First, it is a typical belief in the Chinese culture that people with high social status such as physicians are expected, both by others and by themselves, to behave in a way matching their identity and status, in this case, to make independent clinical decisions (Huang et al. 2008). If they do not, they will lose face. Second, the medical culture rewards physicians’ independence and certainty in their diagnosis and treatment decisions, which reinforces their existing personal norm of valuing professional autonomy (Croskerry and Norman 2008). Prior research con<sup>fi</sup>rms that physicians’ independence is positively related to their concern for face loss (Kirschbaum 2012). The more physicians think they should be autonomous, the more likely they deem it unacceptable to surrender to external instructions and the more sensitive they are to face loss because of impaired autonomy. Consequently, face loss operates via System 1 to implicitly induce stronger CDSS resistance for the physicians who have a higher level of autonomy.

In contrast, for physicians with low professional autonomy (e.g., interns or residents who are still going through training rounds), they need to report to or defer their opinions to more senior physicians. Their ego is not as strong given their followership role at work. It would be easier for them to comply with CDSS recommendations given that they have been following recommendations from other physicians all along. Therefore, even if they perceive face loss because of CDSS use, they do not treat it as equally a serious concern as those physicians with higher autonomy. Hence, their System 1 processing is less likely to be activated to induce CDSS resistance because of such face loss. Overall, these logics suggest that professional autonomy will play a positive moderating role between losing face and CDSS resistance so that face loss more strongly increases resistance for highly autonomous physicians.

Hypothesis 3a. Professional autonomy strengthens the positive relationship between face loss and resistance to CDSSs, so that the relationship is stronger when professional autonomy is higher.

Physicians traditionally form a close-knit social network that views external attempts to exerting control as a challenge to its autonomy (Edwards et al. 2002). Professional autonomy is associated with privileges such as possession of specialized knowledge, control over their own work and the supporting staff’s work, and respectable social image (Walter and Lopez 2008). When physicians have strong professional autonomy, the job characteristic helps them develop a habit of being assertive on judgments and decisions, even to the degree of overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence (Croskerry and Norman 2008). Extensive evidence shows that physicians tend to be overly con<sup>fi</sup>dent and make various diagnostic errors because of it (Berner and Graber 2008).

Although there are many factors potentially causing overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence, the medical culture that advocates autonomy and certainty plays a major role in cultivating it (Croskerry and Norman 2008). If physicians appear uncertain about their decision, it is considered a sign of weakness (Croskerry and Norman 2008). Prior research <sup>fi</sup>nds that physicians’ con<sup>fi</sup>dence in their diagnosis is high regardless of the diagnostic accuracy (Meyer et al. 2013). In addition, autonomous clinical work continues to reinforce this habit of overcon<sup>fi</sup> dence over time, and it can be extrapolated to judgements of all entities or activities related to clinical work in general, for instance, CDSSs. Thus, once physicians believe the the CDSS is useful (or not) for their clinical tasks, they tend to be con<sup>fi</sup>dent about their judgment, and this con<sup>fi</sup>dence varies with their professional autonomy. For highly autonomous physicians, their con<sup>fi</sup>dence is deeply entrenched, and they strongly believe in their evaluation of the value of CDSSs. The boosted con<sup>fi</sup>dence in turn increases their expectancy of reaping bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from using CDSSs. According to expectancy value theory (Vroom 1964), the interaction of value and expectancy determines motivation for a given behavior. Hence, when professional autonomy is high, perceived usefulness as a result of System 2 processing will be more positively related to CDSS use and thus more negatively related to CDSS resistance.

For physicians whose autonomy is relatively weak (e.g., residents), they usually defer to others’ decisions or collaborate with others to make decisions jointly at work and have not developed the habit of being overly con<sup>fi</sup>dent. Even if they use System 2 processing to reach their own evaluation of the usefulness of CDSSs, they are uncertain how they can actually achieve the value from CDSSs. With the low expectancy, perceived usefulness will be less positively related to CDSS use and thus less negatively related to CDSS resistance. Overall, professional autonomy will play a negative moderating role between the usefulness perception and CDSS resistance so that perceived usefulness more strongly decreases resistance for highly autonomous physicians.

Hypothesis 3b. Professional autonomy strengthens the negative relationship between perceived usefulness and resistance to CDSS so that the relationship is stronger when professional autonomy is higher.

Because System 1 processing is fast, whereas System 2 processing is slow, time pressure is inevitably an boundary conditions for DPT (Kahneman 2011, Rand et al. 2012, Protzko et al. 2019). The psychology literature shows that individuals’ cognitive capacity can be exceeded when performing decision tasks under time pressure (Svenson and Maule 1993). Prior research <sup>fi</sup>nds that time pressure has a negative impact on physicians’ diagnostic accuracy (Alqahtani et al. 2021). When time is limited, cognitive resource demanded by quick clinical decisions exceeds physicians’ cognitive capability. In this situation, physicians usually engage in System 1 processing to cope with time pressure, but System 2 processing can also take place. As Evans and Stanovich (2013) posit, System 1 is always activated for ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency purpose, and System 2 will be activated to complement or intervene with

System 1 when the decision really matters. Because CDSS use could affect patient health and human lives may be at stake, it is a decision that cannot be taken lightly. Physicians will not only experientially evaluate how the use of CDSS leads to face loss, but also re-<sup>fl</sup>ectively appraise how to mitigate the effect of time pressure on clinical tasks by using CDSS. These two processes are not in competition because they take place on different brain regions and can have distinct mechanisms (Lieberman et al. 2002, Lieberman 2007).

First, guided by the experiential mind (System 1), physicians will intuitively evaluate how CDSS use can affect their self-representation. The dual process theory suggests that people under time pressure often resort to System 1 processing and rely on heuristics and experiences to make judgments and decisions (Evans 2008).<sup>10</sup> As discussed earlier, a patient encounter is analogous to theatrical performance in which physicians presents themselves to patients and other beholders based on social norms and beliefs (John 1996). Physicians like to maintain pride and create an image of competence. Yet the use of CDSS could be seen as a sign of incompetence by the physician herself or by beholders, thus posing a reputational threat. Consequently, physicians could heuristically interpret the use of CDSS as intrusive in their medical practice. When making a judgment on CDSS, this negative information will become an important consideration. Decision research shows that under time pressure individuals will selectively process information and assign higher weight to negative information (Svenson and Maule 1993). Thus, time pressure will amplify the negative perception of CDSS. Compared with the low time pressure condition, physicians’ judgment of CDSS under high time pressure will be more heavily in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the potential that CDSS could make them lose face. Following a hunch of saving face, physicians are more likely to resist the use of CDSS.

Furthermore, the combination of time pressure and the potential loss of face associated with the use of CDSS could engender stress. Research suggests that time pressure alone does not necessarily lead to stress given that it could even be motivating (Saqib and Chan 2015). However, time pressure could shift one’s focus away from the task at hand and increase anxiety over loss and failure (Essl and Jaussi 2017). It is found that time pressure can amplify other sources of pressure and lead to degraded performance (Bracha and Fershtman 2013). In clinical settings, research shows that time pressure causes stress only when physicians use many EMR features, supporting the role of time pressure as an ampli<sup>fi</sup>er of other pressures (Babbott et al. 2014). As to CDSSs, when physicians are under intensi<sup>fi</sup>ed time pressure, their instinct of avoiding face loss via System 1 processing can be implicitly stimulated which will likely exaggerate the stress of face loss related to CDSS use. To alleviate the intensi-<sup>fi</sup>ed stress, physicians are motivated to avoid the stressor by not using CDSS. This is consistent with Liang and Xue (2009), who articulate that when technology threat causes a stressful situation, a major coping behavior that individuals will undertake is to remove the stressor. Overall, the foregoing discussion suggests that time pressure is likely to strengthen the impact of face loss on resistance to CDSS.

Hypothesis 4a. Time pressure strengthens the positive relationship between face loss and resistance to CDSSs, so that the relationship is stronger when time pressure is higher.

Second, guided by the re<sup>fl</sup>ective mind (System 2), physicians will seek external resources to compensate for the cognitive de<sup>fi</sup>cit because of time pressure. CDSS <sup>fi</sup>ts into the void because it provides instant information (guideline and alerts) or instant feedback based on queries. Prior research suggests that individuals have three major approaches to adapt to timepressed decision situations: acceleration, <sup>fi</sup>ltration, and change of strategies (Payne et al. 1993, Svenson and Maule 1993). First, they can speed up information processing. Second, they can choose to process only the most important information. Finally, they can change their decision strategy from alternative based to attribute based so that less information is processed (Payne et al. 1993). When physicians must make quick diagnostic and treatment decisions within a short time, it is desirable for them to take these adaptive approaches. However, these approaches require sub stantial skills that physicians do not readily possess. They need assistance from CDSSs to take these approaches to cope with time pressure.

A well-designed CDSS could facilitate all three approaches. First, the clinical guidelines and order sets embedded in CDSS can help them accelerate decision speed. Second, CDSS can provide critical information such as drug allergy and drug-drug interactions that could lead to serious harm if ignored. Hence, the more important physicians think the CDSS information is, the less likely they will neglect it if they must use <sup>fi</sup>ltration. Finally, when physicians apply attribute-based decision strategies, they need to compare speci<sup>fi</sup>c features of different decisions. The CDSS allows them to make quick searches and complete the comparison in a timely manner. Therefore, when time is limited, CDSS can streamline physicians’ information processing need for quick decisions. In this scenario, the function of System 2 processing is heightened so that the usefulness perception of CDSS in augmenting physician’s cognitive capability becomes particularly important for physicians and will play a more potent role in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing their behavior. That is, when time pressure is high, if physicians perceive that the CDSS is useful in helping them make better decisions, they are more motivated to use CDSSs. As a result, perceived usefulness tends to have a stronger negative in<sup>fl</sup>uence on CDSS resistance. In contrast, when time pressure is low, physicians can take time to think through the situation at their own pace and their cognitive capacity will not be exceeded. They may not feel the urgency to seek help from the CDSS even if they undertake System 2 processing to perceive CDSSs as useful. Thus, when time pressure is low, the usefulness perception will have a weaker effect on resistance.

Hypothesis 4b. Time pressure strengthens the negative relationship between perceived usefulness and resistance to CDSSs, so that the relationship is stronger when time pressure is higher.

## Method

## Data Collection

We carried out a longitudinal survey study in a large Chinese tertiary hospital. The hospital is recognized as one of China’s top 100 hospitals with 2,623 beds, 2,800 employees, and 429 physicians. The hospital has more than 20 years of IT experience and is a pioneer of using health information technology among Chinese hospitals. Its network infrastructure connects more than 50 functional software systems that involve most hospital units. A CDSS based on big data has been implemented in this hospital and is available to all physicians for voluntary use. The CDSS includes 10 modules such as dosage form, data source category, medication route, use frequency, allergen, operation classi<sup>fi</sup>cation, examination type, examination position, and symptom. The system has a large clinical knowledge base that can be regularly updated by physicians. The CDSS has two levels of major functions. The primary functions include providing real-time relevant clinical information to physicians. For example, when a physician is prescribing a medicine to a patient, all information related to that particular medicine will be displayed on the screen. The advanced functions offer immediate alerts and recommendations by checking the physician’s decision against a consolidation of patients’ personal data and clinical guidelines. The alerts contain suggestions like moderate concern, caution, not recommended, and absolute prohibition. Justi<sup>fi</sup>- cations for the suggestions are provided by the CDSS. The alerts are automatically generated. Physicians can choose to ignore or comply with them. If they comply, they need to read the messages and make changes to their treatment plan or prescription.

We distributed two surveys to physicians in the hospital. The <sup>fi</sup>rst survey was administered one month after the CDSS implementation, and the second survey was six months after the <sup>fi</sup>rst survey. We collected physicians’ perceptions on CDSS usefulness, face loss, and time pressure during the <sup>fi</sup>rst survey. During the second survey, we asked the respondents to rate their IT resistance perception. Both surveys were set up in the hospital’s administration information system. A survey invitation link was displayed on the computer screen once a physician logged onto a workstation. The data collection was endorsed by the hospital management team. The hospital’s IT director announced the study during hospital meetings and encouraged all physicians to participate. We assured the physicians that we will keep their input con <sup>fi</sup>dential and only aggregated data will be analyzed for research purposes.

Among all the 429 physicians in the hospital, 182 completed both surveys, showing a response rate of 42.4%. Among the respondents, 53% are female and 47% have graduate degrees. Their average age is 38 years (SD <sub>-</sub> 7.94), ranging from 22 to 59 years. Physicians’ demographic data were gathered from the hospital’s human resources department by matching their employee IDs in the system.

## Clinical Settings

The physical settings of outpatient encounters in the hospital have four characteristics. First, the doctor and the patient are seated close to each other. Second, the patient is usually seated in a 90-degree angle to the doctor, typically on the near side of the doctor’s desk. Third, the patient is sometimes accompanied by family members, who are allowed to enter the outpatient room to stay with the patient. Finally, when the doctor is using CDSS during the medical encounter, the computer screen is visible to the patient and family members. The alert message is in red font and can be easily distinguished from other information on the screen. Based on the typology of customer contact mode developed by Froehle and Roth (2004), the relative physical locations of the patient, doctor, and computer represent a type of technology-assisted customer contact mode. In our research context, it can be renamed as technology-assisted patient encounter mode. As illustrated in Figure 2, in this mode, the doctor interacts with the patient and CDSS, respectively. Although the patient has no interaction with CDSS, they are physically close, and the patient can notice when the doctor uses CDSS. Being visible to patients and bystanders, it is an existent concern that the doctor’s CDSS use might induce a feeling of face loss. This mode of out patient arrangement is not unique to the investigative hospital. Instead, it is quite common in hospitals across China, which is supported by visual evidence from newspaper, mass media, and social media.

## Measures

Dependent Variable. CDSS resistance is operational ized as the degree to which physicians would not follow CDSS output (recommendations and alerts). We asked the physicians to focus on the advanced functions of CDSS when assessing their resistance behavior. It is necessary to note that for advanced CDSS functions, using CDSS essentially means following CDSS output, because the primary physician-CDSS interaction is that CDSS provides an output message and physicians then decide to reject or comply with it. Therefore, resisting CDSS is semantically equivalent as resisting CDSS output. The three items for CDSS resistance are based on Bhattacherjee and Hikmet (2007). ## Evaluation of Measures

Following Peng and Lai (2012), validity of all the measures was tested using two procedures. First, the square root of each construct’s average variance extracted (AVE) is much greater than the construct’s correlations with all other constructs, suggesting suf<sup>fi</sup>- cient discriminant validity (Table 1). Second, factor loadings and cross loadings were generated by con ducting a principal component analysis (Online Appendix B). All factor loadings on the substantive constructs are more than 0.70, suggesting suf<sup>fi</sup>cient convergent validity. In addition, each item’s factor loading is much higher than its cross-loadings on other constructs, con<sup>fi</sup>rming the suf<sup>fi</sup>ciency of discriminant validity (Hair et al. 1998). We assessed the internal consistency of each construct by examining composite reliability and AVE. As Table 1 shows, all reliability coef<sup>fi</sup>cients exceed Nunnally’s (1978) recommended 0.70, indicating acceptable internal consistency, and all AVEs are above the minimum 0.50 level (Fornell and Larcker 1981).

We <sup>fi</sup>nd support for three of the four moderation effects. Regarding the contextual factors, professional autonomy positively moderates the relationship between face loss and resistance $( \beta = 0 . 2 2 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ , supporting Hypothesis 3a. However, it does not signi<sup>fi</sup>- cantly moderate the relationship between perceived usefulness and resistance $( \beta = - \bar { 0 } . 0 8 , p = 0 . \bar { 2 4 } )$ , failing to support Hypothesis 3b. A plausible reason for the insigni<sup>fi</sup>cance is that when physician have high autonomy, they also have great con<sup>fi</sup>dence in their independent clinical judgment. Although they believe a CDSS recommendation can be useful, they do not necessarily think it is correct. Instead of simply following the recommendations, they tend to compare their own clinical judgment with the CDSS recommendation and decide the next step. As a result, the effect of perceived usefulness on CDSS resistance is not stronger for high-autonomy physicians.

## Follow-up Interviews

To gain more insights into the cognitive processes behind their resistance decisions, by taking an approach similar to Venkatesh et al. (2016), we conducted follow-up interviews with nine physicians from seven different specialty departments at the investigative hospital to complement our survey <sup>fi</sup>ndings. The detailed procedure, protocol, and analysis of the interviews are presented in Online Appendix E.

We <sup>fi</sup>rst explored how the physicians used the CDSS system at work. According to the interviewees, there are two modes of using CDSS. First, the CDSS can automatically generate recommendations for standard clinical pathways or alerts for adverse drug events. This mode does not need the physicians to initiate but requires them to respond by accepting or rejecting the system prompt. As one dermatologist indicated:

“The CDSS system was implemented to serve two main purposes. The first was to help us follow standard clinical pathways to improve quality and reduce costs. The second was to prevent drug-drug and drug-condition interactions. For example, if I forget to order a blood test for a patient with herpes simplex, the system will tell me it’s required based on the clinical guideline. If I prescribe Aciclovir to the patient who has chronic kidney disease, the system will warn me that this drug could lead to renal failure.”

The second mode of CDSS is after the physicians decide to follow the CDSS recommendation or to address the CDSS alert, they can further explore the details of the follow-up action by searching for relevant information from the CDSS system. This mode requires the physicians to proactively interact with the system. As one pediatrician noted:

“The CDSS system shows you an alert but doesn’t tell you how to address it. I once prescribed Ceftriaxone sodium for a newborn baby who had acute pneumonia. The system alerted me that the baby had hyperbilirubinemia and shouldn’t be treated with Ceftriaxone sodium. I had to search the system for other alternative antibiotics and then update my order entry.”

Given the two modes of CDSS use, it is obvious that the physicians need to take more actions after they decide to comply with CDSS. Such compliance implies knowledge insuf<sup>fi</sup>ciency and could be interpreted by the physicians as face loss in front of patients. As one cardiologist admitted:

“Every time I got an alert from the CDSS system, my instant reaction was that this doesn’t reflect well on me. It may expose my knowledge limitation, since the patient may wonder why I change my mind after checking with the computer. They may not ask. But I can feel it’s a bit embarrassing.”

The physicians realized CDSS posed a challenge to their authoritative role of being medical experts. They expressed concerns that CDSS made them appear incompetent and inexperienced. As the dermatologist said:

“You can think of it that way … it [the CDSS system] is indeed a challenge to our authority as doctors. It tells you what you should or should not do. … it makes you look like a greenhorn.”

The physicians’ feedback con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that the physicians’ awareness of being observed contributes to their feeling of face loss. The presence of others creates a mini social context within which the physicians deem it necessary to maintain their public image. As we explained earlier, in the outpatient room in Chinese hospitals, patients can see how physicians use their computers because of their physical proximity. An internist described how he felt:

“In the outpatient room, patients and their family members are in close range to my computer. Sometimes, they are kind of surrounding me. In this situation, it’s a bit tricky when they can see what you are doing with your computer. Seems like I’m learning from the computer on the fly. So, I usually don’t want to check with the [CDSS] system unless it’s absolutely necessary.”

Our interviews reveal that the impact of face loss on CDSS use is a function of the physicians’ professional autonomy. For attending physicians who are highly autonomous, complying with CDSS is more likely to be interpreted as losing face. However, for interns who have limited autonomy, it is more acceptable to comply with the CDSS. As one obstetriciangynecologist intern shared her thoughts:

“For me, it’s not a big deal. I don’t feel any sense of face loss for using CDSS. As an intern, I mainly follow the attending physician who’s my mentor. If I can take advice from him, I can also take advice from the CDSS. I like to take any opportunities to improve myself.”

The physicians reported struggle between comply ing with or ignoring CDSS recommendations. They realized that CDSS compliance could make them lose face, but they also understood that CDSS is intended to help them improve clinical outcomes. As an endocrinologist remarked:

“To be honest, I felt ambivalent when I saw an alert from the system that suggested me to choose a different clinical pathway. As a physician I prefer to have my independent judgment. I have confidence in my treatment plan, you know. I wouldn’t say it’s all about face saving, but it’s more or less related. I guess it’s kind of the pride of being doctors. I don’t think the recommended way from the system is certainly better than mine. But on the other hand, I knew the system could be right. The clinical guidelines are developed for a reason. You have to find a balance between these thoughts.”

The physicians also mentioned that CDSS alerts have different levels of quality based on how relevant they are to the case. The perceived quality affects how they react to the system. For alerts with low quality, they easily ignored them without a struggle. Most struggles took place around high quality alerts. As an internist described:

“Yes, I have seen both high-quality and low-quality CDSS alerts. I often come across false alerts that are not related to the patient. I simply ignore such lowquality alerts because they are not useful … . No. I don’t feel being challenged or embarrassed at all. I’m not obligated to abide by the system. I just click cancel and move on. … Most of the alerts are indeed of high quality. That’s when things get complicated. I know the alert makes sense, but I don’t agree that’s the only correct way. Now if I change my dictation by following the alert, I would feel a little uncomfortable.”

Under high time pressure, the physicians are more likely to associate CDSS use with face loss. It seems that fatigue and negative emotions resulting from the busy schedule play a role in forming this relationship. As the pediatrician complained:

“The workload for doctors is very heavy in my department. Anybody will feel tired and impatient when you have to see so many patients. The CDSS system can be annoying when it showed an alert to tell that you did something wrong. [It asked me] to follow the so-called standard of practice. With all due respect, I’m not crazy about those alerts. I have been a doctor for more than 20 years. I know what I’m doing. … I did feel somewhat offended when the system tried to make me change my medical dictation.”

In contrast, the high time pressure also makes the physicians particularly appreciate the usefulness of CDSS in helping them capture careless errors. As another internist stated:

“Generally, I follow advice from the system. The clin ical guidelines and rules embedded in the CDSS system are validated by medical experts and authorities. I found they are very helpful in my work. Particularly when there are too many patients and too little time, the system can greatly improve patient safety. It’s easy to make a mistake when you have very short time for a patient. I relied on the system to capture my mistakes and it made my job much easier.”

Based on the physicians’ perceptions, CDSS can cause face loss and be useful for clinical tasks, suggesting that the dual systems of information processing are at work. These con<sup>fl</sup>icting perceptions give rise to a dilemma: should the physicians care more about face loss and resist CDSS or should they care more about usefulness and use CDSS? We <sup>fi</sup>nd evidence that System 1 can be intervened by System 2 so that the physicians decided to use CDSS despite their feeling of face loss. The cardiologist explained how he resolved the dilemma:

“As doctors, it’s our responsibility to help patients and save lives. Although CDSS indeed make me appear to lose face in front of patients, I followed the system recommendations most of the time. As long as it tells me to do the right thing, the so-called face is not that important. After all, patient health should be put in the first place.”

We also probed the possible in<sup>fl</sup>uence of legal concerns related to the use or nonuse of CDSS. It turns out that the physicians are indifferent to liability issues because no laws have been developed in China to regulate CDSS use. The physicians recognized that this is an important issue and should be addressed by law makers, but they did not think it was a concern at the moment. As a gastroenterologist noted:

“Hmm … . I have never thought about legal issues related to CDSS. I don’t think there is any medical law in China about this. It’s not mandatory for us to follow every CDSS advice. I don’t think we have legal liabilities for not using CDSS if something happens. It can be controversial if a medical accident takes places after a doctor follows the CDSS recommendation. Then who should be blamed, the doctor or the system? It’s very complicated. I think this is something that the government should address in the future. But at least at present, I don’t worry about such legal issues when using CDSS.”

## Discussion

## Implications for Research

This research makes two major contributions to research on healthcare information systems (HIS) and IT resistance. First, it is one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst attempts to examine the contradictory effects of sociocultural beliefs and cognitive perceptions to understand physicians technology resistance behavior based on the dual process theory. Although some studies have examined how physician resistance to IT is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by personal, technological, cognitive, and organizational factors, they mostly assume physicians’ rationality in making judgements and decisions regarding IT use and few have taken a sociocultural perspective to explain physician behaviors that cannot be attributed to pure rationality (Lapointe and Rivard 2005, Bhattacherjee and Hikmet 2007, Liang et al. 2010). We demonstrate that physicians can perform both System 1 and System 2 processing to decide whether they should accept or reject CDSS recommendations. The two types of information processing put physicians into a dilemma, making it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to decide what to do. Through the lens of dilemma, the opposing impetuses that drive CDSS resistance can be more readily identi-<sup>fi</sup>ed, examined, and understood. A somewhat similar approach is the dual factor model that explains general IT adoption or rejection behavior (Cenfetelli 2004), but it only focuses on the design factors of the IT artifact. Theoretically, the enablers and inhibitors de<sup>fi</sup>ned in the dual factor model all belong to the domain of System 2 processing because they require effortful cognitive evaluation. Our approach advances this literature by incorporating heuristic experiential factors to allow for a more holistic explanation of user behaviors.

By studying Chinese physician’s CDSS resistance, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that perceived usefulness and face loss are a pair of opposing concerns during patient encounters. Although perceived usefulness has been well studied, face loss has received little attention in the HIS and IT resistance literature. It should be noted that face is more than a salient cultural belief in China; it is a general social factor that ubiquitously exists in many other Asian cultures (Kim and Nam 1998) and Western societies and has long received extensive attention from Western academics (Goffman 1978). Although our research is situated in the Chinese context, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are expected to have general implications for explaining physicians’ IT-related behavior in other countries. Given the strong autonomy and pride associated with the medical profession, face is a particularly important concept that can transcend geographical or national boundaries. Our inclusion of face loss based on the dual process theory not only engenders novel insights of physicians’ CDSS resistance but also provides a generic framework within which other System 1 factors can be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed and analyzed to explain users’ IT behavior in general.

Second, we try to build contextualized theory by taking professional autonomy and time pressure into consideration. As Hong et al. (2013) suggest, theories should address the unique features of the context within which the focal phenomena take place. This research answers their call by stressing the moderating roles of professional autonomy and time pressure.

Although professional autonomy has been examined as an antecedent of HIT adoption or resistance, it has rarely been treated as a contextual factor. For example, several studies show that threat to autonomy can reduce physicians’ adoption or increase their resistance of healthcare IT (Walter and Lopez 2008, Xue et al. 2015). As a prominent characteristic of the medical profession, autonomy could play an important role in subtly in<sup>fl</sup>uencing how other factors determine physician behaviors. Our study <sup>fi</sup>lls in this blank by shedding light on autonomy’s moderating effect on physicians’ CDSS resistance behavior. It suggests that professional autonomy could heighten physicians’ activation of System 1 processing to evaluate CDSS which subconsciously sensitizes them to the face loss potential of using CDSS and consequently stimulates resistance.

Similarly, despite the importance of time pressure in healthcare (Babbott et al. 2014), surprisingly no research has investigated how it can affect physicians IT use behavior. We extend the literature by showing that time pressure can strengthen both the negative impact of perceived usefulness and the positive im pact of face loss on CDSS resistance. Hence, we demonstrate that time pressure’s moderating effect is paradoxical: it plays dual roles in both facilitating and reducing resistance through the activation of System 1 and System 2 processing together. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings demonstrate the complexity of human behaviors and help to delineate the boundary conditions of DPT when applied in the context of CDSS resistance, which afford more precise explanation to the research problem. In addition, this research helps to clarify the ambiguous roles of professional autonomy and time pressure. For example, past research suggests that physicians’ reluctance to adopt IT is partially due to their professional autonomy and lack of time (Boonstra and Broekhuis 2010). Our <sup>fi</sup>nding indicates that these factors might have been used as a scapegoat and their role is much more complicated than the literature has suggested.

## Implications for Practice

This research has practical implications for design and implementation of CDSS. Current CDSSs often have poor usability issues, which make it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for physicians to use (Shibl et al. 2013). One consequence of this problem is that physicians’ normal work <sup>fl</sup>ow will be interrupted by the use of the CDSS (Medlock et al. 2019). For example, the automatic alerts and recommendations could pop up any time without physicians’ prompt. This could make physicians feel that the information is forced onto them, which runs against their professional autonomy. Additionally, many alerts generated by CDSSs are irrelevant or false alarms, leading to alert fatigue and reduced perception of CDSS usefulness. Another consequence is that because of poor usability, a physician’s interaction with CDSS can be frustrating and draw attention from observers who then might think the physician is incompetent and cannot make independent decisions without getting advice from the computer. As a result, physicians might feel embarrassed because their expected self-image of being autonomous and competent is inconsistent with the fact they are taking advice from CDSS. To save face, they would choose to resist the use of CDSSs. To address this problem, CDSS vendors should strive to improve the level of CDSS usability so that physicians do not feel the CDSS is intrusive and are willing to carefully consider the CDSS messages. Moreover, given that humans’ tendency to avoid face loss is intrinsic (Goffman 1978), usability improvement cannot fully eliminate its effect on resistance. This requires hospitals and various medical associations to implement behavioral interventions and develop norms that advocate mindful evaluation of face loss in using CDSS. It should be emphasized, especially in Eastern cultures such as China, that using CDSS in making clinical decisions is a responsible act rather than an embarrassing face loss.

From the dual-process perspective, physician’s intuitive process can be monitored and regulated by the re<sup>fl</sup>ective process, which enable them to correct potential biased judgements and decisions (Evans 2008). A meta-analysis of 162 randomized trials reveals that a CDSS is more effective if it requires physicians to provide reasons for overriding advice (Roshanov et al. 2013). This is possibly because when they must justify their behavior, they engage in more conscious rational thinking that may help them realize the irrationality of ignoring relevant CDSS advice and reconsider their decisions. More such type of technological features should be embedded into the CDSS design to motivate physicians’ activation of re<sup>fl</sup>ective thinking and to reduce CDSS resistance. Our research echoes with Yang et al. (2019), who advocate the application of unremarkable AI in designing clinical decision support tools. The central tenet is that CDSSs should naturally <sup>fi</sup>t into physicians’ daily routines and become part of their work. If physicians cannot detect any obtrusiveness when using CDSSs, their System 1 will not be activated, and the concerns of face loss will be mitigated.

## Limitations and Future Research

In this section, we discuss a few limitations and offer suggestions for future research. First, our intention of this study is to build a concise, yet thoughtprovoking, model to explain CDSS resistance. Resistance is a complex phenomenon that could be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by a myriad of factors. Instead of trying to be comprehensive but lose focus, we choose to highlight the contradictory roles played by the dual systems and the paradoxical moderating effects of two contextual factors. We admit that many other factors could in<sup>fl</sup>uence physician resistance. For example, some patient-related characteristics such as disease emergency level and complexity could make physicians more (or less) inclined to use CDSSs. Analyzing the effects of these characteristics requires patient-level data clustered by physicians that are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to collect because of privacy concerns, but the <sup>fi</sup>ndings will provide additional insights.

Second, this study does not explicitly describe CDSS resistance as either a rational or irrational behavior because of its complexities. If the CDSS output message (recommendation or alert) is relevant to the clinical task (e.g., detection of drug-drug interaction), it is irrational for physicians to resist it, particularly in the clinical context in China where patients could see physicians’ computer screen; but if the message is irrelevant (e.g., false alert), resistance would be considered rational. We investigated CDSS resistance at the physician level, which cannot reveal rationality of physicians’ interaction with speci<sup>fi</sup>c CDSS messages. A more granular level of examination regarding how physicians comply with or reject individual CDSS message is necessary to generate more insights. Such granular investigation could determine the rationality of CDSS resistance based on the relevance or quality of the CDSS message, which will shed more light onto this complex phenomenon.

Third, this study was conducted in the setting of a hospital with a speci<sup>fi</sup>c CDSS in China. It is possible that the hospital or the CDSS has some special characteristics that are unknown to us but have caused systematic changes in physicians’ sensitivity to face loss and time pressure. Hence, caution is needed when generalizing the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study into other settings that have different organizational and technological features. In addition, the unique characteristics of China’s culture and medical education might have in<sup>fl</sup>uenced the <sup>fi</sup>ndings. For example, face is deeply connected with Confucianism, which is prevalent in Eastern Asian countries but rare in Western countries. We thus expect consistent <sup>fi</sup>ndings in countries with similar cultural norms as China but anticipate somewhat different <sup>fi</sup>ndings in other parts of the world with different cultures. Moreover, the medical education and training system could also affect the way physicians react to CDSSs. For instance, the medical education and training in the United States is much longer and more challenging than that in China. However, it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to predict whether the longer training time will make American physicians more concerned about face loss or less. It could lead to higher levels of con<sup>fi</sup>dence and pride in physicians and thus a stronger motivation to protect their face, or it could foster a strong sense of professional ethics that overshadows the sel<sup>fi</sup>sh thoughts of face saving during patient visits. The lower time pressure experienced by American physicians during patient visits will add to the uncertainty. In addition, physicians in the United States are among the most respected and highest paid professionals, but physicians in China do not enjoy the same level of respect and could even be victims of patient violence (Hesketh et al. 2012). It would be interesting to make cross-country comparisons to see how our <sup>fi</sup>ndings will vary across countries.

Fourth, another important consideration is legal consequences of medical malpractice that could in<sup>fl</sup>uence physicians’ CDSS resistance behavior. If physicians are legally required to use the CDSS, the fact that patients are observing their interaction with the CDSS might force them to carefully follow CDSS recommendations. However, CDSS use is not mandatory in China given that no laws stipulate whether CDSS resistance constitutes medical malpractice. The National Health Commission of China promulgated a policy in 2018 to regulate EMR issues such as data authenticity, completeness, integrity, and availability, but the policy has no clauses regarding CDSS use.<sup>11</sup> The current national regulation on medical con<sup>fl</sup>icts does not specify whether ignoring CDSS alerts/recommendations should be considered a faulty behavior.<sup>12</sup> Even if a physician is found to be at fault for not following (or following) CDSS in the case of a medical error, the penalty is, according to the regulation, a <sup>fi</sup>ne under 50,000 RMB (about \$7,650). Hence, it is unlikely that Chinese physicians’ CDSS resistance is affected by legal concerns. However, legal consequences for medical malpractice are much more severe in other countries like the United States. For American physicians, legal concerns may dominate face loss when in<sup>fl</sup>uencing CDSS resistance. Future research is needed to <sup>fi</sup>nd out whether face loss can still in<sup>fl</sup>uence CDSS resistance under serious legal concerns in these countries.

Finally, this research cannot assert causal relationships among the variables. We focus on disentangling the nuanced theoretical relationships and must make some sacri<sup>fi</sup>ce on methodological rigor. Although our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are corroborated by follow-up interviews and partially validated by endogeneity tests, the full conditions for causal inference are not completely met. Ideally, a randomized experiment will produce causal inferences. However, it is extremely dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to conduct <sup>fi</sup>eld experiments on physicians and to manipulate their experiential perceptions such as face loss. We call for future research to resolve this methodological challenge.
