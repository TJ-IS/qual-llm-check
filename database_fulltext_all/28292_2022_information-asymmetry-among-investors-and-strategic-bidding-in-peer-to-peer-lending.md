# Information Asymmetry Among Investors and Strategic Bidding in Peer-to-Peer Lending

Kai Lu,<sup>a,</sup>\* Zaiyan Wei,<sup>b</sup> Tat Y. Chan<sup>c</sup>

Abstract. We study how investors in peer-to-peer (P2P) lending use their information advantages in decisions on when to place bids. Literature documents that better-informed bidders may withhold bidding until the last moment (i.e., “sniping”) to avoid competition. We argue that, because a collective effort from investors is required in P2P lending, it could be optimal for informed investors to bid early in projects with a low probability of being funded with the purpose of signaling the quality of these projects. With a unique data set from Prosper.com, we use a matching analysis to show that, for projects with low credit grades, the probability of being successfully funded is positively correlated with early bids from informed investors. For funded loans, informed investors are more likely to bid in the early stage than uninformed investors, whereas uninformed investors will follow and bid at the late stage. Finally, there are more early bids from informed investors for “good” loans than for “bad” loans, indicating that the bids are informative for the quality of those projects. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings have important implications for managing the information asymmetry and strategic behaviors among investors on P2P lending platforms.

## 1. Introduction

Peer-to-peer lending (P2P lending) became a global phenomenon in recent years, and its growth attracts signi<sup>fi</sup>cant interest from institutional and individual investors around the world.<sup>1</sup> Despite their prominence in the “FinTech” (<sup>fi</sup>nancial technologies) era, P2P lending platforms remain a risky investment because of the high default rate of unsecured personal loans funded on such platforms. For example, statistics suggest that the default rate was typically greater than 10% in the early years of P2P lending (Renton 2011). In contrast, the potential rate of return can be much higher than that of other investments if P2P loans are repaid. Several estimates report consistently that the risk-adjusted average return from P2P loans is around 10%, which is higher than the return from other major investments such as stocks, bonds, and real estate (Fast Invest 2017, Galland 2017). Investors of P2P loans are thus in great need of information about borrowers’ ability to repay. P2P platforms typically collect and report detailed credit (and other) information to help address the issue (Lin et al. 2013, Morse 2015, Iyer et al. 2016, Freedman and Jin 2017). Such information, however, may not truly re<sup>fl</sup>ect a borrower’s repayment ability; consequently, some quality loan requests may fail to be funded because their borrowers do not have a high credit rating.

An alternative way for P2P investors to obtain more information is to learn from other investors who may have information advantages (Zhang and Liu 2012). For example, some investors may have private information about borrowers’ backgrounds or can evaluate the potential of the associated projects (Lin and Viswanathan 2016, Freedman and Jin 2017). If informed investors are willing to fund a loan request,<sup>2</sup> other investors are naturally more willing to follow. This type of information spillover from informed investors to uninformed investors, however, will introduce more competition to fund P2P loans, and thus may reduce the anticipated return for informed investors when a project is funded through an auction mechanism. The increased number of bidders can push down the interest rate and therefore reduce the surplus that informed investors can extract from their information advantages. In such cases, as the literature documents, informed investors have the incentive to withhold their information and provide funds only at the last moment (i.e., “sniping”), which under certain conditions is an equilibrium strategy in auctions (Roth and Ockenfels 2002, Bajari and Hortac¸su 2003, Ockenfels and Roth 2006). Such a strategy will hinder the information spillover to uninformed investors, and thus quality borrowers may not receive suf<sup>fi</sup>- cient funds.

This paper studies the bidding strategy of investors with asymmetric information levels on the quality of loan requests on P2P lending platforms. Our focus is on loan requests that have a low ex ante probability of being funded because of the low credit rating of borrowers. Successful funding on P2P lending platforms typically requires a collective effort—the aggregation of money—from multiple investors, as most platforms dictate that the aggregate fund must reach a prespeci-<sup>fi</sup>ed amount (Burtch et al. 2018).<sup>3</sup> If uninformed investors cannot learn from informed investors, those loan requests are likely to fail, generating a type of payoff externality in the sense of Katz and Shapiro (1985). Therefore, for a high-quality project with a low credit rating, informed investors face a tradeoff between helping the project succeed and reducing the return from the investment if it succeeds. This can lead to a new type of optimal bidding strategy: as informed investors need uninformed investors to “co-invest,” they might choose to commit to lending early on, that is, “squatting” (Ely and Hossain 2009), and help generate the information spillover. Given such a tendency, our study differs from the previous research on the bidding strategy in standard eBay-type auctions.

In the paper, we <sup>fi</sup>rst provide a conceptual framework describing how the bidding strategies of informed investors and uninformed investors depend on the public and private information regarding the quality of a project. This allows us to generate several hypotheses that can be tested empirically. Our empirical tests use data from Prosper.com, one of the largest P2P lending platforms in the United States. We use in-group investors<sup>4</sup> as a proxy for informed investors and credit grades as a proxy for the public information available to everyone. The quality of a project is measured by the default status after being funded. Our results lend support to the proposed underlying mechanism. Using a matching approach, we <sup>fi</sup>rst show that for projects with low credit grades, the probability of being successfully funded is positively correlated with early bids from informed investors. We then outline signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different bidding strategies between in-group and out-group investors for funded loans with low credit grades: informed investors are more likely to bid in the early stage than uninformed investors, whereas uninformed investors will follow and bid at the late stage. Finally, there are more early bids from informed investors for “good” loans than for “bad” loans, indicating that the bids are informative for the quality of those projects. The combined results indicate that early bids from informed investors would bene<sup>fi</sup>t uninformed investors as wel as high-quality, low-type borrowers.

After documenting the main results, we follow up by ruling out several alternative explanations, including the altruism and “fake” early bids from in-group investors, which may explain our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. We also <sup>fi</sup>nd evidence inconsistent with informed investors simply utilizing their information advantage to bid early (not necessarily adhering to the strategic considerations in our main arguments). We then perform a falsi<sup>fi</sup>cation test using a different type of “closedwhen-funded” loan to test the proposed underlying mechanism and achieve consistent results. Finally, we conduct a list of robustness checks by varying our measures of key variables and regression speci<sup>fi</sup>cations. Our results remain qualitatively the same.

Our results have implications for the participants on P2P lending platforms. First, the squatting behavior we identify in our empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings bene<sup>fi</sup>ts high-quality borrowers with low credit ratings, who otherwise may not be able to raise funds from P2P lending platforms. Because these are two-sided markets, the participation of both investors and borrowers is vital for the longterm sustainability of P2P lending platforms. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings also imply that the squatting behavior bene<sup>fi</sup>ts uninformed investors, as they can infer the quality of loans from informed investors. This is important for P2P lending platforms because most investors are not endowed with much information and are not as experienced as their institutional counterparts (Lin et al. 2021). Last, by bidding early in certain types of loans, informed investors bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the funding of those projects, which otherwise would not succeed without the coinvestment of uninformed investors.

For P2P lending platforms, our results have implications for how they may manage the information asymmetry and strategic behaviors of investors. For example, both Prosper and LendingClub (the two leading P2P platforms in the United States) adopted a new rule that allows for “partial funding,” under which a project may be funded as long as it collects 60% or 70% of the funding requested by the borrower. Our study suggests that partial funding may reduce the incentive of early bidding and thus may reduce the information spillover. This may lead to unintended negative consequences for both investors and borrowers on P2P lending platforms.

Prosper switched from auctions to a <sup>fi</sup>xed-rate mechanism after our study period. Under the new mechanism, the platform assigns the interest rate for each loan request (Wei and Lin 2017). However, the auction mechanism is still used by many other P2P lending platforms. Examples include Monestro, Thin-Cats, Funding Knight, and Flender. Other types of crowdfunding platforms, such as an equity crowdfunding platform Innovestment, operate under a similar auction mechanism. Fu et al. (2021) argue that the “crowd-based” auction mechanism (using the “wisdom of the crowd” to identify interest rates) has certain advantages over other pricing mechanisms that rely on algorithms. Therefore, understanding investors’ bidding strategy under the auction mechanism still has relevant substantive implications. More broadly, the literature documents herding behavior in P2P lending and other contexts. Our study provides an underlying mechanism that can explain why bidding early, that is, squatting, is particularly important for borrowers with low credit ratings. Finally, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are useful to understand, in an auction setting, how bidders bid strategically when the payoff relies on collective efforts. Examples of such auctions are abundant, including initial public offerings using Dutch auctions (where potential investors enter bids for the number of shares they wish to purchase and the price they are willing to pay) and the Dutch wholesale <sup>fl</sup>ower market (Lu et al. 2016).

## 2. Literature and Contributions

Our study naturally relates to the literature on P2P lending.<sup>5</sup> A set of previous papers in this literature has explored the mitigation of information asymmetry between borrowers and investors, which can lead to adverse selection and moral hazard, with a variety of mechanisms including signaling, reputation, and providing “soft” information (Iyer et al. 2016, Jiang et al. 2020, Xin 2020, Kawai et al. 2021, among others). Our focus, in contrast, is the asymmetry in information between informed and uninformed investors. Most notably, Kawai et al. (2021) also use data from Prosper.com and estimate that 16% of the loss in total surplus can be attributed to adverse selection and that signaling recovers as much as 95% of the loss. The speci<sup>fi</sup>c signal they focus on is the maximum interest rate a borrower sets at the beginning of an auction. Using the same data set, Xin (2020) further separates the welfare loss and estimates that 22% of the loss is because of adverse selection, with moral hazard accounting for the balance. By abstracting away from speci<sup>fi</sup>c signals, Xin (2020) argues that higher interest rates may encourage less creditworthy borrowers to enter the market (adverse selection) and increase their incentive to default (moral hazard). We add to this stream of literature by studying information asymmetry on the investor side of the market and empirically show that investors strategically utilize information advantages in the funding process. We af<sup>fi</sup>rm that the squatting strategy can create information spillover to uninformed investors and thus alleviate the problems caused by information asymmetry (between informed and uninformed investors).

Another strand in the P2P lending literature focuses on the impact of social networks (or social groups) on funding outcomes and transaction ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency (Lin et al. 2013, Freedman and Jin 2017, Hildebrand et al. 2017). This literature documents how borrowers with social ties have a higher chance of being funded and pay at lower interest rates conditional on being funded (Lin et al. 2013, Freedman and Jin 2017). Hildebrand et al. (2017) further demonstrate that these “socialized” loans (in their case, loans with the endorsement of group leaders) have higher default rates. We borrow from the literature to investigate the strategic squatting behaviors of in-group investors who may have information advantages (Lin et al. 2013). Compared with the existing studies that primarily focus on the impact on funding and repayment outcomes, our study extends our understanding of social ties in P2P lending to the funding process by relying on our unique investment-level data. In addition, our results point to a potential mechanism explaining in-group investors’ strategy of bidding early.

Our study adds to the literature on Internet auctions, which has documented the phenomenon of sniping in particular and offers a variety of explanations, both theoretical and empirical.<sup>6</sup> For example, Roth and Ockenfels (2002) show that late bidding is an equilibrium strategy that softens the competition conditional on the “hard ending” auction rule. These authors argue that late bidding is the best response of sophisticated bidders to the existence of naïve bidders. Bajari and Hortac¸su (2003) show that last-minute bidding occurs in models of online auctions with a common value.

More recent studies on online auctions started exploring the opposite of sniping—early bidding or “squatting.” In a study closely related to this paper, Ely and Hossain (2009) conduct a <sup>fi</sup>eld experiment on eBay and show that the squatting strategy may deter the entry of rival bidders. In another study, Groenwegen (2017) uses bid-level data from eBay and <sup>fi</sup>nds inconclusive results about the bene<sup>fi</sup>t of squatting in auctions. We differ from previous studies on squatting in a fundamental way. In a typical eBay auction (that the literature has focused on), the associated product is sold to one single buyer. In such auctions, it has been shown that buyers adopt the squatting strategy to signal competition and deter the entry of rival buyers (Ely and Hossain 2009). As opposed to previous studies, P2P lending auctions (i.e., our research context) require a collective effort from multiple investors to successfully fund a loan request. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that in such settings, informed in vestors have the incentive to bid early to signal the quality of the loans and, therefore, bene<sup>fi</sup>t (low-type) borrowers and uninformed investors.

Last but not least, this paper is related to the information spillover from informed investors to uninformed investors. The literature on herding has documented the information spillover in various settings (Nofsinger and Sias 1999, Simonsohn and Ariely 2008, Duan et al. 2009). Herzenstein et al. (2011a) and Zhang and Liu (2012) study herding and observational learning in P2P lending. In particular, the latter paper documents the rational herding behavior in the Prosper marketplace, that is, uninformed investors follow informed investors as the best response when the market is at equilibrium. We argue that for loans with low credit grades, uninformed lenders will infer the quality from informed investors’ bidding decisions. This is consistent with the main conclusion of Zhang and Liu (2012) that herding is more pronounced among loans with low credit grades. Our paper differs from theirs in its focus on showing how, under certain conditions, facilitating the information spillover by bidding early is an optimal strategy for informed investors. In other words, our results demonstrate the mechanism of the squatting strategy. We also show that this squatting strategy (i.e., bidding early) is more prevalent in low-credit-grade projects and in good loans (than bad loans). In another study, Xiao et al. (2021) show that in reward-based crowdfunding platforms, backers are more likely to contribute in both the early and late stages of the fundraising period. They focus on describing the phenomenon, whereas our paper provides a mechanism that explains an informed investor’s squatting behavior. Furthermore, they study reward-based crowdfunding platforms, which have different funding mechanisms compared with P2P lending platforms that use auctions.

## 3. Hypotheses

In this section, we describe a conceptual framework that helps us generate hypotheses to be tested empirically. The funding is conducted through a Dutch auction mechanism, as adopted by Prosper. If the aggregate fund that investors commit at the end does not reach the amount requested by the borrower, the project will fail. Investors can choose to bid in the early or late stage of a funding process. If some investors bid early, others will observe their decisions and can bid late.

A crowdfunding project becomes a loan if it is successfully funded. A good loan will be fully repaid, and investors will enjoy a positive return on their investment, whereas a bad loan will default, and investors will incur a loss. A public signal indicating whether a project is low or high type is observed by everyone.<sup>7</sup> The chance is higher for a high-type project to be good compared with a low-type project. Informed investors, in addition to the public signal, also observe a private signal. A positive private signal indicates that the project is likely to be good and the ex ante expected return of the project is positive (even though the project is low type). However, the signal is not perfect, as it consists of a “noise.” Uninformed investors have access to public information only. As there are only a few informed investors,<sup>8</sup> a project can be successfully funded only if a suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently large number of uninformed investors bid.<sup>9</sup> The interest rate (and thus the return for investors) will decrease with more bids for the project.

When deciding the timing (bidding in the early or late stage), informed investors face a tradeoff between increasing the probability of successful funding and reducing the return from the investment, that is, the interest rate. Without knowing the private signals, uninformed investors rely on public information. When the ex ante expected return is positive based on the public information, uninformed investors are likely to bid early even when informed investors do not bid early. If enough of them do so, the optimal strategy for informed investors with a positive signal is to bid late—sniping. The reason is that if a suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently large number of uninformed investors bid, the tradeoff that informed investors face will favor the cost side (reducing the return). Hence, they will bid late to avoid competition with uninformed investors. The literature on online auctions has studied this sniping strategy (Ockenfels and Roth 2006), which is also likely in our context.

Compared with the literature, the focus of this study is on informed and uninformed investors’ bidding strategies in a market with a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant proportion of low-type projects. Some of these projects, however, have positive private signals. Without knowing the private signal, the expected return for a low-type project is likely negative. Therefore, few uninformed investors will bid for low-type projects unless they can learn from informed investors (that the project has a positive signal). If a low-type project has a positive signal, informed investors will bid early, and uninformed investors will bid late.<sup>10</sup> The rationale is as follows: <sup>fi</sup>rst, suppose uninformed investors do not bid early, but if they observe informed investors bidding early, they will bid late. In this case, the optimal strategy for informed investors should be to bid early if the private signal is positive. Doing so, the project will be funded as uninformed investors also bid, and thus the payoff for informed investors is positive; otherwise, the project fails, and the payoff will be zero. If informed investors use this strategy, the optimal strategy for uninformed investors is not to bid early and instead to bid late if informed investors bid early. This is because early bids from informed investors indicate the positive (private) signal.<sup>11</sup>

There are several important implications from this simple framework that have not been explored in the previous literature on P2P lending. For low-type crowdfunding projects, early bidding from informed investors creates an information spillover that solves the information asymmetry problem. This mechanism explains why uninformed investors follow the early bidding decisions of informed investors—a “herding” phenomenon documented in the literature (Zhang and Liu 2012). Our framework predicts that the strategic behavior would bene<sup>fi</sup>t not only informed investors but also uninformed investors. The behavior also bene<sup>fi</sup>ts creditworthy borrowers because otherwise their projects may not be suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently funded. As more good projects are funded, it will bene<sup>fi</sup>t P2P platforms because attracting investors to invest and creditworthy borrowers to borrow is important for the business.

The conceptual framework has abstracted away from many other factors. In reality, investors may have heterogeneous beliefs regarding the default rate of a loan. There can be many nonstrategic investors whose bidding decisions are not rational. However, the framework generates hypotheses we can test using data from Prosper. Our hypotheses focus on the differences in bidding strategies of informed and uninformed investors for low-type projects. Furthermore, because our data can only differentiate good ones from bad ones for projects that are funded, our hypotheses will focus on funded loans.

Our <sup>fi</sup>rst hypothesis states that, as the funding probability for low-type projects is low, if they are successfully funded, we should see more early bids from informed investors. It implies the importance of early bids from informed investors for funding success in general.

Hypothesis 1. For low-type projects, informed investors are more likely to bid early for funded projects than nonfunded projects that fail to achieve sufficient funding.

The following hypotheses address the difference in the bidding strategies between informed and uninformed investors in the early stage and in the late stage of a funding process. Our conceptual framework predicts that for good, low-type loans, we will observe informed bidders bidding early and uninformed investors following by bidding in the late stage. This indicates an information spillover or herding phenomenon, which is the focus of this study.

Hypothesis 2a. In the early stage of a funding process, informed investors are more likely to bid than uninformed investors for low-type and good projects that are eventually funded.

Hypothesis 2b. In the late stage of a funding process, uninformed investors are more likely to bid than informed investors for low-type and good projects that are eventually funded. 12

Finally, if early bids from informed investors are informative, we predict that they are more likely to be bids for good loans.<sup>13</sup> Combining predictions from the previous hypotheses implies that early bids from informed investors will bene<sup>fi</sup>t uninformed investors as well as high-quality, low-type borrowers.

Hypothesis 3. For low-type projects that are successfully funded, informed investors are more likely to bid early for good projects than for bad ones.

## 4. Empirical Context and Data

To raise funds at Prosper.com, a borrower will post a crowdfunding project on the website. The borrower must choose the requested amount (between \$1,000 and \$25,000), the term (or the maturity of typically 12, 36, or 60 months), and the reserve interest rate that is the maximum s/he is willing to pay (capped at 35%). In addition to the basic loan characteristics, some credit information about the borrower is displayed on the project page. Some of the information is selfreported, such as monthly income and employment status, whereas other information is obtained by the platform from a credit report bureau along with the borrower’s current FICO (Fair Isaac Corporation) score.<sup>14</sup> Prosper, based on the borrower’s credit score, assigns one of seven grades (AA, A, B, C, D, E, or HR) for the loan. Borrowers of AA projects typically have the highest credit scores. In contrast, HR refers to high risk, as borrowers usually have the lowest credit scores. All the information mentioned previously is available to all Prosper investors.

During our study period from November 2005 to October 2008,<sup>15</sup> Prosper applied a reverse Dutch auction as its funding mechanism and was often considered the “eBay for P2P lending” (see Chen et al. 2014 and Wei and Lin 2017 for more details about Prosper auctions). Potential investors can submit bids during the period the auction process is open. A typical auction lasts 7 or 14 days. Investors can submit bids at any time when the auction is open. In their bids, investors must choose an amount between \$25 and the total amount requested by the borrower. A bid should specify an interest rate, which cannot be higher than the borrower’s reserve interest rate. Winners of an auction are the investors who bid the lowest interest rates, with the aggregate amount supplied by these winners to cover the amount speci<sup>fi</sup>ed by the borrower. After the auction process ends, the interest rate at which the borrower repays the loan will be the lowest interest rate submitted by the losing bidders.<sup>16</sup> Then, the crowdfunding project will become a fully amortized personal loan (unsecured, however, by any personal assets). Investors’ yield equals the contract interest rate (at which the borrower repays) minus a 1%–5% fee charged by the platform. The yield is the same for all investors. 17

The success of funding typically requires collective commitments from many investors. For example, in our sample, the average dollar amount that an investor commits is about \$80, which is notably below the amount requested by the borrower—with an average of \$7,600. Thus, the number of investors needed for a loan request to be funded is large: the average number of investors for funded loans in our sample is 141. A loan request would fail if the total amount committed by investors does not reach the requested amount. As such, the borrower cannot receive any funds, and the committed investors cannot enjoy the promised return.

The reason that our sample period was more than 10 years ago is that only during Prosper’s early period was the auction mechanism used. Although the auction mechanism is not popular these days, many P2P lending platforms use auctions as their primary funding mechanism. Examples are Monestro, ThinCats, Funding Knight, and Flender. Fu and colleagues (2021) argue that the crowd-based auction mechanism (using the wisdom of the crowd to identify interest rates) has advantages over other pricing mechanisms that rely on algorithms.

Prosper borrowers and investors can form groups on the platform, and a wide range has arisen. For example, there are alumni groups of borrowers and investors who attended the same college. There are also geography-based groups formed by individuals from the same state, city, or community. A Prosper member, borrower or investor, can join at most one group on the platform. When a group member posts a loan request, the group leader and other group members can submit bids alongside a notable endorsement (see Freedman and Jin (2017) for a more complete description of the group feature and its evolution over time). Investors from the same group arguably have advantages over other investors in terms of the information about the borrower. This is because, <sup>fi</sup>rst, being in the same group suggests that these investors have common experience, interests, or background so that they are naturally endowed with more information about the borrower. Second, group members may interact with each other outside the platform (Freedman and Jin 2017). In particular, in-group investors can gather more credit information from the borrower by emails, interviews, or even on-site visits (Renton 2011).

## 4.1. Data and Some Summary Statistics

We obtained data from Prosper’s data portal. Our sample contains all personal loan requests posted between the platform’s inception in 2005 and October 2008. For each loan request, we observe the characteristics of the borrower and the associated loan that are publicly posted and known by potential investors. A separate data <sup>fi</sup>le records all winning and losing bids.

We observe all bids submitted during the auction process. This allows us to identify the timing of bids from in-group investors and out-group investors.

For each funded loan, we observe the contract interest rate. The funded amount of dollars is the same as the requested amount.<sup>18</sup> We also observe the <sup>fi</sup>nal payment outcome, as the loans in our sample have all matured by 2017; therefore, we can identify whethe they are eventually paid off or defaulted.

Table 1 reports the summary statistics of all variables used in our empirical analysis. Panel A summarizes the variables for all the 107,778 posted projects. In our sample, about 28% of the projects are initiated by a group borrower. Among these projects, only 21,211 (roughly 20%) are successfully funded, indicating a high chance that the crowdfunding effort fails. The median interest rate at which these loans are funded is 15.42%, and half of these loans are funded with more than \$5,000 (see Panel B). Overall, compared with all projects, funded loans have lower interest rates, smaller amounts of funds requested, higher fractions of homeownership, higher fractions of group loans, and, more importantly, higher fractions of investors who bid in the <sup>fi</sup>rst 5% of the funding duration (see “% investors\_early5%,” a variable we use as a measure for early bidding).<sup>19</sup> Panel C further summarizes the group loans. Group loans are similar to other loans for most of the variables. One difference is that group loans attract a smaller fraction of investors who bid in the <sup>fi</sup>rst 5% of the funding procedure (10.6% versus 13.4%, see rows for “% investors\_early5%”).

We further compare some of the variables for funded loans with different credit grades in Table 2. Panel A summarizes all loans, and Panel B summarizes only group loans. The table clearly indicates that credit grades are a good predictor of loan payment outcomes. For example, more than 80% of AA loans are paid off in full, whereas about 40% of HR-grade loans default. The contract interest rate also increases with the deterioration of credit grades. Finally, the fraction of bids that come in the <sup>fi</sup>rst 5% of the funding duration decreases from AA to HR credit grades. However, compared with all funded loans, early bids are more common for group loans that have E and HR credit grades.

## 5.1. Proxy Measures Used for Hypotheses Testing

Our hypotheses state that the bidding time of informed investors depends on the types of crowdfunding projects, which are determined by the public information (high versus low) and private information (positive versus negative). We use proxy measures constructed from the data to represent the types of projects and investors. This section <sup>fi</sup>rst explains the measures and shows how they are consistent with our predictions. We then illustrate some data patterns that are consistent with the tradeoff between early bidding and the contract interest rate for investors.

5.1.1. Using Credit Grades as the Proxy for Public Types. We use the credit grade Prosper assigns to each project as a proxy for the public information that determines whether the project is high or low type. This information is available to all investors before they make bidding decisions. Although other information is also available, the credit grade is typically displayed at the most prominent place when investors check for the detailed information (see Figure A1 in the online appendix). Table 2 shows the strong correlation between the credit grade of a loan and whether it will be fully repaid. Figure 1 further shows that the fraction of loans funded is monotonically decreasing from the AA to HR grade. More than 35% of AA projects in our sample were successfully funded, whereas in sharp contrast, only about 5% of HR projects were funded. In the regressions we will report in the next sections, we use the HR grade as the proxy for lowtype projects. As a robustness check, we also include D and E credit grades together with the HR grade to represent the low type.

5.1.2. Using In-Group Investors as the Proxy for Informed Investors. We use investors who are in the same group as the borrower to proxy for informed investors for the project. Testing the assumption that those investors are more informed is not straightforward. Suppose we <sup>fi</sup>nd from data that in-group investors on average invest more on good loans than others. It could indicate that these investors are more experienced than the others, not necessarily that they are more informed about the projects they invest in. Therefore, we use an indirect test of the decisions of investors when facing borrowers from the same group and out of the group. We examine all investors who belong to a group and have invested in loans from both in-group and out-group borrowers. The test is based on different types of loans for the same investor; as such, the outcome is not due to the difference in experience between investors.

One important assumption in our proposed underlying mechanism is that uninformed investors follow the early bids from informed investors (so that informed investors can strategically bid early to generate the information spillover). First, the anecdotal evidence that lends support to using in-group investors as a proxy for informed investors is that, for group loans, group leaders can endorse an in-group project. $$

$$

It is worth noting that to account for the “herding” behavior of uninformed investors, we include the total number of investors who place bids in the early 5% of the funding duration (they may bid again later if they are outbid by others) as an additional control variable, that is, # investors\_early5%. 5.1.3. Tradeoff for Early Bidding. Our hypotheses rely on the assumption that in informed investors’ decision of when to make bids, there is a tradeoff between whether projects can be funded at all and the contract interest rates. In the next subsection, we show evidence that early bids are positively correlated with the probability of being funded. To test whether early bids are correlated with lower interest rates based on which investors will be paid, we run a linear regression with the interest rate of a loan as the dependent variable and # investors\_early5% as the key covariate. Because early bids have a stronger impact on lowtype loans, in one regression we further interact # investors\_early5% with an indicator that the loan belongs to the HR credit grade, 1(Credit Grade HR), to capture the additional effect. We also add a list of loan and borrower characteristics to control for the observed heterogeneity among loans. The main regression equation is thus formulated as

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \text {Contract interest rate} _ {i} & = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \cdot \# \text {investors\_early5\%} _ {i} \\ & \quad + \tau X _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i}. \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

Table 5 presents the results from the linear regression of the contract interest rate. The coef<sup>fi</sup>cients for the variable # investors\_early5% are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly negative in both speci<sup>fi</sup>cations. The coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of the interaction term in the second regression is also signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly negative, suggesting that an additional early bid decreases the contract interest rate for HR-grade loans by 0.004. As the average contract interest rate for HR-grade loans in our sample is 0.2484 (or 24.84%), the estimate implies that the additional early bid will decrease the interest rate by about 2%.

Although the magnitude of the decrease may seem small, the true effect should be substantively larger. The reason is that, on the one hand, early bids decrease the interest rate; on the other hand, early bids increase the funding probability of loan requests that otherwise would not be funded (shown in the next sub section). These loans typically require a higher interest rate. The coef<sup>fi</sup>cient represents the combined effect. The gests that the economic impact can be much stronger.

## 5.2. Early Bids from Informed Investors and the Probability of Being Funded

Before we test the hypotheses developed in Section 3, we <sup>fi</sup>rst investigate whether the bidding behaviors of informed investors across credit grades of projects in our data are consistent with the sniping phenomenon documented in the auction literature. We compare the bidding time of in-group investors who have invested in a loan. The results are reported in Table 6. The fraction of their bids in the late 5% (or late 10%) of the funding process is 22% (or 30%) higher than the fraction of their bids in the early 5% (or early 10%) of the funding process. The differences are statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at 1%.

Although informed investors are more likely to bid late with the purpose of holding information, Hypothesis 1 predicts that for low-type funded projects, we expect to observe more early bids from those investors (than nonfunded projects). We test this prediction by comparing their early bids for funded projects with a low credit grade and nonfunded projects with the same credit grade. Since funded projects and the nonfunded can be systematically different in many aspects, including the availability of other projects competing for the funding, we use a matching approach to pin down the effect of early bids. In this set of analyses, we focus on low-type projects for which the borrowers belong to a group. Governed by these requirements, any matched pair of funded and nonfunded projects belonged to the same credit grade and were both group loans (i.e., the borrowers of the projects belong to a group). Starting with the lowtype group projects in our data, our matching method (one-to-one matching) satis<sup>fi</sup>es additional criteria:

(i) The matched nonfunded project was listed during the funding duration of the funded project; and

(ii) The matched nonfunded project is similar to the funded project in other observed characteristics including interest rate, amount requested, homeowner status, and fulltime job status.

Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we adopt a propensity-score matching method. To ensure criterion (i), we separate the sample of projects into subsamples by the week in which a pro ject was posted on Prosper.com and repeat the same pro pensity score matching (PSM) process for each week. Because a project’s funding window (7 or 14 days on Prosper) is at least one week, this strategy guarantees an overlap in funding window for each matched pair of funded and nonfunded projects. In each PSM process, using a week of loans during our study window, we start with a logit regression of the funding status (i.e., indicator for a project being funded) on the observed characteristics speci<sup>fi</sup>ed in criterion (ii)<sup>21</sup> and calculate the predicted probability of being funded, that is, the propensity score. Then, for each funded project, we match it with another nonfunded project with a similar propensi ty score. To ensure the balance, the matching of nonfunded project is without replacement, that is, once a nonfunded project is picked, it will not match with another funded project again. If a funded project cannot <sup>fi</sup>nd a match, it will not be used in the matching analysis.

We use the number of bids from in-group investors in the <sup>fi</sup>rst 5% or 10% of the funding duration (# in-group investors\_early5% (10%)) to measure early bids.<sup>22</sup> We <sup>fi</sup>rst use the HR grade to represent low-type projects. The results are reported in Panel A of Table 7. For funded projects, the number of in-group investors who place bids in the <sup>fi</sup>rst 5% of the funding duration on average is about 8 times higher than that for nonfunded projects, and it is about 8.6 times higher in the <sup>fi</sup>rst 10% of the funding duration (than nonfunded projects). The differences are statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at 1%.

## 5.5. Alternative Explanations

5.5.1. Altruism. The empirical evidence suggests that in-group investors bid early for low-type loans. One may argue that this is because of altruism, as investors may feel obliged to help out borrowers who belong to the same group, especially when the funding probability of these projects is low.

We do not rule out that in-group investors may bid for altruistic reasons. Indeed, the proportion of in-group investors who bid for in-group loan requests is 2.85%, far higher than the proportion of out-group investors (0.49%). If altruism is the only driving force, we would expect that in-group investors are also more likely to bid for projects that still have not received suf<sup>fi</sup>cient funds toward the end of the funding process. We test this argument by running regressions based on the following equation:

$$
\begin{array}{l} Y _ {i} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \cdot 1 (\text { Unfunded by } 95 \%) _ {i} + \beta_ {2} \cdot 1 (" G o o d" l o a n) _ {i} \\ \quad + \tau X _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i}. \end{array} \tag{6}
$$

We run separate regressions for in-group and outgroup investors, using the sample of all funded group loans. The dependent variables are the proportion of in-group investors who bid in the last 5% of funding time among all available investors in the same group by the end of the corresponding auction (Proportion\_in-group investors\_late5%) and a similar proportion of out-group investors (Proportion\_out-group investors\_late5%). We construct a dummy variable, 1 Unfunded by 95%  , to indicate that a project has not received full funding by the <sup>fi</sup>nal 5% of the funding time. Table 15 presents the results. The <sup>fi</sup>rst row shows that the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient for 1 Unfunded by 95%  is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly negative among in-group investors at 10% but not signi<sup>fi</sup>cant among out-group investors. The coef<sup>fi</sup>cient for 1 “Good” loan , however, is signi<sup>fi</sup>- cantly positive and much larger than that for outgroup investors. These results cast doubt on the argument that our <sup>fi</sup>nding is driven by altruism only.

5.5.2. “Fake” Early Bids. Squatting may also be a strategic move from in-group investors. They may place a bid early to stimulate herding from other investors. These investors, however, will not come back after they are outbid; as such, they bear little cost from “faking” early bids. Given there has been a number of studies on the herding behavior of investors on P2P lending platforms, studying whether early bidding from in-group investors is genuine is important to the literature.

We empirically test to what extent squatting is genuine. We calculate the fraction of in-group bidders who bid again for the same listing after their <sup>fi</sup>rst bids in the <sup>fi</sup>rst 5% (10%) of the funding duration were outbid. The fraction is 73.22% (72.77%). Fake bidders (those who do not bid again for the same loan after their <sup>fi</sup>rst bid in the <sup>fi</sup>rst 5% of funding duration was outbid) make up only 26.78% (27.23%). This is an upper bound of the fraction of fake bidders. These bidders may still be genuine in their early bids; they may choose not to bid again simply because the interest rate has been pushed too low. Based on the results, we argue that most of the early bids from in-group investors are genuine.

5.5.3. Off-Platform Communications and the Exploration of Information Asymmetry. There may still be other reasons that in-group investors behave differently from out-group investors. One is that groups may communicate via off-platform channels, as such members becoming aware of in-group loans sooner than other users. They may even be noticed ahead of the fundraising period. This can explain why in-group investors bid earlier than out-group investors.

First, although in-group investors are informed early, they do not necessarily bid early. Sniping can be a better strategy for informed investors. Furthermore, as con<sup>fi</sup>rmed in Section 5.2 (Table 6), informed investors are more likely to bid late rather than bid early in general. We show that informed investors are more likely to bid early for loans with low credit grades.

## 5.6. Falsification Test

During our study window (2005–2008), Prosper allows borrowers to choose the funding mechanism of opening for the entire duration (open-for-duration) or closing immediately if the project has been suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently funded (closed-when-funded). The former helps to push down the interest rate if there are many investors, whereas the latter allows borrowers to get loans as quickly as possible. Our empirical analyses have focused only on the former type of projects. The latter type, however, provides a falsi<sup>fi</sup>cation test for the underlying mechanism we propose: investors in closed-when-funded auctions cannot bid down the interest rate since it is <sup>fi</sup>xed. In other words, as we argued earlier, investors evaluate the tradeoff between enhancing the success probability of a project and reducing the contract interest rate in their bidding decision. When the latter as a cost for early bidding is removed in closed-when-funded projects, the tradeoff should not exist, and sniping should no longer be opti mal for investors. Consequently, we should observe more early bids for closed-when-funded projects than for open-for-duration projects. To test this prediction, we further collect data on closed-when-funded projects and compare the bidding time of the two types of projects. Figure 2 illustrates the distribution of the timing of bids.

We normalize the funding duration to be between zero (starting time) and one (ending time) in the <sup>fi</sup>gure. For each loan and each 5% bin of the funding duration, we calculate the fraction of investors who placed bids during the corresponding period (relative to all participating investors in the current auction). The <sup>fi</sup>gure demonstrates a clear difference. For closedwhen-funded projects, the fraction of bids in the <sup>fi</sup>rst 5% of the funding process is about 25%, far higher than that for open-for-duration projects (about 13%). Furthermore, there is no sniping for closed-whenfunded projects. In contrast, more than 25% of investors come in the last 5% of the bidding process for open-for-duration projects. These results are therefore consistent with our predictions, lending further support to our proposed mechanism.

## 5.7. Robustness Checks

5.7.1. Alternative Measure for Good Loans. We use the default status of a loan to indicate whether the loan is good or bad. One may argue that investors are maximizing the expected return rather than minimizing the default rate. Thus, as a robustness check, we use the rate of return from investment as an alternative measure.<sup>25</sup> Following the method of Lin et al. (2021), for each nondefaulted loan, we use the contract interest rate minus 1% service fee charged by Prosper to measure the return of the loan. For a defaulted loan, we <sup>fi</sup>rst use the contract interest rate, amount requested, and loan term to calculate the required monthly payment. Then, we calculate the fraction of each monthly payment that repays the principal. Next, from the total principal repaid reported by Prosper, we infer the number of months before the borrower stopping payments. Last, we compute the return of the defaulted loans based on the inferred cash <sup>fl</sup>ow.<sup>26</sup> We de<sup>fi</sup>ne a loan as good if it has a positive rate of return and bad if the rate of return is negative.

We repeat the previous analyses using the new measure. We <sup>fi</sup>rst check whether in-group investors are better informed. To save space, we report the results in Table A6 in the online appendix. They are qualitatively similar to the results in Table 3. We then test the ro bustness of our hypotheses with this alternative measure. Tables A6–A12 in the online appendix report the results. Again, the results are qualitatively similar to the results in Tables 3 and 9–14. The results con<sup>fi</sup>rm the robustness of our <sup>fi</sup>nding that in-group investors are more likely to bid early for good loans with low credit grades than are uninformed investors.

5.7.2. Additional Fixed Effects and Control Variables. In our previous analyses, the lending decisions on each P2P project are treated as independent. One may argue that in an online setting where investors can browse all projects and participate over time, investors can compare or communicate over various projects; as such, their investment decisions among projects are not assuredly independent. It is possible that investors may gradually learn how to make wise investment decisions. Or there might be seasonality in the number of participants in the P2P lending platform.

To address this concern, we add year-month <sup>fi</sup>xed effects in regressions. The <sup>fi</sup>xed effects control for the possibility that in some year-months, there may be more listings, and thus investors’ bidding strategies may change. We further include additional variables to control for the potential interdependence among projects. These additional control variables include the following: Average borrower maximum rate, which is the average borrower maximum rate for all other projects that are listed at the start date of a listing; Average amount requested (in \$1,000), which is the average amount requested by borrowers of all other projects that are listed at the start date of a listing; Total number of top/middle/bottom grade loans, which is the total number of the same type or other types of projects that are listed at the start date of a listing (top grade includes grades AA and A, middle grade includes grades B and C, bottom grade includes grades D, E, and HR).

Results are reported in Tables A13–A18 in the online appendix They are all qualitatively similar to the results in Tables 4, 5, 8, 10, 12, and 14.

5.7.3. Alternative Dependent Variables. For many of the regressions, the value of the dependent variable ranges between zero and one. A concern may arise that linear regressions may not be a good speci<sup>fi</sup>cation that captures the correlation between the dependent variable and covariates. We therefore repeat our regressions using the log transformation of the dependent variable. Let y be the original dependent variable; the new dependent variable in this exercise is calculated as ln(y/(1 y)). The results are reported in Tables A19–A21 in the online appendix, which are again qualitatively similar to the results in Tables 10, 12, and 14.

5.7.4. Alternative Sample Period. To address the concern that investors may gradually learn how to make wise investment decisions and hence investors’ behaviors may evolve over time, we repeat our main analysis by removing observations from the <sup>fi</sup>rst year in our data. The results are reported in Tables A22–A33 in the online appendix, which are again qualitatively similar to the results in Tables 3–14.

## 6. Concluding Remarks

We study how informed investors in P2P lending strategically use their information advantage in the decision of when to place bids. The auction literature has documented that informed investors have the incentive to withhold the information and bid until the last moment, that is, sniping. We, however, argue that a collective effort from investors is required in P2P lending. The sniping strategy, therefore, has a potential cost for informed investors. We argue that bidding early, or squatting, can be an optimal strategy for informed investors. Using detailed data from Prosper.com, our empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings lend support to the argument. Using a matching approach, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that for projects with low credit grades, the probability of being successfully funded is positively correlated with early bids from informed investors. We also <sup>fi</sup>nd signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different bidding strategies between in-group and out-group investors for funded loans with low credit: informed investors are more likely to bid in the early stage, while uninformed investors will follow and bid at the late stage. Finally, there are more early bids for good loans than for bad loans, indicating that the bids are informative for the quality of those projects. The results combined indicate that early bids from informed investors would bene<sup>fi</sup>t uninformed investors, as well as highquality, low-type borrowers.

We make several contributions to various strands of literature. First, to the best of our knowledge, we are the <sup>fi</sup>rst to explore the information asymmetry on the investor side of P2P lending. In contrast, the literature on P2P lending has thus far focused on the information asymmetry between borrowers and investors. Second, our study adds to the economics literature on online auctions by providing empirical evidence that the squatting strategy exists in P2P lending and demonstrating a potential underlying mechanism driving this strategy. Our work also extends understanding of the role of social networks in online platforms by uncovering how in-group investors use their information advantage. Last, but not least, we contribute to the lit erature on herding in online markets by identifying a potential reason why information spillover is generated from informed investors to uninformed investors. We also <sup>fi</sup>nd evidence that most of the early bids from informed investors are genuine bids.

Our study has several managerial implications. First, results suggest that squatting behaviors in P2P lending bene<sup>fi</sup>t not only uninformed investors but also borrowers who otherwise may not be able to raise suf<sup>fi</sup>cient funding. Our results also have implications for how P2P lending platforms may manage the information asymmetry and strategic behaviors of investors. For example, the closed-when-funded mechanism on Prosper eliminated the costs of early bidding for informed investors. Thus, it encourages them to bid as early as possible, which facilitates the information spillover that boosts the success rate of a quality project with a low credit grade. In contrast, as noted in the introduction, Prosper and LendingClub adopted the partial funding rule that allows a project to be funded if it collects only a fraction of the funding goal. The new rule may lead to unintended negative consequences for investors and borrowers, as our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that it reduces the incentive of early bidding and thus the information spillover from informed investors to uninformed investors.
