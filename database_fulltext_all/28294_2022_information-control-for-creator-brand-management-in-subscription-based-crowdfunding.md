# Information Control for Creator Brand Management in Subscription-Based Crowdfunding

Abstract. Subscription-based crowdfunding (SBC) is an emerging platform for creators of digital content (e.g., music, comics, stories, and videos) to build their person brands and garner support from fans. A unique feature that sets SBC apart from other crowdfunding models is the creator-centered freemium model. That is, creators can offer free content to attract fans, who can then subscribe to <sup>fi</sup>nancially support a creator; in return, the backers gain access to the creator’s premium content and exclusive perks. In doing so, creators receive regular (e.g., monthly) subscription payments from their backers to sustain their ongoing creative activities. The SBC context engenders unique creator-centric dynamics and mechanisms that are ill understood in crowdfunding literature. In this study, we investigate the effects of SBC creators’ two information control strategies: (1) earnings concealment and (2) private postings. From a brand management perspective, we theorize that earnings concealment improves brand authenticity, whereas private postings foster brand differentiation, cocreation, and attachment. These will lead to a positive impact on the <sup>fi</sup>nancial (backer base) and non<sup>fi</sup>nancial ( fan participation) outcomes of an SBC campaign. Furthermore, we propose a reinforcing dynamic between information control and SBC performance whereby better SBC outcomes will increase the creators’ tendencies to engage in information control. To test our hypotheses, we obtained panel data from a large SBC plat form that contains monthly observations of 92,850 creators from August 2016 to December 2017. The results of our empirical analyses provide evidence for the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of the two information control strategies in SBC and demonstrate the reinforcing relationships between information control and SBC outcomes for creators. We discuss the theoretical and practi cal implications of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

## 1. Introduction

Subscription-based crowdfunding (SBC) has emerged in recent years as a promising avenue for creators (such as artists, podcasters, musicians, writers, and YouTubers) to obtain ongoing <sup>fi</sup>nancial support to sustain their creative work.<sup>1</sup> In SBC, fundraising campaigns are structured as creator-centered membership programs whereby fans subscribe and provide recurring payments to the creator (typically, once a month). Unlike most other crowdfunding models, creators do not need to set time limits and provision points (otherwise known as targets or goals) for their SBC campaigns (cf. Mollick 2014, Burtch et al. 2018). Therefore, SBC’s ongoing campaigns and recurrent payouts to the creator engender unique dynamics pertaining to the creator’s choices vis-a-vis outcomes and behaviors\` that have not been considered in the extant crowdfunding literature.

For one, SBC enables a creator-centered freemium model for crowdfunding. On their SBC pages, creators frequently share information and resources with their fans through posts. Some of these posts are public and accessible to everyone, whereas others are private and restricted to the backers. Consistent with other freemium models, such as those of Dropbox and the New York Times, the public posts are often intended to attract visitors and serve as free samples to reduce the uncertainty of deciding whether to become a backer (Chellappa and Shivendu 2005, Li et al. 2019). The private posts, on the other hand, offer perks to subscribers, who are the primary revenue drivers (Niculescu and Wu 2014, Aral and Dhillon 2021). It is noteworthy that the premium offerings in SBC include not only exclusive content but also opportunities to interact privately with the creators. Through such a freemium model, SBC campaigns aim to attract and retain subscribers so that creators can maintain a steady income stream to support their creative activities.

Another unique feature of SBC is that creators approach SBC campaigns as branding exercises.<sup>2</sup> Although brands are commonly associated with products, services, or companies in the marketing literature, researchers have increasingly been looking into person brands (e.g., Gorbatov et al. 2018, Smith and Fischer 2021). Because SBC backers support a creator, not a project, and pledge to make repeated contributions to the creator, it is natural for individual creators to conceive and manage themselves as a brand and SBC campaigns as branding opportunities. As such, the goal of SBC creators is to cultivate their brands and develop relationships, loyalty, and a desirable brand image to improve SBC performance (McAlexander et al. 2002, Keller and Lehmann 2006).

Recognizing the creator-centered freemium model and the need for branding underlying SBC, we examine how creators can increase their backer base (i.e., the number of backers) and fan engagement (i.e., fans behavioral manifestations beyond subscription payment).<sup>3</sup> In particular, the crowdfunding literature (e.g., Burtch et al. 2015, Mejia et al. 2019) has suggested that information control—that is, the intentional disclosure or concealment of information (Granados et al. 2010)—can affect crowdfunding performance. Consistent with this strand of work, we observe that the SBC context gives rise to two unique information control choices for creators. First, creators have the option to conceal the earnings from their SBC campaigns (Figure 1). SBC creators can receive recurrent contributions in any amount from their backers, with the recurrent payouts across backers representing the creator’s earnings. This information is naturally more sensitive than the amount of fundraising in the one-time, project-oriented campaigns of other crowdfunding models. As such, their decision on earnings visibility should have salient implications for creators’ person branding. This differs from other crowd funding models in which the amount of campaign funding must be displayed to the public to determine whether the campaign is (or will be) successful. The second piece of information creators can control in SBC campaigns is their posts: creators can limit the accessibility of their posts to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c audience (Figure 2).<sup>4</sup> Although other crowdfunding models also often permit private communication between creators and backers, such communication is usually not considered as an incentive for backing. In contrast, the tiered communication in SBC (i.e., private versus public posts) is central to the value proposition in the creator-centered freemium model. By offering backeronly content and social perks through private posts, SBC creators can attract and retain attention from their audience and thereby improve their SBC performance (Smith and Fischer 2021).

We conjecture that earnings visibility and post accessibility, which are creator-centric information controls, will, on average, affect the experience and perceived image of a creator brand in SBC, leading to an impact on backer base and fan engagement.<sup>5</sup> Overall, we seek to answer two research questions: (1) What is the average impact of creators’ information control choices on backer base and fan engagement? (2) From a dynamics perspective, how do information control, backer base, and fan engagement recursively affect each other over time?

This study contributes to the information systems (IS) literature in multiple ways. It adds to the growing research on crowdfunding by elucidating the previously underexplored SBC model and homing in on its distinctive aspects of recurrent payouts and creatorcentered freemium models to understand how a creator can achieve SBC success. Employing a brand management lens, we examine the unique mechanisms underlying the relationships among a creator’s information control choices and his or her SBC outcomes. Our results reveal the positive role of such information control in the SBC context and offer insights into the evolutionary dynamics among information control, backer base, and fan engagement.

This paper proceeds as follows. We begin by reviewing related literature in Section 2 to identify the contextual novelty of SBC and the rationale for our theoretical underpinnings. Section 3 puts forward our conceptual model and hypotheses. We then describe the empirical context and data in Section 4 and econometric methods in Section 5. In Section 6, we present the results of our analyses. Finally, we discuss the contributions, implications, and limitations of our work in Section 7 and conclude in Section 8.

## 2. Related Literature

Our work is related to four literature streams: crowdfunding, freemium, brand management, and information control, which we systematically review to inform our research. The <sup>fi</sup>rst two are digital phenomena related to SBC that we examine to discern the salient contextual novelty of SBC. Building on the novel aspects of SBC that we identify, we draw on concepts from the brand management and information control literatures to inform our theoretical development.

2.1. Discerning the Contextual Novelty of SBC 2.1.1. Crowdfunding. SBC is a distinctive type of crowdfunding. Crowdfunding allows individuals to “raise capital from many people through online platforms” (Agrawal et al. 2014, p. 63). According to Allon and Babich (2020), there are four categories of crowdfunding—(a) reward, (b) charity, (c) debt, and (d) equity—that are differentiated based on how backers are compensated. Reward crowdfunding promises backers a future reward (typically a product) in return for their support (Burtch et al. 2015, 2016). In charity crowdfunding, backers do not receive tangible compensations but achieve altruistic ful<sup>fi</sup>llment (Mejia et al. 2019). Debt crowdfunding compensates backers by interests and repayments (Wei and Lin 2017). In equity crowdfunding, backers are promised a share of the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t (Bapna 2019).

To understand the crowdfunding literature in relation to our work, we conducted a systematic literature review (see Online Appendix A for detailed results and discussions). We were particularly interested in understanding how prior empirical researchers approached the following three topics in crowdfunding that are salient in SBC: (1) long-term creator–backer relationships, (2) modes and topics in creator–backer communications, and (3) information control behavior by crowdfunding backers and creators. Given that information control is a central focus of the current study, we provide a detailed discussion of this research stream in Section 2.2.2. We discuss the main <sup>fi</sup>ndings on the <sup>fi</sup>rst two topics below.

Some creators in reward crowdfunding have multiple projects, leading to potential long-term relationships between creators and their backers, as are formed in SBC. However, we found that few studies highlight such long-term relationships between creators and backers but instead employ creator experience from previous projects as a control in their analyses (see, e.g., Li and Wang 2019, Cornelius and Gokpinar 2020). Consequently, although creators may have multiple projects over time, it is unclear how they should effectively manage their long-term relationships with backers. Meanwhile, evidence is lacking on whether backers in non-SBC crowdfunding form long-term relationships with the creators they support and make future contributions to the same creators based on such relationships. In contrast, the need for long-term relationships is front and center in SBC. Almost by de<sup>fi</sup>nition, SBC campaigns would not succeed if creators failed to establish long-term relationships with their backers.

In terms of creator–backer communications, recent crowdfunding studies have shown that backers often offer feedback to creators, and creators usually provide updates on their crowdfunding campaign pages to inform existing and potential backers (e.g.,

Gleasure et al. 2019, Cornelius and Gokpinar 2020). Such communications have been shown to bene<sup>fi</sup>t crowdfunding performance. However, the extant research focuses exclusively on public communications between creators and backers. In contrast, creator– backer communications in SBC tend to occur behind closed doors in private posts (see Figure 2(c)). Although the extant crowdfunding literature provides evidence on the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of public communications/posts, little is known about the role of private creator–backer communications.

In sum, although SBC promises a way for creators to sustain their creative work, the distinctive aspects of SBC have not been explored in the crowdfunding literature. As such, we focus on these distinctive aspects to uncover the mechanisms that relate information controls to SBC performance.

2.1.2. Freemium Models. As mentioned previously, SBC is typically structured as a creator-centered freemium model for crowdfunding. Freemium combines free and premium offerings in a digital business model. Over the past decade, the freemium model has been employed in numerous contexts, such as software products (Niculescu and Wu 2014), online social networks (Oestreicher-Singer and Zalmanson 2013), online newspapers (Oh et al. 2016), and video games (Dey and Lahiri 2016). Furthermore, the idea of freemium is closely related to product sampling because it provides an opportunity for consumers to experience digital content before signing up for a subscription (Li et al. 2019). The differentiation of multiple tiers of offerings in freemium models is also rooted in the concepts of versioning and vertical segmentation (Bhargava and Choudhary 2001, Chellappa and Shivendu 2005). Because the implementation of a freemium model in the digital content industry tends to rely on paywalls, recent research has focused on the impact of paywall design on subscriptions (Oh et al. 2016, Aral and Dhillon 2021).

We conducted a systematic review of the freemium literature (see Online Appendix B for detailed results and discussions) with a focus on understanding whether and how information control is considered in the extant freemium research and what incentives drive users to pay in freemium business models. We found that few scholars have explicitly examined how a service provider utilizes information control to improve the <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance of its freemium business. This dearth of research is somewhat surprising because freemium models are inherently about limiting access to resources and information to free-tier consumers and offering such access to premium-tier consumers. As a rare exception, Aral and Dhillon (2021) found that greater information control on the New York Times website (i.e., fewer free articles to nonsubscribers) increased number of subscriptions and total revenue.

On the other hand, prior studies have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed several other determinants that in<sup>fl</sup>uence customers willingness to pay and their likelihood of converting from the free tier to the premium tier. In particular, Oestreicher-Singer and Zalmanson (2013) showed that social engagement motivates free customers to become subscribers. Several studies, such as those of Bapna and Umyarov (2015) and Fang et al. (2019), also demonstrated the role of peer in<sup>fl</sup>uence in increasing the likelihood of converting to a premium level. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings have collectively provided valuable insight into how SBC creators may attract subscribers and grow their backer bases.

Although SBC bears signi<sup>fi</sup>cant similarities to the freemium models observed in other business contexts, it presents unique characteristics that help advance our understanding of freemium models. First, SBC creators can dynamically allocate their premium offerings (i.e., putting out more or fewer private posts in a month), whereas in other freemium con texts, the allocation of premium resources tends to be <sup>fi</sup>xed (e.g., <sup>fi</sup>ve free articles per month on the New York Times website or 2 GB of free cloud storage from Dropbox).<sup>6</sup> There is no <sup>fi</sup>xed number of posts to be made accessible to the public on creators’ SBC pages, and the creators can constantly and strategically adjust the number and proportion of private posts. Second, cocreation is a common theme in SBC, but it is rare in other freemium contexts. SBC creators frequently seek feedback from fans, through polls or other means, for the ideation, planning, and prioritization of new content. Consistent with cocreation practices, SBC creators also often acknowledge feedback and recognize <sup>fi</sup>nancial support from key backers in their creations, such as in a video’s end credits. Third, social perks are pervasive in SBC campaigns for subscribers, but as Oestreicher-Singer and Zalmanson (2013) pointed out, social perks have not been used in other freemium business contexts. A key driver of subscription in SBC is the eagerness of fans to interact personally with the creator. Thus, SBC creators often arrange hangouts, meetings, and question and answer (Q&A) sessions with their backers. As demonstrated in Figure 2(c), creators use private posts to arrange these interaction opportuni ties to ensure that only backers can participate in such social events.

Our study adds to this stream of research by exposing a case in which freemium models can be used for crowdfunding. In doing so, we also provide insights into how to utilize the dynamic allocation of premium resources and social perks to convert free customers into premium subscribers.

2.2. Lenses That Inform Theoretical Development 2.2.1. Brand Management. SBC creators are branded entities, and SBC campaigns are branding exercises. A brand can be a person (e.g., Elon Musk), place (e.g., Silicon Valley), product/service (e.g., iPhone), or <sup>fi</sup>rm/organization (e.g., Google), and the brand functions as a set of mental associations held by the consumer to distinguish the branded entity from others in the market (Keller 2012). Hence, brand management involves the selection, implementation, and control of the perceived brand image to in<sup>fl</sup>uence what consumers think and feel about the brand (Keller and Lehmann 2006). Brand management is a central topic with rich and proli<sup>fi</sup>c literature in the marketing <sup>fi</sup>eld.<sup>7</sup> However, marketing scholars, such as Swaminathan et al. (2020) and Smith and Fischer (2021), suggest that the scope of branding research has broadened to include person brands only in recent years. Indeed, in their systematic review of the person branding literature, Gorbatov et al. (2018, p. 2) noticed that “the <sup>fi</sup>rst academic papers on the topic [of person branding] were published in 2005,” and that the extant works are largely conceptual and qualitative.

Managing person brands is likely a more delicate task than managing product or company brands. For one, the trust development process differs when the target is an organization as opposed to an individual (Doney and Cannon 1997). Additionally, people tend to make strong, rapid, and con<sup>fi</sup>dent judgments when evaluating other individuals (Hamilton and Sherman 1996). Overall, the extant person branding literature tends to focus on celebrities, executives, and journalists (Gorbatov et al. 2018) and thus provides limited insight into how creators can achieve person branding in the SBC context.

Finally, it is important to note that although person branding involves self-presentation, the two concepts are not equivalent. In The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life, Goffman (1959, p. 208) conceptualized self-presentation as the art of successfully staging a character, involving a process of “impression management,” where social actors are performers seeking to project a desired impression to the audience. The rise of social media platforms and online activities has greatly increased the practice of self-presentation in digital spaces (Schau and Gilly 2003, Kim et al. 2012). However, Gorbatov et al. (2018) pointed out that person branding involves attributes and processes that are not necessarily present in self-presentation (impression management), including (a) designing a series of coordinated activities, (b) signaling a promise to the target audience, (c) engaging in selfre<sup>fl</sup>ection and feedback seeking, and (d) differentiating from the competition. As a result, Swaminathan et al. (2020, p. 39) argued that it remains an open question how noncelebrities can “build a person brand with clear brand associations that can help them attain speci<sup>fi</sup>c personal or professional goals.”

2.2.2. Information Control. SBC platforms allow creators to control information related to earnings and postings. Information control behavior has been studied in multiple business contexts. For example, corporate disclosure—either voluntary or mandatory—is an information control application in market-based economies in which information is revealed to either affect outsiders’ perceptions of the <sup>fi</sup>rm or reduce the information asymmetry between <sup>fi</sup>rms and their investors (Lev 1992, Leuz and Verrecchia 2000). In the e-commerce literature, studies have shown that information control has implications for marketplace design and suggest that transparency is not necessarily bene<sup>fi</sup>- cial (Granados et al. 2010). More recently, researchers have used the information control perspective to examine how the full disclosure of software vulnerabilities accelerates and magni<sup>fi</sup>es cyberat tacks (Mitra and Ransbotham 2015) and to conside how transparency in riders’ names and pro<sup>fi</sup>le photos leads to gender and racial biases on ridesharing platforms (Mejia and Parker 2021), thereby adding evidence for the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of information concealment.

The impact of information control has also been studied in the crowdfunding context through several different theoretical lenses (Table 1). Focusing on backers, Burtch et al. (2015, 2016) found that accommodating backers’ privacy preferences by concealing their identities and the amounts of their contributions can affect fundraising performance. As for creators’ information control behavior, prior researchers have examined the issues of self-presentation (Kim et al. 2018), information asymmetry (Agrawal et al. 2014), operational transparency (Mejia et al. 2019), and fraud behavior (Siering et al. 2016) in crowdfunding campaigns.

Our study emphasizes creators’ information control behavior in crowdfunding, but we approach it from a novel brand management perspective, which is particularly illuminating for the SBC context. As mentioned, it is necessary for SBC creators to engage in branding decisions and tasks to convert fans to backers and to maintain their backers’ commitment. This naturally motivates creators to control information on their SBC pages to manage the image of their person brand and create value for their backers. In particular, earnings and posts are prominent information in SBC and under the full control of the creators. Creators can choose to conceal (or reveal) monthly earnings from their SBC campaigns; they can also adjust the proportion of private postings. It is thus conceivable that these information control choices may in<sup>fl</sup>uence creators’ person branding, which we elaborate below.

2.2.3. The Connection Between Information Control and Brand Management. Branding inherently involves selectively communicating information related to brand attributes and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to form desired brand images, knowledge, and associations in consumer memory (Keller 2012). To better establish what a brand represents, Keller (2012, p. 93) further noted that marketers often de<sup>fi</sup>ne a brand mantra and use it to create “a mental <sup>fi</sup>lter to screen out brand-inappropriate marketing activities or actions of any type that may have a negative bearing on customers’ impressions of a brand.” In doing so, the marketers engage, implicitly or explicitly, in information control.

The link between information control and brand management is particularly salient in online person branding for the purpose of impression management. For example, Pagis and Ailon (2017) studied business consultants’ self-branding by analyzing their personal web pages and found that the consultants engaged in rhetorical efforts that, on one hand, intentionally distanced themselves from all market-based connotations (e.g., commercial promotion) and, on the other hand, attempted to construct authentic, friendly, and unique images. Similar qualitative <sup>fi</sup>ndings that exemplify the selective information revelation and concealment in person branding were also reported in Duffy and Wissinger’s (2017) study of social media in<sup>fl</sup>uencers and Smith and Fischer’s (2021) analysis of content creators.

Moreover, information control has also been used in person branding to promote engagement and value cocreation. In her ethnographical study of status and celebrity on social media, Marwick (2013, p. 117) pointed out that “Virtually everyone I interviewed was strategic using social media to reveal or conceal certain aspects of themselves.” To illustrate, Marwick documented how microcelebrities strategically reveal intimate information to increase or maintain their fan bases on social media and, in doing so, create a bond with their audiences. In the same vein, she characterized lifestreamers’ information control behavior as strategic and thoughtful: “they monitor their own and other’s actions, publicizing certain aspects of their lives while keeping other parts to themselves, and they may even exaggerate or falsify information to produce a desired effect” (Marwick 2013, p. 242). Despite these insightful observations, it remains largely unclear how creators could employ information control to manage impressions and create value in their SBC campaigns, which we discuss next.

## 3. Theoretical Development

This paper’s objective is to investigate the relationship between creators’ information control choices and their outcomes in SBC. For information control choices, we have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed earnings concealment and private postings as two salient features of SBC platforms whereby creators can choose the visibility of their earnings and the number of private postings. As for SBC outcomes, we focus on backer base and fan engagement. These SBC outcomes are consistent with the freemium literature in which subscription and social engagement are important outcomes in a freemium business (e.g., Oestreicher-Singer and Zalmanson 2013, Bapna et al. 2018, Fang et al. 2019, Aral and Dhillon 2021).

Using brand management as an overarching lens, we theorize the aggregated and dynamic relationships as shown in Figure 3. In sum, and as we elaborate in the following subsections, we argue that earnings concealment enhances the authenticity of the creator brand, leading to a positive effect on backer base and fan engagement. Furthermore, we suggest that private postings can elevate differentiation of the creator brand while fostering attachment to the brand for backers and privileged status to them in cocreating value with the creator, which in turn will have a positive effect on backer base and fan engagement. Given that managing the brand image and the creator–backer relationship are dynamic and progressive processes (Keller 2012), we also expect that SBC outcomes will reinforce information control such that better SBC outcomes will increase the propensity for information control. Finally, because the positive link between backer base and fan engagement has been well established (Oestreicher-Singer and Zalmanson 2013, Bapna et al. 2018), we include this relationship in our model and empirical analyses but omit it in our hypothesis development.

## 3.1. The Effects of Earnings Concealment

Authenticity is a key component of brand image (Beverland 2005). For person brands, prior researchers have conceptualized authenticity as the perception of being true to oneself with a demonstrated passion about and commitment to one’s work (Moulard et al. 2014, Ilicic and Webster 2016). Conversely, inauthenticity is perceived as “selling out.” When the motives behind a behavior are extrinsic and perceived as driven by commercial considerations, the audience <sup>fi</sup>nds the brand inauthentic (Beverland 2005). For example, Hahl (2016) showed that when fans became aware of professional athletes’ decisions to leave their original teams and join different teams for higher pay, they expressed concerns about their inauthenticity. Indeed, when it comes to authenticity, perceived intrinsic motives matter to the audience. From the perspective of consumer psychology, Morhart et al. (2015) found that consumers are increasingly seeking authenticity in brands and that perceived brand authenticity can increase consumers’ emotional attachment to and behavioral engagement with the brand. Recently, Radoynovska and King (2019) examined authenticity in crowdfunding to show that authenticity can affect the likability of a creator and the audience’s willingness to support the campaign.

Creators in crowdfunding campaigns are assumed to have dual motives (Radoynovska and King 2019). On one hand, creators pursue their campaigns for noneconomic causes, such as achievement, passion, and a commitment to their projects, artwork, or creations. On the other hand, they are motivated by the <sup>fi</sup>nancial and social success of their work. Because authenticity rests in the eye of the beholder, the prominent display of monthly earnings is likely to reinforce the perception of <sup>fi</sup>nancial motives behind creators’ SBC campaigns. For the audience, the earnings information naturally raises suspicions about the creators’ brand authenticity. Although we recognize many bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of information transparency such as market ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency and consumer welfare in e-commerce settings (Granados et al. 2010) and agree that some fans may consider creator’s earnings concealment as inauthentic, our theoretical reasoning suggests that earnings concealment is more likely to improve the authenticity of creator brands in SBC. By concealing their earnings, the campaigns avoid reminding the audience of the <sup>fi</sup>nancial motives behind creative activities and focus their attention instead on the passion and commitment of the creators. The improved authenticity should then have a positive effect on backer base and fan engagement (Morhart et al. 2015, Radoynovska and King 2019). Thus, we posit the following.

Hypothesis 1. Earnings concealment in SBC will, <sup>on</sup>, have a positive effect on (a) backer base and (b) fan engagement.

## 3.2. The Effects of Private Postings

Any freemium model, by de<sup>fi</sup>nition, requires free and premium offerings. Viewing SBC as a creator-centered freemium model for crowdfunding, it is intuitive to expect that private postings as a type of premium offering should play a role in creators’ SBC performance. Nevertheless, if subscribers must pay to access private postings as a premium offering, why is it theoretically relevant or useful to consider private postings in SBC from the perspective of brand management? In other words, what can the brand management perspective provide to enrich our understanding of the role of private postings in affecting creator–backer transaction exchanges in SBC beyond what we expect from a rational economic agent in freemium business models (e.g., Niculescu and Wu 2014)? We argue that the branding lens engenders three critical insights in explaining the roles of private postings in the development of creator brands.

3.2.1. Brand Differentiation. The most important function of a brand is to differentiate it from the competition (Keller 2012). More likely than not, there are many creators in the market making content in a similar genre (e.g., comedy, drama, or sports) and format $( \mathrm { e . g . } ,$ music, videos, or podcasts). The quantity (the number or proportion of private posts), quality (based on the number of likes and comments from the backers), and consistency (e.g., two private posts per month) of the private posts signal the exclusivity of the creator’s premium offerings to backers. All else being equal and comparable to other similar creator brands, we expect that when a creator brand shows a higher level of quantity, quality, and consistency in private postings, it should achieve better SBC performance.

3.2.2. Brand Cocreation. Modern branding research emphasizes that brands are shifting toward shared ownership, experience, and meaning (Swaminathan et al. 2020). This is consistent with the common practice of creator–backer cocreation of value and, correspondingly, the creator brand in SBC. Indeed, SBC creators frequently seek feedback from fans when creating digital content. Of course, a creator normally would not have the capacity to incorporate input from every single fan. Therefore, through private posts, which are accessible only to backers, SBC creators can interact with the most avid and valuable fans or with those with whom the creator is seeking to develop a deeper engagement, to identify likes and dislikes, tailor their brand experience, and improve its <sup>fi</sup>t (Dey and Lahiri 2016).

3.2.3. Brand Attachment. With the recurrent payment scheme in SBC, creators need to retain existing backers. According to Patreon, a “good” 12-month retention rate on the SBC platform is about 40%.<sup>8</sup> This suggests that it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult, yet critical, for SBC creators to maintain backers’ attachment to the brand. Private postings function in two ways as an important tool to develop backer attachment to a creator’s brand. First, creators’ private postings are perceived by their fans as “relationship investments,” signaling the creators dedication to their most valuable fans (De Wulf et al. 2001, p. 37). Consumers tend to become more loyal and engaged when they are in communal, intimate relationships with a brand (Henderson et al. 2011). Second, private posts, which feature Q&A and behind-the-scene stories, enable backers to understand why and how creators make their content. Such direct involvement and intimate experience with both the creator and the content allow backers to better appreciate the person and the work behind the brand (Lemon and Verhoef 2016), supporting the development of a creator’s brand.

Considering the roles of private postings in promoting brand differentiation, cocreation, and attachment, we expect that private postings will promote the development of a creator’s brand, leading to an increase in backer base and fan engagement in SBC. To be clear, this is not to say that public postings are unimportant. Like other freemium models, SBC campaigns cannot thrive without free offerings (i.e., public postings). To the extent that SBC creators recognize and leverage the distinct purposes of public and private postings, we propose the following.

Hypothesis 2. Private postings in SBC will, <sup>on aver-</sup>, have a positive effect on (a) backer base and (b) fan engagement.

## 3.3. Dynamic Relationship Between Information Control and SBC Outcomes

Beyond the effects of information control on SBC outcomes, we expect their relationship to be dynamically reinforcing. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we theorize a “cyclic recursive relationship” (Mitchell and James 2001, p. 534) between information control and SBC outcomes. We argue that as SBC creators grow their backer bases and fan engagement, they will have an increased tendency to implement earnings concealment and private postings to further develop their creator brands. This view is consistent with the notion that brand management is an evolving process, and a brand needs to reposition itself and adjust branding strategies in response to changes in customers, social values, and competition (Keller 2012).

As an SBC creator garners more backers, investing in activities that are bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to the retention of the existing subscribers will become increasingly important. This implies a growing incentive for the creator to improve the brand relationship and brand loyalty of existing backers through information control. It is therefore conceivable that the creator will become more likely to conceal earnings to develop the authenticity of his or her brand image (Hahl 2016, Radoynovska and King 2019). Similarly, the creator will be more likely to invest in the creator–backer relationship by offering preferential treatment and increasing the proportion of premium offerings to backers, thereby developing better differentiation and cocreation of the creator brand and backer attachment to it. We therefore propose the following.

Hypothesis 3. Growth in SBC backer base will be positively related to increased tendencies toward (a) earnings concealment and (b) private postings.

With an active community of engaged fans, SBC creators will naturally need to align their brands with a communal ethos (Muniz and O’Guinn 2001, McAlexander et al. 2002). This suggests that as fans become more engaged, creators should seek to develop, or adhere to, communal norms in their relationships with backers (e.g., showing concern for a partner’s needs) as opposed to quid pro quo transactional exchange norms (Aggarwal 2004). More likely than not, showing earnings information on an SBC campaign page could jeopardize the communal ethos because it raises potential concerns about the inauthenticity of the creator. It is therefore likely that creators will increasingly turn to earnings concealment as fan engagement grows. By the same token, creators will be more likely to increase the proportion of private postings to show personalized care for the backers by engaging in more intimate exchanges and creating opportunities for backers to cocreate value. In all, we posit the following.

Hypothesis 4. Growth in SBC fan engagement will be positively related to increased tendencies toward (a) earnings concealment and (b) private postings.

## 4. Context and Data

## 4.1. Data Set

We collected data from Patreon with monthly observations from August 2016 to December 2017. We constructed our panel data set with several screening criteria to safeguard the quality of our data. First, we focused on creators who choose to receive monthly recurring payments from their Patreon backers. Creators on Patreon can receive backers’ recurring payments in two ways: per creation (e.g., \$10 for each new podcast) or per time interval (e.g., \$5 for each month).

The former is a legacy feature of Patreon, and most creators (over 85% in our sample) opted to receive monthly payments. From the perspective of generalizability, it is also worth noting that other SBC platforms such as Ko-<sup>fi</sup>, Subscribestar, and Steady support only recurrent monthly payouts because of their simplicity and predictability for backers. Second, because Patreon allows virtually anyone to register as a creator and set up a campaign page, we further excluded inactive creators who had neither creations nor backers throughout our sample period to ensure the validity of our data. Finally, we excluded observations with missing values for any of our variables (as discussed below). Taken together, these screenings yielded a total of 92,850 unique creators and 1,130,177 creator–month observations.

We constructed two independent variables for information control. Concealed earnings (CE) is a binary variable that indicates whether a creator concealed earnings in a given month. Patreon has provided this feature since January 2017. The second independent variable is private postings (PP), which is the proportion of a creator’s new posts per month that are accessible only by the creator’s backers.

4.2.2. Controls for Creator Heterogeneity. We considered several creator-level factors that could in<sup>fl</sup>uence SBC performance. First, we controlled for the number of public posts a creator makes in a month (public postings). Intuitively, public posts are an effective means of attracting new backers because they provide potential backers with a glimpse of the creator, their creations, and the creator–backer interactions. Second, we accounted for the diversity of post types in a creator’s activity feed. When creators make a post, they need to designate the type of post (text, image, audio, video, link, poll, and so on). To quantify post type diversity, we calculated the entropy of the post type per month based on the types of posts made by a creator during the month. Third, it is reasonable to expect that the SBC outcomes and information control choices may be driven by the creator’s tenure on the platform. Therefore, in our empirical models, we included tenure, measured by the number of months since the registration of the account. Fourth, we controlled for the cumulative number of creations produced by a creator (creation count). It is important to note that the number of creations is not the same as the number of postings because a post does not necessarily include creative content. All else being equal, proli<sup>fi</sup>c creators should be more attractive to backers because a main motivation for backing is access to creations. Fifth, we controlled for the number of reward levels, which has been shown to be related to crowdfunding performance (Mollick and Nanda 2015). Finally, creators have different levels of social media presence. Although Patreon is a platform for relationship management, content creators may rely on other platforms for discoverability. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, Patreon allows creators to display external links to Facebook, Twitter, or YouTube on their pro<sup>fi</sup>le pages to aid discoverability. Individual creators’ activities on other platforms are unobservable to us, but we attempted to partially control for such cross-platform social media presence by using the number of external social media links on a creator’s pro<sup>fi</sup>le page as a proxy.<sup>10</sup>

4.2.3. Controls for Market Conditions. We accounted for three market-related factors: market size, market crowdedness, and market concentration. Because there is a wide variety of creations on Patreon, conventiona categories of creations (e.g., video, music, podcast, and writing) are inadequate for characterizing the creators on Patreon and their creative work because there are numerous and diverse types of content even within a conventional category. For example, podcast creators may produce audio shows featuring news commentaries, educational materials, stories, or interviews. Meanwhile, creators—at least those on Patreon—often produce multiple types of creations at the same time (e.g., videos and podcasts), and some creations are by nature a hybrid of several categories (e.g., music video).

To identify creator markets and operationaliz market-related factors, we implemented a clustering analysis based on deep learning techniques. Speci<sup>fi</sup> cally, we considered each creator’s textual descrip tion in his or her pro<sup>fi</sup>le to be a document, and we trained a Global Vectors (GloVe) word embedding model (Pennington et al. 2014) to represent the words on Patreon creator pro<sup>fi</sup>les. Word embedding models such as GloVe can effectively capture lin guistic regularities in a single text and characterize the relationships among words. However, we are interested in the similarity between documents (i.e., creators). Following Kusner et al. (2015), we used the relaxed word mover’s distance (RWMD) method to obtain pairwise distances between documents RWMD is akin to the edit distance used in computational linguistics, which aims to <sup>fi</sup>nd the minimum number of insertions, deletions, and substitutions necessary to convert one string into another. However, unlike the edit distance, RWMD quanti<sup>fi</sup>es the distance between two documents based on the mini mum cumulative travel distance to move from the embedded words of one document to the embedded words of another document. The pairwise RWMDs between creators can then be used as the input for any distance-based clustering algorithm. We chose hierarchical clustering because it does not require a prespeci<sup>fi</sup>ed number of clusters during model train ing, and the hierarchical output allows <sup>fl</sup>exible cut off points for different groupings of observations. We experimented with different cutoff points and found that the distribution of cluster sizes starts to converge and stabilize at 150 clusters (see Figure C1 in Online Appendix C). We considered each of these to be a unique creator market. After assigning each creator to a market, we calculated the market size (the number of backers), market crowdedness (the number of creators), and market concentration (the Her<sup>fi</sup>ndahl–Hirschman index for the concentration of backing in each market) for the focal creator’s market. Figure C2 in Online Appendix C shows some example markets.

## 5. Empirical Methods

## 5.1. Estimating the Average Effects

To investigate the average effects of information controls on the size of the backer base and fan engagement, our baseline <sup>fi</sup>xed-effect (FE) regression has the following speci<sup>fi</sup>cation:<sup>11</sup>

$$
\begin{array}{r} \ln (y _ {i t} + 1) = \beta_ {1} C E _ {i t - 1} + \beta_ {2} \ln (P P _ {i t - 1} + 1) + \gamma X _ {i t - 1} \\ + \lambda_ {i} + \pi_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t}, \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

where $y _ { i t }$ is an SBC outcome (BB, BC, BL, PC, or PL), and $X _ { i t - 1 }$ is the set of log-transformed controls that were discussed earlier. In Equation (1), all explanatory variables are lagged by one time period to avoid the problems associated with simultaneity. The creator FEs $\lambda _ { i }$ control for any time-invariant, creator-speci<sup>fi</sup>c unobservables. The time FEs $\pi _ { t }$ address time trends and seasonality, along with any month-speci<sup>fi</sup>c unobserved shocks. In this speci<sup>fi</sup>cation and the ensuing ones, we reported robust standard errors, clustered at the creator level, to address general heteroskedasticity and serial correlation. The model was estimated using the ordinary least squares (OLS) approach. The coef<sup>fi</sup>- cients of interest are $\beta _ { 1 }$ and $\beta _ { 2 } ,$ which can be interpreted as the percentage changes in $y _ { i t }$ contributed by CE and PP, respectively.

To produce consistent parameter estimates, the baseline OLS model requires that there be no measurement errors or omitted variables on the righthand side of Equation (1). To address potential sources of endogeneity, we further applied an instrumental variable identi<sup>fi</sup>cation strategy and a twostage least squares (2SLS) estimator. Following Burtch et al. (2016), we construct peer-effect instruments from competitors’ CE and PP choices. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we consider an actor-varying set of competitors to construct our instruments, operationalized based on the average lagged values of the endogenous variables from the top <sup>fi</sup>ve most similar creators in the same market as the focal creator. Because information controls on $\mathrm { P a } \mathfrak { - }$ treon are publicly observable by anyone on the platform, it is plausible that a creator could be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by other creators, especially those who are close competitors, when deciding whether to conceal earnings and the extent of private posting. Indeed, such peerin<sup>fl</sup>uenced and observational learning has been observed in many online and IS contexts (e.g., Mithas et al. 2013, Bapna and Umyarov 2015, Burtch et al. 2016). Patreon has also explicitly encouraged creators on the platform to conduct such “competitor analysis” (Rook 2019).<sup>12</sup> Thus, the requirement of 2SLS that instruments and endogenous variables should be correlated can be theoretically justi<sup>fi</sup>ed.

As for the exclusion restriction in 2SLS, it cannot be empirically veri<sup>fi</sup>ed in our context because our models are just-identi<sup>fi</sup>ed. However, it is unlikely that the information control choices made by close competitors have a direct impact on the focal creator’s backer base and fan participation. The logic here is that although creators in the same market are indeed in direct competition with one another, support from fans and backers of a creator should not be highly associated with the information control choices made by close competitors of the focal creator.

## 5.2. Estimating the Dynamic Effects

To estimate the dynamic reinforcing relationship between information control and SBC outcomes, we implemented a panel vector autoregressive (PVAR) model. The PVAR model is useful for investigating dynamic relationships and has been used in prior IS research (Chen et al. 2015, Moqri et al. 2018, Sun et al. 2020). It allows us to endogenize all the SBC outcomes and information control choices to capture the temporal and feedback relationships and interdependencies among these variables. In particular, we used the generalized method of moments (GMM) to estimate the following PVAR model:

$$
\boldsymbol {\Upsilon} _ {i t} = \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {J} A _ {j} \boldsymbol {\Upsilon} _ {i t - j} + \gamma X _ {i t - 1} + \lambda_ {i} + \pi_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t},\tag{2}
$$

where $\pmb { Y } _ { i t } = [ B \pmb { B } _ { i t } , B \pmb { C } _ { i t } , B \pmb { L } _ { i t } , P \pmb { C } _ { i t } , P \pmb { L } _ { i t } , C E _ { i t } , P \pmb { P } _ { i t } ] ^ { T } , \pmb { Y } _ { i t - j }$ $\mathbf { \Psi } = [ B B _ { i t - j } , B C _ { i t - j } , B L _ { i t - j } , P C _ { i t - j } , P L _ { i t - j } , C E _ { i t - j } , P P _ { i t - j } ] ^ { T } , A _ { j }$ is a matrix of parameters to be estimated, and all the other notations have the same meanings as in Equation (1).

In this speci<sup>fi</sup>cation, the current values of dependent variables are affected by their lagged values in the past J periods. Therefore, PVAR estimation is typically accompanied by a model selection procedure to determine the best number of lags (i.e., J) in the model. In estimating the parameters in the PVAR model, GMM utilizes higher-order (greater than J) lagged dependent variables as instruments. An issue in GMM estimation with a long panel is that the estimator can easily generate a large number of instruments, which can weaken the Hansen test of overidentifying restrictions (Roodman 2009). To avoid the too-manyinstruments problem, we followed Roodman (2009) and employed the curtailing and collapsing techniques to reduce the instrument count. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we used only a limited number of lags to construct instruments (the <sup>fi</sup>rst four lags instead of all valid lags, following Sun et al. (2020)) and collapsed the instrument matrix so that the number of instruments grows linearly (instead of quadratically) in the number of lags. Furthermore, to address the inherent correlation between creator FEs and lagged dependent variables in dynamic panel models (Arellano and Bond 1991), we followed Moqri et al. (2018) and implemented a forward differentiating procedure to control for creator FEs.

The primary interests of the PVAR estimation are the $A _ { j }$ matrices, which characterize the relationship between $Y _ { i t }$ and each $Y _ { i t - j } .$ Because the $A _ { j }$ estimates are not directly interpretable, it is customary in PVAR modeling to use the impulse response functions (IRFs) to provide intuitions for the estimated parameters (Chen et al. 2015, Moqri et al. 2018). The IRFs allow us to isolate the effect of one endogenous variable on another endogenous variable in the PVAR model. To test Hypotheses 3 and 4, we bootstrapped the con<sup>fi</sup>- dence intervals of the IRFs, following Kapetanios’s (2008) procedure.

To validate results from these OLS and 2SLS estimations, we conducted a series of robustness checks to explore potential issues in this analysis. The results of these robustness checks are reported in Online Appendix C and are summarized in Table 5, which we discuss below.

First, a creator’s quality is correlated with his or her SBC outcomes and can be a threat to our identi<sup>fi</sup>cation if it is also associated with his or her information control choices. We addressed this threat in two ways. To begin with, we noted that prior empirical researchers frequently employed brand FEs to address the issue of unobserved product quality (e.g., Nevo 2001). Although it is challenging to directly quantify a creator’s quality, the creator FEs and our control for creators tenure in the estimation should be able to address, at least in part, the quality concern, when a creator’s quality is time invariant or correlates with tenure. Because the assumption of time-invariant quality cannot be directly tested with our data, we relaxed this assumption and assumed instead that quality is time variant but slow changing. In other words, even if a creator’s quality changes over time, it should not vary signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in a short period, especially after we account for tenure. Although our initial sample period was not particularly long (17 months), we replicated our analyses using a shorter sample period with only six months of data, between January and June 2017. To the extent that a creator’s quality does not change signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in this short time period, the creator FEs should more effectively capture the idiosyncratic quality of each creator. The results of this analysis are shown in Table C4 in Online Appendix C and are consistent with our earlier <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

Second, SBC outcomes may be correlated with the creators’ activities or performance on other platforms. Although creators use Patreon to manage subscriptions and distribute public and private contents, they can use other platforms to distribute public content. In our main analysis, we used social media presence to partially control for such cross-platform activities. Here, we formally tested the threat of such cross-platform activities to our identi<sup>fi</sup>cation by looking into a subset of creators whose YouTube channels are listed on their Patreon pro<sup>fi</sup>le pages.<sup>14</sup> Among these creators, we collected their monthly numbers of YouTube channel subscribers from SocialBlade and included them as a control variable in our estimation. Intuitively, the numbers of YouTube subscribers should be associated with creators’ backer bases and fan participation on Patreon. The results of this analysis are shown in Table C5 in Online Appendix C. We observed that the number of YouTube subscribers is positively associated with backer base and fan participation, and the CE and PP estimates are consistent with those reported earlier.

Third, our model could be misspeci<sup>fi</sup>ed. From a Durbin–Watson test for autocorrelation, we found that the test statistics ranged between 1.677 and 1.884 across different SBC outcomes. They were above the commonly used lower critical value of one, suggesting that the degree of serial correlation in the error terms of our models was not substantial. Nevertheless, we examined an alternative model speci<sup>fi</sup>cation in which the lagged dependent variable was included as a regressor. We used the Arellano and Bond (1991) estimator to create the models and reached qualitatively similar results (Table C6 in Online Appendix C). This, along with the results from our PVAR modeling reported below, indicates that our <sup>fi</sup>ndings on the positive links between creators’ information control choices and SBC outcomes are robust to different model speci<sup>fi</sup>cations.

Fourth, the observed effects may be driven by the choice of the number of creator markets. We implemented a text-clustering procedure to detect creator markets, and these clusters played a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in our empirical estimations because they were used to derive our market-related control variables and instruments. Determining the appropriate number of clusters is a challenging task in clustering analysis, especially in contexts like ours where there is no ground truth. In our main analysis, we set the number of clusters to 150. Here, we set the number of clusters to 100 or 200 and found that the results remained consistent (Table C7 in Online Appendix C). This suggests that the effects observed in our main analysis are not sensitive to the number of clusters in our text-clustering analysis.

Finally, because fan engagement is operationalized based on the number of comments or likes on public and private posts in a month, its value is censored if a creator does not make any public or private posts in a given month. It is important to note that this issue would suppress the fan engagement metrics, leading to an underestimation in fan engagement regressions. To check this, we implemented a random-effect Tobit model to estimate the effects of information control on fan engagement. We observed similar results: that earnings concealment and private postings have positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effects on all four types of fan engagement (Table C8 in Online Appendix C). We also found that, as expected, the estimates from the Tobit model are larger than those from 2SLS or OLS estimations.

Aside from the above robustness analyses, we also seek to validate whether brand management is indeed a mechanism driving the positive impact of information control on SBC outcomes. We approach this inquiry in two ways. First, through content analysis, we are able to identify qualitative evidence supporting our theory that SBC creators engage in information control for the purpose of brand management (Table C9 in Online Appendix C). Second, we employ causal mediation analysis as a statistical framework to quantitatively explore the mechanism (Imai et al. 2010). The intuition behind this analysis is that the total effects of CE/PP on SBC outcomes may be decomposed into direct and indirect effects; if brand management is indeed a mechanism channeling the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of CE/PP on the SBC outcomes, the total effect of CE/PP should have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant proportion of indirect effect that is mediated by brand management. Because our study relies on secondary data and we do not have detailed information on individual backers and PP, we use the number of returning public commenters (de<sup>fi</sup>ned as commenters to the creator’s public posts at time t who also commented before time t) as a proxy for brand management effectiveness. We consider this to be a reasonable proxy, as consumers exhibit repeated purchasing behavior when they strongly identify with and attach to a brand. Despite using this coarse proxy for brand management effectiveness, we detect significant proportions of the total effects of CE (3%–28%) and PP (1%–10%) to be channeled by the mediator (Table C10 in Online Appendix C), which supports brand management effectiveness as a mechanism link ing information controls with SBC outcomes.

## 6.2. Dynamic Relationships Between Information Control and SBC Outcomes

We begin by summarizing the results of model selection and diagnosis in our PVAR modeling (details are available in Tables C11 and C12 in Online Appendix C). Based on the model selection criteria proposed by Andrews and Lu (2001), we chose the <sup>fi</sup>rst-order PVAR model (i.e., J 1) because it has the smallest value across the Bayesian information criterion, Akaike information criterion, and Hannan–Quinn information criterion. We then veri<sup>fi</sup>ed that the eigenvalue stability condition was met in the estimated <sup>fi</sup>rst-order PVAR model. The stability condition is required to ensure that the dynamic process will not diverge to in<sup>fi</sup>nity (“blow up”). We found that all the eigenvalues in matrix $A _ { j }$ are strictly less than one in absolute value, suggesting that the PVAR model is stable (Harris and Tzavalis 1999).

Using the <sup>fi</sup>rst-order PVAR model, we derive the IRF results for the dynamics between information control and SBC outcomes as summarized in Table 6 (see Figure C3 in Online Appendix C for the visualization of IRF results over 12 months). Although we focused on the average treatment effects of the information controls in Hypotheses 1 and 2 and found support for them, the IRF results reveal three exploratory insights on the time-varying nature of these effects. First, CE has a sustained effect on fan engagement, but the effect on public fan engagement (PC and PL) attenuates much faster than that on private fan engagement (BC and BL), plausibly because brand authenticity is a persistent consideration for backers who engage with the brand. Second, PP has a sustained effect on private fan engagement (BC and BL), but this effect on public fan engagement (PC and PL) manifests only in the long run, which is likely attributable to backers commenting and liking public posts over time. Third, both CE and PP have a sustained effect on BB, which gets stronger in the long run (plausibly because the images of brand authenticity and brand exclusivity develop over time). These insights render further support to our theorizing that information controls are an effective approach for creators to develop their brands.

To test the reinforcing relationships of information control to the SBC outcome variables proposed in Hypotheses 3 and 4, we focused on the last two columns in Table 6. We found that CE responds positively to the impulse shock from each of the SBC outcome variables. Therefore, Hypotheses 3(a) and 4(a) are sup ported. Interestingly, by comparing the short-term and long-term effects, we found that the effect from each of the SBC outcomes on CE is not only positive but also growing over time. Moreover, we found that PP responds positively to all <sup>fi</sup>ve SBC outcomes in the long run $( p < 0 . 1 )$ . However, in the short run, only three of the SBC outcomes (BB, BC, and BL) had a positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect on PP. Taken together, these results support Hypothesis 3(b) but only partially sup port Hypothesis 4(b). In particular, fan engagement activities on public posts do not have an immediate effect on creators’ private posting tendencies, but they do have a long-term effect. One potential explanation for this is that some of the nonsubscribing fans who engage with public posts may eventually convert to backers (Oestreicher-Singer and Zalmanson 2013), motivating creators to increase private postings. Because this backer acquisition process takes time, the effects of public comments and likes on private postings are detectable only in the long run.

To investigate the effects of information controls on earnings more formally, we employed a predictive modeling approach to predict earnings when they are hidden. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we used observations before January 2017 as training data for which we have complete earnings information, and we used observations thereafter as test data. In our sample, the number of creators with hidden earnings increased from 0% to 20% between January 2017 and December 2017. The rest of the observations in the test data do include earnings information, which we used to evaluate the predictive performance of a wide variety of predictive models. We found that the random coef<sup>fi</sup>cient model with only one predictor, backer base, yielded the lowest mean square error in the holdout sample. This model is simple but expressive because it considers earnings as a multiplication of backer base and the creatorspeci<sup>fi</sup>c average dollar contribution per backer. For this reason, even if two creators have the same number of backers, their earnings will differ if the <sup>fi</sup>rst creator tends to receive a higher contribution from a backer than the second creator does. We proceeded to predict hidden earnings using this random coef<sup>fi</sup>cient model and estimated the effects of information controls on earnings based on the imputed data set. Table C13 in Online Appendix C reports the results from the earnings estimation. Ultimately, we found that earnings concealment and private postings have positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effects on earnings. These results are consistent with those from the backer base regressions reported earlier. This is somewhat expected because earnings and backer base are highly correlated, and the amount of earnings is indeed a function of backer base.

## 7. Discussion

The unique characteristics of SBC lead to several illunderstood dynamics and mechanisms in this emerging crowdfunding model. We seek to advance the understanding of the effects of SBC creators’ information control behavior on their crowdfunding performance. Focusing on two information control choices, earnings concealment and private postings, we theorized through a brand management lens and obtained two principal <sup>fi</sup>ndings. First, earnings concealment and private postings, on average, can help increase backer base and promote fan engagement. Second, there is a reinforcing feedback dynamic from the SBC outcomes to information control, whereby expansions in backer base and fan engagement recursively elevate the creators’ tendencies to undertake earnings concealment and private postings.

## 7.1. Theoretical Contributions

This work makes <sup>fi</sup>ve key contributions. First, we expand the crowdfunding literature by uncovering the distinctive aspects of SBC and their implications for strategies that creators can use to build their brands and achieve success. To our knowledge, this is the <sup>fi</sup>rst study concerning SBC whose hallmark is the recurring funding scheme structured as a creator-centered free mium model. This setting gives rise to unique dynamics in crowdfunding. In particular, because backers are supporting a creator instead of a speci<sup>fi</sup>c project or product, creators need to frame themselves as person brands and their SBC campaigns as branding exercises to attract new visitors, convert <sup>fl</sup>eeting visitors into fans, and entice fans to become devout backers. From a brand management perspective, we propose and demonstrate that creators’ information control choices can lead to improved SBC performance in terms of backer base and fan engagement. Furthermore, we add to the ongoing research on the role of information control in crowdfunding (Burtch et al. 2015, 2016; Mejia et al. 2019) and examine two previously unexplored information control choices (i.e., creators’ earnings concealment and private postings).

Second, this work bridges two emerging streams of research, one on crowdfunding and the other on freemium models. These two strands of literature have been largely separate from each other because freemium research tends to consider freemium models operated by a single <sup>fi</sup>rm, such as the New York Times (Oh et al. 2016, Aral and Dhillon 2021) and Last.fm (Oestreicher-Singer and Zalmanson 2013, Bapna et al. 2018). Our study not only demonstrates the utility of the freemium business model in crowdfunding but also shows how individual creators can each operate their own freemium business to develop their brands and achieve success. This extends our collective knowledge about how freemium models can be differ ently conceived and enacted to build creator brands in a novel crowdfunding context.

Third, although there exists substantial research on information control across disciplines and application contexts (e.g., Lev 1992, Granados et al. 2010, Mitra and Ransbotham 2015, Burtch et al. 2016, Mejia and Parker 2021), it remains largely unclear how creators can effectively utilize information control to develop their brands, thereby improving crowdfunding and freemium performance. This study contributes to this stream of literature by showing why and how speci<sup>fi</sup>c types of information control by creators matter in SBC. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we identify earnings and postings as two key pieces of information to be controlled by SBC creators in building their brands. We <sup>fi</sup>nd positive average effects of information control on <sup>fi</sup>nancial and fan engagement outcomes and a reinforcing dynamic between the two. Together, we advance a nuanced picture of the role of information control in developing creators’ brands and attaining success, from both <sup>fi</sup>nancial and fan engagement perspectives.

Fourth, our research advances a reinforcing dynamics perspective on the relationship between information control and SBC performance. Interestingly, the results of the reinforcing dynamic reveal that the magnitude of the reinforcement of information controls tends to increase gradually over time. This suggests that information control choices are not modi<sup>fi</sup>ed by creators as immediate or abrupt responses to increases in fan engagement or backer base but as more appropriate measured adjustments. Moreover, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that fan engagement with public posts reinforces creators private postings but only in the long run. We conjecture that converting nonsubscribers to backers takes time, leading to this lag. This suggests that the allocation of premium resources in SBC (i.e., the proportion of private postings) is a complex process with rich temporal dynamics from multiple drivers.

Finally, this study adds to the literature on person brands (Thomson 2006, Gorbatov et al. 2018, Smith and Fischer 2021) by generating insights into the approaches and dynamics of person branding in the novel SBC context. Leveraging the distinctive aspects of SBC, we explain how creative professionals can manage and enhance their person brands through information control. In particular, we suggest that earnings concealment improves brand authenticity, whereas private postings contribute to brand differentiation, cocreation, and attachment. These could lead to stronger consumer–brand relationships and loyalty (Keller and Lehmann 2006). Moreover, our results support the view that creators should approach brand building as an evolving process. We explicate why creator brand performance, from both <sup>fi</sup>nancial and fan-engagement standpoints, could reinforce creators tendencies to engage in information control. As SBC creators garner more backers or stimulate greater fan engagement, they will face an increasing need to nurture a communal ethos and invest in creator–backer relationships to retain existing backers and keep them engaged (McAlexander et al. 2002, Aggarwal 2004, Henderson et al. 2011). This will motivate creators to conceal their SBC earnings in congruence with the communal relationship norms and to allocate more private posts on their SBC pages to increase the exclusivity of interactions with subscribers, which will, in turn, increase subscribers’ attachment to the brand while engaging them in differentiating and cocreating the brand. In sum, we expose the cumulative and progressive dynamics of information control employed by creators to develop their person brands.

## 7.2. Practical Implications

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant implications for SBC platforms and creators. SBC platforms play an important role in connecting creators to their audiences. Given that consumers’ taste in digital arts and creations is idiosyncratic, it is in a platform’s interest to ensure that creators can leverage rich platform features to build their distinctive brands and associated fan communities. We have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a unique set of platform features for information control in SBC and showcased their effects on backer base and fan engagement. SBC platforms can use these <sup>fi</sup>ndings to develop or enhance features pertinent to creators’ information control with the goal of empowering creators with the tools to build their brands, which could then bene<sup>fi</sup>t the platform by increasing and retaining participants in various SBC roles.

As for creators, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide actionable insight into how to enhance their person brands and SBC performance. SBC provides creators with a steady income stream directly from backers and greater control over their creative activities because they do not need to cater to popular tastes to attract more “eyeballs” for ad revenue (Sun and Zhu 2013, p. 2314). To that end, it is critical for creators to establish and enhance their brands. We argue that in SBC, earnings concealment bene<sup>fi</sup>ts brand authenticity, and private postings strengthen brand differentiation, cocreation, and attachment. We show robust evidence that earnings concealment and private postings, on average, lead to improvements in backer base and fan engagement. As such, creators should progressively adjust these information controls based on changes in backer base and fan engagement. With the rise of digital content creators, our results could serve as a guide in leveraging information control to develop person brands in SBCs.

Despite the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of information control observed in our study, we should acknowledge that the loss of transparency may raise two ethical concerns in practice. The <sup>fi</sup>rst drawback is the risk of fraud (Baucus and Mitteness 2016, Siering et al. 2016). Earnings concealment and private postings could make it harder for prospective backers to determine whether an SBC campaign is legitimate or fraudulent, although such risks may be mitigated by the display of total backer count and public postings. The second concern involves the allocation of funds. Some crowdfunding contributors may prefer to support a creator who is destitute rather than someone who has amassed substantial funding (even if the latter is perceived as a better creator). Such behavior has been observed in crowdfunding (Burtch et al. 2013) and charitable giv ing in general (Andreoni 2006), as backers/donors seek to maximize the impact of their contributions. Earnings concealment could undermine such moral principles in contributors’ funding decisions because the importance of a contribution to a creator becomes less apparent.

More broadly, as creators develop their person brands on SBC platforms, they could face the tension between framing their images as relatable persons and as commercial brands (Pagis and Ailon 2017). A person brand is simultaneously a person and a brand; the implementation of person branding thus requires balance and consistency in the portrayal of the person versus the brand (Fournier and Eckhardt 2019). However, the drive for one can contradict the other (Whitmer 2021). On one hand, SBC creators, as humans, need to resonate as authentic, intimate, and relatable. On the other hand, they, as brands, are required to be promotional and market driven. Although it is beyond the scope of this study to prescribe how to manage or reconcile this tension, SBC creators should be attentive to the paradoxical imperatives in the contradictions and interdependency between the person and the brand in their branding.

## 7.3. Limitations and Future Research

Our work has limitations. Although we observe aggregated backer information, we do not have individual backer-level data; that is, although we are able to identify the aggregated numbers for a particular creator’s backer base and for fan participation in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c month, we do not know who these backers are or who commented on or liked the creator’s posts. This precludes analysis of the reactions of individual fans or backers in SBC campaigns. Similarly, we only know the net gain or loss in the number of backers for any given creator–month observation. Therefore, we cannot tease apart the number of new backers who are acquired and the number of existing backers who unsubscribe. Once such backer-level data become available, they could provide a richer understanding of the conversion and retention of premium subscribers in SBC. Another promising future direction would be to further explore the contingencies and dynamics behind the information control choices. For example, the importance of both public and private postings in SBC campaigns raises the questions of how SBC creators should balance their public and private postings and when it would be more bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to use public postings rather than private postings (and vice versa). The answers may depend on creators’ tenure on the platform, the size of their current fan/backer bases, the market competition, and other creator- and platform-related factors. Delving more deeply into these contingencies could enhance our ability to explain why the information control effects may be stronger or weaker for certain SBC creators.

## 8. Conclusion

SBC, which has become increasingly popular in recent years, is unique in enabling a creator-centered freemium model for crowdfunding. It allows creators to acquire a steady income stream from fans’ recurring subscription payments to sustain their ongoing creative activities. This crowdfunding model naturally encourages creators to approach their SBC campaigns as branding exercises and themselves as person brands. From a brand management perspective, this study contributes to the understanding of how SBC creators can utilize two information control strategies—earnings concealment and private posting—to develop their person brand and grow both backer base and fan engagement. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings add to the crowdfunding literature and offer implications for SBC platforms and creators.
