# Pay-What-You-Want Pricing in the Digital Product Marketplace: A Feasible Alternative to Piracy Prevention?

Abstract. Pay-what-you-want (PWYW) pricing is a pricing scheme under which buyers pay any amount for a product, often including zero. Digital product <sup>fi</sup>rms may particularly bene<sup>fi</sup>t from PWYW pricing because it can be utilized as an alternative to their costly piracy prevention efforts. In this paper, we examine PWYW pricing based on the established social preference theory where consumers may be concerned with how fairly they are being treated by <sup>fi</sup>rms. Utilizing a two-segment model for consumers (self-interested versus fair minded), we study PWYW pricing against traditional, posted pricing in a monopolistic digital product market. We show that under posted pricing, it is optimal to eliminate piracy when the quality development cost is suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently high. Interestingly, high quality of the illegal copy associated with low quality development cost makes PWYW pricing an effective alternative to posted pricing. We also examine the impact of network externalities on PWYW pricing. Counterintuitively, despite the full market penetration, PWYW pricing invariably becomes less pro<sup>fi</sup>table than posted pricing as network externalities become suf-<sup>fi</sup>ciently strong. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings explain why PWYW pricing is rare in the established digital product marketplace with strong network externalities despite near-zero marginal costs.

## 1. Introduction

In pay-what-you-want (PWYW) pricing, buyers are allowed to pay any amount they desire for a given commodity, including zero. Standard economic theory prescribes that consumers are driven solely by their own material payoffs and will always pay nothing under PWYW pricing, thus driving <sup>fi</sup>rms out of business. Nonetheless, PWYW pricing has been occurring in the marketplace. For example, the popular band Radiohead adopted PWYW pricing with resounding success for their album In Rainbows Tyrangiel (2007). In addition, stand-up comedian Louis C.K. continues to successfully sell his videos and voice <sup>fi</sup>les on his website using a PWYW pricing format (Kleigman 2015). Two restaurants, Just Around the Corner and Panera Bread Cafe, have used a PWYW pricing strategy profitably as well (White 2012, Drummond 2013).

Digital products are especially suited to PWYW pricing because they are prone to piracy, being relatively easy to copy and having a low marginal cost (Gerpott 2017). The beauty of PWYW pricing is that it makes piracy irrelevant, as otherwise pirating consumers can obtain the product for free under a PWYW pricing scheme. As a result, classic posted pricing and PWYW pricing exhibit different cost implications. Under posted pricing, both pirating consumers and piracy prevention efforts incur costs for the <sup>fi</sup>rm, whereas, under PWYW pricing, piracy concerns are mitigated but nonpaying consumers pose a revenue loss.

This paper examines the application of PWYW pricing to digital products and its ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy in eliminating piracy. First, we provide a clear analytical framework for PWYW pricing by building on established social preference theory (Charness and Rabin 2002). In our study, unlike in previous research, a consumer’s voluntary payments are endogenously determined according to fairness concerns embedded in the model. Second, we examine the role of PWYW pricing as a piracy prevention mechanism. Finally, we study the impact of network externalities on the viability of PWYW pricing. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide digital product providers with informed guidelines on how to best protect their intellectual property against piracy while avoiding the need to sacri<sup>fi</sup>ce pro<sup>fi</sup>ts.

## 2. Literature Review

PWYW pricing has been studied in various contexts, including music (Regner and Barria 2009), cinema (Kim et al. 2009), software (Barone et al. 2017), restaurants (Riener and Traxler 2012), travel (Gautier and Klaauw 2012), and amusement parks (Gneezy et al. 2010). Most studies were based on either <sup>fi</sup>eld/laboratory experiments or empirical validations. They all aimed to describe the conditions applicable to PWYW pricing.

Several social preference theories have been advanced to explain nonzero payments, including the theory of altruism (Andreoni and Miller 2002), inequity aversion (Fehr and Schmidt 1999), and intention-based reciprocity (Rabin 1993, Dufwenberg and Kirchsteiger 2004). A further line of investigation considered how market structure affects the pro<sup>fi</sup>tability of PWYW. Schmidt et al. (2015) found, for instance, that the greater the buyer’s valuation and the seller’s cost, the more buyers pay. Chen et al. (2017) argued that PWYW pricing could be considered a consumerdriven price discrimination structure from which <sup>fi</sup>rms can bene<sup>fi</sup>t. It is likely to dominate posted pricing in highly competitive marketplaces. Strategic pricing implications regarding consumers with social preferences have also been examined (Feinberg et al. 2002, Cui et al. 2007, Chen and Cui 2013).

Our model extends the prior research by leveraging one such social preference lens, a theory developed by Charness and Rabin (2002). It models the speci<sup>fi</sup>c utility formulation of consumers through a consideration of varying social preferences and builds on the literature about similar social preference concepts (Loewenstein et al. 1989, Fehr and Schmidt 1999, Bolton and Ockenfels 2000). We adopt this model because it explains an exhaustive set of social preference concepts such as behindness aversion and charity, within a parsimonious and uni<sup>fi</sup>ed framework with only two parameters. Therefore, this model can capture the essence of consumer behavior with respect to social preferences.

Many piracy studies have also examined the optimal strategies for preventing or limiting piracy. For example, digital rights management (DRM), for one, is a technological innovation that can be employed to <sup>fi</sup>ght intellectual property piracy. Chen and Png (2003) compared various policy options including subsidies and penalties as DRM devices. August and Tunca (2008) found that an increased level of DRM enforcement might even hurt pro<sup>fi</sup>ts. Sun et al. (2015) examined an optimal DRM strategy in the presence of piracy uncertainties. Danaher et al. (2010) investigated the impact of channel competition on piracy and found that removing TV content from iTunes increases piracy. Amid the recent shift in the focus of antipiracy efforts from the demand side to the supply side, Dey et al. (2018) developed an economic model to examine the impacts of demand-side and supplyside enforcement on innovation and welfare. Their results suggest that although both types of enforcement have similar impacts on the manufacturer’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t, supply-side enforcement is more desirable than the demand-side enforcement.

Within digital piracy research, this paper is one of the few to propose PWYW pricing as an alternative (or pro<sup>fi</sup>table supplement) to technological piracy prevention measures such as DRM. The work of El Harbi et al. (2014) is the most relevant to our study, as they propose PWYW pricing as an alternative to DRM enforcement in the context of the music industry. In addition to difference in context, our work differs from that of El Harbi et al. (2014) in several ways. First, they examine a case with network externalities, whereas we study cases both with and without net work externalities. They identify positive network externalities as a main driver of viable PWYW pricing, <sup>fi</sup>nding that network externalities work in favor of PWYW over posted pricing. Surprisingly, our results differ from those of El Harbi et al. (2014), as we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the posted pricing bene<sup>fi</sup>ts more from network externalities than PWYW despite the full market penetration under PWYW. Our results provide an explanation for why PWYW is rare in established digital product markets despite strong network externalities. Second, we consider two consumer types grounded in social preference theory. Unlike in the work of El Harbi et al. (2014), where all consumers have access to piracy, we model consumer segments based on access to piracy, in line with the literature (e.g., Chen and Png 2003, Dey et al. 2018). Third, in our model, a consumer’s voluntary payment under PWYW is endogenously determined, whereas it is exogenously given under particular assumptions by El Harbi et al. (2014). Our modeling re<sup>fl</sup>ects reality more accurately by rely ing on conventional social preference theory instead of limiting assumptions. Last, consumer utility parameters change from one pricing mechanism to the other for the same consumer in the paper by El Harbi et al. (2014). Our modeling of consumer utility is consistent across different pricing schemes, which stresses the core pro<sup>fi</sup>t difference between posted and PWYW pricing.

## 3. Model Setup

A monopolistic <sup>fi</sup>rm produces and sells a single prod uct at price p with a zero marginal production cost. The timeline of the game is as follows: (1) the <sup>fi</sup>rm determines the product quality q, where the cost of producing the product of quality q is $c q ^ { 2 }$ with the quality development scale parameter $c > 0 ;$ (2) the <sup>fi</sup>rm sets the price of the product, p; (3) the <sup>fi</sup>rm chooses its piracy prevention effort level, denoted by e, at cost $\eta \hat { e ^ { 2 } }$ , where $\eta > 0$ is the effort scale parameter; (4) consumers simultaneously make their purchase decisions; and (5) pro<sup>fi</sup>ts and consumer utility are realized.

There are two types of consumers: self-interested and fair minded. A self-interested consumer cares only about her own material payoffs, whereas a fairminded consumer considers her own and the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s material payoffs when making purchasing decisions. The speci<sup>fi</sup>c utility formulation of each type of consumer is described below. It is reasonable to assume that under posted pricing, a fair-minded consumer will be reluctant to purchase the product at a price they deem unfair to the <sup>fi</sup>rm and may make some form of nonzero payment under the PWYW pricing regime. The coexistence of self-interested and fairminded consumers in the marketplace also re<sup>fl</sup>ects such real-world circumstances. We operationalize parameter α as the fraction of each consumer type, where α denotes the fraction of fair-minded consumers and the remaining 1 α indicates the fraction of self-interested consumers.

The <sup>fi</sup>rm’s piracy prevention effort level, e, captures the disutility for the users of pirated copies (e.g., Lahiri and Dey 2013, Sun et al. 2015). We formulate this disutility in the following section. Following the extant literature, we assume that the piracy prevention efforts expended by the <sup>fi</sup>rm are costly yet effective (Danaher and Smith 2014; Danaher et al. 2014, 2017), and speci<sup>fi</sup>cally that their cost to the <sup>fi</sup>rm follows a convex structure as in Chen and Png (2003).

## 3.1. Consumer Utility

Next we describe the utility of a self-interested and a fair-minded consumer, respectively. There is a unit mass of consumers, 1 α fraction of which is selfinterested and α fraction of which is fair minded. First, a self-interested consumer considers only her own material payoff, and her utility is expressed as $v q - p ,$ where v refers to consumer’s quality valuation for the product and is uniformly distributed over 0, 1 , that is, v \~ U 0, 1 . A self-interested consumer purchases the product if and only if the consumer’s utility is greater than or equal to zero.

Second, a fair-minded consumer’s utility is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as follows. Contrary to the standard economic theoretical prediction, nonzero payments by consumers are frequently observed under PWYW pricing, and any full-blown model for PWYW pricing must intrinsically capture this phenomenon. We thus adopt Charness and Rabin’s (2002) social preference lens in order to endogenously determine the voluntary payment levels made by consumers. In a nutshell, their theory builds on the literature on social preference theories with similar utility framework (Loewenstein et al. 1989, Fehr and Schmidt 1999, Bolton and Ockenfels 2000) and posits that fair-minded consumers incorporate both their own and the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s material payoffs into their decision making and seek to reduce the difference between their own and other’s payoffs $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ difference aversion). Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the utility of a fairminded consumer with quality valuation $v ,$ also uniformly distributed over 0, 1 , is expressed as

$$
U = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \rho p + (1 - \rho) (v q - p) & \mathrm{if} v q - p > p, \\ \sigma p + (1 - \sigma) (v q - p) & \mathrm{if} v q - p \leq p, \end{array} \right.\tag{3.1}
$$

where $\sigma \le 0$ and $\textstyle { \frac { 1 } { 2 } } < \rho \leq 1$ are called the distributional fairness parameters. Note that a fair-minded consumer’s utility is a weighted sum of the consumer’s own material payoff $( \mathrm { i . e . , } v q - p )$ ) as well as the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s material payoff $( \mathrm { i . e . , } p )$ . The critical feature of this utility representation is that the weights assigned to the two payoff components (one’s own and the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s) depend on whether one’s own payoff is more or less than the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s, that is, whether $v q - p$ is higher or lower than $p .$

On the one hand, if $v q - p > p$ and the consumer’s payoff is more than the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s, the utility reduces to $\rho p + ( 1 - \rho ) ( v q - p )$ . When $\textstyle { \frac { 1 } { 2 } } < \rho \leq 1$ , the consumer’s utility increases in the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s payoff because the consumer’s utility bene<sup>fi</sup>ts more from an increase in the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s material payoff than it loses from an equal decrease in her own payoff. Parameter $\rho$ is thus called the charity parameter. On the other hand, if $v q - p <$ p and the consumer’s payoff is lower than the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s, the utility becomes $\sigma p + ( 1 - \sigma ) ( v q - p )$ . With $\sigma \leq 0 ,$ this utility decreases in the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s payoff, unlike when the consumer is better off than the <sup>fi</sup>rm. Hence, σ is called the behindness aversion parameter. We observe that the model endogenously determines fair-minded consumers’ voluntary payment levels depending on the assigned distributional fairness parameter values.<sup>2</sup>

## 3.2. Consumer Utility Under Posted Pricing

Under posted pricing, we determine the market demand by identifying the fraction of consumers choosing the legal copy in each of the two consumer segments, that is, self-interested (1 α fraction) and fai minded (α fraction). First, a self-interested consumer faces three choices: (1) buying a legal copy at quality q; (2) opting for an illegal, pirated copy with quality δq at the cost of penalty $e ,$ where $\delta \in ( 0 , 1 )$ is the quality discount factor and e is the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s piracy detection effort as described above; or (3) choosing neither. Note that an illegal copy user’s utility decreases as the <sup>fi</sup>rm exerts more effort to detect piracy because the chance of having to pay copyright infringement penalties increases. Then, a self-interested consumer’s utility is described as

$$
U = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} v q - p & \text { if   the   consumer   purchases   a   legal   copy }, \\ \delta v q - e & \text { if   the   consumer   uses   an   illegal   copy }, \\ 0 & \text { if   the   consumer   chooses   neither }. \end{array} \right.\tag{3.2}
$$

For fair-minded consumers, we highlight the standard approach adopted by prior research in piracy and copyright enforcement. Chen and Png (2003), August and Tunca (2008), Jeong et al. (2012), Lahiri and Dey (2013), and Sun et al. (2015) adopted a twosegment approach and divided consumers into ethical and unethical groups, assuming that ethical users would never copy because of their ethical standards, whereas unethical users might copy if the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of copying outweighed the potential risk of detection. In a similar vein, we posit that fair-minded consumers are ethical and would never copy, as using an illegal copy would exclude the <sup>fi</sup>rm from surplus sharing completely, which would violate their ethics. Note that this does not necessarily equate ethical consumers to fair-minded consumers: some ethical consumers may be self-interested and be comfortable choosing to pay zero under PWYW because it is allowed and thus ethical. As a consequence, a fair-minded consumer’s utility becomes identical to Equation (3.1) when the consumer purchases the legal copy or zero when the consumer purchases nothing.

## 4. PWYW Pricing as a Piracy Prevention Mechanism

PWYW pricing makes piracy activities unrewarding because consumers would rather gain access to the product by paying nothing than taking the risk of obtaining an illegal copy. Therefore, when piracy is present in a marketplace, the <sup>fi</sup>rm faces two options. On the one hand, it can sell its product at the posted price and attempt to deter nonpaying pirating consumers via piracy prevention efforts. On the other hand, it can release the product under PWYW pricing and incur the expense of self-interested consumers who pay a price of zero. It is unclear as to when and to what extent the <sup>fi</sup>rm should opt for PWYW pricing instead of posted pricing when both consumer types exist in the market space $( 0 < \alpha < 1 )$ . We analyze equilibrium solutions subject to different pricing strategies amid a mix of consumer preference types in the presence of piracy threats.

Before beginning the analysis, we highlight the experimental results of Charness and Rabin (2002), where the behindness-aversion parameter $\sigma$ is estimated to be not statistically different from zero. This suggests that the price coef<sup>fi</sup>cient becomes –1 when the consumer’s payoff is less than the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s. Thus, we likewise set $\sigma = 0$ as a sensible parametric value in the analyses. Additionally, because the charity parameter $\rho$ does not affect the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s decision variables, we assume $\rho = 1$ without loss of generality. Note that even when $\sigma = 0$ , utility is different from the conventional model, that is, $p$ instead of $v q - p ,$ when the consumer’s payoff is greater than the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s.

## 4.2. PWYW Pricing

Piracy becomes immediately infeasible under PWYW pricing because self-interested consumers have no incentive to use the illegal copy. The self-interested segment would purchase the legal copy at a voluntary payment of zero rather than acquiring an illegal copy of lesser quality at a potential risk of being discovered as a pirate. Thus, all self-interested consumers should opt for the legal copy and pay nothing to the <sup>fi</sup>rm. As a consequence, the only pro<sup>fi</sup>table segment to the <sup>fi</sup>rm under PWYW pricing is the fair-minded consumer and the <sup>fi</sup>rm does not need to expend any effort to prevent piracy, that is, $e _ { w } ^ { * } = 0$ , where the subscript w denotes PWYW pricing. Based on the utility formulation in Section 3, a fair-minded consumer with valuation v purchases the product and makes a voluntary payment of $\frac { v q _ { w } } { 2 }$ . The <sup>fi</sup>rm’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t under PWYW pricing when piracy is present reduces to $\pi _ { w } = \alpha \int _ { 0 } ^ { 1 } \frac { v q _ { w } } { 2 } \mathrm { d } v - c q _ { w } ^ { 2 }$ . The equilibrium solution is obtained as $\begin{array} { r } { q _ { w } ^ { \ast } = \frac { \alpha } { 8 c } , p _ { w } ^ { \ast } ( v ) = \frac { v q _ { w } ^ { \ast } } { 2 } . } \end{array}$ , and $\begin{array} { r } { \pi _ { w } ^ { * } = \frac { \alpha ^ { 2 } } { 6 4 c } . } \end{array}$

Counterintuitively, PWYW pricing always remains pro<sup>fi</sup>table regardless of the parametric conditions. The illegal copy’s quality does not affect PWYW pricing because the quality of the pirated copy is always worse than that of the legal copy. High quality development costs do not affect the feasibility of PWYW pricing because product quality only affects the price consumers voluntarily pay, not the total size of demand. Thus, all fair-minded consumers would buy the product, regardless of product quality, by making a voluntary payment proportional to the product quality. As a consequence, higher quality development costs are less of a setback for the <sup>fi</sup>rm than for some cases of posted pricing (i.e., the piracy case); thus, PWYW pricing always remains pro<sup>fi</sup>table.

Can PWYW be more pro<sup>fi</sup>table than posted pricing? Comparing pro<sup>fi</sup>ts under the two pricing schemes between Lemmas 1 and 2, we propose the following proposition.

Proposition 1. As $c \to \infty ,$ , posted pricing with sufficient effort to prevent piracy becomes the optimal strategy. As $c \to 0$ and $\delta  \dot { 1 } _ { \cdot }$ , PWYW becomes the optimal pricing strategy, whereas as $c \to 0$ and $\delta  0 ,$ , PWYW becomes suboptimal to posted pricing.

Proposition 1 suggests that when the quality development cost is high, a high quality product becomes costlier to produce, and it becomes easier to defend a lower quality product at some level of piracy prevention effort. On the other hand, as the quality development cost decreases, the <sup>fi</sup>rm can easily produce a high quality product and set a high price. Piracy becomes an attractive alternative because of the high quality of the product. If the pirated product has substantially worse quality (low δ), the <sup>fi</sup>rm will choose to sell through posted pricing because it is not costly to implement piracy measures. However, if the pirated product is quite similar to the manufactured product (high δ), the <sup>fi</sup>rm would rather let consumers choose their own price, relying on their fairness mindset.

Next, we compare pro<sup>fi</sup>ts under different pricing strategies through simulation. We set $\eta = 5$ and vary α from 0.2 to 0.5 to study the impact of growing α. Figure 1 illustrates the three regions where posted pricing with and without piracy and PWYW pricing are optimal, respectively. As shown, for all values of α that are simulated, there exists a region where PWYW pricing is more pro<sup>fi</sup>table than either case of posted pricing. In particular, this happens when the quality development cost is small but the illegal copy’s quality is high, because when the quality development cost is small, the <sup>fi</sup>rm is better off improving the prod uct quality and selling at an increased level of voluntary payment under PWYW pricing. At the same time, posted pricing becomes less pro<sup>fi</sup>table as the illegal copy’s quality increases.

It is worthwhile to probe into how PWYW pricing differentially outperforms each case of posted pricing. On the one hand, when δ is high but c is low, PWYW pricing is more pro<sup>fi</sup>table than the piracy case of posted pricing because when δ is high, self-interested consumers have a better alternative to the legal copy, opting for the copycat product. In this case, PWYW pricing uniquely removes piracy from the market while generating more pro<sup>fi</sup>ts as well.

On the other hand, even when piracy does not exist under posted pricing and self-interested consumers purchase only the legal copy $\begin{array} { r } { ( p < \frac { e } { \delta } ) _ { \ast } } \end{array}$ , higher δ would mean the <sup>fi</sup>rm has to charge an even lower price or increase its piracy prevention effort level at a cost in order to keep piracy out of the market. Hence, when δ is high, PWYW pricing becomes more pro<sup>fi</sup>table because its pro<sup>fi</sup>t is unaffected by δ. Note that even though no piracy is observed in both posted and PWYW pricing, these no-piracy outcomes are driven by different causes. Under posted pricing, piracy does not occur because the <sup>fi</sup>rm invests in a certain positive level of piracy prevention effort $( e > \delta p )$ to disincentivize pirating activities. Contrariwise, under PWYW pricing, piracy does not exist even when the <sup>fi</sup>rm expends no piracy prevention effort. The lack of piracy under PWYW pricing is a purely consumer-driven outcome, as consumers voluntarily choose the legal copy of better quality, which is available at a price of zero, even with no piracy prevention measures in place.

## 4.3. Network Externalities

Network externalities play an important role in the digital product marketplace. Network externalities have been an extensively studied topic in the information systems literature (e.g., Conner and Rumelt 1991, Shy and Thisse 1999, Kim et al. 2018). Conner and Rumelt (1991) showed that software providers bene<sup>fi</sup>t from piracy in the presence of positive network externalities. They argue that network externalities exist in software because the software vendor’s incentive to develop complementary products increases as more people use the product. Thus, growth in the user base increases opportunities to economize on postpurchase costs. Smartphone apps may serve as another example that manifests network externalities. The literature has generally found that positive network externalities open up more pro<sup>fi</sup>t opportunities, as pirating users can positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence the demand for legal copies. Thus, piracy may help the <sup>fi</sup>rm charge a higher price under posted pricing because legal copy users bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the network externalities generated by pirates.

However, given that market demand is always fully covered under PWYW pricing, which in turn will positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence the users’ utility to a maximal extent, network externalities might favor PWYW pricing more than posted pricing. It is therefore not clear as to how network externalities interact with PWYW pricing. For this reason, the impact of network externalities on PWYW pricing deserves further investigation. In this section, we seek to understand how the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s choice between posted and PWYW pricing is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the extent of network externalities. To investigate this, we extend our model setting to incorporate network externalities, following the conventional approach adopted in the literature, such as Shy and Thisse (1999).

With positive network externalities, a self-interested consumer’s utility under posted pricing is

$$
U = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} v q - p + \mu D _ {n} & \text { if   the   consumer   purchases   a   legal   copy }, \\ \delta v q - e & \text { if   the   consumer   uses   an   illegal   copy }, \\ 0 & \text { if   the   consumer   chooses   neither }. \end{array} \right.
$$

where $\mu > 0$ is a scale parameter that measures the strength of network externalities, and $D _ { n }$ denotes the size of the demand that induces network externalities. In the piracy literature, network externalities are modeled as an integral factor driving <sup>fi</sup>rm decisions because software providers may bene<sup>fi</sup>t from piracy when positive network externalities exist (Conner and Rumelt 1991). The larger the user base, the greater the software vendor’s incentive to develop complementary products. However, not all digital products are characterized by the same level of network externalities. For example, movies may exhibit weak network externalities, as one person’s review may affect another consumer’s choice of movie but usually not to a critical extent. Conversely, some dig ital products, such as popular online games and social network platforms, exhibit strong network externalities.

We assume that the size of demand, $D _ { n } ,$ , which affects the magnitude of network externalities, is the sum of demand from both legal and illegal copy users. Thus, illegal copy users help increase positive network externalities (Conner and Rumelt 1991). For example, a higher number of total users can help a product to be adopted as the industry standard, which, in turn, increases willingness to pay for the legal copy users.

Note, however, that the bene<sup>fi</sup>t to individual consumers from network size materializes only for the legal copy users because these bene<sup>fi</sup>ts often take on some form of support services offered by the <sup>fi</sup>rm, which illegal copy users cannot access. Thus, it is reasonable to assume that positive network externalities impact only legal copy users, but not illegal copy users. In sum, legal copy users both contribute to and enjoy positive network externalities, whereas illegal copy users contribute to network externalities but receive no bene<sup>fi</sup>t from them.

Next we consider the segment of fair-minded consumers. Consistent with the previous section, we set σ 0. Recall that all fair-minded consumers are ethical and thus choose between buying the legal copy and not buying at all. A fair-minded consumer’s utility is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as

$$
U = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} p & \text { if   the   consumer   purchases   a   legal } \\ & \text { copy   and   } v q - p + \mu D _ {n} > p, \\ v q - p + \mu D _ {n} & \text { if   the   consumer   purchases   a } \\ & \text { legal   copy   and   } v q - p + \mu D _ {n} \leq p, \\ 0 & \text { if   the   consumer   does   not } \\ & \text { purchase   a   legal   copy. } \end{array} \right.
$$

Based on this framework, we obtain the following propositions on posted and PWYW pricing.

Proposition 2. When piracy occurs under the equilibrium solution for posted pricing, the optimal profit $\pi ^ { * }$ decreases with the quality of the illegal copy δ , the piracy prevention effort cost $( \eta ) .$ , and the product quality development cost (c), but increases with the number of fair-minded consumers α and network externalities μ . That is, $\begin{array} { r } { \frac { \partial \pi ^ { * } } { \partial \delta } < 0 , \frac { \partial \pi ^ { * } } { \partial \eta } < 0 , } \end{array}$ $\begin{array} { r } { \frac { \partial \pi ^ { * } } { \partial c } < 0 , \frac { \partial \pi ^ { * } } { \partial \alpha } > 0 . } \end{array}$ , and $\begin{array} { r } { \frac { \partial \pi ^ { * } } { \partial \mu } > 0 } \end{array}$

Proposition 2 shows how the optimal pro<sup>fi</sup>t under posted pricing with piracy changes in the underlying parameters. We observe that the pro<sup>fi</sup>ts decrease as the illegal copy’s quality (δ) increases, as the piracy prevention effort becomes costlier (η), and as the quality development costs increase (c). These results are quite intuitive and consistent with those of previous analyses conducted without network externalities as well. For example, the higher the illegal copy’s quality, the less incentive consumers have to choose the legal copy, hence reducing the pro<sup>fi</sup>t. Conversely, the more fair-minded consumers there are in the population (α), the higher the pro<sup>fi</sup>t from posted pricing, because fair-minded consumers do not consider the illegal copy at all and thus are a better revenue source for the <sup>fi</sup>rm. Most importantly, we observe that pro<sup>fi</sup>ts increase as the strength of network externalities increases (μ). This suggests that network externalities indeed help both PWYW and posted pricing, which further bolsters our initial motivation to determine which pricing strategy bene<sup>fi</sup>ts more from network externalities in this model extension.

PWYW pricing immediately undermines piracy because consumers have no incentive to use an illega copy under this pricing scheme. As a result, the <sup>fi</sup>rm has no incentive to expend any effort on piracy prevention and thus exerts zero effort $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , e _ { w } ^ { \ast } = 0 )$ . Every consumer would purchase the product under PWYW pricing; hence, the demand size that affects network externalities becomes the entire population $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , D _ { n } =$ 1). Hence, network externalities positively shift every consumer’s utility by $\mu D _ { n } = \mu$ . Thus, a fair-minded consumer with valuation v makes a voluntary payment of $\frac { v q _ { w } + \mu } { 2 }$ . The <sup>fi</sup>rm’s pro<sup>fi</sup>t under PWYW pricing becomes $\pi _ { w } = \alpha \int _ { 0 } ^ { 1 } { \frac { v q _ { w } + \mu } { 2 } } ~ \mathrm { d } v - c q _ { w } ^ { 2 }$ , and the equilibrium solutions are $\begin{array} { r } { q _ { w } ^ { * } = \frac { \alpha } { 8 c } , p _ { w } ^ { * } ( v ) = \frac { \alpha v + 8 c \mu } { 1 6 c } } \end{array}$ , and $\begin{array} { r } { \pi _ { w } ^ { * } = \frac { \alpha \mu } { 2 } + \frac { \alpha ^ { 2 } } { 6 4 c } . } \end{array}$

This result shows that under PWYW pricing, each consumer’s voluntary payment level increases as network externalities become stronger, leading to higher pro<sup>fi</sup>t. Note that demand is always fully covered under PWYW pricing, and thus every consumer enjoys the full bene<sup>fi</sup>t $\mu$ from network externalities. This extra utility gain is then transferred to pricing power, which in turn increases pro<sup>fi</sup>ts. Because the demand is always full, irrespective of quality, the optimal quality remains unaffected by the strength of network externalities. Interestingly, the consumer’s utility gain from network externalities, which is of size $\mu ,$ is not fully transferred to price; that is, if $\mu$ increases by one, the voluntary payment $p _ { w } ^ { \ast } ( v )$ increases by one-half for any valuation v, because fair-minded consumers voluntarily choose the payment level that they consider fair and pay extra only half as much of this utility gain. We further propose the following proposition regarding network externalities.

Proposition 3. The profit from PWYW is lower than the profit from posted pricing $i f \mu$ is greater than ${ \frac { \alpha ^ { 2 } } { 3 2 c ( 2 - \alpha ) } } ,$ that $\begin{array} { r } { i s , \overline { { \pi ^ { * } } } > \pi _ { w } ^ { * } \forall \mu > \frac { \alpha ^ { 2 } } { 3 2 c ( 2 - \alpha ) } . } \end{array}$

Proposition 3 indicates that pro<sup>fi</sup>t increases due to network externalities under posted pricing are higher than those under PWYW when the network externalities are suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently strong. Despite the full market penetration under PWYW, the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from network externalities are greater with posted pricing than with PWYW. The intuition behind this result is as follows. The bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from network externalities are fully re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the optimal price in the case of posted pricing, whereas they are only partially re-<sup>fl</sup>ected in the optimal price under PWYW. Thus, even though the market is always fully covered under PWYW, network externalities work more in favor of posted pricing if they are suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently high. Our paper counters the result of El Harbi et al. (2014) that PWYW is more pro<sup>fi</sup>table than posted pricing in the presence of network externalities. In their work, a consumer’s voluntary payment is exogenously given, whereas it is endogenously determined in our model. This modeling difference explains the contrast <sup>fi</sup>ndings in the two papers. Notably, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings can explain why PWYW pricing is rare in mature digital product markets where network externalities are strong. Interestingly, the results show that start-up digital product <sup>fi</sup>rms with a noncritical mass of consumers would be wise to consider adopting PWYW for effortless piracy prevention.

Figure 2 illustrates how network externalities affect pro<sup>fi</sup>t levels as well as decision variables under the two different pricing schemes when $\alpha = 0 . 4 , \delta = 0 . 9 ,$ $\eta = 3 ,$ , and $c = 0 . 0 1$ . Consistent with the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of prior studies on network externalities, pro<sup>fi</sup>ts increase alongside the size of network externalities for both pricing schemes. Note that when network externalities are limited, PWYW can be more pro<sup>fi</sup>table than posted pricing, which is consistent with the previous section’s result without network externalities. As net work externalities grow, however, pro<sup>fi</sup>ts from posted pricing eventually become greater. It is also interesting that <sup>fi</sup>rms using posted pricing provide poorer quality products. Several studies have examined the impact of enforcement on product quality, generating mixed results. Some of the research shows that <sup>fi</sup>rms lose incentives to innovate and thus lower their product quality when the market lacks enforcement (Bae and Choi 2006, Jain 2008). By contrast, Lahiri and Dey (2013) and Dey et al. (2018) argue that lower demand side enforcement $( \mathrm { i . e . } ,$ , penalizing illegal consumption) may lead to higher quality, because of the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s ability to leverage a higher product quality to compete against piracy. In our framework, PWYW pricing can be viewed as an extreme case of a lack of enforcement. The higher product quality under PWYW pricing suggests that our results are consistent with the literature’s <sup>fi</sup>nding that lower demand-side enforcement can lead to improved product quality. Moreover, when the market demonstrates strong network externalities, the <sup>fi</sup>rm may lose the incentive to <sup>fi</sup>ght piracy, allowing it to fully utilize the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of network externalities. This explains why piracy prevention efforts decrease under posted pricing as network externalities become stronger.

## 5. Conclusion

In this paper, we analytically examined PWYW pricing in a monopolistic digital product market and considered its effectiveness as a strategic alternative to piracy prevention, which is often costly to the <sup>fi</sup>rm. We built upon social preference theory, whereby some consumers are fair minded. Unlike prior studies, we examined PWYW pricing through the lens of social preference theory, in which consumers with social preference endogenously choose utility-maximizing voluntary payments. We further compared PWYW pricing to posted pricing both with and without network externalities.

Although PWYW is not yet a mainstream pricing strategy, this paper examined its potential value as a feasible alternative to piracy prevention for digital products, given their ever-increasing vulnerability to piracy threats. Several implications regarding management practice can be deduced from our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. First, if the quality development cost is high, the <sup>fi</sup>rm may be better off implementing posted pricing and investing in piracy prevention measures. Second, when the pirated copy’s quality is suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently high and the quality development cost is relatively low, the <sup>fi</sup>rm may comfortably opt to pursue PWYW pricing. Finally, PWYW may lead to greater pro<sup>fi</sup>ts if network externalities are low, making it a particularly suitable strategy for start-up <sup>fi</sup>rms with a noncritical mass of consumers.
