# Seems Legit: An Investigation of the Assessing and Sharing of Unverifiable Messages on Online Social Networks

Abstract. Unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages abound on the Internet. Why do people share messages they cannot verify? This study develops an in-depth understanding of how messages containing unveri<sup>fi</sup>able product information differ and why users share such messages over online social networks (OSNs). We develop a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation that identi<sup>fi</sup>es different types of unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages that OSN users encounter prior to the release of a new product and conduct two studies to investigate the resharing of true (information leak) and false (ru mor) messages originating from unof<sup>fi</sup>cial channels. We contend that such differences (true vs. false) are likely to result in differentiating message characteristics. Employing a dual-processing theoretical lens, we further hypothesize that because these messages are unveri<sup>fi</sup>able, recipients will take a holistic approach and rely on both content (plausibility) and noncontent (vividness, sender credibility) message characteristics when assessing the message. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, when faced with an unveri<sup>fi</sup>able message, the presence of content characteristics ampli<sup>fi</sup>es the effect of noncontent characteristics, suggesting that plausibility enhances the value of vividness and sender credibility. These characteristics jointly help recipients assess a message’s diagnosticity and novelty, which are the primary psychological factors driving the reshare decision. We employ a multimethod approach with Study 1 leveraging secondary data collected from Twitter to assess objective behavior and Study 2 employing a controlled experiment to assess psychological processes. Together, the studies offer compelling evidence in support of our model, indicating that leaks and rumors ex hibit different message characteristics; that recipients employ a synergistic processing strategy when assessing unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages; and that unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages are reshared when they are perceived to be helpful or novel. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings from this research have impli cations for both research and practice.

## Introduction

Six weeks before the iPhone 6S was of<sup>fi</sup>cially announced, Forbes.com reported that, according to a Chinese web forum, the base model of Apple’s upcoming smartphone would have 32 gigabytes (GB) of storage. Three weeks later, 9to5mac.com, a news outlet that provides breaking news about Apple products, reported that packaging purportedly from the iPhone 6S showed the base model would have a storage capacity of 16 GB. Though the device had not yet been announced and neither claim was veri<sup>fi</sup>able at the time it emerged on Twitter, both would be seen by millions and retweeted by thousands of Twitter users, but one would turn out to be true and the other false.

The spread of such unveri<sup>fi</sup>able product messages can create multiple problems for the recipient, the manufacturer, and the platform. First, unveri<sup>fi</sup>ed claims can create false expectations for a product. For example, one’s expectation that a new device has double the storage capacity may lead to a poor adoption decision, increased regret, or lower satisfaction. Second, as false claims spread, they can become ingrained in the social consciousness, regardless of veracity. For example, in 1978, McDonald’s was falsely accused of using worm meat as hamburger <sup>fi</sup>ller (Tybout et al. 1981). Though the claim was denied and discredited, belief in the claim persists (Saul 2014). Third, false claims perpetuate uncertainty. One need not believe a rumor to be af fected by it. If a claim is plausible, the mere existence of the claim is enough to nurture doubt in the recipient and excite whatever anxieties predate receipt of the rumor (DiFonzo et al. 1994). Fourth, the spread of false claims jeopardizes the reputation of online social networks (OSNs) and may have legal or <sup>fi</sup>nancial consequences (Needleman 2021). As OSNs grapple with this, many struggle to apply an appropriate level of editorial control in response to the spread of false claims on their networks.

To date, despite the many problems associated with the sharing of unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages on OSNs, there is little understanding of how recipients assess such unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages and what would entice them to share information they cannot verify. The literature has well established that OSNs offer many content sharing affordances (Karahanna et al. 2018) and provide an unrivaled platform for sharing information with others (Chakravarty et al. 2010, Xia et al. 2011, Yi et al. 2017). Also, we know well how users assess high-quality information and why it is valuable (Petter et al. 2013). Unfortunately, we still know little about why low-quality and possibly false information is so prevalent on these platforms, and why users might value information they cannot verify, much less why they would share it with others. In an effort to advance the <sup>fi</sup>eld’s understanding of these issues, this research explores the phenomenon of unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages and the user’s decision to share such messages. We are motivated by two research questions (RQ): (1) How do true and false unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages exhibit distinguishable characteristics? (2) Why do people share unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages on OSNs?

To answer these questions, we develop a theoretical model based on a dual-processing perspective (Chaiken 1980, Petty and Cacioppo 1986, Kahneman 2011), which explains why users reshare unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages. We begin by introducing the concept of unveri<sup>fi</sup>ability and proposing a conceptualization that discriminates between different types of unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages based on their channel of origin and veracity. In this research, we focus on the assessment and spread of true and false unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages that originate from unof<sup>fi</sup>cial channels. We refer to these messages as rumors (false) and leaks (true) and argue that the fundamental differences between the two in<sup>fl</sup>uence the presence/absence of content (message plausibility) and noncontent (sender credibility and message vividness) message characteristics. Such characteristics help recipients assess the diagnosticity and novelty of the message, which consequently affect one’s decision to reshare an unveri<sup>fi</sup>able message.

We employ a multimethod, multistudy approach to evaluate our theoretical model (Venkatesh et al. 2016). Using Twitter data collected prior to the release of the iPhone 6S, Study 1 aims at differentiating rumors and leaks based on the presence of content and noncontent characteristics and showing how those characteristics affect user resharing (retweeting) behavior. In Study 2, we extend the results of Study 1 by employing an experimental design to investigate the mechanisms by which message characteristics affect user perceptions and the subsequent reshare intention. The results from Study 1 and Study 2 largely con<sup>fi</sup>rm our hypotheses. In particular, Study 1 veri<sup>fi</sup>es that rumors and leaks exhibit discriminating message characteristics and that the presence of these characteristics is predictive of resharing behavior. Study 2 complements Study 1 by revealing the importance of perceived diagnosticity and novelty in motivating the reshare decision. The two studies jointly suggest that OSN users rely on systematic and heuristic processing to assess unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages. These processing strategies work together such that if a recipient believes a message to be plausible (systematic processing), the effects of noncontent characteristics—sender credibility and message vividness (heuristic processing)—on resharing behavior are ampli<sup>fi</sup>ed.

This research contributes to the <sup>fi</sup>eld in several ways. First, it offers a rigorous conceptualization of unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages circulating prior to the release of a new product, clarifying conceptual boundaries that exist among current terms used in the literature. In so doing, we highlight the concept of leaks—which have received little attention in information system (IS) research—and compare them with rumors. Second, this research identi<sup>fi</sup>es three major message characteristics that message recipients use to assess the diagnosticity and novelty of unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages. Third, we develop a theoretical framework that explains why recipients reshare messages they cannot verify.

## Conceptual Development: Unverifiable Messages

To better understand unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages, it is necessary to begin with the concept of veri<sup>fi</sup>ability. Message veri<sup>fi</sup>ability refers to the extent to which the content of a message can be con<sup>fi</sup>rmed by means that are independent of its source and available to its receiver (Rosenthal 1971). Veri<sup>fi</sup>ability is believed to be an essential component of effective communication, as the presumption that a message can be veri<sup>fi</sup>ed invites belief in its recipient (Rosenthal 1971). In fact, researchers have shown that veri<sup>fi</sup>able messages are easier to assess, more believable, and lead to better decision outcomes than less veri<sup>fi</sup>able or unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages (Jain et al. 2000, Lee et al. 2016).

Though veri<sup>fi</sup>ability is believed to be an important factor in decision making, IS research has largely overlooked this aspect of information quality. Many have argued that dimensions such as accuracy, precision, currency, timeliness, suf<sup>fi</sup>ciency, and understandability (DeLone and McLean 1992, Wang and Strong 1996, Lee et al. 2002, Petter et al. 2013) are essential components of high-quality information, but only a few have considered veri<sup>fi</sup>ability (Alkhattabi et al. 2010, 2011). Part of this oversight is likely due to the context in which these studies occur and the role early information systems played: earlier studies could assume veri-<sup>fi</sup>ability because information systems primarily served to collect, store, and retrieve task-relevant organizational data sourced from of<sup>fi</sup>cial sources (Goodhue 1995, Goodhue and Thompson 1995). Independent veri<sup>fi</sup>cation is less relevant in such a setting. However, with the emergence of social technologies and usergenerated content, the foundation of this assumption has weakened. Consequently, as information is increasingly shared through unof<sup>fi</sup>cial channels, IS researchers have begun to acknowledge that the ability to verify information plays an important role in the user’s assessment of and response to information (Nicolaou and McKnight 2006, Nicolaou et al. 2013, Torres et al. 2018).

This research eschews the implicit assumption that information is veri<sup>fi</sup>able by examining the spread of messages that are inherently unveri<sup>fi</sup>able. We leverage a cross-discipline perspective on veri<sup>fi</sup>ability to inform our conceptualization and de<sup>fi</sup>ne an unveri<sup>fi</sup>able message as a message whose contents cannot be con<sup>fi</sup>rmed by means independent of the message source. This definition is informed by works on rumors (Rosnow 1991, DiFonzo and Bordia 2007) and product marketing (Jain et al. 2000, Jain and Posavac 2001) that explore issues related to how recipients respond to messages they cannot verify.

Despite an apparent negative connotation, unveri<sup>fi</sup>- able messages do not always arise from unof<sup>fi</sup>cial sources and are not necessarily false. In fact, most prerelease information about an upcoming product—even that from the manufacturer—is inherently unveri<sup>fi</sup>able because there rarely exists an independent party who can attest to the validity of the claims. Further, though manufacturers are typically reliable sources of information about their products, Bayus et al. (2001) note that they have been known to mislead and that such practices are not unusual in <sup>fi</sup>ercely competitive markets where premature announcements have been used to dissuade or distract competitors (Farrell and Saloner 1986). Conversely, messages from unof<sup>fi</sup>cial sources may be true as accurate-but-uncorroborated messages are frequently shared prior to the release of a new product. Thus, we use channel of origin and veracity to discriminate between four types of unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages (Table 1) that circulate prior to the release of a new product: preannouncements, vaporware, leaks, and rumors. Though all four types could contribute to our understanding of the spread of unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages, we believe that those messages originating from unof<sup>fi</sup>cial channels are of greater concern. First, manufacturers have little control over user-generated messages (i.e., messages from unof<sup>fi</sup>cial channels). From a strategic standpoint, it is important for organizations to develop an understanding of the forces beyond their control so that they may plan for and respond appropriately to the emergence and spread of such messages. Second, just as of<sup>fi</sup>cial preannouncements affect potential consumers (Eliashberg and Robertson 1988), these user-generated messages are likely to spread and have similar effects. To date, there is little understanding of how users interpret and respond to such unof<sup>fi</sup>cial and unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages. Finally, whereas of<sup>fi</sup>cial communications are comparatively rare and tend to appear nearer to the product launch, user-generated unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages are common on OSNs and circulate at different points in time throughout the prerelease period. For these reasons, the remainder of this article focuses on the assessment and resharing of true (leaks) and false (rumors) unveri<sup>fi</sup>- able messages from unof<sup>fi</sup>cial channels.

In this research, we refer to a rumor as a false claim about a forthcoming product and an information leak (leak) as private and true information shared through unof<sup>fi</sup>cial channels by entities that are authorized to access but not disseminate that information (see Online Appendix B for a detailed discussion of leaks as privacy violations). Because true (leak) and false (rumor) messages differ in veracity, it is likely they exhibit different characteristics. For example, Vosoughi et al. (2018) found that true and false messages often differ in the emotional content of the message and others contend that false messages are more likely to contain exaggerated claims (Miller 2005, DiFonzo and Bordia 2007, Jung 2011). Also, false messages tend to be more ambiguous whereas true messages tend to be more speci<sup>fi</sup>c (DiFonzo and Bordia 2007, Oh et al. 2013). These discriminating characteristics are likely to have distal effects such that recipients will respond differently to different types of messages. For example, false messages exhibit traveling patterns that differ from those of true messages (Vosoughi et al. 2018), suggesting that veracity, though unknowable, is somehow inferred by senders.

In summary, veri<sup>fi</sup>ability often serves as a shortcut to establishing trust between senders and recipients. In IS research, veri<sup>fi</sup>ability is often assumed, but the rise of social media and user-generated content necessitates an investigation of recipients’ responses to messages that are not veri<sup>fi</sup>able. To aid such an investigation, we discriminate along the lines of channel of origin and message veracity and focus on two types of unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages (i.e., rumors and leaks) OSN users are likely to encounter prior to the release of a new product. Given the inherent differences between rumors and leaks, it is likely they will exhibit discriminating characteristics and prompt differentiating reactions, as we will explain.

## Theoretical Background Dual-Process Information Processing

We leverage dual-process information processing theory (Chaiken 1980) to explain how OSN users assess unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages and why such messages are shared. The dual-processing theory posits that messages are assessed through two distinct processing strategies: systematic and heuristic (Chaiken 1980, Petty and Cacioppo 1986, Kahneman 2011). When employing a systematic approach, individuals judge a message by “actively attending to and cognitively elaborating persuasive argumentation” (Chaiken and Maheswaran 1994, p. 460). Systematic processing tends to be deliberate and cognitively demanding. Comparatively, heuristic processing requires less effort as it relies on noncontent heuristics or cues (e.g., sender reputation and message format) to assess the message (Chaiken 1980). Despite the distinction between the two strategies, recipients may sometimes use both strategies to assess messages “when message content or other individuating information is ambiguous and thus amenable to differential interpretation” (Chaiken and Maheswaran 1994, p. 469; Chen et al. 1996).

We maintain that the processing strategies will have a synergistic (i.e., interactive) effect on the decision to reshare unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages. Because unveri-<sup>fi</sup>able messages contain information of unknowable veracity, recipients may not feel con<sup>fi</sup>dent in their systematic assessment of the message and may leverage additional information gleaned from noncontent characteristics. Table 2 highlights several studies that have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed both content and noncontent message characteristics that are likely to be in<sup>fl</sup>uential in assessing unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages. In particular, plausibility aids in systematic processing as it refers to the extent to which information is conceptually coherent and consistent with prior knowledge (Connell and Keane 2006). Absent veri<sup>fi</sup>able information, plausibility improves the recipient’s judgment of the message by con<sup>fi</sup>rming what the recipient believes is likely to be true of the product. However, the uncertainty inherent in unveri-<sup>fi</sup>able messages entices recipients to also consider noncontent characteristics such as message vividness—the extent to which messages create a sensorially rich mediated environment that provides additional information (Steuer 1992, Hess et al. 2009)—and sender credibility—the extent to which a recipient perceives the sender to be trustworthy (Cheung et al. 2012). Though these noncontent characteristics do not convey information directly related to the content of the message, they are likely to trigger a heuristic judgment of message validity.

Though a dual-process perspective emphasizes the role of systematic and heuristic processing in assessing message validity, the theory’s primary concern is in explaining how message characteristics in<sup>fl</sup>uence attitude formation within the recipient (Chaiken and Maheswaran 1994). That is, whether a recipient processes messages through careful consideration, simple and intuitive heuristics, or some combination thereof, these efforts are “used to guide subsequent attitudes, judgments, and behaviors” (Chaiken and Ledgerwood 2012, p. 247). As our study focuses on the assessment and spread of unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages on OSNs, we are therefore interested in the set of judgments most likely to in<sup>fl</sup>uence resharing behaviors. As highlighted in Table 2, prior research suggests two such judgments—novelty and diagnosticity—as important beliefs that presage resharing. Although these studies do not focus on the sharing of unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages per se, many (e.g., Herr et al. 1991, Kempf and Smith 1998, Jiang and Benbasat 2004) involve assessing issues about which the recipient lacks direct knowledge about the content of the message. Thus, there is reason to believe that diagnosticity—the extent to which information is helpful for assessing a product (Jiang and Benbasat 2004)—and novelty—the extent to which information is new or exciting (Wells et al. 2010)—play an essential role in the decision to reshare unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages.

Perceived diagnosticity is valuable because it alleviates uncertainty about an upcoming product, a common problem consumers face before a product is released (Pavlou et al. 2007). Also, perceived diagnosticity has been shown to in<sup>fl</sup>uence product intentions (Herr et al. 1991, Kempf and Smith 1998, Biehal and Sheinin 2007, Pavlou et al. 2007), decision outcomes (Yi et al. 2017, Ghasemaghaei and Calic 2019), and information adoption (Filieri 2015). Similarly, novel information is valuable because it represents the introduction of new information into the void that exists when one has less information about an upcoming product than they need or want. As with diagnostic information, novel information has been shown to be a key determinant of information adoption (Daley and Kendall 1964, Gray et al. 2011, Fang 2014, Shi et al. 2014), virality (Berger and Milkman 2012), and product intentions (Edwards and Gangadharbatla 2001, Sundar and Kalyanaraman 2004).

In conclusion, when presented with an unveri<sup>fi</sup>able message, recipients employ both systematic and heuristic processing strategies to assess the message. Because unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages contain inherently ambiguous information, recipients are likely to employ a synergistic strategy whereby they supplement their judgment of message plausibility with an assessment of noncontent characteristics such as message vividness and sender credibility. The presence of these factors alters the recipients’ attitude toward resharing the message to the extent that they <sup>fi</sup>nd the message diagnostic or novel. We employ a multistudy, multimethod approach to examine this framework. Study 1 uses unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages about the iPhone 6S shared on Twitter prior to its release to examine the extent to which the two types of unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages (rumor and leak) exhibit differentiating message characteristics and how those characteristics affect resharing behavior (i.e., retweets). Because the data from Twitter can only tell us what happened and not why it happened, we supplement Study 1 with a controlled experiment in which we examine the psychological factors (i.e., perceived diagnosticity and perceived novelty) that in<sup>fl</sup>uence the intention to reshare. We focus each study on a different technology to avoid potential confounding effects from product attributes and to cross-validate our <sup>fi</sup>ndings in different settings. Using archival data analysis and an experiment together maximizes the internal and external validity of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings and generates more robust support for our research model (Venkatesh et al. 2013, 2016).

## Study 1: Assessing

## Unverifiable Messages

In Study 1, we investigate the extent to which rumors and leaks differ in their presentation of content and noncontent characteristics and the extent to which those characteristics are associated with resharing behavior. Figure 2 illustrates the research model for this study. Rumor theory maintains that individuals share unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages because such messages help them make sense of an uncertain situation (Allport and Postman 1947, Shibutani 1966, DiFonzo and Bordia 2007). A dual-processing perspective contends that such an assessment is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the presence of content and noncontent message characteristics and that such characteristics would consequently affect the reshare decision. Given that leaks and rumors differ in veracity, there is reason to believe that each would exhibit discriminating levels of plausibility, vividness, and sender credibility. For example, acceptance of a rumor often depends upon ambiguity (Allport and Postman 1946, Rosnow 1991) because the inclusion of speci<sup>fi</sup>c details can serve as a clue that a message is false (DiFonzo and Bordia 2007). This is not the case for leaks because they emerge from true information. So, whereas rumors are fabricated, leaks are relayed, and this distinction is likely to result in detectable differences in content and noncontent characteristics. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, leaks are likely to appear more plausible, include more vivid information, and be shared by more credible senders than rumors. In the following sections, we explain why these differences exist and why they are likely to in<sup>fl</sup>uence resharing behavior.

## Hypothesis Development

Content Assessment: Systematic Processing. During systematic processing, recipients actively engage the content of a message for the purpose of assessing the validity of its claims (Chaiken 1980). Prior to the release of a new product when the message is unveri-<sup>fi</sup>able and the veracity unknowable, a systematic assessment focuses on the message’s plausibility (i.e., could this information be valid?) rather than veracity (i.e., is this information valid?). Because plausible messages are those that are consistent with prior knowledge, assessing plausibility is believed to in volve two steps whereby the recipient <sup>fi</sup>rst draws on prior relevant knowledge and then assesses the extent to which the message content is consistent with that knowledge (Connell and Keane 2006). Plausibility is essential in helping recipients make sense of information when complete information is unavailable (Weick et al. 2005).

Though both rumors and leaks are shared through unof<sup>fi</sup>cial channels and may exhibit an implausible chain of communication (e.g., manufacturer X would never allow pictures like this), there is reason to believe they will exhibit distinct levels of plausibility. Plausible messages tend to include more details, more context, and more veri<sup>fi</sup>able sources, whereas implausible messages tend to hew to a more simplistic account (Vrij et al. 2021). Because leaks originate from the manufacture or its supply chain, they are more likely to include details and nuance that would be consistent with the recipients’ knowledge, thus enhancing message plausibility. Conversely, rumors are unlikely to exhibit high levels of plausibility because fabricating details is cognitively demanding and likely to lead to detection (Nahari et al. 2014). Therefore, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 1. Leaks will exhibit higher levels of message plausibility than rumors.

Noncontent Assessment: Heuristic Processing. Heuristic processing relies on noncontent characteristics to assess message validity. Heuristics are often generic mental rules (e.g., experts are usually correct) that are applied as a shortcut to judgment formation. Noncontent characteristics such as sender credibility and message vividness are likely to inform the recipient’s judgment when the message is ambiguous (Chaiken and Maheswaran 1994), as unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages are.

Sender credibility refers to the extent to which a recipient perceives the sender to be trustworthy (Cheung et al. 2012) and has long been considered an important indicator of message persuasiveness (Chaiken 1980). Researchers have shown that recipients tend to believe that credible senders are trustworthy and possess some expertise relative to the message topic (Hovland and Weiss 1951, Fragale and Heath 2004, Pornpitakpan 2004). Stemming from the experts are usually correct heuristic, a credible sender serves as an ef<sup>fi</sup>cient cogni tive shortcut for assessing the veracity of a message (Tversky and Kahneman 1974, Watts and Zhang 2008).

Message vividness refers to the extent to which a message is impactful and memorable. Because vivid information attracts attention by being “(a) emotionally interesting, (b) concrete and imagery-provoking, and (c) proximate in a sensory, temporal, or spatial way” (Nisbett and Ross 1980, p. 45), many believe it is encoded in a way that makes it a more accessible heu ristic at the point of recall (Tversky and Kahneman 1973, Kisielius and Sternthal 1984). Thus, messages are not objectively vivid but are vivid to the extent a recipient perceives that the message employs multimedia representations or sensational language (e.g., Jiang and Benbasat 2004, 2007).

Leaks and rumors should exhibit different levels of heuristic characteristics. First, rumors are likely to contain generic information because fabricating speci<sup>fi</sup>c details is cognitively demanding—a burden that results in the ambiguity typically found in rumors (Arif et al. 2017)—and is likely to lead to detection (Nahari et al. 2014). Conversely, because leaks emerge from valid product information, they are likely to contain rich depictions of the product. For instance, whereas a rumor may mention generic features related to camera quality such as megapixel count, a leak could include more vivid information such as diagrams of camera circuitry or pictures of the camera body. Second, credible senders are unlikely to share information they know or perceive to be false (Rosnow 1980). This may be especially true for senders who value their reputation and wish to avoid doing anything to damage it (Wasko and Faraj 2005). Further, it is likely that true messages would attract credible senders who seek to use the novel or helpful information to enhance their standing within the network (Shi et al. 2014). Therefore, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 2. Leaks will exhibit higher levels of (a) vividness and (b) sender credibility than rumors.

We expect plausible messages containing vivid information sent by credible senders will positively affect a recipient’s resharing behavior. Our view which we explain more fully in Study 2 is based on the dualprocessing perspective contention that systematic and heuristic processing strategies affect the recipient’s judgment of and attitude toward messages (Chaiken 1980). First, because systematic processing strategies involve careful consideration of the message contents, messages that are consistent with the recipient’s prior knowledge about the product will be judged more favorably. For example, a message suggesting that the iPhone 6S will include the force touch feature would be deemed plausible because, though the feature had not yet appeared in an iPhone, it had appeared in other recent Apple devices. When information is unveri<sup>fi</sup>able or incomplete, plausibility serves as a type of validity assessment in that recipients are more likely to notice, believe, and share messages that are consistent with their prior knowledge (DiFonzo and Bordia 2007). Additionally, researchers have shown that plausible information is more likely to be believed (Mazzoni et al. 2001) and to be a foundation for decision making (Navis and Glynn 2011), irrespective of veracity (Mazzoni et al. 2001).

Second, in addition to the content of the message, the recipient would also attend to sender credentials or the vividness of the message. Credible senders enhance the persuasiveness of a message and increase the likelihood that it will be reshared (Pornpitakpan 2004). Additionally, it has been shown that vivid information is more persuasive and more likely to be reshared (Hoch and Deighton 1989, Herr et al. 1991). In summary, a message from a credible source sender or a message using vivid language is often more persuasive and thus may be judged more favorably. A more favorable attitude toward the message, whether formed through systematic or heuristic processing, is likely to result in resharing (Ajzen and Fishbein 1980). Therefore, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 3. Message plausibility is positively associated with an individual’s resharing of unverifiable messages on OSNs.

Hypothesis 4. (a) Message vividness and (b) sender credibility are positively associated with an individual’s resharing of unverifiable messages on OSNs.

Though systematic and heuristic processing strategies are often treated as independent, Chaiken and Maheswaran (1994) contend that they can have an interactive relationship in some circumstances. Speci<sup>fi</sup>- cally, they found that when subjects are presented with accurate but ambiguous information, heuristic cues’ effects are ampli<sup>fi</sup>ed. The rationale may be that when a message is likely to be true but ambiguous, the recipient may turn to noncontent characteristics for additional support. In our context, unveri<sup>fi</sup>able product messages are inherently ambiguous: though the message may be clear and direct, the veracity is in doubt. Therefore, we argue that a recipient would employ a joint processing strategy whereby systematic processing of content characteristics interacts with the processing of noncontent characteristics. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, when the content of a message is highly plausible, the noncontent message characteristics such as sender credibility and message vividness will be leveraged and play a larger role in the reshare decision. Alternatively, when a message is of low plausibility, the recipient is less likely to consider such noncontent characteristics. Therefore, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 5. Message plausibility positively moderates the effects of (a) message vividness and (b) sender credibility on resharing so that the effects are stronger when plausibility is higher.

## Method

Data Collection. Study 1 focuses on the spread of tweets about the iPhone 6S prior to its release. We chose this technology for several reasons. First, Apple is well known for its secrecy regarding upcoming devices as well as its doggedness in controlling leaks (Lee 2017). Second, the iPhone is an exceptionally popular device in U.S. markets and is likely to generate large amounts of prerelease speculation and message sharing. Finally, though there is an of<sup>fi</sup>cial Apple Twitter account (@Apple), the account is not used for communicating prerelease product information, ensuring that all unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages would travel through similar channels without interference from the manufacturer.

We test our hypotheses by analyzing Twitter messages (i.e., tweets) about the iPhone 6S tweeted over a four-month period from May 24, 2015, through Sep tember 25, 2015 (the iPhone 6S launch date). We took several steps to collect, clean, and reconstruct the tweets downloaded from the Twitter <sup>fi</sup>rehose. First, we used keywords (e.g., iPhone 6S, rumor, leak) to identify relevant tweets. Further, because tweets are often short and dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to assess for veracity, we included only tweets with at least one hyperlink to additional information. To identify a representative sample of unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages, we trained two coders to classify the links according to the conceptualization described earlier (Table 1). After three rounds of training, agreement among coders reached an acceptable level (raw agreement: 86%; Cohen’s kappa: 0.794) (Cohen 1960, Viera and Garrett 2005, McHugh 2012), and the remaining links were then randomly divided among the two coders. Finally, we removed spam tweets to ensure a fair comparison. This process resulted in a <sup>fi</sup>nal collection of 1,085 unique links that were tweeted/retweeted 37,584 times (7,130 rumor tweets, 30,454 leak tweets). For more details, please refer to Online Appendix C.

Measures. Our data set contains values that identify unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages, illustrate message characteristics, and are indicative of resharing behavior. Each is discussed next and outlined in Online Appendix C (Table C-4). Reshare behavior was operationalized as the logged total number of retweets for a message. Unveri<sup>fi</sup>able message type was assigned a value of 1 (rumor) or 2 (leak) as determined by two independent coders. Vividness was measured by two variables: Image Count and Emotion Intensity. Image Count is a count of the images included in a message. For Emotion Intensity, we used the linguistic inquiry and word count (LIWC) lexicon (Pennebaker et al. 2007), a widely adopted dictionary for text analysis (e.g., Coppersmith et al. 2014, Chau et al. 2020), to measure the strength of the tone (either positive or negative) embedded in a message (Cohn et al. 2004). Emotional messages have been found to be more memorable because they are more vivid than pallid messages (Todd et al. 2012, White et al. 2019). According to Pennebaker et al. (2007), language becomes more charged as the emotional score departs from 50 (neutral tone), with higher values representing positive tone and lower values representing negative tone. Therefore, we calculated emotional intensity as the absolute difference between the message’s emotional score and neutral.

Sender credibility is measured by three indicators. First, Twitter has implemented a veri<sup>fi</sup>ed badge to identify verified accounts. According to Twitter, veri-<sup>fi</sup>ed accounts are of public interest and may include accounts maintained by “users in music, acting, fashion, government, politics, religion, journalism, media, sports, business, and other key interest areas.”<sup>1</sup> A second measure included in each message is the sender’s default profile image. It is likely that users who have not changed their pro<sup>fi</sup>le image and instead use the default blank image are perceived to be less trustworthy. Both measures are dichotomous with 1 representing true and 0 representing false. The third indicator of sender credibility is the user’s ratio of followers to followings (fofo ratio). This measure emerges from research on spammers (Lee et al. 2010, Yang et al. 2013), and researchers have found that it is an adequate proxy of credibility such that as the ratio grows, the user’s reputation on Twitter improves.

To assess plausibility, we adapted one previously studied characteristic of plausible messages (i.e., domain rank) and two measures from the LIWC content analysis (i.e., authenticity and analytic information).

First, we used Alexa’s domain ranking service to rank the domains of the links embedded in each tweet. Higher ranked domains are inherently mainstream (e.g., the rank of the google.com domain is 1).<sup>2</sup> Thus, it is likely that users will <sup>fi</sup>nd links from higher ranked domains to be plausible because such sites are more likely to be familiar (e.g., “I’ve found similar information on this site in the past.”) and trustworthy (e.g., “This site does not feature wild claims.”). Authenticity is composed of multiple LIWC categories (e.g., <sup>fi</sup>rstand third-person pronouns, negative emotion, exclusive words, motion verbs) that measure the honesty or deceptiveness present in a message (Newman et al. 2003, Cohn et al. 2004). Similarly, analytic information assesses the use of articles, prepositions, auxiliary verbs, pronouns, adverbs, conjunctions, and negations to re<sup>fl</sup>ect the extent to which a message is clear and logical (Pennebaker et al. 2014). Both measures are likely to enhance the perception that the information is plausible.

As control variables, we include an engagement score (average activity per day) for senders, sender’s account age, and message age as control variables. We also include LIWC’s message drive—the forcefulness of a message—which is re<sup>fl</sup>ected by the use of achievementoriented words such as better, superior, and danger (Pennebaker et al. 2007).

Descriptive Statistics. Detailed descriptive statistics and correlations of the variables are presented in Online Appendix D. Figures 3 and 4 provide an overview of the diffusion of rumors and leaks. Figure 3 shows the number of times each hops (a hop occurs when a message is retweeted) and the average time needed to hop further. The more a message hops, the more robust it is believed to be (Backstrom et al. 2012, Arna boldi et al. 2017). Further, the faster a message hops, the more public attention it garners as recipients make the retweet decision faster (Yoo et al. 2016). As shown in Figure 3, though most rumors and leaks stopped at their second hop (95.68% and 93.86%, respectively), leaks travel farther (more hops) and faster (11.57 minutes on average vs. 21.03 minutes) than rumors.

Figure 4 shows the cumulative retweets of rumors and leaks at a given point in time. A high point on the diffusion line indicates either many messages appearing or a single message being retweeted many times on that date. In general, leaks had more peaks than rumors during the four-month diffusion period.

## Study 2: Sharing Unverifiable Messages

Though the results of Study 1 are compelling, they only hint at a satisfactory explanation. They highlight what unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages recipients reshare but offer little insight into why they reshare it. To address this gap, we present a second study that focuses on the psychological factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence the reshare decision. It is likely that user perceptions of diagnosticity and novelty would be most in-<sup>fl</sup>uential on the reshare decision during the product prerelease period because veri<sup>fi</sup>ed details are scarce, and users would be interested in <sup>fi</sup>lling any product knowledge gaps that presently exist. ## Hypothesis Development

The presence of content and noncontent message characteristics will in<sup>fl</sup>uence the recipient’s perception of message diagnosticity. Prior research has shown that plausible messages are believed to be helpful in assessing products. For example, in a study of product trials, Kempf and Smith (1998) found that trials are most diagnostic when they are consistent with what the consumer believes to be a legitimate trial of the product. Also, Filieri (2015) found that customer reviewers are rated as more diagnostic when the review is believed to be based on valid information. Thus, it is likely that unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages exhibiting high plausibility are perceived to be more helpful.

The perception of diagnosticity will be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the presence of noncontent message characteristics as well. Though noncontent characteristics only convey information about the message presentation (i.e., vividness) or the sender (i.e., sender credibility), researchers have found that the presence of such cues can affect the perception of diagnosticity. For exam ple, Jiang and Benbasat (2004, 2007) found that vivid portrayals of product information are believed to be more helpful in evaluating products. They contend that such an effect is particularly salient when product information is communicated through online media, as is the case with OSNs. Also, Filieri et al. (2018) found that product reviews from credible sender were deemed more diagnostic. Though these results con<sup>fl</sup>ict with other works that found no relationship (e.g., Filieri 2015, Yi et al. 2017), it is likely that the “experts are usually correct” heuristic would in<sup>fl</sup>uence the perceived diagnosticity of unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages. Therefore, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 6. Message plausibility is positively associ ated with the perception of message diagnosticity.

Hypothesis 7. (a) Message vividness and (b) sender credi bility are positively associated with the perception of message diagnosticity.

Similarly, the perceptions of novelty will be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the presence of content and noncontent message characteristics. Hypothesis 8. Message plausibility is positively associated with the perception of message novelty.

Hypothesis 9. (a) Message vividness and (b) sender credibility are positively associated with the perception of message novelty.

In Study 1, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that an assessment of unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages is likely to result in an interaction between systematic processing (i.e., message plausibility) and heuristic processing (i.e., message vividness) strategies. Because this effect is certainly mediated by perceptions (Ajzen and Fishbein 1980), we argue that the interaction between processing strategies would affect behavior indirectly through perceptions of diagnosticity and novelty. In fact, Chaiken and Maheswaran (1994) found evidence of such an effect: when recipients are presented with ambiguous information, the importance of noncontent characteristics increases. In our context, this means that when recipients are presented with a highly plausible message, they give greater weight to vividness and sender credibility when assessing the diagnosticity and novelty of an unveri<sup>fi</sup>able message. Therefore, we hypothesize the following.<sup>3</sup>

Hypothesis 10. Message plausibility positively moderates the effect of (a) message vividness and (b) sender credibility on message diagnosticity.

Hypothesis 11. Message plausibility positively moderates the effect of (a) message vividness and (b) sender credibility on message novelty.

Diagnostic messages are believed to be valuable when product uncertainty is high because such messages have been shown to alleviate anxieties about product quality (Pavlou et al. 2007) and increase the likelihood of an optimal adoption decision (Jiang and Benbasat 2004, Yi et al. 2017). Furthermore, social exchange theory explains that individuals who have been helped are likely to reciprocate by helping others. For instance, Constant et al. (1994) found that employ ees will share information to the extent that they have concern for helping others within the organization. Similarly, users share helpful information because they feel helping is the right thing to do (Wasko and Faraj 2000), sharing enhances their reputation (Wasko and Faraj 2005), and they enjoy helping others (Kankanhalli et al. 2005). Likewise, rumor researchers have found similar effects for messages that, though false, are believed to be helpful (Kamins et al. 1997). Thus, OSN users are likely to reshare messages they believe to be helpful.

Also, users are likely to share messages they <sup>fi</sup>nd novel, interesting, or arousing. Researchers have found that arousing content is more likely to spread on OSNs (Berger and Milkman 2012). Some attribute this to the social reward structure inherent in OSNs whereby a latent bene<sup>fi</sup>t of enhanced status, approval, or respect within one’s network incentivizes the sharing of novel information (Shi et al. 2014). Also, this effect may be stronger on OSNs because when relationships lack personal involvement, as many OSN relationships do, people tend to prioritize sharing interesting or attention-grabbing information (Stevens and Fiske 1995). Therefore, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 12. An individual’s perception of message diagnosticity is positively associated with the person’s intention to reshare unverifiable messages.

Hypothesis 13. An individual’s perception of message novelty is positively associated with the person’s intention to reshare unverifiable messages.

## Method

In Study 2, we conducted a controlled experiment to understand how people perceive content (i.e., plausibility) and noncontent (i.e., sender credibility, message vividness) message characteristics in unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages and how they in<sup>fl</sup>uence their intention to reshare the message. The focal technology in the experiment was Facebook’s latest virtual reality (VR) device—Oculus Quest 2. We selected this product for two reasons. First, VR devices are an emerging technology that is not well known but is of interest to many people. Second, while Oculus Quest 2 is comparable to the iPhone, it is not as popular. This ensures that subjects are more likely to rely on the treatment messages rather than prior knowledge to inform a resharing decision.

Experimental Design and Procedure. The experiment uses a 2 (sender credibility, high vs. low) X 2 (message vividness, high vs. low) X 2 (message plausibility, high vs. low) design, with eight treatment groups (Table 6). To control for message comprehension, the initial design employed a within-subject approach. However, during the pretest, we found that the sender credibility treatment was particularly salient such that participants began to anticipate the responses they thought we expected, thus biasing the results. Therefore, we opted for an incomplete block design (Hinkelmann and Kempthorne 2008) in which sender credibility serves as the between-subject treatment whereas message vividness and plausibility serve as within-subject treatments. We conducted two pilot studies (n 53, n 61) to assess the validity of the measures and the effectiveness of the manipulations. One hundred and seven (56 male, 49 female, two not disclosed) participants were recruited for the main study and were randomly assigned to either the high or low Sender Credibility condition (high: n 53, low: n 54). All participants were older than 18 and had an average age of 23.09 (std. 5.72). The sample size in each condition (54 or 53) satis<sup>fi</sup>ed the requirements for statistical power needed for both within and between treatment analysis (Roscoe 1975, Hair et al. 2009, Sekaran and Bougie 2016).

Manipulations for the high and low levels of the three treatments were developed according to best practices, prior literature (Canter et al. 2003, Hess et al. 2009, Kuan et al. 2015), construct de<sup>fi</sup>nitions, and when possible, operationalizations from Study 1. To ensure that the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from both studies are comparable, we contextualized manipulations to the Twitter environment. Message plausibility was manipulated by varying message veracity, the domain hosting the supplementary information, and community engage ment with the message. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, our messages contained information that was consistent (e.g., powered by a Qualcomm processor) and inconsistent (e.g., contains 100x the storage capacity of the at-that-time highest capacity mobile device) with what one might expect. Also, hosting domains were manipulated to re<sup>fl</sup>ect highly ranked (e.g., Forbes.com) and lowly ranked (e.g., winbuzzer.com) domains from the sample of domains collected in Study 1. For message viv idness, we varied the presence of images, the text of the tweet, and the use of hashtags and emojis. As vivid messages employ concrete and evocative language, the text was manipulated to include either speci<sup>fi</sup>c (e.g., Snapdragon XR2) or generic (e.g., a medium-sized lithium-ion battery) details and to include or omit attention-grabbing features (e.g., emojis and hashtags).<sup>4</sup> We manipulated sender credibility by selecting senders with large/small followings and senders who do or do not have veri<sup>fi</sup>ed checks and default pro<sup>fi</sup>le images. High credibility accounts were selected from among the veri<sup>fi</sup>ed accounts with more than one million followers collected during Study 1 and the low credibility accounts were fake and invented by the authors. Further details of the manipula tions are described in Table 7 and Online Appendix E.

The experiment was conducted within an online survey tool that randomly assigned participants to either high or low sender credibility conditions. Participants were then presented with the <sup>fi</sup>rst of four randomized tweets. After viewing the tweet message, manipulation checks assessed the participants’ perceptions of message vividness, sender credibility, and plausibility. Then, they answered questions regarding the perceived diagnosticity and novelty of the tweet and reported their intention to reshare this message with their peers. This process was repeated for each of the four sample tweets. After evaluating all four messages, participants provided demographic information and responses to the control variables. For more details, please see Online Appendix E.

Data Analysis and Results. The descriptive statistics (construct correlations, square roots of the average variance extracted (AVE) of each latent variable, loadings, etc.), presented in Online Appendix G, suggest satisfactory validity and reliability. As Table 8 shows, there are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences between the high and low treatment groups for each characteristic, indicating all three manipulations were effective. Next, we assessed the common method variance using two methods (Malhotra et al. 2006). First, we used the marker variable technique suggested by Lindell and Whitney (2001) and selected the second-smallest positive correlation among the factors (0.051, between retweet intention and PIIT) as our marker variable (Lindell and Whitney 2001). After using the marker variable to adjust the correlations, the differences between the original and the adjusted correlations (range from 0.026 to 0.070) are not suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to infer a common method bias (Malhotra et al. 2006). Second, we followed Harman’s single-factor test to construct a single factor with all indicators. With a comparative <sup>fi</sup>t index (CFI) of 0.298, root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) of 0.229 and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR) of 0.223, the single factor model does not meet the threshold of a good model (MacCallum et al. 1996, Hu and Bentler 1999), and has <sup>fi</sup>t indices that are inferior to our full model. Therefore, we conclude that common method variance is not a major concern in this study.

We used repeated measure (RM) analysis of variance (ANOVA) to test our hypotheses because each participant evaluated four different messages (i.e., withinsubject treatments for vividness and plausibility). Table 9 presents the results of this analysis. We found signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences in perceived diagnosticity between the high/low conditions for plausibility (mean difference 0.485, p < 0.001) and message vividness (mean difference 0.318, p 0.009) conditions, but no differences among high/low conditions for sender credibility (mean difference 0.392, p 0.104). These <sup>fi</sup>ndings offer support for Hypotheses 6 and 7(a), but not for Hypothesis 7(b). For perceived novelty, we found signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences between high/low conditions for plausibility (mean difference <sub>-</sub> 0.283, p <sub>-</sub> 0.028), message vividness (mean difference 0.266, p 0.030), and sender credibility (mean difference 0.594, p 0.004), offering full support for Hypotheses 8 and 9.

## Discussion

This research is motivated by the need to understand why OSN users share messages they cannot verify. We leverage a cross-discipline approach to conceptualize unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages and use the channel of origin (of-<sup>fi</sup>cial vs. unof<sup>fi</sup>cial) and veracity (true vs. false) of the underlying message to distinguish between four types of unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages: preannouncements, vaporware, leaks, and rumors. Of these, we focus our investigation on true and false messages originating from unof<sup>fi</sup>cial channels (i.e., rumors and leaks). We argue that recipients of these messages rely on systematic and heuristic processing strategies to process both content (plausibility) and noncontent message characteristics (sender credibility and message vividness) to form their reshare decision.

## Major Findings

The results of hypothesis testing are summarized in Table 12 and point to several interesting <sup>fi</sup>ndings. First, rumors and leaks exhibit differentiating characteristics when shared on OSNs. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, leaks are more likely than rumors to be plausible (higher in authentic ity), vivid (more images), and shared by credible send ers (higher fofo ratio and fewer default pro<sup>fi</sup>le images). Second, our results show that message characteristics affect resharing behavior with plausibility (domain rank, authentic language), vividness (image count), and sender credibility (fofo ratio, default pro<sup>fi</sup>le image) each having a positive effect on resharing behavior in Study 1. Similarly, in Study 2, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that plausibility, vividness, and sender credibility have a positive effect on perceived novelty and that plausibility and vivid ness have a positive effect on perceived diagnosticity. Further, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that perceived diagnosticity and novelty signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uence intention to reshare. Finally, we <sup>fi</sup>nd evidence that recipients employ a synergistic processing strategy when judging unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages whereby vividness and sender credibility have a stronger effect when the message is plausible. Speci<sup>fi</sup> cally, in Study 1 we <sup>fi</sup>nd that domain rank and authentic language strengthen the effect image count has on resharing behavior. In Study 2, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that plausibility strengthens the effect of vividness on perceived diagnosticity, and it strengthens the effect of vividness and sender credibility on perceived novelty.

It is also important to note two unexpected but interesting results. First, in Study 1, we <sup>fi</sup>nd no evidence for the hypothesized moderating effect of plausibility on the relationship between sender credibility and resharing behavior. Findings from Study 2 somewhat help explain this nonsigni<sup>fi</sup>cant moderating effect of plausibility. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, Study 2 shows that plausibility ampli<sup>fi</sup>es the effect of sender credibility on perceived novelty, but not the effect on perceived diagnosticity. Such a partial interaction effect may not be suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly change the relationship between sender credibility and resharing behavior in this research setting.

Second, the effect of sender credibility on resharing intention is only mediated by perceived novelty, whereas the effect of plausibility is only mediated by perceived diagnosticity. This suggests that the associated message characteristics affect resharing through different routes. It may be that as a content characteristic, plausibility prompts the recipient to believe “that’s helpful,” whereas sender credibility, a noncontent characteristic, prompts a response of “that’s interesting.” Thus, it is possible that recipients reshare plausible information primarily because they <sup>fi</sup>nd such information helpful, regardless of whether they <sup>fi</sup>nd it interesting. For messages from credible senders, it is possible that the nature of the OSN context and the reward structures inherent therein have trained in<sup>fl</sup>uencers to seek and followers to expect novel information. Thus, recipients reshare novel information not because it is novel per se, but because it was sent by someone who is known for sharing interesting information; it is novel ipso facto.

## Contributions and Research Implications

This research makes several contributions to academic research. First, using veracity and channel of origin, we develop a conceptualization of unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages that highlights the theoretical and functional distinctions between four types of unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages: leaks, rumors, preannouncements, and vaporware. Though this classi<sup>fi</sup>cation was developed in the context of product messages, it is likely generalizable to other domains that are similarly concerned with the circulation of different types of unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages. For example, there is growing interest in so-called fake news and its spread on social media (Torres et al. 2018). Increasingly, public <sup>fi</sup>gures use the term to describe unveri<sup>fi</sup>ed claims that spread across social networks. Though “fake” suggests false and “news” suggests of<sup>fi</sup>- cial, our classi<sup>fi</sup>cation illustrates that fake news may emerge from of<sup>fi</sup>cial or unof<sup>fi</sup>cial channels and contain true or false information and is thus likely to have different characteristics and elicit different responses.

Second, because both rumors and leaks originate from unof<sup>fi</sup>cial channels and lack conceptual clarity, we show that these concepts are distinguishable in terms of the message characteristics they exhibit. Through a literature review, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed three key message characteristics—message vividness, sender credibility, and message plausibility—that play an essential role in helping recipients assess unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages. Our results show that rumors and leaks do exhibit discriminating characteristics, with leaks exhibiting more authenticity, including more images, and being sent by users who are more likely to customize their pro<sup>fi</sup>le image and have a higher fofo ratio. Despite these differences, rumors and leaks exhibit important similarities that highlight the challenges associated with assessing unveri<sup>fi</sup>able information. For example, leaks and rumors do not exhibit signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences in either domain rank (plausibility) or veri<sup>fi</sup>ed badges (sender credibility), suggesting that the sites hosting and the users promoting messages from unof<sup>fi</sup>cial channels are equally likely to provide a platform for true and false information. This is particularly interesting given that leaks travel faster and farther than rumors, suggesting that, in aggregate, OSN users have a capacity for snif<sup>fi</sup>ng out the truth even if leaks and rumors have similar origins.

Third, this research shows that when faced with unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages, recipients will use both systematic and heuristic processing strategies when deciding to reshare such messages. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that plausibility positively moderates the effect of noncontent characteristics (message vividness: Study 1 and 2, sender credibility: Study 2). This indicates that recipients employ an interactive processing strategy when assessing unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, when messages are deemed to be plausible (systematic processing), the effects of vividness and sender credibility (heuristic processing) are ampli<sup>fi</sup>ed. This contributes to the dual-processing literature, as prior studies have seldom examined how the two processing strategies complement each other in decision making.

Fourth, this research reveals possible reasons that people may want to reshare an unveri<sup>fi</sup>able message. A persistent question in rumor literature and in popular culture is, why would someone share information they cannot verify and might not be true? In other research, the act of sharing unveri<sup>fi</sup>able information has been described as dangerous or foolish (Miller 2005), but our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest an alternative explanation. It may not be that the senders are deviant rumormongers, but rather that they choose to share a message that is unveri<sup>fi</sup>able and possibly false because they believe it to contain novel or helpful information about something that is of interest. This offers a more benign explanation for the spread of rumors and/or false information on OSNs. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings are consistent with and offer further credence to Shibutani’s (1966) conceptualization of rumors as sense-making tools that primarily serve to help communities make sense of confusing and uncertain events.

## Limitations and Future Research

As with all research, our study has limitations. First, to highlight distinctions between the types of unveri<sup>fi</sup>- able messages, we limit our focus to an examination of rumors and leaks. Future researchers may expand on our work and explicitly consider unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages from of<sup>fi</sup>cial channels (preannouncements, vaporware) as they are likely to exhibit characteristics that are unique to of<sup>fi</sup>cial communications. For instance, in Study 1, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that leaks and rumors exhibit some similar characteristics (e.g., domain rank, analytic information, emotion intensity, veri<sup>fi</sup>ed) and this may be due to a common diffusion channel (i.e., unof<sup>fi</sup>cial). It would be valuable to compare messages from of<sup>fi</sup>cial and unof<sup>fi</sup>cial channels, and future researchers may consider how users process these messages and the extent to which channel of origin in-<sup>fl</sup>uences perceptions of plausibility. Also, given the short lifespan and rapid spread of OSN messages (Kwak et al. 2010), it would be interesting to investigate how the OSN context in<sup>fl</sup>uences the costs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of preannouncing unveri<sup>fi</sup>able product details that may not appear in the <sup>fi</sup>nal product.

Second, as this research has a general focus on the resharing of unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages, we overlook interesting questions such as the emergence of rumors and leaks. Future researchers may consider addressing these issues directly. Though rumor researchers have explored the emergence of rumors (e.g., DiFonzo and Bordia 2007), it is not clear that the motivations for starting a rumor and leaking private information are the same. Whereas rumors are simple mental fabrications, leaks represent the theft or misuse of private information. As such, the motivations for rumoring and leaking are likely to differ. Future researchers may want to investigate the social (Cropanzano and

Mitchell 2005) or psychological (Deci and Ryan 1985) factors that motivate leaking private information. Also, given the differences between rumors and leaks, future researchers may want to consider whether rumors and leaks propagate among distinct clusters of users and the extent to which they are capable of crossing boundaries.

Finally, this research focuses on unveri<sup>fi</sup>able product messages. Although our results diverge from prior research (e.g., Vosoughi et al. 2018), it is possible they do so because the messages in our context are less controversial than similarly unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages about topics such as politics, urban legends, and terrorism. Despite such differences, future researchers may consider responding to calls for devoting greater attention to the abundance of fake news and misinformation available on social media (Torres et al. 2018) by exploring these issues from a perspective of veri<sup>fi</sup>- ability. For example, Janze and Risius (2017) note that the spread of unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages sent on social media platforms in<sup>fl</sup>uenced people’s political views and voting behavior during the 2016 U.S. presidential election, and Vosoughi et al. (2018) found that in some cases, false information diffuses faster than true but neither study explains why such phenomena occur. Given that concern over the spread of unveri<sup>fi</sup>able political messages—true and false—is causing OSNs to reconsider their editorial role, it would be bene<sup>fi</sup>cial for future researchers to extend investigations of the resharing of unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages to different contexts.

## Practical Implications

Undeniably, the prevalence of unveri<sup>fi</sup>able information is negatively affecting how people view social media, with only one-third of users indicating they trust the information on social media (Watson 2020). Companies like Facebook and YouTube have already developed antifake-news interventions to help users better understand the information sources, but there is concern that such interventions may not work well (Dias et al. 2020). Our research offers insight into why this might be. First, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that message characteristics (plausibility, vividness, sender credibility) in<sup>fl</sup>uence resharing. Second, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that recipients reshare unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages because they believe the message to be helpful or novel. These two <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that interventions seeking to suppress false information may not be suf<sup>fi</sup>cient and that a more fruitful tack may be to focus on message characteristics and/ or perceptions of diagnosticity and novelty.

For interventions aimed at message characteristics, our work shows that contra direct approaches such as fact checking or editorializing, targeting message characteristics may help recipients as they assess unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages. Given the direct effect of sender credibility on reshare behavior, platforms could consider additional mechanisms for credentialing senders. Though our results suggest a veri<sup>fi</sup>ed badge has no effect on the resharing of unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages, it could be that such a badge conveys too little information. For example, online communities such as Reddit.com and the Steam video game service offer a variety of badges that can be earned by (via OSN) or bestowed upon (via OSN users) users to highlight their skills or reputation. Additionally, accounts tied to professional email addresses could have the option of attaching the organization’s logo to their avatar as an additional cue of credibility. Also, just as premium/freemium accounts have different levels of access, OSN platforms could incentivize the acquisition of credentials or restrict accounts that eschew such op portunities by capping the number of followers or posts for uncredentialed users. Similar efforts could be targeted to plausibility and vividness. Email applications currently warn users that links may be associated with disreputable organizations, and OSNs could similarly provide information about host domains to help recipi ents discriminate between plausible and implausible messages. Also, arti<sup>fi</sup>cial intelligence technologies could be used to detect altered images or to provide additional context, such as when users misattribute the events portrayed in images.

Interventions targeting perceptions of diagnosticity and novelty are also possible. Many shopping platforms allow users to vote on the helpfulness of a review, and a similar tactic could be deployed for sharing or resharing links on OSNs. As links ultimately resolve to a single location, helpfulness votes could be local to the user and global to the network. This would help users know whether the link is likely to be helpful before sharing it with their friends. Similarly, platforms could develop metrics to assess the novelty of messages before they are shared or reshared. Twitter currently highlights network-wide trending topics, but this feature could be married with a textual analysis of links or posts to help users know whether their message is of much interest to their friends or others on the network.

## Conclusion

This research concludes with four major takeaways. First, OSN users are likely to encounter four types of unveri<sup>fi</sup>able product messages prior to the release of a new product: rumor, leak, vaporware announcement, and preannouncement. Second, unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages from unof<sup>fi</sup>cial channels (rumor and leak) exhibit three key content and noncontent characteristics— sender credibility, message vividness, message plausibility—that inform the recipient’s reshare decision. Third, recipients of unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages take a holistic approach, applying both systematic and heuristic processing strategies. Finally, users are willing to reshare messages with their social network to the extent that they <sup>fi</sup>nd those messages diagnostic and novel. Findings from this study contribute to both research and practice by answering questions about how rumors and leaks differ, and why recipients would reshare messages they cannot verify. This work can serve as a foundation for future research on the effects of unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages and how unveri<sup>fi</sup>able messages diffuse online.
