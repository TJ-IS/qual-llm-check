# Does Virtual Reality Help Property Sales? Empirical Evidence from a Real Estate Platform

Abstract. Virtual reality (VR) has emerged as a transformative addition to real estate platforms, revolutionizing the presentation of property details. In contrast to traditional presentation technologies (e.g., pictures and videos), VR constructs an interactive threedimensional (3D) environment for consumers to obtain property information. Prior literature mainly examined VR’s effects (e.g., vividness and interactivity) on selling lowinvolvement products such as books and apparel from a behavioral perspective. Real estate properties are typical high-involvement products, differing significantly in prod uct value, importance, risk, and agent participation, necessitating comprehensive product information. We use a large-scale data set from a leading real estate platform to investigate VR’s influences on properties’ market outcomes (including the time-onmarket and selling price) from an informative perspective. We find that VR is an “efficiency enhancer,” accelerating properties’ time-on-market rather than increasing properties’ selling price as a “market value influencer.” VR shows a greater acceleration effect for properties with higher product involvement, higher product quality, and lower agent service quality. This demonstrates VR’s ability in enhancing information richness to satisfy consumers’ information needs for a higher level of product involvement; establishing information credibility in discerning product quality; and serving as an alternative information source in lieu of low-quality agent services. We also disentangle the direct effect of VR Tour from the indirect effect of VR badge on consumers decision making to reveal VR’s effectiveness in product evaluation. This work contributes to the literature on nonimmersive VR and offers managerial implications, particularly in the context of property sales, high-involvement product marketing, and product presentation technologies in e-commerce.

## 1. Introduction

Virtual reality (VR) is an emerging visual technology that constructs a computer-generated, three-dimensional (3D) environment to mimic the real world. Consumers can interact with the simulated environment to receive instant feedback and collect information specific to their personal needs. Given the above benefits, this technique has been used in various fields, such as gaming,<sup>1</sup> medicine, architecture, and military (Lele 2013, Portman et al. 2015, Li et al. 2017). In recent years, the public’s interest in VR has further been raised with the growing interest in the metaverse (Dwivedi et al. 2022), which relies on VR to create a virtual world. The commercial prospects of VR are enormous. The global market size reached 4.42 billion USD in 2020 and is projected to be 84.09 billion USD by 2028.<sup>2</sup>

Regarding technological implementation, VR can be categorized into immersive VR and nonimmersive VR (Suh and Lee 2005). Immersive VR relies on a head mounted set to construct the 3D environment, whereas nonimmersive VR presents the simulated environment using a computer screen. As nonimmersive VR enables consumers to interact with virtual scenarios without device constraints, most large-scale VR applications in current e-commerce practice are of the nonimmersive type, such as in online advertising and product presentation (Suh and Lee 2005, Papagiannidis et al. 2013, Van Kerrebroeck et al. 2017, Yeh et al. 2017, Bogicevic et al. 2019).

Our work concentrates on a novel nonimmersive VR application on the leading real estate platforms (e.g., Zillow in the United States, Beike in China, and ImmobilienScout24 in Germany) for empirical analysis, which is an online house tour function to assist property sales. This VR function generates a 3D presentation of a property and allows consumers to interact and explore each room based on their preferences. The online house tour experience provided by VR is analogous to consumers’ offline house visits. Hence, we refer to this function as “VR Tour,” which is similar to Matterport’s 3D Tour and Zillow’s 3D Home Tours<sup>3</sup> despite the different names given.

Even though a few seminal works have explored the influences of nonimmersive VR on consumer responses in e-commerce (Suh and Lee 2005, Debbabi et al. 2010, Papagiannidis et al. 2013), our research context of property sales has its uniqueness, significantly differing from the traditional e-commerce setting in product involvement and real estate agent participation. Product involvement refers to the extent to which consumers engage in processing and evaluating product information (Traylor and Joseph 1984, Laurent and Kapferer 1985, Zaichkowsky 1985). The level of involvement is related to the value of the product, its importance, and the associated purchase risk. Lowinvolvement products are often inexpensive and do not hold much importance or relevance in the consumer’s life. Thus, consumers’ demand for information is not very urgent when purchasing low-involvement products. Prior literature has discussed VR’s influences on low-involvement product marketing, such as books (Papagiannidis et al. 2013), apparel (Debbabi et al. 2010), and tables (Suh and Lee 2005). This study focuses on the real estate industry, where properties serve as representative examples of high-involvement products. High-involvement products have significant economic value and are crucial to consumers’ wellbeing (Traylor 1981).

One critical issue in high-involvement product marketing is consumers’ reluctance to purchase expensive products online due to high-level product uncertainty (Kim and Krishnan 2015). Previous literature shows that consumers’ product uncertainty can be alleviated by diagnostic product descriptions (Dimoka et al. 2012). As a result, consumers require comprehensive information to reduce product uncertainty (Zaichkowsky 1986, Pavlou et al. 2007). However, it is unclear whether VR can satisfy the demand for product information as product involvement increases.

The other critical issue in high-involvement product marketing is the increased concern for product quality. Previous literature indicates that consumers’ attitudes toward high-involvement products are firmly held and are only influenced by strong quality arguments (Petty and Cacioppo 1979, 1981; Zaichkowsky 1986). A false judgment on the quality of a high-involvement product can lead to a wrong purchase and substantial financial loss. Therefore, consumers require credible information that can faithfully represent the property’s attributes to learn product quality. However, it remains unknown whether VR can provide consumers with credible product information to distinguish product quality in property sales.

Another important distinction between property sales and traditional e-commerce is the real estate agent’s participation. The agent serves as an intermediary between buyers and property owners to facilitate the real estate transaction (Arnold 1992, Benjamin et al. 2000). An agent offers brokerage services to help property owners list their properties on the platform and assist potential buyers in acquiring property information, scheduling offline house visits, and negotiating the price. Even though both VR and the agent may affect real estate transactions, it remains unclear how VR interacts with agent services in property sales, which contributes to a deep understanding of the relationship between VR and real estate agents.

Moreover, most existing literature investigates the perceptual effects of VR (e.g., vividness, interactivity, and telepresence) on consumer behavior (Suh and Lee 2005; Jiang and Benbasat 2007a, b; Debbabi et al. 2010; Papagiannidis et al. 2013). In essence, VR is an information tool for consumers to access the visual details of properties. Beyond the behavioral perspective, little is known about VR’s informative effects on market outcomes in property sales, including the time-on-market and selling price, which separately reflect the transaction efficiency and product market value (Miller 1978).

Given the unique challenges in the real estate context, previous findings cannot be easily extended to our context to understand VR’s informative effects on property sales. In this paper, we aim to fill the literature gap by answering the following research questions:

1. Whether and how does VR influence the market outcomes (including time-on-market and selling price) of property sales?

2. How does the influence of VR differ with product involvement, product quality, and agent service quality?

Compared with traditional property presentation techniques, such as pictures and virtual tours (Carrillo 2008, Allen et al. 2015, Yu et al. 2021), we conceptualize VR’s novel aspects from an informative perspective. To begin with, a picture only captures a static viewpoint of a property, and a virtual tour (i.e., videos) presents a sequence of snapshots of property pictures. Consequently, consumers can only access limited visual information selectively presented by photographers in videos and pictures. Conversely, VR can capture all the visual information about a property to reconstruct a 3D replication, simulating comprehensive details in the house. Thus, we argue that VR has a strong potential to offer consumers richer visual features of a property compared with pictures and virtual tours, defining it as VR’s information richness.

Second, VR allows consumers to interact with a simulated 3D environment, exploring different aspects and viewpoints of a product according to their personal interests. VR’s 3D presentation brings a higher level of telepresence and enjoyment than the 2D format (Nah et al. 2011). The interactive experience of VR helps to overcome the issues of selective presentation by photographers in traditional pictures and videos. With VR, sellers cannot exaggerate positive features or downplay the flaws of a property to attract buyers. Compared with traditional product presentation technologies, we argue that the product information conveyed through VR is a more truthful reflection of a property’s product quality, defining this as VR’s information credibility.

Third, VR is a product information tool that offers consumers property details through the online channel, whereas agent services primarily operate in the offline channel to help consumers learn the product’s attributes. On one side, consumers can obtain a property’s information from both online VR and offline agent services, VR could serve as an alternative information source to agent services in property sales. On the other side, VR presents properties’ visual details in a 3D and interactive approach. The usage of VR is more complex than traditional pictures and virtual tours. High-quality agent services can help consumers better employ VR to obtain product information. Therefore, VR could also be a synergetic information source for agent services.

Using a data set from China’s leading real estate platform, we conduct a series of empirical analyses to examine VR’s impacts. We adopt the survival models to investigate the influence of VR on properties’ time-onmarket and use the hedonic pricing model to examine VR’s impact on selling price. We analyze the heterogeneous effects of VR across different levels of product involvement, product quality, and agent service quality. We also examine VR’s effects on consumers’ decisionmaking to demonstrate VR’s effectiveness in evaluating products. To address the strategic adoption of VR and potentially endogenous price, we use propensity score matching and instrumental variable approaches to enhance identification. We also employ an alternative matching method, use different models, and conduct sensitivity analysis as additional checks to demonstrate the robustness of our findings.

Our results show that VR Tour reduces properties selling time by 28%–49% but does not affect the selling price. This demonstrates that VR enhances the efficiency of property sales rather than affecting market value. We find that the acceleration effect of VR is greater for properties with a higher level of product involvement. Nota bly, when consumers purchase a property with higher involvement, they demand more information for a comprehensive evaluation. Thus, this finding reveals VR’s information richness. VR’s acceleration effect is also greater for properties of higher quality, indicating VR’s information credibility that can help consumers discern property quality. Furthermore, VR tends to show a higher acceleration effect on agents with lower service quality. This finding implies that properties with lowquality agent services are more likely to benefit from VR, revealing VR as an alternative information source to agent services. Additional analysis disentangles the VR function’s direct (actual) effect in the alternative evaluation stage from the VR badge’s indirect (nominal) effect in the information search stage, demonstrating VR’s effectiveness in consumers’ product evaluation.

This study contributes to the literature in several ways. First, we enrich the literature on nonimmersive VR in e-commerce by attributing VR’s substantial acceleration effect to its information richness and credibility and VR as an alternative information source over agent services in property sales. Second, we enhance the literature on VR in property sales by showing VR’s augmented effect with the increased product involvement level, VR’s ability to differentiate product quality, and VR’s alternative relationship with the agent. Beyond that, the findings can also add to the broad literature on VR in high-involvement product marketing to facilitate the online sales of other similar high-involvement products, such as automobiles, which witness similar challenges regarding online product presentation, highlevel product involvement, strong product quality concerns, and dealer participation. Third, we contribute to the literature on product presentation in e-commerce by showing VR as a virtuous product presentation technology that improves transaction efficiency without affecting market value.

Our work offers several managerial implications for practitioners. First, online platforms can leverage VR to build an efficient and sustainable real estate market, thereby improving cash flow, attracting more customers, and generating extra profits. Additionally, our findings highlight VR’s ability to provide rich and credible information, making it especially beneficial for high-quality properties and a useful tool when agent service quality is lacking. Moreover, VR tours significantly reduce properties’ time-on-market, thus allowing agents to manage properties more efficiently.

## 2. Related Literature

In this section, we will review related works on VR, product involvement and property sales, and product presentation. After that, we summarize the literature gap and propose the theoretical framework.

## 2.1. Virtual Reality

VR refers to a computer-generated simulation of a reallife 3D environment that allows interactions through sensory stimuli (Steuer 1992). The 3D environment can create a sense of telepresence and enjoyment (Nah et al. 2011). The interactivity of VR allows users to explore various aspects of a product based on their personal interests (Suh and Lee 2005).

Immersive VR generates a simulated environment using a headset, which immerses the user in a threedimensional virtual world (Suh and Lee 2005). Because of technological immaturity and expensive hardware (Pizz et al. 2019, Dincelli and Yayla 2022), immersive VR is currently used in a limited number of fields, such as clinical medicine (Li et al. 2017), military (Lele 2013), and architecture (Portman et al. 2015). However, there are few largescale immersive VR applications in e-commerce practice.

Nonimmersive VR displays the simulated 3D environment on a screen and enables consumer interactions using their mouse (Suh and Lee 2005). A few seminal works have discussed VR’s effects in the e-commerce context from a behavioral perspective, examining how VR’s vividness, interactivity, and telepresence affect consumer responses. For instance, Jiang and Benbasat (2007b) conduct a laboratory experiment and find that the virtual product experience can offer consumers higher perceived website diagnosticity than pictures. Similarly, Jiang and Benbasat (2007a) use an identical setting and reveal that the vividness and interactivity of the virtual product experience can affect consumers intentions to revisit the website and make purchases. Spielmann and Mantonakis (2018) show that VR can improve consumers’ attitudes toward the ad through increased interactivity and telepresence. Pleyers and Poncin (2020) find that VR may lead to consumers’ positive attitudes toward products and agencies.

Most prior studies focus on the impact of VR on selling products of relatively low value and importance (Suh and Lee 2005, Debbabi et al. 2010, Papagiannidis et al. 2013). For instance, Papagiannidis et al. (2013) examine the impact of consumers’ engagement in purchasing books and clothing in a simulated virtual retail store. Debbabi et al. (2010) find that the 3D advertising format is effective in displaying watches and coats. Suh and Lee (2005) demonstrate that the VR interface can affect consumers’ learning on tables and computers. However, books, coats, and tables are typical lowinvolvement products, which are significantly different from the properties in this study.

## 2.2. Product Involvement and Property Sales

Product involvement indicates the extent to which consumers engage in processing product information. It relates to consumers’ perceived value, importance, and risk to the product category (Traylor and Joseph 1984, Laurent and Kapferer 1985, Zaichkowsky 1985).

Extant literature categorizes products into two types: high-involvement products and low-involvement products (Traylor 1981). High-involvement products have significant economic value and possess important functions that may affect consumers’ welfare. Properties and cars are representative examples of high-involvement products. On the contrary, low-involvement products are those with low product values and low purchase risk, such as soft drinks, watches, clothes, and candies.

Consumers are unwilling to purchase expensive products relying on online information due to a high degree of product uncertainty (Kim and Krishnan 2015). Given the significant value and importance of highinvolvement products, consumers require great efforts to collect comprehensive product information to reduce the risk of purchase decisions (Clarke and Belk 1978, Zaichkowsky 1986). As product involvement increases, consumers demand more information for product eval uation (Zaichkowsky 1986).

In a low-involvement scenario, consumers’ attitudes are influenced by their expertise. Hence, consumers beliefs are not firmly held and are easily influenced (Zaichkowsky 1986). In contrast, consumers’ attitudes toward high-involvement products are influenced by strong quality arguments (Petty and Cacioppo 1979, 1981; Zaichkowsky 1986). Because of the high value and importance of high-involvement products, a misunderstanding of product quality can lead to a false purchase decision and result in a significant loss. Therefore, consumers require credible information to ascertain the authentic quality of high-involvement products before making purchases.

Additionally, real estate agent participation is a crucial and distinctive aspect that sets property sales apart from conventional product sales in e-commerce. Specifically, agents are knowledgeable professionals who provide brokerage services to assist buyers in searching for properties, bargaining their prices, and helping sell ers list properties (Arnold 1992, Benjamin et al. 2000). Previous literature indicates that agents can acquire a lower house price because they can leverage their information advantages to obtain bargaining power (Agarwal et al. 2019). A lower selling price may increase the property’s chances of being sold and improve the agent’s likelihood of earning a commission fee from a successful transaction. Previous literature shows that high-quality agent services can assist consumers in reducing the selling price of a property but may prolong the selling time (Turnbull and Dombrow 2007).

## 2.3. Product Presentation

Previous literature indicates that information asymmetry between buyers and sellers regarding the product is a critical issue in e-commerce (Pavlou et al. 2007, Dimoka et al. 2012). Online product presentation is crucial to alleviate consumers’ uncertainty about product quality. For instance, Pavlou et al. (2007) show that website informativeness can reduce consumers’ perceived information asymmetry, fears of seller opportunism, and information privacy concerns. This, in turn, reduces consumers’ perceived uncertainty, ultimately boosting purchase intentions and actual purchases. Luo et al. (2012) discover that retailers’ website design can mitigate the negative impact of high product uncertainty on online consumers’ satisfaction. In addition, Dimoka et al. (2012) reveal that both textual and visual product descriptions can reduce consumers uncertainty and lead to a price premium.

Visual content proves to be more effective than textual and audio content in people’s learning processes (Dale 1969). Before using VR, pictures and virtual tours (namely videos) were the most common visual techniques for property presentation. They serve as informative tools to present the visual details of a product, providing consumers with additional information for their product evaluation.

Both pictures and virtual tours can influence the market outcomes of property sales (Carrillo 2008, Allen et al. 2015, Yu et al. 2021). Previous literature indicates inconsistent results regarding the effects of visual techniques on properties’ selling time. Specifically, Allen et al. (2015) show that extra pictures can extend a property’s time-on-market, whereas Carrillo (2008) finds that additional pictures can expedite the selling process. Additionally, Carrillo (2008) shows that a virtual tour can accelerate properties’ selling process, whereas Yu et al. (2021) indicate the virtual tour prolongs properties’ selling time-on-market.

Another stream of literature consistently shows a positive impact of pictures and virtual tours on the selling price of properties (Carrillo 2008, Allen et al. 2015, Yu et al. 2021). Diagnostic product descriptions may reduce product uncertainty and bring a price premium (Dimoka et al. 2012). However, product presentation technologies are not components of product attributes and may not determine the product’s true quality or genuine market value. Therefore, we argue that a virtuous product presentation technology can provide additional information to enhance product transaction efficiency but may not impact product market value. That is, the technology could reduce consumers’ uncertainty without requiring them to pay a price premium for additional product information.

To conclude, VR is a novel visual product presentation technology on real estate platforms. Consumers can interact with a simulated 3D environment of a house to obtain rich property details. This experience of VR differs from the widely discussed pictures and virtual tours in prior literature (Carrillo 2008, Allen et al. 2015, Yu et al. 2021). Even though previous literature has examined VR-alike techniques in e-commerce, these works primarily discussed the impacts of VR on selling low-involvement products (Suh and Lee 2005, Debbabi et al. 2010, Papagiannidis et al. 2013). In our research context, properties are typical highinvolvement products that require richer information as the level of product involvement increases (Clarke and Belk 1978, Traylor 1981, Zaichkowsky 1986), need more credible information to differentiate product quality (Petty and Cacioppo 1979, 1981; Zaichkowsky 1986), and require the real estate agent’s participation in property transactions (Arnold 1992, Benjamin et al. 2000, Turnbull and Dombrow 2007, Agarwal et al. 2019). Prior works have explored the impact of VR’s vividness, interactivity, and telepresence on consumer responses from a behavioral perspective (Suh and Lee 2005; Jiang and Benbasat 2007a, b; Debbabi et al. 2010; Papagiannidis et al. 2013; Spielmann and Mantonakis 2018; Pleyers and Poncin 2020). However, little is known about how VR affects market outcomes from an informative viewpoint.

To fill the above research gaps, we propose the theoretical framework in Figure 1. To begin with, we investigate VR’s impacts on properties’ time-on-market and selling prices, which separately reflect the transaction efficiency and market value. Second, and perhaps more importantly, we uncover the underlying mechanism of VR by examining its heterogeneous effects across different levels of product involvement, product quality, and agent service quality. These three factors highlight VR’s capabilities in information richness, information credibility, and its role as an information source, respectively. Specifically, by examining properties with different product involvement levels, captured by room numbers, we assess VR’s information richness. Analyzing VR’s impact across properties of different ages allows us to evaluate its information credibility in discerning product quality. Understanding how VR’s effectiveness interplay with the agent’s service quality provides insights into its role as a synergetic or alternative information source. Lastly, we follow consumers’ decision-making literature (Ha¨ubl and Trifts 2000, Choudhury and Karahanna 2008) to examine VR’s impacts on consumers’ web visits, number of attentions, and conversion rate/odds, revealing VR’s effectiveness in product evaluation.

## 3. Empirical Context

In this section, we will provide an overview of the real estate platform, introduce the VR Tour function, and present data descriptions and summary statistics.

## 3.1. Real Estate Platform

The real estate industry is not only important to the economy but also crucial for households.<sup>4</sup> A property purchase decision is critical to the welfare of a family. Offline house visits provide buyers with the most authentic experience to evaluate a property’s value. However, because of the considerable cost of time and effort of offline house visits, consumers cannot afford to physically tour many houses. Real estate platforms, such as Zillow and Realtor, provide a convenient way for consumers to obtain rich property details through online listings. Our work is based on China’s largest online real estate platform, similar to Zillow and Realtor in the United States. The platform lists more than one hundred million properties in 300 cities. We focus on the secondary housing market, which contributes to 64.5% of the platform’s transactions.

Real estate agents play a critical role in the secondary housing market by providing a wide range of services. When a property owner decides to sell a house on the platform, the owner can seek an agent to create an online listing showcasing property details. Afterward, potential buyers browse these listings to find properties that match their preferences, contact a real estate agent to obtain additional information, and schedule offline house visits. If a buyer decides to purchase a property, she may ask the agent to negotiate the price with the property owner.

## 3.2. VR Tour

The platform launched the VR Tour in late April 2018. Consumers can interact with the interface to change viewpoints and locations and explore properties in a 3D environment. Given the 3D nature of VR Tour, a few real estate platforms also refer to the identical function as 3D Home Tour (by Zillow) and 3D Virtual Tours (by Matterport).

In our context, the production of a VR Tour typically takes several hours. An agent will first use a panorama camera or a smartphone to scan all visual details and then upload the pictures to the platform server to generate a 3D model of the property using computer vision techniques. VR Tour will be displayed on the property’s detail web page to assist consumers’ evaluation. The VR Tour function is free for both property owners and buyers. The cost of VR Tour production is less than 100 CNY and is covered by the platform.

VR Tour presents the property’s 3D visual details on a screen. Consumers can use their mouse to interact with the 3D model to access property information. Figure 2 provides an example of a VR Tour. Figure 2(a) presents the view when the consumer stands at the living room’s entrance. The consumer can move to the center of the room by clicking on the position to obtain the view in Figure 2(b). At the center, she can view from different directions by clicking and dragging. Figure 2, (c) and (d), presents two different views observed by the consumer. The consumer can zoom in for details and zoom out for an overview, as shown in Figure 2(e). She can also refer to the 2D floor plan to locate her current position and determine the orientation of the current view, as shown in Figure 2(f). By using the VR Tour, consumers can explore the property’s detailed visual information online in an interac tive manner.

The novel VR Tour differs significantly from traditional visual product presentation technologies, such as pictures and virtual tours. We argue that VR offers advantages in both information richness and information credibility. On one side, a picture demonstrates a single snapshot of the property. A virtual tour is a prerecorded video or animation depicting a few snapshots. Consumers can only access limited visual information about a property using pictures and virtual tours. However, VR contains all the visual information about a property, which constructs a 3D environment for consumers to explore all the property details. Therefore, we argue that VR shows higher information richness than traditional product presentation technologies. On the other side, the visual content depicted by pictures and virtual tours may be prone to the problem of selective presentation. The seller tends to highlight several positive aspects and may not present the flaws in pictures and videos, which could potentially bias consumers evaluation. However, VR offers consumers an interactive way to explore comprehensive property details based on their interests, avoiding selective presentation. Consumers can obtain authentic property information using VR for an in-depth evaluation of product quality. Therefore, we argue that VR also has better information credibility than traditional technologies.

## 3.3. Data

We collect property transaction records from Shanghai, Guangzhou, and Nanjing, three cities in China with active real estate markets. We focus on properties listed on the platform between May 1, 2018, and July 31, 2019, which is the period immediately following the launch of VR Tour (in late April 2018). As the platform does not display canceled listings (i.e., listings that owners withdraw for various reasons), properties are either sold or still listed on the platform upon data collection (August 1, 2019). To prevent the strategic adoption of VR Tour based on sales performance, we remove properties that use VR Tour after their initial listings. Thus, we only consider properties that either offer the VR Tour throughout their entire duration on the market (hereafter VR-enabled properties) or never use the VR Tour (hereafter VR-disabled properties).

3.3.1. Dependent Variables. We consider two essential market outcomes in property sales: time-on-market and selling price. Specifically, we use ToM to denote the number of days that a property stays on the market. If a property has been sold upon data collection, timeon-market is the number of days from its initial listing to the final sale. If a property has not been sold upon data collection, time-on-market is the number of days from its initial listing to the date of data collection. Besides, we use the total selling price (in million CNY) as another dependent variable, Selling\_Price, which indicates product market value.

Additionally, we also consider the outcomes in consumers’ decision-making process: (1) Web\_Visits is the number of visits to the property’s detail web page, and (2) Attentions is the number of consumers saving the property in their attention lists. Web\_Visits and Attentions are cumulative values from a property’s initial listing date to the final selling date, which can be observed after selling. Table 1 presents descriptions of the variables.

3.3.2. Independent Variables. The independent variable of our primary interest is VR\_Tour, which equals one for a VR-enabled property and zero for a VR-disabled property. We control for three sets of covariates: the property’s attributes, the web display information, and the real estate agent attributes. Additionally, we collect the property’s neighborhood to account for the impact of its location.<sup>5</sup> We also collect the property’s listing month and selling month to account for the real estate market’s seasonality. Table 2 presents the summary statistics of the numerical variables.

Our data set contains 42,903 property records. 18% of them support VR, and 31% of them have been sold upon data collection. The observations of Selling\_Price, Web\_ Visits, and Attentions are fewer than the total number of records because they are only observed for sold properties. The average time-on-market is 125.95 days in the entire data set, including sold and unsold properties. The average selling price is 3.12 million CNY. The summary statistics of the categorical variables are reported in Online Appendix A.

## 4. Model and Identification

Because properties’ time-on-market is time-to-event data with right-censored records $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ unsold properties), we follow the literature on failure time data analysis (Kalbfleisch and Prentice 2011) and use survival models to examine the impact of VR on properties time-on-market. Besides, we follow previous literature and use the hedonic pricing model to investigate the effect of VR on properties’ selling price (Sirmans et al. 2005, Fowler et al. 2018).

## 4.1. Survival Models

4.1.1. Cox Proportional Hazard Model. The Cox proportional hazard model (hereafter Cox model) is the most widely used model in survival analysis (Cox 1972). The Cox model examines the impact of variables on the hazard rate, which represents the probability of the property i being sold at time t. The model can be specified as

$$
\begin{array}{l} \lambda (t | \boldsymbol {X} _ {i}) = \lambda_ {0} (t) e x p (\alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} V R \_ T o u r _ {i} + \alpha_ {2} L i s t i n g \_ P r i c e _ {i} \\ \qquad + \boldsymbol {\alpha} _ {3} P r o p e r t y _ {i} + \boldsymbol {\alpha} _ {4} A g e n t _ {i} \\ \qquad + \boldsymbol {\alpha} _ {5} W e b _ {i} + N e i g h b o r h o o d _ {i} + L i s t i n g M o n t h _ {i}), \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

where VR\_Tour equals one if VR Tour is available for the property i throughout its entire duration on the market (i.e., VR-enabled properties) and zero if VR Tour is never adopted (i.e., VR-disabled properties). We control for the property’s listing price (Listing\_Price<sub>i</sub>), property attributes (Property ), agent characteristics $( A g e n t _ { i } ) ,$ , and the web display information $( W e b _ { i } )$ as covariates.<sup>6</sup> Additionally, we include a set of neighborhood dummies (Neighborhood ) to account for the location fixed effects and listing month dummies (ListingMonth ) to account for the real estate market’s seasonality.

$\lambda ( t | X _ { i } )$ is the total hazard rate at time $t ,$ and $\lambda _ { 0 } ( t )$ is the baseline hazard rate at time t. The parameter of interest, $\alpha _ { 1 }$ , represents the effect of VR on the hazard rate. Thus, a significantly positive (negative) $\alpha _ { 1 }$ implies that VR-enabled properties are more (less) likely to be sold than VR-disabled ones.

We estimate the Cox model using the partial likelihood estimation method (Cox 1975). The partial likelihood function is specified as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{c} L (\boldsymbol {\alpha}) = \prod_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left\{\frac {\lambda_ {0} (t) \exp \left(\boldsymbol {X} _ {i} \boldsymbol {\alpha}\right)}{\sum_ {j \in R _ {i}} \lambda_ {0} (t) \exp \left(\boldsymbol {X} _ {j} \boldsymbol {\alpha}\right)} \right\} ^ {\delta_ {i}} \\ = \prod_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \left\{\frac {\exp \left(\boldsymbol {X} _ {i} \boldsymbol {\alpha}\right)}{\sum_ {j \in R _ {i}} \exp \left(\boldsymbol {X} _ {j} \boldsymbol {\alpha}\right)} \right\} ^ {\delta_ {i}}, \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

where $\delta _ { i }$ is a binary indicator that equals one for sold properties and zero otherwise (right-censored time-onmarket for unsold properties); $X _ { i }$ represents all covariates controlled in the model. $R _ { i } \overset { - } { = } \left\{ j : T o M _ { j } > T o M _ { i } \right\}$ indicates the set of other unsold properties when the property i is sold. No functional form is assigned to the baseline hazard function $\lambda _ { 0 } ( t )$ because it can be canceled out in the partial likelihood function. This makes the Cox model versatile and applicable in a wide range of scenarios.

4.1.2. Accelerated Failure Time Model. The Cox model assumes that the hazard functions for different individuals are proportional to each other. This assumption of constant hazard ratios between individuals may be untenable, and in such situations, the accelerated failure time (AFT) model is a useful alternative (Wei 1992, Kalbfleisch and Prentice 2011, Suder and Molstad 2022). Thus, we also adopt the AFT model to estimate the effect of VR Tour on properties’ time-on-market. The model is specified as

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & {l o g (T o M _ {i}) = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} V R \_ T o u r _ {i} + \beta_ {2} L i s t i n g \_ P r i c e _ {i}} \\ & {\qquad + \pmb {\beta} _ {3} P r o p e r t y _ {i} + \pmb {\beta} _ {4} A g e n t _ {i} + \pmb {\beta} _ {5} W e b _ {i}} \\ & {\qquad + N e i g h b o r h o o d _ {i} + L i s t i n g M o n t h _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i}.} \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

The AFT model assumes that the survival rate, $S ( t | X _ { i } )$ at time t is a function of covariates $X _ { i } .$ . We control for the same set of covariates as those in the Cox model. To simplify computation, the AFT model requires a parametric assumption on the distribution of the error term, $\varepsilon _ { i } .$ . In our main analysis, we assume that $\varepsilon _ { i }$ follows a generalized extreme value distribution. In this case, the survival rate will follow an exponential distribution, $S ( t | X _ { i } ) = 1 / e x p \bigl ( e x p ( - X _ { i } \pmb { \beta } ) t \bigr )$ . In the robustness check section, we consider several alternative distribution assumptions. The parameter of interest, $\beta _ { 1 } ,$ , represents the effect on the survival rate rather than the hazard rate. Thus, a significantly negative (positive) $\beta _ { 1 }$ suggests that VR can decrease (increase) properties time-on-market.

We estimate the AFT model using the maximum likelihood approach, with the likelihood function expressed as

$$
L (\boldsymbol {\beta}) = \prod_ {i = 1} ^ {n} f (t | \boldsymbol {X} _ {i}) ^ {\delta_ {i}} S (t | \boldsymbol {X} _ {i}) ^ {1 - \delta_ {i}},\tag{4}
$$

where $f ( t | X _ { i } )$ and $S ( t | X _ { i } )$ are the density function and survival function of property i at time $t ; \delta _ { i }$ equals one if the property is sold or zero for an unsold property when the time-on-market is right-censored.

## 4.2. Hedonic Pricing Model

The hedonic pricing model is widely used in the real estate literature to estimate the economic value of properties (Malpezzi 2002, Sirmans et al. 2005, Fowler et al. 2018). This model breaks down the features that affect a property’s selling price into internal and external factors. Internal factors refer to the characteristics of the product or service itself (i.e., property attributes), and external factors refer to other aspects outside the product or service that influence its value, including agent attributes, web page display, and the fixed effects of the location and market condition. Thus, the hedonic pricing model is specified as

$$
\begin{array}{l} \log \left(S e l l i n g \_ P r i c e _ {i}\right) \\ = \theta_ {0} + \theta_ {1} V R \_ T o u r _ {i} + \boldsymbol {\theta} _ {2} P r o p e r t y _ {i} + \boldsymbol {\theta} _ {3} A g e n t _ {i} \\ + \boldsymbol {\theta} _ {4} W e b _ {i} + N e i g h b o r h o o d _ {i} + S e l l i n g M o n t h _ {i} + v _ {i}, \end{array}\tag{5}
$$

where the dependent variable is the logarithmic transformation of the property $i ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ total selling price. $\theta _ { 1 }$ is the parameter of interest that captures the semielasticity of the selling price with respect to VR.

## 4.3. Identification

There are two main concerns with the estimation. First, the agent and property owner strategically decide whether to adopt VR Tour based on property attributes, which may introduce selection bias to the effect of VR (Rubin 1974). Second, the property owner may adjust the listing price based on the property’s time-on-market, resulting in price endogeneity. To address these concerns and enhance identification, we use propensity score matching and instrumental variable approaches.

4.3.1. Matching. We use propensity score matching (PSM), a widely used approach, to address the selection bias from observable features (Rosenbaum and Rubin 1983). The propensity score is defined as the probability that a property supports VR Tour given the observed features:

$$
p (\mathbf {X} _ {i}) = \operatorname{Prob} (V R \_ T o u r _ {i} = 1 | \mathbf {X} _ {i}),\tag{6}
$$

where $X _ { i }$ denotes the same set of covariates controlled in the model. As the control group (i.e., VR-disabled) has more observations than the treatment group (i.e., VR-enabled), we apply k-nearest neighbor matching. This method uses the k most similar observations in the control group to construct the counterfactual, which can effectively reduce the variance of the matched sample (Stuart 2010). We set $k = 5$ to align with the ratio of control and treated observations (around 4.6). To demonstrate the validity of the matching method, we conduct balance checks and find no significant difference between the treatment group and the control group after matching. Besides, we generate a density plot of propensity scores and confirm that the overlap assumption is also satisfied (see Online Appendix B for more details).

4.3.2. Selection on Unobservable Features and IV Estimation. The PSM method can address the selection bias that arises from observable features. However, it is important to note that selection bias can also arise from unobserved characteristics. For example, properties situated in a clean environment may be more inclined to use VR Tour compared with those located in a distressed setting. Likewise, a technologically proficient agent may be more likely to adopt VR Tour. Both the esthetic appeal of the property and the agent’s level of technological expertise can significantly impact consumers purchase decisions. Consequently, this introduces a hidden bias into the estimation process, resulting in the endogeneity issue.

We use the instrumental variable approach to address the above concern. Specifically, we utilize Other\_VR\_Ratio, the average VR Tour adoption rate in other neighborhoods within the same city one month prior to the focal property’s listing month, as the instrument for the endogenous VR\_Tour of the focal property. This instrument satisfies the relevance restriction because properties in the same city receive similar VR Tour services from the platform. Suppose newly listed properties in other neighborhoods show a high VR adoption rate. In that case, it is likely that the focal prop erty, which is listed on the platform one month later, will also have a high likelihood of adopting VR. This instrument also satisfies the exclusion restriction, as the real estate market varies significantly across different locations. Different neighborhoods are typically considered distinct markets. Therefore, the past VR Tour adoption in other markets (i.e., other neighborhoods)

should not influence the focal property’s current market outcomes. 7

Notably, we use different estimation strategies for the hedonic pricing model and survival models. Because the hedonic pricing model is linear, we use the two-stage least squares (2SLS) approach for estimation. Because the survival models are nonlinear, 2SLS is inapplicable. Extant literature indicates that the two-stage residual inclusion (2SRI) method offers a well-performing and consistent estimator for nonlinear models (Terza et al. 2008, Atiyat 2011, Jiang et al. 2018). Therefore, we follow the widely accepted empirical practice and use 2SRI in survival models (Danaher et al. 2015, Choi et al. 2017).

4.3.3. Price Endogeneity and IV Estimation. The property’s listing price can also be endogenous due to the owner’s price adjustment based on the property’s sales outcomes. To address the potential endogeneity of the property’s listing price, we employ the instrumental variable approach and follow the above idea to generate the instrument. Specifically, we use the average selling price of properties in other neighborhoods within the same city prior to the focal property’s listing month (referred to as Other\_Price). This instrument is considered relevant because property owners may consider the average selling price of properties in the same city when determining the initial listing price for their property. Therefore, Other\_Price correlates with the focal property’s listing price, satisfying the relevance condition.

Besides, properties in other neighborhoods that have been sold previously differ from the focal property in terms of both location and time. As a result, these sold properties in other neighborhoods do not compete with the focal property. Consequently, Other\_Price may not directly influence the sales outcomes of the focal property, thus satisfying the exclusion restriction.

## 5.3. Mechanism Analyses

In this section, we investigate how VR accelerates the selling process. Specifically, we examine VR’s heterogeneous effects on the time-on-market for properties with different product involvement, product quality, and agent service quality to reveal VR’s informative effects on property sales.

5.3.1. VR’s Information Richness. If VR Tour can provide comparatively comprehensive information about a property, it will likely be more effective when consumers exhibit a higher demand for property information. For a higher level of product involvement, consumers need to put more effort into collecting and processing product information (Traylor and Joseph 1984, Laurent and Kapferer 1985, Zaichkowsky 1985). In this case, the impact of VR Tour might vary with the level of product involvement. For example, if a property has more rooms, consumers will spend additional effort exploring each room to figure out its details. Therefore, more rooms indicate higher levels of product involvement as well as a greater demand for prop erty information. Thus, if VR provides rich product information, VR-enabled properties with more rooms will demonstrate a greater acceleration effect.

5.3.2. VR’s Information Credibility. If VR Tour provides credible information to demonstrate the authentic conditions of a property, VR could help consumers differentiate product quality. The finding suggests that VR shows a greater acceleration effect for properties of higher quality, which reveals VR’s information credibility to help consumers discern product quality, in turn improving the efficiency of property sales.

5.3.3. VR’s Relationship with Agent Services. In our context, VR is a product presentation technology that provides property information to facilitate property sales through the online channel. Beyond that, the real estate agent’s participation is also an important aspect of property sales (Arnold 1992, Benjamin et al. 2000). Agents provide services for potential buyers, including acquiring property information, scheduling offline visits, and negotiating the transaction price, mainly through the offline channel.<sup>10</sup>

Even though both the VR Tour and agent services can affect property sales, it remains unknown how VR interacts with agent services. On one side, consumers can obtain property information from both online VR and offline agent services. If VR is an alternative information source to the real estate agent to obtain property details, consumers may additionally rely on the VR Tour function when agent services are poor. In this case, VR tends to show a greater acceleration effect for agents with lower service quality. On the other side, consumers can benefit from the guidance of highquality agents to better use VR for property details. Therefore, if VR is a synergetic information source for the agent, consumers may increasingly use VR when agent services are of high quality. In this case, VR will show a more significant acceleration effect for agents with higher service quality.

To understand the VR-agent relationship in property sales, we examine the heterogeneous effects of VR for agents with different service quality. In our context, AG\_SerQual is an exogenous variable evaluated by the real estate platform, reflecting an agent’s service quality.<sup>11</sup> We add an extra interaction term between VR\_Tour and AG\_SerQual in the model and report the estimation results in Table 5, columns (5) and (6). The result suggests that VR is more likely to benefit low-quality agents. This finding implies that VR is an alternative information source to agent services in facilitating property sales.

## 5.4. VR’s Effects on Consumers Decision-Making

In previous sections, we analyze VR’s effects on properties’ final market outcomes. One potential concern is whether VR’s influence comes from the indirect (nominal) effect of consumers seeing the “VR badge” (i.e., a symbol indicating VR’s availability) or the direct (actual) effect of consumers using “VR Tour” (i.e., using the VR function for product evaluation). To examine the effectiveness of VR in consumers’ product evaluation, we further examine VR’s effects on consumers information search and alternative evaluation (Ha¨ubl and Trifts 2000, Choudhury and Karahanna 2008).

In the information search stage, consumers search for properties in accordance with their needs. They will select a small number of promising properties from the entire listing and visit each property’s detail web page for next-step consideration. For a VR-enabled property, consumers can find a VR badge on the search result web page. Web\_Visits is the outcome of consumers’ information search stage, measuring how many consumers open the detail page for further evaluation. Notably, consumers cannot employ the VR badge to obtain detailed property information. That is, the VR badge simply indicates the availability of VR. Thus, the influence of VR Tour on web visits reflects VR’s indirect effect.

In the alternative evaluation stage, consumers make comprehensive evaluations using product information on the property’s detail web page. At this stage, for a VR-enabled property, consumers can use the VR Tour function to evaluate a property’s attributes visually. After evaluation, consumers may save favored properties on their attention lists. Attentions is the number of consumers saving the property in their attention lists. We employ Attentions as a proxy of the outcome of consumers’ alternative evaluation. The influence of VR on Attentions reflects VR’s total effect on property sales, including both the indirect effect of the VR badge in the search stage and the direct effect of the VR Tour function in the evaluation stage.

To further disentangle VR’s direct and indirect effects, we generate two variables: Conversion\_Rate and Conversion\_Odds. ## 6.1. Alternative Survival Models

To eliminate the impact of misspecifications in the survival model, we consider different distributions for the hazard rate and survival rate (Chung et al. 1991). Particularly, we consider three alternative distributions for the hazard rate, Weibull, Gompertz, and exponential, and two alternative distributions for the survival rate: gamma and Loglogistic. As shown in Table 7, columns (1)–(5), VR\_Tour shows significantly positive effects on the hazard rate and significantly negative effects on the survival rate, consistent with our main findings.

The above survival models consider properties time-on-market as a continuous variable. Alternatively, the time-on-market can be considered a discrete variable because it is measured in days. In this case, we can apply the discrete time logit hazard model, which allows us to control for the time-varying impact (Allison 1982). Specifically, we construct a panel where each property appears repeatedly by month on the market. For example, if a property is listed on the platform in February and sold in May, it will appear in March, April, and May. We include Month × City dummies to account for the time-region fixed effects, which capture the real estate market dynamics in each city over a property’s selling process. Table 7, column (6), shows that VR Tour reduces properties’ time-on-market after controlling for the time-varying impact, consistent with our main findings.

## 6.2. Mahalanobis-Distance Kernel Matching

In the above sections, we use the k-nearest propensity score matching to partially address the imbalance issue. However, a potential concern is the appropriateness of the choice of k, which can affect the efficacy of matching and estimation results. To address the concern, we fol low the suggestion of King and Nielsen (2019) by adopting a fully blocked matching method, Mahalanobisdistance kernel matching. Compared with propensity score matching, Mahalanobis-distance matching can better detect multivariate outliers and construct the counterfactual using all records in the control group rather than a selected subset of records (Caliendo and Kopeinig 2008).

## 6.3. Sensitivity Analysis

We follow Rosenbaum (2002) to conduct a sensitivity analysis, further addressing the selection issue from unobservable factors. The sensitivity analysis identifies the extreme condition that the bias of unobserved factors on treatment assignment can change the conclusion. Specifically, the method uses an odds ratio Γ to represent the magnitude of hidden bias due to unobservables. A large Γ implies a high deviation from the randomized treatment assignment. Table 8 shows that the acceleration effect of VR on properties’ time-on-market is still significant when Γ reaches 2.1. The result indicates that our conclusion will not change, even if the unobserved factors increase the odds ratio of treatment assignment by 2.1 times. The sensitivity analysis serves as additional evidence, showing the robustness of our findings to the hidden bias from unobservables.

## 7. Conclusion

VR is an emerging visual product presentation technology that provides a 3D and interactive way for consumers to explore property attributes on real estate platforms. Most existing research either focuses on the impact of traditional visual presentation formats (such as virtual tours and pictures) on property sales or investigates VR’s impacts from a behavioral perspective on low-involvement product sales. In this study, we summarize the unique challenges of property sales in the real estate context, conceptualize VR’s novel aspects from an informative perspective, and conduct large-scale empirical analyses to study the effects of VR on market outcomes in property sales.

Our results show that VR significantly decreases properties’ time-on-market but does not influence their total selling price. Mechanism analyses demonstrate that VR has a greater acceleration effect for properties with higher product involvement, revealing VR’s information richness. Similarly, VR has a more pronounced acceleration effect for properties with higher product quality, revealing VR’s information credibility. In addition, VR shows a more significant acceleration effect for agents with lower service quality, revealing VR as an alternative information source to agent services. Moreover, VR shows significant and positive effects on consumers’ web visits, attention numbers, and conversions in the decision-making process.

This study makes a series of theoretical contributions. First, it contributes to the literature on nonimmersive VR in e-commerce. Previous works discussed VR’s effects from a behavioral perspective to show its impacts on consumer responses (Suh and Lee 2005; Jiang and Benbasat 2007a, b; Debbabi et al. 2010; Papagiannidis et al. 2013; Spielmann and Mantonakis 2018; Pleyers and Poncin 2020). To the best of our knowledge, this study is among the first to examine VR’s impacts on market outcomes on real estate platforms. Nonimmersive VR’s acceleration effect on the time-on-market and its insignificant impact on the selling price demonstrate VR as an efficiency enhancer instead of a market value influencer. Further mechanism analyses reveal VR’s informative effects in facilitating property sales, including VR’s information richness, VR’s information credibility, and VR as an alternative information source over agent services.

Second, this study enhances the literature on VR in property sales and, more broadly, VR in high-involvement product marketing. Most previous literature examined VR’s impact on selling low-involvement products (Suh and Lee 2005, Debbabi et al. 2010, Papagiannidis et al. 2013). However, properties are typical high-involvement products with relatively high value, importance, and purchase risk and need agent services to facilitate real estate transactions. This study focuses on three unique issues in high-involvement product marketing, including product involvement (Clarke and Belk 1978, Traylor 1981, Zaichkowsky 1986), product quality (Petty and Cacioppo 1979, 1981; Zaichkowsky 1986), and agent service quality (Arnold 1992, Benjamin et al. 2000, Turnbull and Dombrow 2007, Agarwal et al. 2019), examining VR’s influences in real estate transactions. Our results indicate that VR can satisfy consumers’ demand for information with the increase in product involvement, provide credible information to help consumers discern product quality, and offer an alternative source for consumers to obtain product details, complementing poor agent services. These findings can be extended to other high-involvement products, such as automobiles. In this case, buyers are faced with similar challenges, including high levels of product involvement, significant concerns about quality, and dealer participation.

Third, this study extends the literature on product presentation technologies in e-commerce. Previous literature shows that diagnostic product descriptions can reduce product uncertainty and bring a price premium (Dimoka et al. 2012). Traditional pictures and virtual tours provide consistently positive effects on the selling price but conflicting effects on properties’ selling time (Carrillo 2008, Allen et al. 2015, Yu et al. 2021). Our results show that VR can accelerate properties’ selling process but may not affect their final selling price. Given that presentation technologies are merely product information providers rather than components of product attributes, our finding suggests that VR can improve product transaction efficiency without affecting product market value. Consumers will not pay a price premium for additional information provided by VR. VR does not change consumers’ assessments of the product’s intrinsic value. Therefore, VR is a virtuous product presentation technology with a strong potential in e-commerce.

Our work offers several managerial implications for practitioners. First, property owners and agents can use VR tours to expedite property sales. Our findings suggest that VR could reduce the time-on-market by 28%–49%, equivalent to approximately 19–34 days shorter listing durations. This benefit not only allows property owners to accelerate the selling process but also enables agents to manage more properties efficiently, potentially reducing their workload. Second, our study underscores VR’s capabilities in information richness and credibility. Thus, we particularly recommend properties of higher quality and greater involvement to prioritize adopting VR for product presentation. Additionally, in situations where agent service quality is lacking, property owners might use VR as a compensatory tool. Third, real estate platforms can indirectly benefit from the more efficient market facilitated by VR. With each transaction generating commission fees between 31,200 and 93,600 CNY, platforms could improve their cash flow. The enhanced transaction and increased historical transaction count may help attract new consumers. Platforms should weigh the marginal cost of VR (100 CNY in our context) against the substantial benefits, and target properties most likely to be influenced by VR first when resources are limited.

Our study can be extended in the following directions: First, we are unable to directly observe buyers’ attributes and their interactions with VR and agents across both online and offline channels. Future studies could collect individual-level data to deepen understanding of the mechanisms behind VR. Second, we use a binary indicator, VR\_Tour, to indicate whether a property is VR-enabled. Future studies may use computer vision techniques to quantify novel aspects of VR and analyze their effects, potentially offering useful insights to enhance VR design. Third, we do not perform a horizontal comparison between VR and traditional visual techniques. Future efforts could explore the interaction effects between different product presentation techniques in property sales.
