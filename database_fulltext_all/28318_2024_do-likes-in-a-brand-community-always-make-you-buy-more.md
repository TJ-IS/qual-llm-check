# Do “Likes” in a Brand Community Always Make You Buy More?

Abstract. Online brand communities often use social plug-in features, such as the Like button, to facilitate social interactions and engage users with the communities and brands. However, whether and how such a community feature affects the purchase decisions of users remain open questions. Leveraging the staggered adoption of the Like feature by users in an online brand community, we empirically investigate how the purchase behavior of users changes after the adoption of this feature. Surprisingly, we find that the order number and expenditure of an average user decrease by 4.1% and 25.0%, respectively, after the user starts to use this feature. Specifically, after the adoption of the Like feature, users order number and expenditure preeminently decrease by 3.4% and 21.1% online, respectively, and slightly decrease by 0.7% and 4.7% offline, respectively. The treatment effect of the adoption is not always negative but varies over time and across users. First, the Like feature adoption has a positive effect on users’ purchases in the first two months (primarily through enhancing their community participation), and the treatment effect turns negative in subsequent months, leading to the overall negative treatment effect on purchases. Second, the negative treatment effect likely stems from unflattering social comparison and can become weaker or even positive when users accrue more Likes from other users. However, only a small proportion of users receive sufficient Likes from other community users to be motivated to purchase more. We further conduct a scenario-based online experiment to corroborate our findings and elucidate the underlying mechanisms. Our results caution against potential downsides of the Like feature in online communities and provide valuable managerial implications.

## 1. Introduction

Online brand communities (OBCs) are a mainstay of firms’ digital marketing strategies. It is estimated that more than 74% of large firms have OBCs,<sup>1</sup> and over 200 million small businesses use Facebook pages to communicate with customers.<sup>2</sup> By hosting OBCs on their own websites or social media platforms, firms aim to reach and engage existing customers, potentially leading to higher customer retention and sales (Herna´ndez-Ortega et al. 2022, Liadeli et al. 2023).<sup>3</sup> There are many ways to improve consumer engagement in OBCs. One increasingly popular way is to introduce social plug-in features that allow lightweight acts of communication with minimal effort, such as the Like feature on Facebook, WeChat, and Instagram (Bapna et al. 2019) and the Upvote feature on Reddit (Hayes et al. 2016).

Given the popularity of the community feature design for lightweight acts of communication (called the Like feature thereafter) in OBCs, it is paramount to understand whether and how the introduction of the Like feature can increase users’ purchases. Here, the Like fea ture refers to the one-click community design that allows users to show their enjoyment or support for others postings in online communities without leaving textual comments.<sup>4</sup> Because the Like feature in OBCs is designed for social interactions among users, its effect can be different from that of users’ Likes on a firm’s Facebook pages or postings, which are designed for interactions between users and firms and whose economic benefits are more straightforward and have been established in prior studies (e.g., Bapna et al. 2019).

Although not empirically validated, the Like feature is usually considered as a design to encourage users to climb the engagement ladder and strengthen thei brand attachment. For one thing, the Like feature is expected to facilitate the establishment, development, and maintenance of users’ interpersonal relationships in online communities (Hayes et al. 2016) and thus, increase community participation. The literature on OBCs suggests that increased community participation can result in improved store visits (Rishika et al. 2013) and brand preference (Thompson and Sinha 2008), which in turn, may lead to more purchases. For another, when users receive many Likes, they tend to perceive the community caring (Zell and Moeller 2018) and evoke feelings of belonging (Tobin et al. 2015), suggesting a strong attachment to the community and the brand (Ren et al. 2012, Oestreicher-Singer and Zalmanson 2013). By prompting users’ participation in and attachment to the OBC, the Like feature has the potential to increase users’ purchases.

However, not all users receive many Likes. In this regard, the Like feature adoption may lead to divergent effects on users’ purchases depending on the number of Likes they receive. Specifically, as a representation of quantifiable social support by nature, the Like feature makes it easily accessible for social comparison (Rosenthal-von der Pu¨ tten et al. 2019), which may evoke negative emotions, such as stress (Fox and Moreland 2015), upset (Vogel et al. 2015), and envy (Rosenthal-von der Pu¨ tten et al. 2019), among users receiving few Likes. These negative emotions experienced in an OBC with the Like feature can translate into a negative brand affect (Chaudhuri and Holbrook 2001) and induce a weaker brand attachment, resulting in fewer future purchases. Thus, what the overall impact of the Like feature adoption on users’ purchases is still remains an open question and requires a rigorous empirical investigation.

In addition, as most firms are selling products in both online and offline channels,<sup>5</sup> even if the Like feature can increase users’ participation in the online channel, we cannot simply extend this finding to users’ purchases in other channels. Surprisingly, there is little research that investigates how introducing a feature for lightweight acts of communication (e.g., the Like feature) into OBCs affects users’ purchases in the multichannel retailing context. This is likely because of the challenge of accessing both the fine-grained user activity data within OBCs and the intricate transaction records across multiple channels.

To fill the gap, we obtain rich proprietary data from a prominent apparel firm in China, which allows us to track users’ community participation and purchase behavior in multiple channels simultaneously. During our observation window, the firm directly sold products to customers online through its e-store and offline through its physical stores. The price and promotion (if any) in both online and offline channels were always consistent. In particular, the firm built an OBC that allows consumers to share their opinions and experience related to various topics (e.g., holidays, movies, or new product releases). Our data include all the orders made in both online and offline channels and all users’ activities in its OBC.

We leverage the exogenous shock in which the firm first rolled out the Like feature in its OBC without any preannouncement. Following the prior literature (e.g., Bapna et al. 2018, Jung et al. 2019, Gu and Kannan 2021), we focus on the treatment effect of adopting the Like feature (i.e., the change in users’ purchases between preand postadoption periods). Our identification is based on the difference-in-differences (DID) model with users staggered adoption. By using later adopters (who had not adopted the feature at that time but did adopt it at a later time) as the counterfactual, we find that, surprisingly, the order number and expenditure on average decrease by 4.1% and 25.0%, respectively, after a user adopts this feature. Specifically, after the adoption of the Like feature in the OBC, users’ order number and expen diture preeminently decrease by 3.4% and 21.1% online, respectively, and slightly decrease by 0.7% and 4.7% offline, respectively.

To better understand why users decrease their purchases after adopting the Like feature, we examine the effect of the Like feature adoption on users’ community participation. By including multiple leads and lags in our DID model, we further validate the parallel trend assumption for the pretreatment period and estimate the dynamic treatment effect during the posttreatment period. We find that the Like feature adoption can increase users’ community participation for about five months and online purchases for two months. However, even though the Like feature can increase users’ purchases indirectly through encouraging community participation, this positive effect wears off quickly, followed by a long-standing negative treatment effect on users purchases in both online and offline channels.

Furthermore, we observe significant heterogeneity in the treatment effect of the Like feature adoption. Specifically, the negative treatment effect of the feature adoption is primarily driven by the decrease in users’ purchases online and offline when they receive zero Likes or one Like in the OBC, and the effect magnifies as social comparison becomes more salient. For users who receive more than two Likes, the treatment effect of the Like feature adoption on users’ online purchases becomes significantly positive, whereas the effect on offline purchases is insignificant. Our findings are highly consistent when we use a look-ahead coarsened exact matching (LA-CEM) method to match users who adopt the Like features at different times or use a group of matched noncommunity users as the control group. Our identification strategy is also supported by a series of robustness checks (e.g., parallel trend test, placebo test, sensitivity analysis, alternative treatment definitions, and alternative model configurations).

As a further step to uncover additional mechanisms that we cannot observe directly in the observational study, we conduct a scenario-based online experiment. In the experiment, we randomly assign participants into groups with different Like feature conditions. Consistently, we find that the treatment effect of the Like feature on users’ (online and offline) purchase intention is significantly negative when users receive zero or a low number of Likes, whereas it becomes positive when users receive a high number of Likes. Furthermore, users’ brand attachment mediates the relationship between the Like feature treatment and users’ purchase intention, and users’ social comparison orientation (i.e., how frequently or how much users are concerned about social comparison) moderates the relationship between the Like feature treatment and users’ brand attachment. Overall, the experiment results corroborate our findings from the observational study, demonstrate the generalizability of our results, and reveal additional mechanisms behind the effect of the Like feature on purchases and its heterogeneity.

Our work contributes to the academic literature in several important ways. First, we extend the prior literature on OBCs by highlighting the impact of the Like feature adoption on users’ purchases. Prior literature primarily focuses on the effect of firm-solicited Likes on the brand’s social media page on users’ purchases (e.g., John et al. 2017, Mochon et al. 2017), leaving the economic benefit of introducing the Like feature into customer-to-customer (C2C) interactions in OBCs out of the spotlight. The impact of the Like feature in C2C interactions may differ from its impact in consumerto-business/brand (C2B) interactions because of the potential for social comparison among users, which is not present in C2B contexts. We fill this gap in the literature by showing that the Like feature adoption in OBCs is associated with an average decrease in users’ purchases when most users receive few Likes. Although the negative effect on purchases can turn positive if users receive more Likes to offset the negative emotion arising from unflattering social comparison, in practice, there may be only a small proportion of users receiving sufficient Likes to motivate them to purchase more. Among the first attempt to investigate the effectiveness of the Like feature in increasing sales, we provide guidance on the feature design of OBCs and how to mitigate the potential unintended negative effect of the Like feature by enhancing users’ brand attachment (e.g., encouraging employees to Like users’ comments or leveraging artificial intelligence (AI) to provide customized responses to users’ comments).

Second, we advance the extant literature on OBCs by documenting the impact of community feature design on users’ purchases in a multichannel retailing context. Prior studies on multichannel retailing (Gill et al. 2017, Bar-Gil and Reichman 2021) propose that features offered in the online channel can shift consumers’ attention, interactions, and thus, sales across channels. However, it is unclear a priori how a new feature design in the online channel affects consumers’ purchase behavior in both online and offline channels. To fill in this gap, with the proprietary data from a prominent apparel firm, we estimate the impact of the Like feature on users purchases in both online (wherein the community is hosted) and offline channels. We find that the Like feature in the OBC may serve as a hygiene factor for offline purchases (Lacey et al. 2015). The adoption can have a significant negative spillover effect on offline purchases when users receive few Likes, whereas the positive crosschannel spillover effect is marginal or insignificant when users receive more Likes. Our study provides insights for firms using the OBC as a strategic tool in the multichannel retailing context.

Third, our paper complements the extant research that primarily concerns the short-term impact of the community design on the community per se (e.g., Ren et al. 2012, Bapna et al. 2019). We document the impact of the Like feature on users’ community participation and purchases in both the short term and the long term. We find a trade-off when firms introduce the Like feature into OBCs; although the Like feature adoption can increase users’ community participation and online purchases in the short term, the negative effect on users’ purchases is long standing if they receive few Likes, resulting in low brand attachment that aggravates over time. We suggest that firms should carefully evaluate the economic benefit of the feature design of OBCs and consider strategies to maintain its positive effect in the long term.

## 2. Theoretical Background 2.1. Online Brand Community

Muniz and O’Guinn (2001, p. 412) define a brand community as a “specialized, non-geographically bound community, based on a structured set of social relationships among admirers of a branded good or service.” McAlexander et al. (2002) go a step further and describe the brand community as relationships not only among customers themselves but also between the custome and firm and between customers and the product in use. Research examining these relationships in the technology mediated setting has referred to them as OBCs (Bapna et al. 2019). Overall, OBCs are composed of people who engage in social interactions about their shared enthusiasm for or use of specific brand products, retail environ ments, or consumption activities in the online setting (Algesheimer et al. 2010). In general, OBCs include those that operate independently of the brand or firm (e.g., Thompson and Sinha 2008) and those hosted by a firm (i.e., firm-sponsored OBCs) (e.g., Manchanda et al. 2015).

OBCs have long been touted as a possible path to “the Holy Grail of brand loyalty” (McAlexander et al. 2002, p. 38). Firms that operate OBCs are said to have the opportunity to boost users’ purchases by encouraging their community participation and cultivating brand attachment (Rishika et al. 2013, Park et al. 2018). There is ample evidence supporting the positive impact of community participation in OBCs. For example, previous studies have demonstrated that community participation can increase the propensity for new product adoption (Thompson and Sinha 2008), store visit frequency (Rishika et al. 2013), and willingness to pay for premium service (Oestreicher-Singer and Zalmanson 2013).

OBCs can also foster a stronger brand-self connection, leading to enhanced brand attachment and a subsequent increase in purchases. Research has shown that when users have more communication with others and build more social connections within OBCs, they tend to develop a stronger connection with the brand and make more purchases (Park et al. 2018). Moreover, extant literature suggests that users’ emotional response while participating in the OBC may translate into their emotions toward the host brand, known as brand affect, which in turn, affects brand attachment and loyalty (Marzocchi et al. 2013). Therefore, the extent to which OBCs can enhance brand attachment and loyalty among users is contingent on their ability to elicit users’ positive emotions from community participation.

Despite the potential value and importance of OBCs, several studies have found that many community initiatives fail to deliver enjoyable OBC experiences and affectively engage users (e.g., John et al. 2017). Simply creating an internet-mediated environment is not sufficient to generate positive customer value. A successful OBC requires the brand to effectively utilize online tools and design appropriate mechanisms to foster brand attachment (Harmeling et al. 2017). Although the technical design of OBCs is recognized as a crucial factor in determining important community outcomes (Ma and Agarwal 2007), it has received limited attention, and empirical investigation on whether and how feature design of OBCs can contribute to their success in increasing consumer purchases is still lacking.

## 2.2. The Like Feature in Consumer Interactions

To strengthen customer value and enhance customer engagement, firms can design OBCs with a wide array of features. Community features that enable user activities have evolved over time, but one feature that has remained relatively constant is that designed for lightweight acts of communication, such as Like (e.g., on Facebook) and Upvote (e.g., on Reddit). Hayes et al. (2016) conceptualize these features as paralinguistic digital affordance technologies that are lightweight means of communication activated by a single click and represented by a static icon. Cheikh-Ammar and Barki (2016) also define the Like feature as a sociotechnical design used to drive social interactions among users.

The Like feature can be designed to encourage consumers to interact with the firm or brand (i.e., C2B interac tions). Consumers can send Likes to the firm’s social media page or posts in the OBC. Recent research has focused on the economic benefit of such C2B Likes (see Table A1 in Online Appendix A for a review). For instance, Mochon et al. (2017) conduct a field experiment on firm-solicited Facebook page Likes and find a positive causal effect on offline purchases. Manchanda et al. (2015) find a significant increase in customer expenditures resulting from Liking the firm’s community. In contrast, John et al. (2017) find that Liking a brand’s social media page has no positive effect on consumer attitudes or purchases. These studies define community Liking behavior as the click of the Like button on social media to endorse the OBC without examining the potential usage of the Like feature as an OBC interaction tool. Recently, a few studies focus on the impact of Liking a firm’s posts in OBCs and reveal that such C2B Likes tend to be a crucial determinant of important community/ business outcomes. For instance, Bapna et al. (2019) find that Liking a brand’s posts is positively associated with subsequent community activities.

Nevertheless, none of the previous studies have considered the Like feature as an online design for C2C interactions in OBCs, and there is a paucity of empirical research on whether and how such a feature design can bring economic benefit. On the one hand, the Like feature for C2C interactions serves as a low-cost means to create or strengthen social connections and can be used to convey signals of social appreciation and support, thereby increasing users’ community participation and brand attachment. On the other hand, the quantitative and visible nature of C2C Likes may present opportunities for unflattering social comparison in OBCs (Rosenthal-von der Pu¨ tten et al. 2019), which might lead to negative emotions and a reduction in their brand attachment and future purchases.

Specifically, the Like feature for C2C interactions in OBCs is designed to serve as a convenient interpersonal communication tool. Compared with other feedback features in C2C interactions, such as “Comment” and “Share,” sending Likes by clicking the button is convenient and requires little effort. This convenience is expected to help create and strengthen social connections among consumers in the OBC (Xu et al. 2020), which can provide more incentive for them to participate in the OBC. Furthermore, the Like feature for C2C interactions can strengthen users’ community identity and enhance brand attachment. Kietzmann et al. (2011) report that receiving Likes can be perceived as getting approval from others and validating their community identity and status. Rosenthal-von der Pu¨ tten et al. (2019) and Lindstro¨m et al. (2021) reveal that Likes from others are usually perceived as social rewards. Overall, previous research suggests that the Like feature may amplify users’ feelings of social approval and rewards from the

OBC, thereby improving their attachment to the OBC and the host brand.

However, the Like feature, which is quantifiable, uncontrollable, and publicly visible (Sherman et al. 2016), can lead to positive or negative emotions following consumers’ Like feature adoption depending on the number of Likes they receive. Specifically, the Like feature serves as a quantified criterion by which consumers can judge their own posts and triggers painful social comparison. Rosenthal-von der Pu¨ tten et al. (2019) find that unflattering social comparison enabled by the Like feature (comparison with others receiving more Likes) frequently leads to negative emotions, whereas flattering social comparison (the opposite case) can lead to positive emotions. Lee (2014) and Vogel et al. (2015) further point out that users with a higher social comparison orientation (i.e., a higher tendency to compare themselves with others) tend to have a more negative affect balance from social comparison. Because positive and negative emotions experienced in an OBC with the Like feature can translate into positive and negative brand affect (Chaudhuri and Holbrook 2001), they can substantially impact users’ brand attachment and future purchases, particularly for those with a high social comparison orientation.

In sum, although the Like feature for C2C interactions can facilitate social interactions and support among consumers, both positive and negative user emotions may occur after firms embed the Like feature into the design of OBCs. However, to our knowledge, no study has examined the impact of customers’ adoption of the Like feature in facilitating C2C interactions in OBCs on their purchase behavior, which motivates our first research question.

## 2.3. Online and Offline Channel Interdependencies

As more and more consumers embrace various retailing channels to optimize their shopping experience, multichannel retailing is becoming a go-to tactic, and firms are facing immense pressure to integrate the best of both digital and physical channels. In this work, multichannel retailing refers to the retailers’ use of integrated online and offline channels to sell products and services to customers, and it can be formally defined as the practice of simultaneously offering information, products, services, and support to consumers through both online and offline channels (Deleersnyder et al. 2002, Ghose and Yao 2011). With the advance of technology, multichannel retailing has evolved into omnichannel retailing.

Previous studies have shown mixed results for the impact of integrating online and offline channels on sales, with findings varying depending on the product category and the length of the observation window (Weltevreden 2007). In regard to the effect of adding the online channel to an already established offline channel, Deleersnyder et al. (2002) find that online channels rarely cannibalize offline channels, whereas Weltevreden (2007) reports that in the long run, online purchases substitute for offline purchases. Regarding the impact of adding the offline channel to an established online channel, Forman et al. (2009) show that, in the book industry, offline store entry decreases consumers’ sensitivity to online product discounts and lowers sales of the online retailer in the area of store entry, whereas Avery et al. (2012) show that online sales of an apparel retailer increase in the areas where it opens physical stores.

Apart from selling products, firms offer online and off line channels as touchpoints for customer interactions, which have the potential to retain customers and increase consumer purchases (Gill et al. 2017). Therefore, prior literature has also examined how customer involvement in one channel leads to purchases in another (Kumar et al. 2019, Bar-Gill and Reichman 2021). Recently, Kumar et al. (2019) studied the complementary effect of store presence on online purchases and find that customers make more online purchases because of higher engagement from store interactions. The Wiesel et al (2011) conceptual framework for the online-offline funnel states that online participation moves consumers from the initial consideration level to the decision-making level and thus, eventu ally leads to purchases. Bar-Gill and Reichman (2021) examine the effect of online customer interactions with the brand’s website on offline sales for products that only sell offline $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ luxurious cars) and find that a highly engaging website leads to a decline in offline sales. In contrast, Gill et al. (2017) find that online customer interac tions can yield a positive effect on industrial materials sales. Although prior work on the effect of online cus tomer interactions has mixed findings, both Gill et al. (2017) and Bar-Gill and Reichman (2021) emphasize that online customer interactions play an important role in the online-offline funnel, and managers should incorporate appropriate features to encourage customer interactions in the online channel and foster brand attachment. The present studies propose that the features offered in the online channel can shift consumers’ attention and interac tions across channels and thus, affect sales across chan: nels. However, it is unclear a priori how the new feature design in the online channel affects consumers’ purchase behavior across both online and offline channels. Specifi cally, the Like feature in OBCs is designed for users to sig nal attention and appreciation, acting as a tool to enhance online customer interactions. Therefore, in this study, ou second research question investigates how introducing the Like feature to the brand community in the online channel affects customers’ multichannel purchase behav ior both instantaneously and in the long term.

## 3. Research Context and Data

## 3.1. Research Context

To answer our research questions, we collaborate with a prominent apparel firm in China. Its products are moderately priced (approximately \$23.8 on average), and customers frequently make repeat purchases (4.7 times per year on average).<sup>6</sup> Like Zara or Everlane, the firm’s products are private-label products and are only available through the firm’s retail channels. There are mainly two channels: (1) the online channel (the firm’s e-store, which allows consumers to view product details and purchase online) and (2) the offline channel (the firm’s local stores). The product price and promotion (if any) are consistent across channels. The firm requires consumers to provide their member IDs at the time of purchase,<sup>7</sup> enabling it to track their orders online and offline seamlessly.

## 4. Empirical Analysis

Following the prior literature (e.g., Bapna et al. 2018, Jung et al. 2019, Gu and Kannan 2021), we exploit the variation in users’ timing of the first time using the Like feature and use the DID model to estimate the impact of the Like feature adoption on users’ purchases. To estimate the causal effect of the usage of the Like feature on users’ purchases, we use a two-way fixed effects (TWFE) model to compare the change in the purchases of current feature users between the pre- and postadoption periods with the change for later feature users (as well as noncommunity users as an alternative control group). The TWFE model with the DID specification controls for the time-invariant confounders with the user fixed effects and the common time confounders using the time fixed effects that capture the (calendar) week-specific temporal shocks. We index users by i and index time (calendar weeks) by t. As different users start to use the Like feature at different times, we use a DID setup with staggered adoption as follows:

$$
Y _ {i t} = \alpha + \beta T r e a t e d _ {i t} + \pmb {\gamma} _ {i} + \pmb {\delta} _ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t}.\tag{1}
$$

Here, $Y _ { i t }$ is a dependent variable that denotes the user i’s purchase behavior at time $t ,$ including the number of orders and the total expenditure. The independent variable of interest is Treated , a binary variable indicating whether user i is a Like feature user at time t, and it is equal to one if he or she started to use the Like feature (including sending or receiving a Like) and zero otherwise. Specifically, $\gamma _ { i }$ denotes the user fixed effects. The term $\delta _ { t }$ represents the time fixed effects, and $\varepsilon _ { i t }$ is an error term. We cluster the errors by users to allow correlation in the error terms across time within the same user. Notably, our model setup accounts for the impact of time-varying factors (e.g., seasonality, brand promotion, product line expansion, new competition, and exogenous temporal shocks) and time-invariant heterogeneity at the user level (e.g., personality and demographic information). As shown in Section 6.4, our results are robust if we also include user-level time-varying controls (e.g., the lagged term of user i’s community participation activities). However, because the lagged terms are likely to be affected by the treatment and thus, partially absorb the treatment effect, we do not include the lagged terms as controls in our main analysis.

As with most observational studies, the identification challenge is that we cannot observe the counterfactual of feature users’ behavior if they had not used the Like feature. In our main analysis, we employ the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT) estimator based on the DID model to overcome the identification challenge:

$$
A T T = E (Y _ {i t} (1) | t r e a t _ {i} = 1) - E (Y _ {i t} (0) | t r e a t _ {i} = 1),\tag{2}
$$

where treat denotes whether users had ever been treated in our observation window. $Y _ { i t } ( 1 )$ and $Y _ { i t } ( 0 )$ denote the potential outcomes of user i at time t with and without treatment, respectively. Here, we use the ATT based on the DID between current feature users (treated users) and future feature users (untreated users) and rely on the common trend assumption that, without treatment, the outcome variable follows the same evolution over time for all the feature users (de Chaisemartin and D’Haultfoeuille 2020). As suggested by Jung et al. (2019), the DID within the treatment group (feature users) tends to be subject to less selection bias than the DID between the treatment group and the control group (nonfeature users).

To account for the potential bias in a staggered TWFE model (Goodman-Bacon 2021), we employ three additional analyses to showcase the robustness of our findings in Section 6. First, we use a DID model with leads and lags $( \mathrm { i . e . } ,$ , the event-study estimate), which is suggested to be more reliable than the canonical two-by-two DID estimate that assumes the treatment effect is constant over time (Goodman-Bacon 2021). Second, we leverage the behavioral data observed prior to the treatment time and employ the LA-CEM approach to match current feature users (treated users) and future feature users (untreated users). Third, we estimate the treatment effect based on the DID between feature users and users who cannot access the interface of the OBC and thus, are not affected by the feature (a stable control group) to address any potential concern over using future treated users as the control group in the DID analysis (de Chaisemartin and D’Haultfoeuille 2020, Goodman-Bacon 2021). This analy sis also alleviates the concern about the potential violation of the stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA) (Rubin 1990).

## 4.3. Main Results on Purchases via Different Channels

To understand the effect of the Like feature adoption across channels, we further decompose users’ purchases into two main channels: online and offline (i.e., in-store purchases). Interestingly, we find that the Like feature adoption has different effects on users’ purchases across channels. As shown in Table 3, on average, the Like feature adoption significantly decreases the number of online orders by 3.4%, whereas the decline in the number of offline orders is a modest 0.7%. The effect size on expenditure is more preeminent, and the effect is again more significant for online orders than offline orders, probably because the community users primarily purchase online. In particular, the Like feature adoption remarkably decreases online expenditure by 21.1% and offline expenditure by 4.7%. The finding suggests that adopting the Like feature (i.e., a lightweight acts of communication feature) does not lead to an expected positive effect on users’ online or offline purchases. Furthermore, users’ online purchases are more sensitive to the feature adoption, which largely dominates the decrease in users purchases.

## 5. Conceptual Framework for Mechanism Exploration

Although our main analysis shows that the Like feature adoption in the OBC leads to a negative average treatment effect on users’ online and offline purchases, it is important to explore the underlying mechanism behind the effect. As discussed in Section 2, the Like feature designed for C2C interactions in OBCs can have two competing effects: (1) enhancing users’ community participation and subsequent purchases by serving as a low-cost interpersonal communication tool and increasing opportunities for social rewards and (2) leading to negative or positive emotions from social comparison and thus, a significant impact on brand attachment and future purchases because of the quantifiable, visible nature of Likes. When users receive few Likes and experience unflattering social comparison, it can lead to negative emotions and decrease their brand attachment and purchases. By comparison, positive emotions from the flattering social comparison can enhance brand attachment and purchases. In addi tion, the negative (positive) effect of unflattering (flattering) social comparison can be more pronounced when the social comparison is more intense (e.g., when the discrepancy is more salient or when users have a higher social comparison orientation). ## 6.2. Dynamic Treatment Effect

We employ an extension of the DID model by including multiple leads and lags to examine the dynamic treatment effect and the parallel trend assumption of the DID model (Autor 2003, Angrist and Pischke 2008). This practice not only can assess whether users who are treated at different times follow the parallel trend during the pretreatment period but also can examine when the treatment effect starts to emerge and how it evolves dynamically over time. We do this by adding a set of dummy variables to trace out the month-by-month treatment effect as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} Y _ {i t} = \alpha + \beta_ {1} T r e a t e d _ {i t} ^ {- 1 0} + \beta_ {2} T r e a t e d _ {i t} ^ {- 9} + \dots + \beta_ {1 9} T r e a t e d _ {i t} ^ {+ 9} \\ \quad + \beta_ {2 0} T r e a t e d _ {i t} ^ {+ 1 0} + \boldsymbol {\delta} _ {t} + \boldsymbol {\gamma} _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i t}. \end{array} \tag {3}
$$

Here, the series of dummy variables $\{ T r e a t e d _ { i t } ^ { - 1 0 } .$ $T r e a t e d _ { i t } ^ { - 9 } , \ldots , T r e a t e d _ { i t } ^ { 9 } , T r e a t e d _ { i t } ^ { \mathrm { i 0 } } \}$ is defined by user $i \prime \mathrm { s }$ adoption status at time t. The variable $T r e a t e d _ { i t } ^ { - k }$ equals one for user i in the kth month before his or her adoption month, whereas $T r e a t e d _ { i t } ^ { k }$ equals one for user i in the kth month since his or her adoption month. Earlier and later time periods outside the range [�10, 10] are pooled in the periods of the 10th month before and after the adoption month, respectively. We detrend and center all the relative time parameters relative to the month right before the adoption $( \mathrm { i . e . , } T r e a t e d _ { i t } ^ { - 1 }$ is the baseline).

We report the detailed estimation results in Table C1 in Online Appendix C and visualize the monthly dynamic treatment effect estimates on users’ community participation and purchases with the 95% confidence intervals in Figure 3. The nonsignificant relative time coefficients (except sporadically a few marginally significant ones, particularly in the model regarding community participation) before the adoption time suggest that the untreated users (who had not adopted the Like feature yet but would adopt it in the future) follow the parallel trend as the treated users and serve as a reasonably good counterfactual.<sup>10</sup> With regard to community participation, Figure 3(a) indicates that users’ participation immediately increases since their adoption of the Like feature. How ever, the treatment effect on community participation becomes insignificant five months after their adoption and continues into later months. Further, regarding users online and offline purchases, Figure 3(b) suggests that once users adopt the Like feature, they increase online purchases immediately, which leads to a transitory positive effect on their total purchases. However, after this short transition period, users decrease their online purchases two months after adopting the Like feature, and the negative effect remains stable afterward. In terms of users’ offline purchases, it takes time for the feature adoption to spill over and take effect offline, and its treatment effect is also smaller. Once it takes effect (about two months after adoption), there is a negative posttreatment trend in users’ offline purchases. Specifically, the negative treatment effect on the number of orders lasts over 10 months, with a more pronounced impact on expenditure (shown in Figure C1 in Online Appendix C).

To better demonstrate how users’ community participation varies in the short term and the long term and how it affects their purchases, we further follow the prior literature (e.g., Bronnenberg et al. 2020) and classify the posttreatment relative time dummies into three treatment phases $( \mathrm { i . e . , } S h o r t T e r m _ { \{ k \in ( 0 , 1 ) \} }$ $M e d i u m T e r m _ { \{ k \in ( 2 , 6 ) \} } .$ and $L o n g T e r m _ { \{ k > 6 \} } ) .$ . Specifically, we use the lincom Stata command to estimate a short-term treatment effect of the Like feature within the first two months since the adop tion, a medium-term treatment effect of the Like feature between two and six months after the adoption, and a long-term treatment effect of the Like feature more than six months after the adoption. Furthermore, taking users community participation levels (log NumComments) as the mediator and their purchases as the dependent vari ables, we investigate how the indirect effect of the Like feature on users’ purchases through community participation and the remaining effect change over the three treatment phases.

Detailed results are summarized in Table C3 in Online Appendix C. We further visualize the mediation results in Figure C2 in the online appendix. We find that, in the short and medium term, the Like feature adoption leads to an increase in users’ community participation, which indirectly increases their (online) purchases. In other words, community participation partially mediates the relationship between the Like feature adoption and (online) purchases in the short and medium term. However, in the long term, the treatment effect on users community participation becomes insignificant. After accounting for the effect through community participation, the Like feature adoption has a remaining significant negative effect on users’ online and offline purchases in the medium and long term. As discussed earlier, this remaining effect likely reflects the effect of the Like feature adoption on purchases through brand attachment, as depicted in Figure 2, which will be directly investigated in Section 7.

This analysis provides suggestive evidence for the role of social comparison as part of the mechanism behind the unintended negative effect of the Like feature adoption on purchases. We will examine the roles of social comparison and brand attachment more directly in the experiment in Section 7.

## 6.4. Robustness Checks

Besides the analyses to explore the mechanism behind our main results, we also perform a series of robustness checks to validate the main findings from the observational study. First, to alleviate the concern of potentially violating SUTVA, we conduct a robustness analysis with the noncommunity users as the control group that does not have access to the interface of the OBC and thus, is unlikely affected by the Like feature adoption in the OBC. Second, we test three alternative definitions of treatment by considering whether a user has sent a Like and whether the user has received a Like separately. Third, we employ the LA-CEM approach (Bapna et al. 2018, Jung et al. 2019) to match existing adopters to users who are currently nonadopters but will adopt it in the future to improve their comparability. Fourth, we conduct a placebo test (Abadie et al. 2015, Ozer et al. 2023) by randomly reassigning the treatment time to the users in our sample to alleviate the potential concern of spurious causality.<sup>11</sup> Fifth, we perform the sensitivity analysis (Altonji et al. 2005, Oster 2019) to assess to what extent our results are susceptible to selection on unobservables. Finally, we conduct additional robustness checks to (1) account for the count nature of the dependent variables, (2) control for additional lagged terms of user activities, and (3) control for possible unobserved time-varying confounders by adding province-specific time fixed effects or cohort-specific time fixed effects. All these robustness checks corroborate our main findings. The detailed analyses are reported in the online appendices, as summarized in Table 6.

## 7. Mechanism Exploration with a Scenario-Based Experiment

To investigate further into the underlying mechanism in the conceptual framework in Figure 2 that we cannot directly observe in the observational data, we conduct an online experiment to focus on the roles of users’ brand attachment and social comparison orientation by holding users’ participation level constant (i.e., one post). We first introduce our experiment design and data collection in Section 7.1. In Section 7.2, we analyze the data from the experiment and validate the following main findings of the observational study. (i) The presence of the Like feature in the OBC on average negatively affects users purchases, and (ii) the treatment effect of the Like feature varies by the number of Likes a user receives. We will then focus on the mediation effect of brand attachment and the moderating effect of social comparison in Sections 7.3 and 7.4.

## 7.1. Experiment Design and Data Collection

We recruit participants from Wen Juan Xing (also known as Sojump) (e.g., Huang et al. 2020), a leading online crowdsourcing platform in China that provides functions similar to Amazon Mechanical Turk. Participants are not restricted to users of a specific OBC but include those who have participated in various OBCs. ## 7.2. Average Treatment Effect and Heterogeneous Treatment Effect by Number of Likes Received

We conduct a series of statistical tests to determine whether and how the presence of the Like feature and the Like count condition in the OBC affect participants purchase intentions. Figure 4(a) presents the overall effect of the Like feature on online and offline purchases, respectively. The t-test result reveals that the presence of the Like feature has a significant negative effect on online purchase intention (p < 0.001). Specifically, participants in the NoLikeFeature (control) group (mean � 5.38, standard deviation � 1.28) have higher online purchase intention than those exposed to the Like feature (i.e., the other three groups pooled together; mean � 4.17, standard deviation � 1.95). The result is highly consistent when comparing the two groups in offline purchase intention (p < 0.001). The average offline purchase intention is 5.39 (standard deviation � 1.31) in the NoLikeFeature group compared with the mean of 4.25 (standard deviation � 1.88) when there is a Like feature in the OBC.

We further examine how the treatment effect of the Like feature varies by the number of Likes shown in the experiment scenarios. Figure 4(b) presents the heterogeneous treatment effect. Our t-test results indicate that compared with the NoLikeFeature group, receiving zero Likes or a low number of Likes has a significant negative impact on both online and offline purchase intention $( p < 0 . 0 0 1$ for both NoLikeFeature versus ZeroLike and NoLikeFeature versus LowLikes comparisons). In contrast, participants in the HighLikes group have significantly higher online $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and offline $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ purchase intention than those in the control group.<sup>13</sup>

## 7.4. Moderating Effect of Social Comparison

We also examine the moderating effect of social comparison in the relationships between the two dimensions of Like treatment and purchase intention through a moderated mediation analysis. In contrast to our observationa study, where we use social comparison salience as a proxy for social comparison intensity, in the experiment, we use an individual trait measure (i.e., social comparison orientation (SocialComparison)), which directly captures how frequently individuals compare themselves with others and how much they are concerned about social comparison (Gibbons and Buunk 1999). Because users with a higher social comparison orientation are more susceptible to social comparison and the subsequent emotional response (Lee 2014, Vogel et al. 2015), we expect that the two dimensions of Like treatment will have a stronger effect on them through brand attachment.

As shown in Table 9, the moderator (i.e., users’ social comparison orientation (SocialComparison)) significantly moderates the relationship from the Like feature to users’ (online and offline) purchase intention through brand attachment. Consistent with the results in Section 7.3, we first confirm that the Like feature has a significant negative effect on users’ purchase intention, which is mediated by brand attachment. Furthermore, when users have a stronger social comparison orientation, the negative effect of the Like feature on users’ brand attachment intensifies, as evidenced by the coefficient of the interaction term LikeFeature × SocialComparison $( \beta = - 1 . 1 2 4 ,$ $p { < } 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , resulting in a more substantial decrease in their purchase intention. We also calculate the index of moderated mediation (Hayes 2017, Grosser et al. 2018), which is the product of two regression coefficients, capturing the effect of LikeFeature × SocialComparison on the mediator (brand attachment) and the effect of the mediator on purchase intention $( \beta = 0 . 9 4 6 , p < 0 . 0 0 1$ for online and $\beta = 0 . 8 6 1 , p < 0 . 0 0 1$ for offline). This analysis confirms that the conditional indirect effect of the Like feature on purchase intention significantly intensifies when users social comparison orientation is high in both the online and offline channels.<sup>14</sup>

Similarly, we show in Table 10 that social comparison orientation also moderates the relationship between low Like count and its impact on users’ (online and offline) purchase intention. As shown in Section 7.2, compared with users in the HighLikes group, users in the ZeroLike and Low-Likes groups report a significantly lower purchase intention, and this negative effect of low Like count is mediated by brand attachment. Notably, when users have a stronger social comparison orientation, the negative effect of a low Like count on purchase intention, mediated by brand attachment, becomes more pronounced.

First, in the first few months since the adoption, users increase their community participation, translating to a transitory positive effect on online purchases. However, the positive effect of the feature adoption on community participation only lasts in the first few months and fades away thereafter.

Second, the feature adoption has a remaining negative effect on online and offline purchases, primarily when users receive few Likes. In fact, whether the treatment effect of the Like feature adoption is positive or negative largely depends on the number of Likes users receive. For users receiving more than two Likes, the treatment effect is significantly positive on online purchases but insignificant on offline purchases. For users receiving zero Likes or one Like, the treatment effect is significantly negative on both online and offline purchases. Overall, our analyses provide supporting evidence for an unintended negative effect of Like feature adoption. It decreases users’ purchases online and offline if they fail to receive Likes in the OBC, and the effect magnifies as social comparison becomes more salient. We conduct a series of robustness checks to validate our identification strategy.

In the scenario-based experiment, we replicate the main results of our observational data analysis and further directly examine the underlying mechanism related to brand attachment and social comparison. We consistently find that the treatment effect of the Like feature on users’ (online and offline) purchase intention depends on the number of Likes they receive. When users receive zero Likes or a low number of Likes, the Like feature leads to a negative effect mediated by brand attachment. Conversely, when users receive a high number of Likes, the Like feature can increase their purchase intention. The treatment effect is more pronounced among users with higher social comparison orientation, lending support to the social comparison mechanism.

Although our observational data are from a specific OBC in China, the Like feature and OBC setting are similar to many other OBCs worldwide. Therefore, we believe that our findings can be generalized to other OBC contexts, which is empirically supported by our scenario-based experiment. Although the magnitude of the effect may vary, our findings call for attention to the possible unintended negative effect when introducing social features into OBC settings. The underlying mechanisms through brand attachment and social comparison we identify can also transcend the specific context and country we study.

Our findings shed light on several insightful managerial implications. First, firms should carefully design the interaction technologies in OBCs to be effective in encouraging users’ participation and yet, avoid triggering negative emotions on the users’ end. For instance, nowadays, some social platforms (e.g., Facebook and Instagram) are testing whether allowing users to hide Like counts or making the number of Likes only visible to posters can protect those users who receive few Likes from the painful social comparison.<sup>15</sup> Firms may also consider other creative ways to encourage users’ community participation while alleviating the potential negative effect of social comparison on the communities.

Second, firms should comprehensively evaluate the impact of community design on users’ purchases from a multichannel retailing perspective. Despite the slight positive treatment effect of the Like feature adoption on users online community participation, the treatment effect on their online and offline purchases is negative when they receive few Likes, which unfortunately, describes a prevalent occurrence in many OBCs. Therefore, when firms introduce new designs to facilitate social interactions among consumers online, they should carefully collect data from multiple sources to check if the change can influence users’ purchase behavior favorably in different channels. We find that when the new designs in one channel lead to users’ disappointment and detachment from the brand, this will also have a negative spillover effect on other channels. However, when the new designs in one channel lead to more fondness and attachment to the brand, this need not have a positive spillover effect on other channels. Therefore, firms are suggested to optimize their community designs and interactions to maximize the benefits of their OBCs in the multichannel retailing context.

Third, firms can use group rules and reward design to help create a welcoming and supportive environment. For example, firms can promote friendly group rules (e.g., being kind and courteous to others) and nudge their members to show kindness and appreciation to others in OBCs. They can also provide incentives, such as rewards or points, to users who send Likes to encourage users to support others’ posts. As shown in our study, when users actively show appreciation to others by sending Likes, it can lead to stronger brand attachment among users, ultimately benefiting the brand in the long term.

Fourth, firms may consider providing more feedback or confirmation to users to make them feel valued and provide social support to the unattended majority. We find that those users who do not receive Likes tend to decrease their purchases the most. In this case, firms may consider providing customized responses in different means to make those consumers feel socially supported and have a sense of belonging in the OBC, which can encourage them to keep posting and purchasing. For instance, with the development of AI, firms may consider using tools such as chatbots to provide automatic but relevant responses to more users’ posts to enhance social support and strengthen their brand attachment. Moreover, those responses or Likes from the firm may help alleviate the cold-start problem and encourage more users to send Likes to others.

Our study has several limitations that future research may address. First, community users’ login and browsing data are unavailable to us. Our research only examines the treatment effect of the Like feature adoption on users’ purchases and comment posting behavior. It would be interesting for future research to examine whether the Like feature also leads to other behavioral changes. Second, we cannot observe and estimate the impact of the Like feature on user acquisition in this study. As with many firms’ Facebook groups,<sup>16</sup> the content posted in this OBC is only accessible to community users. As a result, the effect of the Like feature on attracting new users is likely to be minimal. Future research with user acquisition data could investigate how the community feature design affects user acquisition in OBCs where the interface is also accessible to noncommunity users. Third, our results may not be generalized to platforms wherein the visibility of the Like count is low. In our research context, users are aware of the number of Likes they and other users receive, making social comparison easily accessible. Recently, Facebook and Instagram tested the option to allow people to hide their public Like counts.<sup>17</sup> The option to hide public Like counts may alleviate the negative effect of unflattering social comparison for users who receive a low number of Likes, but it could also lead to an upshift in the Like count distribution that users choose to reveal, which further intensifies social comparison among active users, ultimately reducing their brand attachment and purchases. We encourage future research to investigate the shortand long-term effects of the Like feature on purchases under varying levels of Like count visibility. Finally, we acknowledge that the constantly evolving social media landscape, such as the emergence of chatbot-only communities (e.g., Chirper<sup>18</sup>) and potential mixed communities with both human and chatbot users, may impact people’s social comparison perception within these platforms. Whether the observed impact of the Like feature in our study generalizes to these emerging contexts needs to be investigated by future research.

We view our study as a first step in the literature toward a better understanding of the impact of the Like feature for C2C interactions in OBCs on users’ community and purchase behavior. Our study can alert and warn firms about the potential negative effect of this Like feature. We hope our study can inspire future research in understanding and improving the design of OBCs in the omnichannel context.
