# Time to Stop? An Empirical Investigation on the Consequences of Canceling Monetary Incentives on a Digital Platform

Abstract. Digital platforms commonly use monetary incentives to motivate users to perform specific tasks. Extant studies have shown the effects of introducing such monetary rewards on the outcomes of interest (e.g., participation and performance) on public platforms. However, little is known about the impact of canceling rewards (i.e., whether it simply reverses the effect of their introduction), and particularly less attention is paid to corporate platforms. Our study examines the impact of canceling monetary incentives using quasi-natural experiments on a corporate platform. Similar to prior studies focusing on public platforms, we find that introducing quantity-based monetary incentive increases participation (contribution quantity), but has no significant effect on performance (contribution quality). Yet, in contrast, our main empirical analysis reveals that canceling monetary incentives is not simply the reverse process of their introduction. In particular, compared with the increase in participation when monetary rewards were initially introduced, the cancellation of these rewards leads to a sharper decrease in participation. Thi suggests that canceling rewards has a net negative impact on participation. In addition, canceling monetary rewards also causes a significant decline in performance, which indicates that the effects of canceling and introducing rewards on performance are not simply the opposite of each other. Furthermore, we examine the heterogeneous responses of indi viduals with different self-motivation types and working competency levels to monetary incentives, highlighting the “asymmetry” between canceling and introducing incentives. We also discuss the similarities and differences between corporate and public platforms regarding the impact of monetary incentives. Our results provide important practical implications for enterprise information systems and general information systems regarding their design of platform strategies.

## 1. Introduction

Many digital platforms, including corporate platforms (e.g., corporate knowledge-sharing communities) and public platforms (e.g., e-commerce sites), often incentivize users to perform tasks ranging from sharing knowledge and writing product reviews to developing open-source software (OSS). Digital platforms of all kinds have been gaining increasing research attention, especially with the rapid expansion of information technology infrastructure (Carter et al. 2020, Hukal et al. 2020, Wang et al. 2022b, Petryk et al. 2023). However, one common problem, widely recognized in practice, is low engagement (Maruping and Magni 2015, Huang et al. 2019b, Cao et al. 2023). Prior studies have explored various ways to incentivize users, commonly involving monetary incentives (Liu and Feng 2021, Wang et al. 2022a). Platforms often introduce monetary rewards in an initial period; however, some may choose to cancel the rewards or reduce the reward amount at a later stage, once the user behavior pattern possibly becomes well-established (Helft 2006, Khansa et al. 2015). Fo example, Tencent canceled monetary incentives for contributing exclusive content on its platform in 2019,<sup>1</sup> and ByteDance gradually reduced monetary rewards for the users of its question-and-answer (Q&A) platform (namely, Wukong Q&A) from 2017 to 2019.<sup>2</sup> Although extensive studies have found significant effects of monetary rewards on user participation and performance (Burtch et al. 2018, Khern-am-nuai et al. 2018), little is known about whether these effects can be sustained or changed after the rewards are canceled, which is both theoretically and practically important. Furthermore, previous studies have primarily focused on investigating the impact of monetary incentives on public platforms. However, there has been a notable lack of attention toward corporate platforms, where the users are also employees.

In this study, we examine the impact of canceling monetary incentives on individuals’ participation (contribution quantity) and performance (contribution quality) pertaining to specific tasks, using quasi-natural experimental data obtained from a corporate platform of an international conglomerate subsidiary in China. We further dig deeper into the heterogeneous treatment effects with regard to users’ motivation types and levels of working competency, and we also compare our empirical results on a corporate platform with prior studies on public platforms. Our comprehensive investigation reveals that canceling monetary incentives on digital platforms may not be the reverse process of their introduction. In other words, the effects of canceling and introducing monetary incentives on user contribution are not simply the opposite of each other, and, thus, the impact of canceling incentives cannot be directly inferred from existing studies about their introduction. We also discuss the similarities and differences between corporate and public platforms, and we provide insights into how user contributions on digital platforms can be incentivized.

## 1.1. Motivation

Most prior studies on public platforms find that quantity-based monetary incentives are effective in boosting overall participation (contribution quantity), but have no significant impact on performance (contribution quality) (e.g., Wang et al. 2016, Burtch et al. 2018, and Chen et al. 2019a), and a few studies show that quantity-based incentives can sometimes be detrimental to performance (e.g., Khern-am-nuai et al. 2018).

To date, most extant studies have focused on the introduction of new monetary incentives, and the impact of removing existing incentives is less understood. On the one hand, one may argue that the effect of canceling existing monetary incentives would simply reverse the effect of their introduction. In other words, the positive effect of introducing monetary rewards would correspond to a negative effect of a similar magnitude when canceling the rewards. This seems reasonable because the effect of financial incentives can easily fade out or vanish after the incentives are removed (Camerer 2010). On the other hand, there is also evidence showing that monetary incentives can substantially change individuals’ behavior patterns (Kremer et al. 2009, Hussam et al. 2022). In this sense, canceling existing monetary incentives may not have a significant impact because users’ established habits (or even addiction) might dominate their behavior in information systems (IS) contexts (Khansa et al. 2015, Vaghefi et al. 2023). Thus, both cases are theoretically plausible, presenting a viable opportunity for empirical testing. Moreover, different types of users may respond to canceling monetary incentives in various ways. Therefore, it is difficult to predict how users will change their participation (contribution quantity) and performance (contribution quality) without empirical justification. To the best of our knowledge, limited empirical research in the IS literature has investigated the impact of canceling existing monetary incentives.

Furthermore, prior studies on monetary incentives predominantly focus on public platforms, whereas there is a notable lack of investigation into the effects of monetary incentives within corporate platform settings. Corporate platforms are gaining increasing attention in the IS field due to their growing use and importance in industrial practices (Greenwood et al. 2019, Chen et al. 2022). Compared with public platforms (e.g., e-commerce platforms and social media), which can provide both hedonic and utilitarian benefits to users, corporate platforms (i.e., enterprise information systems) mainly provide utilitarian benefits (Moravec et al. 2022, Adam et al. 2023, Vaghefi et al. 2023). In other words, public platforms are more like online places where users can have fun, and corporate platforms are more like tools that mainly offer instrumental values. Therefore, public platform users are more likely to develop “habits” (or “addiction”) to the IS use, whereas addiction to corporate platforms is not very probable (Vaghefi et al. 2023). In addition, social relations (i.e., user-platform and user-user relations) on corporate platforms can differ from those on public platforms (Pu et al. 2022, Zhou et al. 2022). Thus, although the basic premise of economics suggests a universal effect of incentives, the differences in platform nature and social relations can cause corporate platform users to respond differently to monetary incentives compared with public platform users.

Therefore, it is necessary to empirically examine the impact of monetary incentives on corporate platforms and compare it with that on public platforms. This investigation allows us to provide both generic implications for general information systems and novel insights for enterprise information systems.

## 1.2. Research Questions and Contributions

To fill the research gap comprehensively, we begin by asking the first research question: (1) What is the impact of canceling monetary incentives on participation (contribution quantity)? We leverage the quasi-natural experiments conducted on a corporate platform to empirically identify the causal effect. Specifically, a large international conglomerate subsidiary in China developed a digital platform and encouraged all of its employees to perform two types of tasks. To motivate employees to use the system, the company initially provided monetary rewards when employees completed a task. As employees became more familiar with the system, the company decided to cancel monetary incentives. However, given the potentially uncertain consequences, the company decided to first gauge employees’ reactions by canceling the rewards for only one type of tasks. Because the policy change was unknown to any individual before its implementation, this can be seen as an exogenous shock; thus, the difference-in-differences (DID) strategy can be employed to estimate the effect of this policy change (i.e., canceling monetary rewards). To establish a solid benchmark for evaluating the impact of canceling monetary rewards, we also conduct an exploratory analysis of the initial effect of introducing those rewards. We find that after the incentives are removed, employees decrease their participation to a greater extent than they increased their participation when the incentives were initially introduced, suggesting a net negative impact of canceling incentives on user participation. This can be explained by the well-known “reference point” effect in prospect theory, which states that the disutility of monetary losses is greater than the utility of monetary gains (Kahneman and Tversky 1979).

Canceling monetary incentives affects not only users participation (contribution quantity), but also their performance (contribution quality). More importantly, the impact of monetary incentives on participation might differ from that on performance, according to prior literature (Burtch et al. 2018). Thus, we ask our second research question: (2) What is the impact of canceling monetary incentives on performance (contribution quality)?

As mentioned earlier, most prior studies have shown that monetary incentives have little impact on performance on public platforms (e.g., Wang et al. 2016, Burtch et al. 2018, and Chen et al. 2019a), suggesting that performance may not change significantly after incentives are terminated. However, a few studies have also found that introducing monetary incentives can negatively affect performance (e.g., Khern-am-nuai et al. 2018). The intuition is that because the incentives are linked to the number of contributions instead of performance, people may be incentivized to perform more tasks and put less effort into each task. Similar to our approach to the first research question, we use the DID strategy to estimate the main causal effect of canceling rewards, and we also conduct an exploratory analysis of the initial effect of introducing those rewards. Surprisingly, the empirical results show that there is no significant change in performance after the initial reward introduction, whereas the performance declines significantly after reward cancellation. This is because although users may tend to decrease their efforts for each task when rewards are introduced, this purely money-driven tactic may not be significant because the users can develop “additional reciprocity” toward the platform owner due to the provision of mon etary rewards, especially in corporate settings (Arazy and Gellatly 2012, Zhou et al. 2022). However, this additional reciprocity established by monetary incentives would disappear after the rewards are canceled, leading to a significant decline in contribution quality. There fore, canceling monetary incentives causes a net detrimental effect on performance.

Summarizing our answers to the first two research questions, we find that canceling monetary incentives does not simply reverse the effects of their introduction. In other words, the effects of canceling and introducing rewards are not the exact opposite of each other. Furthermore, users are more sensitive to the cancellation of incentives, which means that the adverse effects of canceling incentives outweigh the beneficial effects of introducing them. In addition, the results of our exploratory analysis are consistent with most existing studies on introducing monetary rewards on public platforms, indicating similarities between corporate and public platforms. However, our investigation of the next research question highlights several unique aspects of corporate platforms.

To better understand the possible mechanisms driving the impact of monetary incentives on user contribution, we ask the third research question: (3) What are th heterogeneous responses of different types of individuals to the cancellation of monetary rewards? Earlier research has explored heterogeneity in the motivation types of indi viduals when introducing monetary incentives (Alexy and Leitner 2011, Khern-am-nuai et al. 2018, Wang et al. 2023), but has not examined the heterogeneity in their levels of working competency. Yet, working competency is a critical factor in determining how best to incentivize people (Reinholt et al. 2011). Thus, we investigate the heterogeneity in both motivation types and competency levels to further understand the mechanism. First, we find that monetary rewards can be asso ciated with a positive image (e.g., praise and recognition) and boost the participation of highly internally motivated users. This is different from public plat forms, where prior studies have shown that monetary rewards make users want to avoid being perceived as greedy, undermining their internal motivation (Ariely et al. 2009, Alexy and Leitner 2011). Our results further reveal that this positive effect of image gains on highly internally motivated users is simply reversed after the rewards are canceled. In contrast, for less internally motivated users, canceling monetary rewards causes a decline in participation that is much sharper than the initial increase when the rewards were introduced.

Second, prior studies on public platforms have shown that the performance of less internally motivated users may decrease significantly after monetary rewards are introduced, but the performance of highly internally motivated users remains unchanged (Sun et al. 2017, Khern-am-nuai et al. 2018). In our context of a corporate platform, we find that, similar to public platforms, monetary rewards also have no significant impact on the performance of highly internally motivated users. But, unlike public platforms, less internally motivated users in corporate settings can develop stronger additional reciprocity toward the company due to the provision of monetary rewards, as the users are also corporate employees (Arazy and Gellatly 2012, Zhou et al. 2022). Therefore, their performance would not significantly change when the monetary rewards are introduced. However, their performance would significantly decline after the additional reciprocity vanishes when the rewards are canceled. Third, we do not observe significant heterogeneity between high-competency and lowcompetency users regarding the impact of monetary incentives on participation, but we do find that the negative performance impact of canceling incentives is less significant for high-competency users than it is for lowcompetency users, probably because high-competency users can still achieve relatively good performance, even with a lower effort.

It is a common practice for platforms to strategically introduce monetary incentives and then cancel them later on. Apart from theoretical significance, understanding the causal impact of canceling monetary incentives also has critical practical implications. Specifically, a platform must carefully evaluate the trade-offs between costs and benefits when considering the implementation of monetary incentives throughout the platform’s lifespan. Without quantifying the effects of canceling rewards, it is unclear whether existing rewards should be canceled at a specific time point. More importantly, our empirical results reveal that the negative effects of canceling rewards are more pronounced than the initial positive effects of introducing the rewards. Thus, digital platforms should be aware of this “asymmetry,” which can help them make longterm decisions, such as whether to adopt monetary incentives in the first place. In addition, our heterogeneity analysis provides deeper implications for how platforms can better incentivize different types of users. With the identified heterogeneous effects and underlying motivation mechanisms, platforms can more accurately adjust their monetary incentive strategies and design more customized motivation approaches.

## 2. Literature Review

Our study is related to the IS literature on the motivation of contribution behavior on digital platforms, with a particular focus on monetary incentives. As motivations can be divided into two broad categories: internal (or non-money-driven) and extrinsic (or money-driven) (Ariely et al. 2009, Gubler et al. 2016, Liu and Feng 2021), we review three streams of relevant literature: (i) internal motivations without monetary incentives, (ii) the impact of monetary incentives, and (iii) heterogeneous responses to monetary incentives. Here, we also highlight our contributions by comparing and contrasting our work with existing studies. Then, based on prior literature, we build a theoretical framework and develop key hypotheses.

## 2.1. Internal Motivations Without Monetary Incentives

Previous literature suggests that in the absence of monetary incentives, there are two main types of internal motivations that prompt users to contribute benevolently on digital platforms: pure intrinsic motivation and image-related motivation (Toubia and Stephen 2013). Pure intrinsic motivation is based on the inherent satisfaction or enjoyment of a task (Cerasoli et al. 2014). Prior research has examined the critical role of pure intrinsic motivation in various IS contexts, such as the knowledge contribution of employees in corporate communities (Kankanhalli et al. 2005), the participation of volunteers in online crowdsourcing (Alam and Camp bell 2017), and the discussion of members in public forums (Bateman et al. 2011).

Image-related motivation is rooted in the perception or recognition of others (Ariely et al. 2009), and it is especially important on digital platforms, as online user behavior is much more visible than offline actions. Many IS studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of image-related motivation from different perspectives, including peer recognition or reputation (Jabr et al. 2014, Goes et al. 2016, Chen et al. 2018), social exposure (Zhang and Zhu 2011, Qiu and Kumar 2017, Baek and Shore 2020), social capital (Johnson et al. 2015, Chen et al. 2019b, Huang et al. 2019a), and network connectedness (Zhang and Wang 2012, Shriver et al. 2013, Zeng and Wei 2013). There are two types of image motivation on digital platforms: volume-based image gained by doing more tasks and effort-based image gained by exerting greater effort on each task. For example, on usergenerated content platforms, on the one hand, users can obtain a volume-based image by increasing their contribution quantity (e.g., answering more questions) (Goes et al. 2016); on the other hand, driven by an effort-based image, users can spend more effort on each contribution and achieve higher quality (Qiu and Kumar 2017, Jin et al. 2022). By studying a policy shock (i.e., identity disclosure) on a corporate platform, Pu et al. (2020) demonstrated the significant impact of both volume-based images and effort-based images on the quantity and quality of employees’ knowledge-sharing behavior.

Compared with public platforms, image-related motivation may be more crucial for corporate platforms. This is because users’ contribution behavior may have an impact on their careers (Pu et al. 2022), and recognition from their employer and colleagues probably matters more to them than recognition from anonymous users would matter on a public platform (Bradler et al. 2016). We note that whereas this stream of literature studies users’ internal motivation on both public and corporate platforms, our study focuses on the impact of monetary incentives and their interaction with internal motivation.

## 2.2. The Impact of Monetary Incentives

The impact of monetary incentives on user contribution to public platforms has been extensively studied. Prior studies have found that introducing monetary rewards can boost contribution quantity. For example, Roberts et al. (2006) demonstrated that paying developers to contribute to OSS projects leads to above-average participation levels. However, most studies have shown that these rewards have little impact on contribution quality. Wang et al. (2012) investigated the effect of introducing monetary payments on online product reviews through a laboratory experiment. They found that monetary incentives have no significant impact on review quality in terms of both review helpfulness and length. Wang et al. (2016) also found that monetary rewards can increase the quantity of online reviews, but not their helpfulness. Burtch et al. (2018) conducted both field and laboratory experiments to explore the effects of monetary incentives and social norms on online reviews. They showed that monetary rewards can induce a larger volume of reviews, but they are not particularly lengthy, whereas social norms have a significant positive effect on review length.

Similar results regarding the effect of monetary incentives can be found in other contexts beyond online reviews. For example, Chen et al. (2019a) studied the impact of introducing monetary rewards on stock opinions on social media and concluded that those rewards are effective in improving the participation, but not the performance, of stock recommendations. Furthermore, some research suggests that monetary rewards may be detrimental to contribution quality (Sun et al. 2017, Khern-am-nuai et al. 2018). Apart from these studies on public platforms, there is also interview-based or survey-based evidence within corporate platforms showing that monetary rewards can increase the quantity of contributions, but the quality is still in question (Garud and Kumaraswamy 2005, Kankanhalli et al. 2005). However, despite the increasing popularity of corporate platforms and the widespread usage of extrinsic rewards in firms (Gubler et al. 2016), there exists a limited amount of empirical research, based on observational data, regarding the impact of monetary incentives on corporate platforms. In addition, after introducing monetary rewards, there seems to be no apparent time trend in how these rewards affect the incentivized con tributions (He et al. 2023).

As mentioned earlier, platforms commonly deploy short-term monetary incentives in practice, but most of the research reviewed above focuses on reward introduction, rather than their removal. One related IS study by Qiao et al. (2020) explored the impact of receiving incentives on subsequent unincentivized contributions. Specifically, they found that the receipt of financial incentives for writing product reviews has a negative spillover effect on the quality of users’ unincentivized reviews. Although it provides insights into the effect of disincentivizing users, this spillover effect cannot be simply considered as the effect of canceling monetary incentives, given that the rewards for incentivized reviews remain in place, and writing subsequent unincentivized reviews is an inherently different task from writing incentivized reviews.

To summarize, this body of literature mainly studies the effect of introducing monetary incentives or the spillover effect of receiving such incentives on unincentivized tasks on public platforms, whereas our research systematically investigates the impact of monetary incentives on contribution quantity and quality on a corporate platform, with a particular focus on canceling incentives. Our results reveal both universal and unique characteristics of corporate platforms and suggest that canceling monetary incentives is not the reverse process of introducing them.

## 2.3. Heterogeneous Responses to Monetary Incentives

To understand the underlying mechanism behind the impact of introducing monetary incentives, researchers have examined users’ heterogeneous responses. First, we note that the effects of monetary rewards on contribution quantity are heterogeneous. Alexy and Leitner (2011) investigated the effect of payment on the motivation of OSS developers using a scenario experiment. They found that when offered financial rewards, inter nal motivation decreases for individuals with norms against payment, although payment has a positive effect on overall motivation. Second, studies have found that users also respond differently to monetary rewards in terms of contribution quality. Using field data from an online review community, Sun et al. (2017) demon strated that monetary rewards induce the members with more social connections to contribute less content with unchanged quality, but the rewards can cause those with fewer connections to contribute more content with significantly lower quality. Similarly, Khern-amnuai et al. (2018) studied the impact of monetary incentives on the participation and performance of users writing product reviews on an e-commerce platform. They found that after monetary rewards are introduced, existing reviewers who are highly internally motivated reduce the quantity, but not the quality, of reviews. In contrast, new reviewers generate a large number of lowquality reviews. Overall, prior studies consistently suggest that users with high internal motivation may become demotivated, reducing contribution quantity, but their contribution quality remains unchanged. However, extrinsically motivated users increase their number of contributions and deliver lower quality.

These empirical findings about the impact of monetary incentives on public platforms can be well explained by the theoretical framework of Be´nabou and Tirole (2006), which is also supported by more recent literature (Gneezy et al. 2011, He et al. 2023). Specifically, without monetary incentives, a focal user’s contribution is perceived by others as completely disinterested (or “altruistic” or “prosocial”), which creates a positive image and enhances internal motivation. However, when the platform features monetary incentives, others might think that the focal user’s true motive for contributing is to earn more money. This leads to a negative image of the focal user as greedy, which undermines their internal motivation (Ariely et al. 2009, Khern-amnuai et al. 2018). Therefore, for those users who care about image (i.e., highly internally motivated users), the detrimental effect on their internal motivation can outweigh the potential monetary gains, leading to a decline in contribution quantity. Their contribution quality remains unchanged because the incentives are awarded based on the number of contributions and only undermine the user’s volume-based image, but not their effort-based image. In contrast, monetary incentives dominate the motives of extrinsically motivated users. Thus, to maximize their monetary benefits, they make more contributions, while exerting less effort in each one.

Compared with previous research that investigates the heterogeneous responses of users with different motives to the introduction of monetary incentives, our study mainly focuses on the heterogeneous impact of canceling monetary incentives. Furthermore, we consider heterogeneity in both motivation types and working competency levels to uncover the underlying mechanism.

It is worth noting that the monetary incentives in the abovementioned studies are all completion-contingent incentives, meaning that individuals uniformly receive rewards as long as they complete the tasks. There are also some studies focusing on performance-contingent incentives, meaning that users are rewarded based on performance. For example, research has shown that performance-contingent monetary incentives can have a significant positive effect on the quality of online product reviews (Yu et al. 2022, Qiao and Rui 2023). In our context, the monetary rewards are completioncontingent. Thus, we review and compare our results with those of prior research on completion-contingent monetary incentives. ## 2.4. Theoretical Framework and Hypothesis Development

Our study aims to understand the net impact of canceling monetary incentives on user contribution behavior on a corporate platform. Specifically, if the platform introduces rewards and cancels them later, what is the impact of this whole “introduce + cancel” process? In other words, what is the difference between users behavior after the rewards are canceled and their behavior before the rewards are introduced? Theoretically, it depends on how monetary incentives change user motivation. Based on prior literature, we build a coherent framework to demonstrate the theoretical foundation, as illustrated in Figure 1.

Specifically, we borrow and extend the theory in behavioral economics (Be´nabou and Tirole 2006, Ariely et al. 2009), in which user motivation is mainly divided into three parts, including pure intrinsic motivation, image motivation, and extrinsic motivation. As elaborated in Section 2.1, pure intrinsic motivation is based on the inherent satisfaction or enjoyment of a task (Cerasoli et al. 2014), whereas image motivation is rooted in the perception or recognition of others (e.g., votes or acceptance from other users and virtual ranks or badges granted by the platform) (Ariely et al. 2009, Goes et al. 2016, Chen et al. 2018, Kumar and Qiu 2022). Note that when applying this theoretical framework in empirical studies to investigate the effects of extrinsic rewards (i.e., monetary incentives), researchers often do not explicitly differentiate the first two (i.e., pure intrinsic motivation and image motivation) because both of them stem from nonreward factors and cannot be easily distinguished in most empirical settings (Gubler et al. 2016, Khern-am-nuai et al. 2018). More importantly, given the research focus on the impact of extrinsic rewards, whether the “non-reward-motivated” (i.e., internally motivated) users care more about pure intrinsic motiva tion or image motivation is not key to the argument or analysis (Gubler et al. 2016). Based on this theoretical framework, the impact of monetary incentives depends on two factors: (1) the direct effect of incentives through increasing extrinsic motivation; and (2) the interaction of incentives with internal motivation, primarily driven by image motivation. The first factor is intuitive, as indi viduals directly receive monetary rewards, whereas the second factor may be more complex. According to Be´nabou and Tirole (2006) and Ariely et al. (2009), the change in monetary rewards does not directly change users’ pure intrinsic motivation, but it can change image motivation through the “signaling effect,” meaning that the amount of monetary rewards can change whether others perceive the focal user as more greedy or altruistic. Simply put, when observing the prosocial behavior of a person in a context where monetary incentives are present, others will wonder: “Is the individual doing this out of intrinsic altruism or just for money?” Thus, obtaining monetary rewards creates a negative image of the focal individual as greedy and damages their image motivation. As discussed in Section 2.3, this theory can effectively explain both the incentivizing and crowd-out effects of monetary rewards on users’ extrinsic and internal motivation in the context of public platforms (Ariely et al. 2009, Khern-am-nuai et al. 2018). We then extend this theory to fit our research context in two ways: (1) adding the counterpart of canceling incentives and (2) categorizing image as volume-based or effortbased.

On corporate platforms, we can readily infer that introducing monetary rewards can also boost extrinsic motivation because the potential monetary gains would directly motivate a rational user to contribute more. After being incentivized for some time, users may think of the rewards as realized gains and establish a new reference point, which suggests that they would experience monetary losses when existing rewards are canceled. According to the prospect theory, the disutility of monetary losses is typically greater than the utility of monetary gains at the reference point (Kahneman and Tversky 1979). This indicates that the negative impact resulting from the cancellation of rewards (where users experience monetary losses) outweighs the positive impact generated by the introduction of rewards (where users benefit from monetary gains). As extrinsic motivation is directly influenced by users’ utility or disutility from monetary gains or losses, the withdrawal of incentives would result in a more pronounced decline in extrinsic motivation compared with its initial increase during the introduction of incentives.

Apart from directly changing extrinsic motivation, monetary incentives can also influence image motivation. Because image is a consequence of what others think (Qiu and Kumar 2017), its interaction with monetary incentives can vary, depending on what people think of those rewards. In public platforms or offline charitable donations, the offered incentives are seen as material ben efits and linked to a negative image of greediness. However, in corporate platforms, monetary incentives may instead induce a positive image and increase users’ inter nal motivation. This is because corporate platforms have several unique characteristics that public platforms often do not have. (1) In corporate contexts, monetary incen tives can be associated with praise and recognition from the employer (Grant 2012, Bradler et al. 2016). For exam ple, on the corporate platform in our study, the company publicly announces and ranks the quantity of monetar rewards obtained by each user every week and expresses appreciation for their hard work. This can generate a pos itive image for the users who earn more rewards by contributing more. (2) Users’ contribution behavior on corporate platforms is related to their careers (Hann et al. 2013, Pu et al. 2022). As the company has demonstrated its attitude toward the incentivized task and shown that it values the task, users may want to comply with the company policy in order to build a positive image that may benefit their careers. (3) Apart from the user-platform (or employee-employer) relationship, the user-user relation ship on corporate platforms is also more solid and transparent than that on public platforms because most users on public platforms are strangers to each other, whereas users on corporate platforms are colleagues (Hwang et al. 2015, Zhou et al. 2022). This indicates that, compared with public platform users, corporate platform users may care more about what others think of them, which furthe enhances the positive image induced by receiving more rewards.

In summary, when monetary incentives are introduced on a corporate platform, the direct effect of material rewards increases extrinsic motivation, and the recognition associated with the rewards can also boost internal motivation by creating a positive image. However, when the rewards are canceled later on, the positive image disappears, and, according to the reference point effect in the prospect theory, the monetary losses may demotivate the users more than the monetary gains motivate them. Thus, our first hypothesis emphasizes the asymmetric impact of canceling monetary incentives on contribution quantity.

Hypothesis 1. Compared with the increase in contribution quantity when the incentives were initially introduced, the cancellation of monetary incentives leads to a sharper decrease in quantity, suggesting a net negative impact of canceling incentives on participation.

As previously discussed, there are two subcategories of user image in IS contexts: volume-based image and effort-based image (Goes et al. 2016, Qiu and Kumar 2017, Pu et al. 2020). Because we focus on completioncontingent incentives (i.e., rewards provided based on the number of contributions), the praise and recognition associated with the rewards are also based on contribution quantity and can only strengthen the volume-based image. Thus, the introduction and cancellation of monetary incentives should not affect the effort-based image related to contribution quality.

As for extrinsic motivation, most studies on public platforms found that increasing quantity-based extrinsic benefits has no significant effect on contribution quality (e.g., Wang et al. 2016, Burtch et al. 2018, and Chen et al. 2019a). However, a few studies observed users’ strategic gaming behavior in light of the trade-off between quantity and quality. Specifically, after quantity-based monetary rewards are introduced, users may choose to complete more tasks, while expending less effort on each task to maximize monetary benefits (Sun et al. 2017, Khern-am-nuai et al. 2018). In fact, “contributing more” and “contributing with high quality” can be seen as different tasks. Under the theory of multitasking incentives, users tend to shift their focus toward the incentivized task (e.g., contributing more) and away from the other tasks (e.g., contributing with high quality) (Holmstrom and Milgrom 1991, Qiu et al. 2022). On corporate platforms that have introduced rewards, users may also tend to decrease their efforts expended on each task after rewards are introduced. Nevertheless, this purely money-driven gaming behavior may not be significant because, compared with public platform users, corporate platform users can develop stronger additional reciprocity toward the platform owner (i.e., the company) due to the provision of monetary rewards (Cabral and Li 2015, Khern-am-nuai et al. 2018), as the users are also corporate employees (Arazy and Gellatly

2012, Zhou et al. 2022). After the rewards are canceled, the users have no reason to increase their efforts. How ever, the users’ additional reciprocity toward the platform owner established by monetary incentives would disappear, leading to a decline in user effort. In short, the establishment of the additional reciprocity when introducing rewards can prevent user efforts from declining, whereas the disappearance of the additional reciprocity when canceling rewards may cause user efforts to decline. This indicates that there can be a reference point effect of reciprocity (i.e., the removal of reciprocity is asymmetric with its introduction), which is analogous to the literature suggesting that the reference point effect not only applies to monetary trade-offs, but also applies to other money-related factors, such as online sellers reputations (Cheng et al. 2020). Accordingly, we propose our second hypothesis about the impact of canceling monetary incentives on contribution quality.

Hypothesis 2. The cancellation of monetary incentives causes a significant decline in contribution quality, compared with the nonsignificant change when the incentives were initially introduced.

As depicted in Figure 1 and discussed above, monetary incentives can change both extrinsic motivations through material rewards and internal motivation through the interaction with image. Therefore, users with different motivation types might respond differently to monetary incentives. (1) Highly internally motivated users do no care much about material benefits, but certainly care about their image. Because the rewards are associated with a positive volume-based image, the users are motivated to contribute more when the rewards are introduced, and this effect can simply be reversed when the rewards are canceled later. However, their contribution quality may not be influenced because the effort-based image remains unchanged by quantity-based monetary incentives. (2) In contrast, less internally motivated users care about material rewards, so their number of contribu tions increases when rewards are introduced. However, according to the reference point effect in prospect theory (Kahneman and Tversky 1979), canceling the rewards would lead to a decrease in their contribution quantity greater than the initial increase. In terms of contribution quality, we hypothesize that when the rewards are introduced, extrinsically motivated (i.e., less internally motivated) users would not significantly decrease their efforts expended on each task. The reason is that even though they may tend to shift focus toward “contributing more” with less effort, unlike highly internally motivated users who do not care much about material benefits, these extrinsically motivated users indeed appreciate the mate rial benefits of monetary rewards and can develop additional reciprocity toward the platform owner (Khern-am nuai et al. 2018). More importantly, this reciprocity of corporate platform users can be stronger than that of public platform users. This is because the user-platform relationship on corporate platforms is stronger than that on public platforms, as corporate platform users are also employees officially affiliated with the company (Arazy and Gellatly 2012, Pu et al. 2020). However, after the rewards are canceled, the reciprocity driven by rewards vanishes, potentially leading less internally motivated users to reduce their efforts on each task. Our third hypothesis focuses on the heterogeneous impact of canceling monetary incentives with regard to user motivation types.

Hypothesis 3(a). For highly internally motivated users, canceling monetary incentives has a negative effect on contribution quantity, in which the initial positive effect of introducing the incentives is simply reversed; for less internally motivated users, canceling monetary incentives causes a decline in contribution quantity that is greater than the initial quantity increase when incentives are introduced.

Hypothesis 3(b). For highly internally motivated users, the impact of monetary incentives on contribution quality is not significant; for less internally motivated users, canceling monetary incentives has a significant negative impact on contribution quality.

Apart from motivation types, users with different working competency levels may also respond to monetary incentives differently. As discussed before, monetary rewards can be considered as recognition, and highly internally motivated users care about recognition more than less internally motivated users. By contrast, working competency refers to the ability to complete tasks with high quality, and highcompetency users do not care about recognition more than low-competency users. Therefore, we do not expect any heterogeneity over working competency levels in terms of the impact on contribution quantity. However, when canceling monetary incentives leads the overall contribution quality to decrease, we assume that the quality decline of high-competency users is not as significant as that of low-competency users. This is because high-competency users can achieve good performance, even if they spend less effort on each task. Accordingly, we propose our fourth hypothesis about the heterogeneity in working competency levels.

Hypothesis 4(a). There is no significant heterogeneity in the impact of monetary incentives on contribution quantity between high-competency and low-competency users.

Hypothesis 4(b). The negative impact of canceling monetary incentives on contribution quality is less significant for high-competency users than for low-competency users.

## 3. Research Context and Data

In this study, we collect data on the task-level behavior of employees in an international conglomerate subsidiary, one of the largest construction companies in China. In the construction industry, it is important to ensure the safety of the construction environment and processes. Thus, the company set up a specialized safety depart ment to conduct safety inspections on a daily basis, which is a common practice to prevent potential safety incidents. However, the safety department only had seven employees and might not be able to thoroughly identify all safety issues. To improve the efficiency of safety inspection, the company developed its own online platform for the employees outside of the safety department to voluntarily report and fix safety issues.<sup>3</sup> The safety department provided guidelines for this task procedure, supervised the platform’s daily operation, and could also perform these tasks by itself. In this way, the online system serves as a crowdsourcing platform through which all employees can participate in the safety inspection tasks. After the platform was launched, all the tasks were required to be reported through the online system to create a full record. Figure 2 depicts the task procedure in detail.

Specifically, when observing a safety issue at the construction site, each employee can choose to report it via the designed online platform. Accordingly, the reporter first needs to write a brief summary to describe the issue and indicate who might be responsible and who should be involved in fixing it. Further, the reporter specifies the number of days in which the issue should be fixed. This timeframe is determined based on the issue’s urgency and can only be chosen from integers between 1 and 7 (e.g., 1, 3, or 7 days). Once the issue is uploaded on the platform, the reporter can send messages through the system to those individuals who might be responsible for it and form a group to complete the task. The reporter is the team leader, and the involved employees are obligated to collaborate with the reporter to fix the issue. It is worth noting that the reporter may not accurately identify the employees responsible for the task. For example, in Figure 2, the reporter identifies E1, E2, E3, and E4 during reporting, yet only E2 and E3 are truly related to the task. It is also possible for the reporter to exclude an employee who is responsible for the task; for instance, the reporter excludes E5, who is later identified and joins the team during the task process. Thus, we can infer that a reporter with higher proficiency or capability is more likely to identify the responsible individuals initially and correctly. After reporting the issue, it is the reporter’s task to lead the team and fix the issue as soon as possible. After fixing the issue, the reporter confirms it online, indicating that the task is completed. An example of our data is presented in Table B1 of Online Appendix B.

To incentivize employees to participate in the safety inspection tasks through the platform, the company introduced a monetary reward mechanism on June 1, 2016. For each completed task, the reporter was awarded 2 CNY (the official currency of China) directly through the system. The total reward amount that an individual receives could be substantial. There are two types of safety issues: event-related and norm-related. The former is driven by specific events or procedures, such as broken fire hydrants caused by operating heavy machinery, whereas the latter is driven by construction norms, such as the inappropriate placement of materials according to safety rules. On April 1, 2019, the company terminated the monetary reward for norm-related issues, but retained it for event-related issues.<sup>5</sup> The company made this decision based on two considerations. First, the cost of rewarding employees is not negligible in the long run. Second, the company was interested in understanding whether the employees had become used to reporting and fixing safety issues and would continue to do so even without monetary incentives. After canceling the monetary incentives for one type of task, the company still encouraged employees to participate in both types of tasks through the system, and all the procedures for the two types of tasks remained the same and transparent on the platform. It is worth noting that, according to our interviews with the company’s managers, the cancellation of monetary rewards for norm-related tasks was the only policy change for both types of tasks. Therefore, we can treat this as an exogenous shock, which, in turn, provides us with a unique quasi-natural experiment to study the effects of canceling monetary incentives.

To support our empirical analysis, we collected two data sets of task records spanning different time periods. (1) The first (exploratory) data set spans from March 2016 to August 2016, and it is used to analyze the effect of introducing monetary incentives in our context. Because the incentives were introduced on June 1, 2016, the tasks were not incentivized in the first three months, and then both types of tasks were incentivized with monetary rewards in the last three months. (2) The second (main) data set spans from January 2018 to December 2019, and it is used to conduct our main analysis on the effect of canceling monetary incentives. During this two-year time span, both types of tasks were incentivized with monetary rewards in the first 15 months; the norm-related task rewards were removed on April 1, 2019, so there were no rewards for norm-related tasks in the last 9 months. Fo the main data set, we focus on the employees who reported at least one task before January 2018 and continued to participate throughout the study period. Thus, the data set for our main analysis contains 18,387 tasks reported by 180 employees. The summary statistics of the exploratory and the main data sets are shown in Tables B2 and B3, respectively, in Online Appendix B.

## 4. Empirical Analysis

Platforms design various strategies to motivate user contributions related to specific tasks, and platforms also often expect users to respond with high participa tion and performance. Prior studies have empirically demonstrated that monetary incentives are a valid and common approach that affects user contribution behavior. To systematically study the impact of canceling monetary incentives on user participation and performance in our research context, we conduct a two-step empirical analysis, as illustrated in Figure 3. (1) Using the exploratory data set, we adopt fixed-effects models combined with the regression discontinuity in time (RDiT) strategy to analyze the effect of introducing monetary rewards (i.e., A in Figure 3). This exploratory analysis, together with previous research on introduc ing monetary rewards, serves as a solid benchmark for our main analysis of canceling monetary rewards. (2) Then, using the main data set, we conduct the main analysis with a DID strategy to estimate the effect of canceling monetary rewards (i.e., B in Figure 3). In this way, we can understand the net impact of canceling monetary incentives by comparing the two effects (i.e., A versus B).<sup>6</sup> Furthermore, we investigate the heterogeneity of the impact regarding individuals’ different motivation types and working competency levels.

## 4.1. Impact of Canceling Monetary Incentives

4.1.1. Impact of Canceling Monetary Incentives on Par ticipation. In this section, we aim to answer our first research question regarding the impact of canceling monetary incentives on users’ participation in reporting tasks. We measure an individual’s participation in each month using two variables: (1) a continuous variable denoted by the logarithm of the number of tasks reported by that individual (i.e., log(NumReport))<sup>7</sup> and (2) a dummy variable defined as whether the individual reported at least one task (i.e., IsReport), equaling one if reported and zero otherwise. To measure the performance of each task, we define several proxy variables from two major perspectives as follows.

First, we follow the platform’s guidelines for writing task reports to measure the quality of the contribution. Specifically, the platform requires each task report to be “as detailed as possible” and “focused on the major issue.” This is because a more detailed and focused report is beneficial to the task completion. This is because, according to our interview with the company’s managers, the standard for completing a task (specified in the safety instructions) is pretty clear, and the only requirement for a task is to meet this standard in a timely manner to avoid potential safety incidents. In this way, a higher Normalized\_ Length\_Write indicates that the employee is willing to write a longer description, given the same level of task complexity. We then take the average Normalized\_ Length\_Write of all the task reports written by each employee before the policy change to measure the internal motivation of each employee. This is because longer summaries given the same task complexity suggest that the individual is willing to expend more effort in performing the tasks, even if there are no additional monetary incentives (Cerasoli et al. 2014, Gong et al. 2021).

Based on the average Normalized\_Length\_Write of each individual, we classify them into two groups. If the average Normalized\_Length\_Write is above the median, we label the individual as highly internally motivated, with more internal and less extrinsic motivation.

Otherwise, they are labeled as less internally motivated, with less internal and more extrinsic motivation. To study the heterogeneous impact of canceling rewards, we perform the same procedures of empirical analysis used in Section 4.1 on the two groups separately. Specifically, we adopt the fixed-effects models characterized in Equation (1) of Section 4.1 and Equation (C1) of Online Appendix C for exploratory analysis on the effect of initially introducing the rewards to establish a benchmark for the main analysis; then, we adopt the main DID models characterized in Equations (2) and (3) of Section 4.1 to estimate the effect of canceling monetary rewards.

Existing research on public platforms finds that when monetary incentives are introduced, users with low internal motivation tend to exhibit a decline in task performance, but highly internally motivated users do not show significant changes in performance (Khern-amnuai et al. 2018). However, our study suggests that less internally motivated users do not significantly change their task performance when the rewards are introduced in our context, but their performance declines after the rewards are canceled. In contrast, the performance of highly internally motivated users remains unchanged, even after the rewards are canceled. This is because, in our context, canceling quantity-based monetary rewards only affected the quantity-based image, but the quality-based image remained because all task processes and text descriptions were still visible to all users. Thus, highly internally motivated users decreased their contribution quantity (i.e., task participation), but not contribution quality (i.e., task performance). These results support our Hypothesis 3(b).

Our main findings are as follows. (1) There is no significant difference between high-competency and low-competency users in terms of the impact of monetary incentives on participation, which supports our Hypothesis 4(a). (2) Canceling rewards has no sig nificant effect on the performance of high-competency users, but causes a significant performance decline in low-competency users, which supports our Hypothesis 4(b). ## 5. Discussion and Conclusions

Although introducing and canceling monetary rewards are common practices adopted by digital platforms, prior studies have mainly focused on the effects of introducing incentives in the context of public platforms. Our study fills an important research gap by systemati cally investigating the impact of canceling monetary incentives based on a quasi-natural experiment conducted on a corporate platform designed for fulfilling specific tasks. To understand the net impact of canceling monetary rewards on both participation and performance, we first conduct exploratory analysis with fixedeffects models combined with RDiT strategy to estimate the effects of introducing rewards. Then, in the main analysis, we employ the DID strategy to examine the effects of canceling rewards. Furthermore, we dig deeper into the underlying mechanism by exploring the heterogeneity with regard to motivation types and working competency levels. In addition, we compare our results with existing studies on public platforms to analyze the similarities and differences between corporate and public platforms regarding the impact of monetary incentives.

## 5.1. Managerial Implications

Digital platforms can adopt different ways to motivate user contributions and engagement, including both monetary and nonmonetary approaches. To formulate appropriate strategies, it is important to understand the impact of both introducing and canceling monetary incentives, as well as the heterogeneous responses of different types of individuals. The main findings in the existing literature (on introducing rewards in public platforms) and in our study (on introducing and canceling rewards in a corporate platform) are summarized in Table 7, including the results of the overall impact and heterogeneity analysis. These results indicate that removing monetary incentives is not simply a reverse process of introducing them: in many cases, the effects of canceling and introducing monetary incentives are not the opposite of each other, and the whole process of introducing and canceling rewards can lead to a net negative impact. However, it can still be worth providing temporary incentives, because the value of user contributions to the platform may vary over time. Specifically, the marginal benefit that the platform derives from users’ contributing to a single task may decrease over time. For example, a corporate platform can be crafted as a tool for a specific project, and the significance of user contributions on the platform may diminish after the critical stage of the project.<sup>12</sup> This phenomenon can also exist on public platforms. For example, when an e-commerce platform is first established, it is critical to accumulate customer reviews, so one customer review has a higher marginal benefit for the platform in the initial stage compared with that in later stages. Therefore, the platform’s optimal long-term strategy of introducing and canceling monetary rewards can depend on various factors, such as the declining rate of the marginal benefit of each task and the platform’s longevity.

Specifically, based on our empirical results, particularly about the net negative impact of canceling monetary incentives on both participation and performance, a platform may implement different strategies of monetary incentives under different conditions as follows.

1. Strategy 1: Introduce monetary incentives first and cancel them later. If the platform experiences a decrease in the marginal benefit gained from users contributing a single task over time, or if the platform’s lifespan is not too long, the optimal strategy may involve initially introducing monetary rewards. This approach aims to boost users’ contribution quantity when the marginal benefit of user contribution is at its peak. Over time, as the marginal benefit decreases, the platform should opt to discontinue rewards to save on monetary costs when the incentives are no longer justified. Although canceling incentives can lead to a net negative impact, this “introduce and cancel” strategy should be optimal in the long term if the marginal benefit decreases relatively fast or the platform’s lifespan is not too long. This is also consistent with the existing literature and our observations in the real world—that is, sometimes it suffices for the incentives to be effective in the short term (Gneezy et al. 2011).

2. Strategy 2: Introduce monetary incentives and keep providing them. If the platform’s marginal benefit from users’ contributing to a single task decreases very slowly or does not decrease over time, or if the platform has a relatively short lifespan, then it is consistently worthwhile to offer incentives. This holds true as long as the benefits outweigh the monetary costs during the initial introduction of incentives.

3. Strategy 3: Never introduce monetary incentives. As previously mentioned, if the platform’s marginal benefit from users’ contributing to a single task diminishes over time, the platform may opt to discontinue rewards to save on monetary costs after incentivizing users for a specific duration. However, if the platform has an exceptionally long lifespan and the marginal benefit of user contribution does not decline rapidly, the enduring negative consequences of ceasing incentives might result in a substantial loss. In such instances, contemplating the long-term impact, the platform might choose not to introduce monetary incentives at all.

In addition, according to the heterogeneity analysis and the comparison of our study (on corporate platforms) with prior literature (on public platforms), we can better understand the mechanism by which monetary incentives impact user behavior. Based on the mechanism, digital platforms can design different incentive policies to motivate different types of users.

For example, unlike prior studies on public platforms, where monetary rewards lead to a negative image of the user as greedy and undermine their internal motives (Ariely et al. 2009, Alexy and Leitner 2011), monetary rewards in corporate platforms can be associated with a positive image (e.g., praise and recognition) and boost the participation of internally motivated individuals. This positive image can also be more effective than pure monetary gains, which highlights the importance of image motivation. Therefore, for users with high internal motivation, platforms may leverage various approaches to enhance user image, such as creating a sharing culture (Garud and Kumaraswamy 2005), designing gamification elements (Liu et al. 2017, Wang et al. 2020), and connecting tasks with prosocial missions or symbolic awards (Kosfeld and Neckermann 2011, Cassar 2019). We also observe that, compared with internal motivation (e.g., image motives), extrinsic motivation (e.g., monetary incentives) or other moneyrelated factors (e.g., the reciprocity established by material rewards) are more likely to lead to asymmetric outcomes. This suggests that the net adverse impact of canceling rewards is mainly attributed to less internally motivated users, who are more sensitive to monetary losses than gains. Moreover, unlike on public platforms, on the corporate platform, the introduction of monetary rewards does not significantly decrease the performance of less internally motivated users, owing to the stronger reciprocity in corporate settings (Arazy and Gellatly 2012, Zhou et al. 2022). Thus, platform owners should be more careful about canceling monetary rewards for less internally motivated users and try to strengthen reciprocity to enhance their motives. Furthermore, we notice that after rewards are canceled, only low-competency users exhibit a significant decline in performance because high-competency users can achieve better performance with less effort or lower costs than low-competency users. Thus, this finding suggests that digital platforms lower user costs of conducting specific tasks, which can make task performance less sensitive to monetary rewards. For example, platforms can develop better artifacts, optimize user interfaces, and provide more personalized instructions to help users complete tasks more easily and quickly.

## 5.2. Limitations and Future Research

Our work is not without limitations. First, our research context is a corporate platform of an international conglomerate subsidiary. The external validity of our results might need to be further examined for other types of digital platforms. Nevertheless, based on our theoretical framework and the comparison of our empirical results with prior literature, the identified underlying mechanisms by which monetary incentives impact user motivation are probably generalizable and applicable to different contexts. Second, for privacy reasons, we lack access to the detailed characteristics (such as age, gender, and detailed working experience) of those users in the data, so we cannot incorporate them into heterogeneity analysis. Third, our exploratory analyses of the effects of introducing monetary incentives are conducted with fixed-effects models and the RDiT strategy, which may not be ideal for causal identification. However, our theoretical development and many prior studies have provided additional support for the credibility of the analyses.

There are several possible extensions to this study. First, as previously mentioned, compared with public platforms (e.g., social media), where users can have fun, corporate platforms are more like tools, and the employ ees are less likely to develop habits (or addiction) to the IS use. In addition, public platforms usually combine monetary incentives with various other strategic opera tions (e.g., community formulation, social interactions, and personalized recommendations) to shape user behavior patterns,<sup>13</sup> whereas the corporate platform context is mainly utilitarian without the confounding factors of other strategies and habit formation. This provides us with a great opportunity to investigate the pure effects of monetary incentives, which is also the focus of this study. However, in the future, it might be interesting to further explore the effects of habit formation driven by monetary incentives and the combination of monetary rewards with other strategies on public platforms. Second, to study the net impact of canceling monetary incentives, we separately estimate and compare the effects of introducing and canceling rewards using data from quasi-natural experiments. Future research can design field experiments combining the introduction and cancellation of rewards for more rigorous identification and comparison. Third, the detrimental effects of canceling monetary incentives can be mitigated by including social and psychological elements. For example, Qiao et al. (2021) proposed mitigating the adverse effects (e.g., lower contribution quality) of introducing monetary incentives by goal setting and challenge seeking. It is also possible for platforms to strengthen internal motivation in various ways, such as by introducing social norms and social comparisons (Chen et al. 2010, Burtch et al. 2018). In the future, it would be meaningful to investigate such approaches for alleviating the adverse effects of canceling monetary rewards.
