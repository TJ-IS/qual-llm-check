# Making Lemonade from Lemons: A Transaction Cost Economics Perspective on Third-Party Disruptions in a Multivendor Information Technology Service

Abstract. In modern enterprise computing environments, multiple information technology (IT) services from first and third parties are often integrated to form coherent solutions for business customers. Using transaction cost economics (TCE) as a theoretical foundation, we seek to understand how uncertainties introduced by third-party services shape enterprise customers’ use of various IT services in these multivendor service settings. Specifically, we analyze a case of service disruption caused by a third party that affects the multivendor service but does not directly affect the first-party services. In line with the tenets of TCE, we find a temporary increase in the use of first-party services that can serve as a similar-goal substitute to fulfill the organization’s needs during the disruption; however, on average, we observe a net decline in the total use of services in the long run. We empirically analyze the role of first-party technical support during the disruption. Based on textual data from the first party’s technical support log, we use deep learning to assess what actions the first party can take during such disruptions to turn the challenge into an opportunity. We find that if the first party offers high-quality technical support that specifically addresses issues related to its product, it may be able to make lemonade out of lemons. Such technical support effectively boosts customers’ use of first-party services in the long run. Curiously, however, similar efforts by the first party in the predisruption period are ineffective in achieving the same effect.

## 1. Introduction

A large body of information technology (IT) services literature in the information systems field has examined how various governance mechanisms, contract designs, and task modularity can mitigate issues associated with outsourcing IT services to external vendors (e.g., Susarla et al. 2009, Mani et al. 2012). Most prior studies focus on the dyadic relationship between a single IT vendor and one customer as well as the insourcing versus outsourcing decision. As noted by Bapna et al. (2010)—an observation that holds true more than a decade later—there is a paucity of empirical research that seeks to understand enterprise customers’ IT sourcing and use decisions in a modern computing environment where multiple vendors’ IT services are integrated together to form a coherent solution. Although such multivendor services offer a variety of new features that a single vendor is unable to provide, enterprise customers also face higher uncertainty because of the involvement of multiple IT vendors. For instance, data security issues or the sudden failure of any of these IT vendors’ services may lead to a disruption of customers’ business activities.

An important framework that is frequently adopted by the IT services literature is the transaction cost economics (TCE) theory (e.g., Williamson 1985, 1991). An application of TCE suggests that an organization’s decisions on what IT service to use hinges on the uncertainties involved in transactions with external parties and the frequency of transactions as well as on the level of specific investments needed for transactions with the external parties. Then, how do uncertainties, such as disruptions involving multivendor service models, shape the use of IT services in both the short and long runs? How can the first-party vendor—the one that directly interacts and transacts with the customers— address the challenges and maintain long-term service usage?

Motivated by these observations, we seek to understand enterprise customers’ patterns of IT service use in an environment where some IT services are provided solely by a first party (first-party services) and where others are supported jointly by both the first and third parties (multivendor services). We focus on a scenario in which some first-party services and the multivendor service serve a similar purpose in different ways (i.e., as similar-goal substitutes). On the one hand, the multivendor services can provide customers with unique functions and thus, may be more attractive than the first-party services; on the other hand, such a service model also can be riskier as a result of the uncertainties and coordination involving multiple parties. To make the research question tractable, we consider one specific type of uncertainty: a service disruption caused by a third party that directly affects the multivendor service but not the first-party services. In addition to investigating customer response in the short run (i.e., during the disruption), we also seek to understand how customers adjust their use of the multivendor and first-party services in the long run (i.e., after the disrupted service is restored).

Built upon TCE theory, our study argues that the high level of uncertainty created by the third-party disruption may cause enterprise customers to reduce the use of multivendor services in the long run (i.e., even after the service is restored) to lower the reliance on such services. Meanwhile, because of the high level of asset specificity involved with enterprise computing, customers may increase their use of services offered solely by the first party (i.e., first-party services) that can serve as similar-goal substitutes, at least in the short run, instead of exploring services from a different IT vendor. However, how customers’ use of the first-party services will change in the long run is unclear. In one scenario, the use of first-party services may experience only a temporary increase followed by a drop to predisruption levels; another possibility is that customers accessing the first-party services during the third-party disruption may perceive the services as more reliable and less uncertain than the disrupted multivendor service and thus, maintain a high level of use in the long run.

As the primary customer-facing vendor, the first party must devise effective strategies to maintain customer satisfaction and favorably influence customer choices in the face of third-party disruptions. We focus on two types of first-party technical support during the disruption: (1) technical support that addresses product issues (i.e., product support) and (2) technical support that addresses customer issues (i.e., customer support). We argue that because product support may address the uncertainty concern regarding first-party services, it motivates customers to invest time and resources in exploring such services. Such exploration may lead to the discovery of useful features and is an important step for a customer to adapt its business processes and routines according to these features. As a result, custo mers increase the long-term use of first-party services. However, although successful customer support is important for overall customer satisfaction, it does not effectively shape the customer’s perception of the stability of the IT services in the context of a disruption. Accordingly, it may not address the uncertainty concern and motivate customers’ exploration and adaptation of the first-party services.

We collect data from an IT service vendor that offers customer relationship management (CRM) software as a service (SaaS) using a subscription-based model. It offers a range of IT services for enterprise customers to manage their distributed sales workforce and to facili tate the sales and communication processes. The mod ules used to track and manage sales workers’ daily activities include Customer Visit, Daily Report, Task, and Check-In/Out, which we consider to be similargoal substitutes for each other. Among these, the Check-In/Out, Daily Report, and Task modules are supported solely by the first-party vendor, whereas the Customer Visit module involves a third party that runs a Global Positioning System (GPS) tracking service to get workers’ location data. More specifically, the Customer Visit module allows sales workers to record the full history of each visit to their clients, including retrieving client profiles, registering their physical loca tion based on the third-party data, and recording the transaction involved.

During our sample period, the GPS tracking service provided by the third party experienced a serious disruption for 11 weeks (i.e., it returned inaccurate worker locations). During this period, sales workers were unable to use the Customer Visit module to record their locations, but they could still use the module to record other information about each visit. The other modules run by the focal first party alone worked properly during this period. Both the disruption and the recovery of the third-party GPS service were beyond the control of the first-party vendor and its customers; thus, we use these two exogenous events to understand how enterprise customers adjusted their use of the multivendor service (i.e., the Customer Visit module) and the firstparty services (i.e., the Daily Report and Task modules<sup>1</sup>) during and after the disruption.

One of the key data challenges is measuring productrelated versus customer-related support. Based on the complete service log provided by the focal first party, we used the bidirectional long short-term memory (Bi-LSTM) deep learning approach to classify the nature of the technical support interaction and its outcomes. Following these classifications, we measured the quality of technical support received by an enterprise customer based on the percentage of issues successfully addressed.

Our empirical analysis suggests that customers reduced their use of the multivendor service by 20%–21% per week during the disruption period compared with the predisruption period. Surprisingly, this decline persisted even during the postrecovery period. On average, customers’ use of the multivendor service remained 13%–16% lower than in the predisruption period. This drop suggests that enterprise customers did not simply switch back to the multivendor service after it was restored. Instead, the service disruption had a long-lasting effect—one that might reflect enterprise customers’ propensity to avoid unstable services that potentially interrupt their business operations (e.g., Aflaki and Popescu 2014).

After providing some evidence on the substitution relationship between the disrupted multivendor service and first-party services, we focus on how customers adjusted their use of these first-party services as a result of the disruption. Our results show that during the disruption, a customer affected by the disruption increased its weekly use of the first-party Daily Report module by 8% and that of the first-party Task module by 12% per week. This increase indicates that customers explored alternatives to fulfill their organizational needs and tried to compensate for the lost functionality. However, the increase in the use of first-party services did not last beyond the disruption. An exception to this pattern emerged if customers received high-quality product support on the first-party services during the disruption. For these customers, the increased use of first-party services continued after the service recovery. In contrast, high-quality support that sought to address customer-related issues (i.e., customer support) did not increase the long-term use of first-party services.

Although we demonstrate the positive effect of product-related technical support during a third-party disruption, the natural question that follows is whether a disruption is needed for this effect to emerge. In our theoretical framework, we posit that a disruption leads to perceptions by customers of greater uncertainty; we also posit that if the first party can effectively address such perceptions by demonstrating the quality of its own offerings, the sharp contrast can motivate customers’ exploration of the first-party services and subsequently, influence their use behavior. Indeed, we find that similar efforts by the first party in the predisruption period do not achieve the same effect.

Through our empirical analysis of a multivendor setting, we contribute to the IT services literature, where studies have largely focused on the dyadic relationship between a customer and a single vendor (e.g., Chen and Bharadwaj 2009, Mani et al. 2010). Note that our multivendor setting is significantly different from one in which a single vendor has multiple IT modules. In the latter context, the first party offers multiple IT service modules to its customers. When disruptions to its services happen, the first party may have better evaluation of the recovery process, as well as being able to communicate and coordinate with the customers more easily. As a result, the first party can better manage customer expectations of the recovery process (e.g., Husted and Folger 2004, Charness and Dufwenberg 2006). The uncertainty perceived by customers in a single-vendor setting would be substantially lower. Accordingly, customers may not adjust their use across different first-party modules in the same way we observed in a multivendor setting. Studies have investigated the multivendor setting only very recently, and the few studies in this literature stream mainly explore a firm’s choice between a single vendor and multiple vendors (e.g., Bhattacharya et al. 2018, Bapna et al. 2023). In contrast, the context of our study enhances understandings of how customers adjust their use of both the multivendor module and the first-party modules in the long run when a third-party disruption is beyond the direct control of the first-party provider and thus, leads to a high level of uncertainty. We demonstrate that such a disruption may have a negative effect on the customers overall consumption level in the long run, which may lead to customer churn. However, we analyze whethe the first-party vendor can turn this challenge into an opportunity. The rich textual data from customer service logs allow us to classify the category and quality of technical support provided by the first party. We argue that high-quality, product-related technical support can help to motivate customers to invest their resources in explorations of new features of the first party’s services and that this exploration, in turn, increases thei use beyond the predisruption level in the long run.

## 2. Theoretical Motivation

## 2.1. Third-Party Disruptions in a Multivendor Setting

A body of literature has investigated the role that service failure and recovery play in customers’ subsequent behavior (e.g., Hart et al. 1990, Maxham and Netemeyer 2002, Kau and Loh 2006). Most studies in this stream have examined single-vendor settings, where customers may subscribe either to one module from one provider (i.e., a single vendor with a single module) or to multiple modules from one provider (i.e., a single vendor with multiple modules). For instance, in the context of enterprise resource planning, an enterprise customer may get all modules (e.g., inventory management, material management, sales management, etc.) from a single software provider. When a disruption occurs, the vendor can assess the recovery process and coordinate with the customers more easily. Further, with direct communication with customers, the vendor may be able to take customer feedback as a source of information for future service offerings (Umashankar et al. 2017). As a result, the focus of this stream of literature has been on understanding whether and how a service provider can manage its own disruption to turn frustrated customers into loyal ones and to create more goodwill than if no disruption had occurred in the first place (e.g., Hart et al. 1990, De Matos et al. 2007).

In contrast, our study focuses on a multivendor setting in the IT services industry, where multiple IT vendors collaborate to offer a coherent solution to customers. In this context, the first party calls unique thirdparty functions through application programming interfaces and then integrates them with its own services. We call such offerings multivendor services. Multivendor services often can still work, even without the third-party functions—but without important features.

A salient issue in a multivendor setting is that customers may face additional uncertainty brought on by disruptions in third-party services. More specifically, because a third-party disruption often is out of the first party’s immediate control, when such a disruption happens, customers may be uncertain not only about when the service can be restored but also about whether similar disruptions might happen again in the future. In addition, the absence of direct communication between the customers and the third party suggests that the customers have no firsthand knowledge regarding the progress being made to restore the service. Such opacity would also result in uncertainty for the customers. In contrast, as noted, because of the direct communication and coordination with customers, for a disruption of first-party services, the first party often can manage customers’ expectation and alleviate their concerns more easily.<sup>2</sup>

Our conjectures regarding the effects of third-party disruptions are built upon TCE theory (e.g., Williamson 1985, 1991). The existing TCE literature has mostly studied the implications of transaction costs for a firm’s choice of governance mode in a steady state: market (i.e., no dependency relations exist between transacting parties), hierarchy (i.e., internal organizational structure is developed for the good or service), and hybrid (i.e., a high level of interdependency and coordination exists between transacting parties, and thus, there are costs of replacing transacting parties). However, as noted by David and Han (2004), the central claim of TCE theory is that transactions would be handled with a goal to minimize the associated costs (including costs related to coordinating, controlling, and managing the transactions). Other than the governance choice, a body of literature has also looked at the implications of transaction costs for other firm choices and behaviors, such as the degree of integration within hybrid forms (e.g., Hennart 1993), joint actions among transacting parties (e.g., Heide and John 1990), information processing capabilities (Mani et al. 2010), and contract choices in IT outsourcing (Susarla et al. 2009).

In the context of contemporary IT services, business processes, routines, and workflows are often heavily dependent on the specific offerings of the IT service provider. Further, as we observe in our empirical setting, customers often ask for customization of features and capabilities. Therefore, modern IT service arrangements may often be characterized as hybrid forms, thus making a comparison between market, hierarchy, and hybrid less relevant. Motivated by these observations, our focus is to apply the TCE framework to understand how customers make the usage choice on the disrupted multivendor service and alternative IT services in a way to minimize the expected future costs involved with a given service provider.

In particular, the three factors that influence transaction costs proposed in the TCE framework (Williamson 1991) are relevant to our setting: (1) uncertainty involved in transactions with a party, (2) asset specifi city (that is, the extent of specific investments made when transactions involve an external party; e.g., training and integrating business processes and workflows with the vendor’s technological capabilities and offerings), and (3) frequency of transactions (that is, the volume of transactions with a party). An increase in uncertainty increases the expected costs with the cur rent IT service provider, which may prompt customers to seek new IT service providers. However, both a high level of asset specificity and high frequency of transactions are likely to justify the costs incurred with the current IT service provider, making it challenging to choose a completely new provider.

## 2.2. How Third-Party Disruption and Recovery Shape the Use of Multivendor Service

When a third party causes a disruption in the multivendor service, it is intuitive that customers would reduce their use of the affected service because of the temporary loss of functionality. How the disruption and recovery of the service shape customers’ use of the multivendor service in the long run (i.e., after the recovery) remains unclear.

On the one hand, because of the mission-critical nature of enterprise systems, disruptions could lead to organizational inefficiency and missed business opportunities. Several studies have demonstrated that enterprise customers greatly value the stability of their technology service (e.g., Aflaki and Popescu 2014). Thus, even if a current disruption is resolved, it may send enterprise customers a strong signal that such stability is at risk because similar third-party disruptions could happen again in the future. Because of the lack of direct communication with the third party, enterprise customers may perceive a high level of uncertainty regarding the stability of such a multivendor service. This line of argument suggests that third-party disruptions might lead to expectations of higher uncertainty and costs in using the multivendor service, which may cause enterprise users to reduce their service use in the long run to lower their reliance on the multivendor service.

On the other hand, IT services often require specific investments made for a given service provider. For example, business processes, data, and workflows need to be fully aligned with the enterprise systems. Moreover, significant investments are often made to help enterprise users to overcome knowledge barriers and get accustomed to a new enterprise system (Edmondson et al. 2001, Robey et al. 2002, Kim and Kankanhalli 2009). A high level of asset specificity as a result of these different types of investments may prompt enterprise customers to stay with the current multivendor service and may help to explain why they may resume a similar level of use as in the predisruption period after the restoration of service. Moreover, for enterprise customers that heavily rely on the current multivendor service and conduct high volumes of transactions, they may in particular see that the benefits of staying with the current multivendor service exceed the expected costs of using the service.

## 2.3. How Third-Party Disruption and Recovery Shape the Use of First-Party Services

As noted, an important distinction between third- and first-party disruptions is the elevated uncertainty caused by the former. TCE predicts that enterprise customers, when faced with such a situation, will seek to adapt either by internalizing the transactions (i.e., building their own IT services) or by exploring other IT services to fulfill their needs. We refer to such IT services that have similar goals of supporting the enterprise business processes as similar-goal substitutes (Shocker et al. 2004).

In the context of enterprise computing, enterprise customers would have to spend significant time and effort to build their own IT solutions because of the high complexity of both hardware and software configurations; thus, they are unlikely to adopt this strategy in most circumstances. Alternatively, enterprise customers may explore first-party services that can serve as similar-goal substitutes, instead of exploring services from a different IT vendor, for the following reasons. The high asset specificity of the focal relationship may discourage customers from seeking a completely new service provider. IT modules from the same vendor often have a consistent interface for users to operate and are based on compatible data formats; thus, the costs of learning and data transfer are lower than such costs in adopting modules from a different IT vendor. Moreover, customers have communication and coordination channels already established with the first party; the benefits from learning as a result of prior association may be critical to the success of the outsourcing arrangement (Barua and Mani 2014, Mani and Barua 2015). Meanwhile, the monetary costs of exploring the first-party services could be significantly lower than a completely different IT vendor. In the current enterprise computing environment, pricing is often based on a bundle of various IT modules from the first party; thus, customers face little to no marginal monetary cost in using an additional IT module from the same bundle.

Whether such an increase in the use of first-party services is temporary or long lasting depends on customers’ experiences of using first-party services during the disruption. As suggested by the TCE theory (e.g., Williamson 1985), under high asset specificity, interdependence and continuity between the transacting parties are important. Significant adaptation efforts are often needed for continued partnership, but the willingness to adapt depends on a transacting party’s evaluation of the other party’s services (e.g., Williamson 1991, Carney 1998). In the case of enterprise computing where asset specificity is high, a focal customer may prefer IT services of high quality so that costly adaptation is worth the efforts for continued relationship with the service provider. During third-party disruptions, customers would perceive a high level of uncertainty from using the IT services. If the first party could demonstrate the quality of its own services and address the uncertainty concern, customers may consider the firstparty services as the long-term solution and accordingly, explore features that they had not used before. Such exploration is an important step for customers to adapt their business processes and routines based on the features of the first-party services. In this case, we expect customers to increase their use of these firstparty services in the long run as a part of their new routine. Conversely, if customers do not find the first party’s services reliable, they are less likely to invest significant amounts of time exploring the first-party services and adapting themselves for long-term use; in this case, any increase in use would only be transient and may go away once the disruption is over.

In sum, as a result of the disruption, we expect a short-term increase in the use of first-party services that serve as similar-goal substitutes for the disrupted mul tivendor service. More importantly, customers’ perceptions on the quality of first-party services during the disruption may play an important role in shaping their exploration and subsequent long-term use of first-party services. In the next section, we examine how the first party can effectively shape customers’ perceptions of its service during the disruption, potentially leading to a long-lasting positive effect.

## 2.4. The Moderating Effect of First-Party Technical Support

Our focus on first-party technical support is motivated by a body of literature that emphasizes the role of technical support in shaping the quality of IT services (e.g., Das 2003, Retana et al. 2012). Technical support is a form of postsales service that involves interacting with customers that have questions or are experiencing problems with a firm’s product or service (Gray and Durcikova 2005). Technical support has been shown to reduce knowledge barriers for customers (e.g., Attewell 1992), retain customers and expand market share (e.g., Davenport and Klahr 1998), and increase the use of IT services (e.g., Retana et al. 2018). In our context, the third-party disruption is beyond the immediate control of the first party, but the first party is able to improve its technical support for its own IT services. We discussed that a demonstration of first-party service quality may encourage customers to explore alternative first-party services and therefore, increase their long-term use. If the first party’s technical support encourages such exploration, the first party may be able to make lemonade out of lemons—that is, leverage the disruption to promote use of its own services.

We follow the existing literature that studies the interaction between technical support teams and customers (e.g., Deng et al. 2015), categorizing technical support based on the causes of the problems reported by customers. The two categories include (1) product support, which addresses product deficiencies such as product malfunction or product errors, and (2) customer support, which addresses customer deficiencies or customer needs such as customer errors, customers lack of knowledge or experience, requests for help, or customization requests. Product support satisfies customers by improving the product, whereas customer support satisfies customers by smoothing out their use experiences.

Prior work has suggested that product-related service activity is positively related to a firm’s product development efforts and technological strength (e.g., Eggert et al. 2011, Leek and Christodoulides 2012). Technical support that successfully addresses product issues, such as fixing product errors or bugs (in the case of software products), may signal a firm’s capability in providing high-quality services through constant improvement as well as its responsiveness in correcting product-related issues. Such positive perception of the first party’s service quality may encourage customers to explore features that they did not try before, leading to a higher level of long-term use (e.g., Albietz and Assimakopoulos 2014). Thus, we posit that when customers receive high-quality technical support on firstparty products during a third-party disruption, there may be an increase in the use of the first-party services in the long run.

The second form of technical support addresses customer problems or needs, such as providing detailed instructions or fixing customer mistakes. Although such support may enhance customers’ use experience by helping them better understand how to use a product (e.g., Hartline et al. 2000), it does not address their concerns about the lack of reliability of the first-party services and the resulting expected costs and uncertainty from using them. Thus, although successful customer support is important for overall customer satisfaction, it may not motivate customers to explore new features of first-party services in order to adapt their business processes during a third-party disruption. Indeed, customer support has been found to be most effective when customers already have a trusting and close relationship with the provider (e.g., Saxe and Weitz 1982). During a disruption, customers are likely to be frustrated by uncertainty, and they may not be moved to increase long-term use simply as a result of such customer support. In sum, the effect of third-party disruptions on the use of first-party services in the long run may be similar across customers that receive highversus low-quality technical support on customerrelated issues during the disruption period.

## 3. Empirical Approach 3.1. Empirical Context

Our empirical setting is an SaaS firm that provides mobile CRM solutions to enterprise customers. We focus on one particular CRM solution that has four modules that its enterprise customers can use to manage and monitor their sales force. These four modules, which can function independently of each other, include the following. First, the Check-In/Out module is used for taking attendance and recording when sales workers begin and leave work every day. Second, sales workers use the Daily Report module to report their daily activities to their manager in a narrative format. Third, the Customer Visit module is used by sales workers to record their real-time physical movements as well as the details of each visit. Fourth, sales workers use the Task module to compile a to-do list that they can then share with their manager. In addition to these four modules, the CRM solution also offers three administrative modules: (1) Broadcast, a module that allows managers and salespeople to send notifications; (2) Campaign, a module that helps salespeople to retrieve information related to various sales campaigns; and (3) Order, a module that generates documents ranging from purchase orders to invoices. The SaaS company offers this entire solution to its enterprise customers based on a yearly subscription (instead of on a pay-per-use basis). These modules offer a consistent user interface, and customers may choose to use some or all of these modules based on their needs for one subscription fee. Our data show that almost all customers use the Check-In/Out module almost every day, whereas we see greater variation in the use of the other modules.

The SaaS company (i.e., the first party) alone runs all modules except for the Customer Visit module. The Customer Visit module is supported jointly by the first party and a third party that runs a GPS tracking service. Whenever a sales worker turns on the Customer Visit module to record her customer visit details, the module automatically connects to the third party, which in turn, determines her exact location based on mobile phone data and then feeds the location data back into the Customer Visit module. This tracking service also can record how much time during a day a sales worker is visible through the tracking service, which is an indicator that managers can use to determine workers efforts. To ensure the reliability of the information, the module does not allow sales workers to manually type their locations into the system. Besides recording exact locations, the Customer Visit module also can be used to retrieve information, such as customer profiles, transaction histories, and potential sales promotions for the customer; these functions are supported solely by the first party. For purposes of our study, we consider the Customer Visit module to be a multivendor service and all other modules to be first-party services. See Online Appendix A for a diagram of the various parties involved in our context.

Our sample period spans January 1, 2018 to February 27, 2019. During this period, the GPS tracking service offered by the third party underwent a major disruption, lasting from June 8, 2018 until August 23, 2018. Based on Hogreve et al. (2017), we categorize the length of the disruption as long. Although the disruption did not affect the use procedure of the module during this period, it returned an inaccurate location for workers. As noted, the module was built so that it did not allow for manual entry of the location data; additionally, it was not able to interact directly with a mobile phone’s location data. On the fourth day after the disruption started, the first party published a notification message on the Customer Visit module about the disruption. In this message, the first party informed the customers that the problem was caused by a third party’s GPS tracking service. It also offered a temporary backup solution; however, this backup was not well integrated with the Customer Visit module, and workers would have to complete many steps every time they wanted to report their location.<sup>3</sup> Based on the customer service log, we note that customers were unhappy about the disruption and complained to the first party. However, in our sample of 4,360 customers, only 9 customers requested a refund during the disruption. Because the pricing is based on a yearly subscription to the entire set of CRM modules, the first party did not offer its customers affected by the disruption any monetary compensation.

## 3.2. Empirical Strategy

The third-party disruption and recovery are beyond the immediate control of both the focal SaaS company and its customers. Thus, we consider them to be exogenous shocks to customers’ use of both the multivendor service $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ , the Customer Visit module) and the firstparty services (i.e., all the other modules). With the two exogenous events (both disruption and recovery), we can define three time periods: before disruption, during disruption, and after recovery.

To understand customer responses to the third-party disruption—that is, how they adjust their use of the multivendor service—we use the following empirical model, in which the unit of analysis is a customerweek, with i denoting the customer and t denoting the week:

$$
U s e _ {i t} = \beta_ {1} \times D u r i n g D _ {t} + \beta_ {2} \times A f t e r R _ {t} + \mathbf {C o n t r o l} _ {\mathrm{it}} + v _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i t}.\tag{1}
$$

The dependent variable $U s e _ { i t }$ in specification (1) denotes customer i’s use of the Customer Visit module at time t. Time dummy variables $D u r i n g D _ { t }$ and $A f t e r R _ { t }$ are turned on for the during-disruption and afterrecovery periods, respectively. $\mathbf { C o n t r o l { \mathrm { i } } _ { i t } }$ includes two variables: the duration of the relationship between customer i and the first party at time t and the number of terminals (i.e., smartphones) that can access the service from customer i at time t. We also include customerlevel fixed effects $v _ { i }$ in all analyses.

We expect $\beta _ { 1 }$ to be negative because the disruption would certainly render the Customer Visit module less useful and would thus lead to less use than in the predisruption period. Regarding $\beta _ { 2 } ,$ customers may resume the same level of use as in the predisruption period if high asset specificity and transaction frequency prevent them from choosing a different service. In this case, we may expect $\beta _ { 2 }$ to be close to zero, suggesting no significant change compared with the predisruption period. However, if customers are concerned about the uncertainty and instability of the service in the long run, we may expect $\beta _ { 2 }$ to be significantly negative.

Before studying how third-party disruption shapes the use of first-party services that broadly serve similar purposes $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ serving as similar-goal substitutes), it is important to first verify the existence of a similar-goal substitute relationship between the disrupted multivendor service and some of the first-party services. We obtained the textual descriptions of all modules and performed a document-to-document cosine similarity calculation between the descriptions of these modules. As shown in Table C1 in Online Appendix C, the Daily Report, Task, and Check-In/Out modules have higher similarity scores to the disrupted Customer Visit module than the other modules (e.g., the Broadcast, Campaign, and Order modules). This result seems to be consistent with the intended purposes of these modules described earlier. The Customer Visit, Task, and Check-In/Out modules are mainly used to monitor and manage the sales force, whereas the Broadcast, Campaign, and Order modules help to facilitate the communications and sales processes for sales workers. Besides the textual similarity analysis, based on the entire sample period for the full set of customers in our sample, we use the following specification (2) to examine the similar-goal substitute relationship. Similar to other analyses, the unit of analysis is still at the customerweek level, with i denoting the customer and t denoting the week:

$$
\begin{array}{c} \text {FirstPartyModuleUse} _ {i t} = \beta_ {1} \times \text {CustomerVisitModuleUse} _ {i t} \\ + \mathbf {C o n t r o l} _ {\mathbf {i t}} + \eta_ {t} + v _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i t}. \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

The dependent variable FirstPartyModuleUse denotes customer i’s use of a first-party module in week $t ,$ and we analyze each of the first-party modules separately. CustomerVisitModule denotes customer $i ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ use of the Customer Visit module in week t. To address the skewness of FirstPartyModuleUse and Customer $V i s i t M o d u l e _ { i t } ,$ we took log transformations. Control is the same as in specification (1), and we incorporated customer-level fixed effects $v _ { i }$ and the full set of weekly time dummies $\eta _ { t } .$ If the first-party modules could serve as a similargoal substitute for the Customer Visit module, we would expect $\beta _ { 1 }$ to be negative. One important concern of this specification (2) is that time-varying unobservables could be correlated with the use of different modules. As a result, we used the third-party disruption DuringD × Treated (equal to one for customers affected by the disruption during the disruption period and equal to zero otherwise) as an instrumental variable (IV) for CustomerVisitModuleUse .

If we could identify the existence of a similar-goal substitute relationship between the multivendor Customer Visit model and certain first-party modules, we use the following difference-in-differences estimation to investigate the effect on the use of these first-party modules. More specifically, we assign to the treatment group customers that had used the Customer Visit module prior to the disruption and thus, were likely to be affected by the events; the control group comprises customers that had never used the Customer Visit module throughout the entire sample period and are, therefore, unlikely to be affected by the events. Thus, the baseline model can be written as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{c} U s e _ {i t} = \beta_ {1} \times D u r i n g D _ {t} \times T r e a t e d _ {i} + \beta_ {2} \times A f t e r R _ {t} \\ \times T r e a t e d _ {i} + \mathbf {C o n t r o l} _ {\mathbf {i t}} + \eta_ {t} + v _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i t}. \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

The dependent variable $U s e _ { i t }$ here denotes customer i’s use of a first-party module that serves as a similar-goal substitute at time t. Time dummy variables DuringD<sub>t</sub> and $A f t e r R _ { t }$ are the same as the ones we have in specification (1). Because we use the full set of weekly dum mies, we are unable to estimate the direct effects of DuringD and $A f t e r R _ { t } ,$ which are dropped from the regression. Dummy variable Treated<sub>i</sub> is equal to one if customer i used the Customer Visit module prior to the disruption and thus, was likely to be affected by the disruption. $\mathbf { C o n t r o l { \mathrm { i } } _ { i t } }$ is the same as in specification (1), and we incorporate customer-level fixed effects $v _ { i }$ and the full set of weekly time dummies $\eta _ { t }$ in all analyses.

We next use specification (4) to explore how the first party’s technical support during the disruption shaped the use of its services after the multivendor service was restored:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & U s e _ {i t} = \beta_ {1} \times A f t e r R _ {t} \times T r e a t e d _ {i} + \beta_ {2} \times A f t e r R _ {t} \times T r e a t e d _ {i} \\ & \qquad \times H i g h T e c h S u p p o r t _ {i} + \beta_ {3} \times A f t e r R _ {t} \\ & \qquad \times H i g h T e c h S u p p o r t _ {i} + \mathbf {C o n t r o l} _ {\mathbf {i t}} + \eta_ {t} + v _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i t}. \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

Several variables, including $U s e _ { i t } , A f t e r R _ { t }$ , Treated<sub>i</sub>, and Controls<sub>it</sub>, are defined in the same way as in specification (3). HighTechSupport is a dummy variable indi cating whether a customer i received high-quality technical support during the disruption. As discussed, we are interested in understanding whether a difference arises across different types of technical support (product support versus customer support), and so, we examine their moderating roles separately. If customers that received high-quality product support during the disruption had greater use of first-party services after the multivendor service recovery, we expect $\beta _ { 2 }$ to be positive when HighTechSupport<sub>i</sub> is measured based on product-related technical support. If customer-related technical support during the disruption makes no difference, we would expect $\beta _ { 2 }$ to be close to zero when HighTechSupport is measured based on customerrelated technical support.

To estimate specification (4), we use only the predisruption period and the postrecovery period to implement the regression analysis, and we use observations from the during-disruption period to construct the HighTechSupport variable. We do so for several reasons. First, on a theoretical level, our key objective is to understand the role of technical support in shaping the long-term use of first-party services $( \mathrm { i . e . }$ , after the multivendor service is restored) rather than its short-term effects. Second, on the empirical level, a customer’s service use during the disruption and the level of technica support it received during the same period would be endogenous. Nevertheless, unobservables might be correlated both with the quality of technical support received by customers in the treatment group during the disruption $( \mathrm { i . e . }$ , the three-way interaction terms) and with the first-party module use (i.e., the dependent variable). We implement an IV estimation approach to address this concern, as we discuss in greater detail in the later section.

In our sample, many customers did not interact with the first party for any technical support during the disruption. These customers could be significantly different from the customers that tended to interact with the first party. In the baseline analysis, we drop these customers from the sample, but we also implement a robustness check by incorporating them using a slightly different empirical model as shown:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} U s e _ {i t} = & \beta_ {1} \times A f t e r R _ {t} \times T r e a t e d _ {i} + \beta_ {2} \times A f t e r R _ {t} \times T r e a t e d _ {i} \\ & \times H i g h T e c h S u p p o r t _ {i} + \beta_ {3} \times A f t e r R _ {t} \\ & \times H i g h T e c h S u p p o r t _ {i} + \beta_ {4} \times A f t e r R _ {t} \times T r e a t e d _ {i} \\ & \times L o w T e c h S u p p o r t _ {i} + \beta_ {5} \times A f t e r R _ {t} \\ & \times L o w T e c h S u p p o r t _ {i} + \mathbf {C o n t r o l} _ {\mathbf {i t}} + \eta_ {t} + v _ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i t}. \end{array}\tag{5}
$$

In specification (5), HighTechSupport<sub>i</sub> is a dummy variable indicating whether customer i received high-quality technical support during the disruption. LowTechSupport<sub>i</sub> is a dummy variable indicating whether customer i received low-quality technical support during the disruption. For customers that received no support, both $H i g h -$ TechSupport and LowTechSupport turn off, and $\beta _ { 1 }$ captures the effect of the disruption on their postrecovery use of first-party services. Similar to our baseline analyses, we investigated the effect of product support and the effect of customer support separately.

## 3.3. Data and Measurement

Our sample period starts on January 1, 2018 and ends on February 27, 2019. Based on the research design, our initial sample is composed of all enterprise customers that had started to use any module included in the CRM solution before the disruption (a total of 5,498 customers). During this period, these customers interacted with the technical support team of the first party 86,727 times, providing rich service log textual data for us to analyze and understand the nature and outcome of these interactions. Because our objective is to examine how third-party disruptions influence the multivendor service as well as the first-party services that may serve as similar-goal substitutes (in particular, the Daily Report module and the Task module), we restrict our analysis sample to customers that showed some interest in any of these three modules (i.e., customers that had ever used any of these modules throughout the sample period). This resulted in a total sample size of 4,360 customers.

The key dependent variable across all specifications, $U s e _ { i t } ,$ is measured by the number of weekly use instances from customer i in week t for the respective module. Because this variable is highly skewed, we take the log transformation for all analyses. The quality of technical support in specifications (4) and (5) is measured based on the rich technical support log files. Every time a customer calls the first party, the call handler records the interaction, including time, client name, a description of the customer’s request, and the handler’s response. We used the last two items as the basis to determine the category and outcome of each instance. In particular, we performed two tasks: (1) classify each technical support instance into one of the service categories, some of which addressed productrelated issues, whereas others addressed customerrelated issues, and (2) classify each technical support instance into either a “successfully resolved” category or a “not successfully resolved” category.

A simple keyword search or even topic modeling did not work well in our setting because of the nonstandardized and short entries in the service log (e.g., Cheng et al. 2014). Thus, we adopted a deep learning approach involving Bi-LSTM. More specifically, as a first step, we used skip Gram negative sampling (Mikolov et al. 2013) to create custom embeddings for our context. We used the embeddings as inputs into a Bi-LSTM model and classified all the records into 1 of 14 categories: GPS issues (the focal third-party disruption), minor software bugs, major software bugs, request for instructions, request for customization, terminal configuration, terminal debugging, user account, network errors, missing functions/features, file transfer errors, request for a refund, potential business opportunities, and call disconnected. We used a similar approach to classify whether each technical support instance was successful. We achieved 82.17% accuracy in predicting the category of an interaction and a 90.72% success rate in predicting whether a technical support instance was successful. The details are discussed in Online $\mathrm { A p \mathrm { - } }$ pendix B. For product-related technical support $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ product support), we focused on records that belong to the “minor software bugs” and “major software bugs” categories (labeled as Minor Bugs and Major Bugs hereafter). For customer-related technical support (i.e., customer support), we used records that belong to the “request for instructions,” “terminal configuration,” and “request for customization” categories (labeled as Instruction, Configuration, and Customization hereafter).<sup>4</sup>

To measure HighTechSupport in specifications (4) and (5) and LowTechSupport in specification (5), we implement the following steps. First, given the different levels of effort and resulting success rates in addressing the subcategories Minor Bugs versus Major Bugs within the product support category, we used different benchmarks to decide whether a given success rate is high or low in these two subcategories. For each subcategory, we computed the success rate for each customer during the disruption (i.e., the percentage of requests successfully addressed); we considered the success rate to be high if it was above the sample median in that subcategory and otherwise, to be low. Then, for analyses related to product support, HighTechSupport in specifications (4) and (5) is equal to one if customer i either received a high success rate of support on Minor Bugs or received a high success rate of support on Major Bugs; otherwise, it is equal to zero. In specification (5), LowTechSupport is equal to one if the customer received neither a high success rate of support on Minor Bugs nor a high success rate of support on Major Bugs. As noted, both HighTechSupport<sub>i</sub> and LowTechSupport<sub>i</sub> are equal to zero if customer i did not receive productrelated technical support.

Similarly, regarding customer-related technical support, two of the subcategories, Instruction and Configuration, often could be easily addressed, whereas the subcategory Customization often needed several days to address or simply could not be addressed because of its complexity. Thus, we use different benchmarks to decide whether a given success rate is high or low for each of these subcategories. That is, for each subcategory, we computed the success rate for each customer during the disruption, and we set the success rate as high if it was above the sample median in that subcategory and set it as low otherwise. Then, for analyses related to customer support, HighTechSupport in specifications (4) and (5) is equal to one if a customer i received a high success rate of support on Instruction, Configuration, or Customization and otherwise, is equal to zero. In specification (5), LowTechSupport is equal to one if customer i did not receive any of the customer support with high success rate.

Table 1 reports the summary statistics of the key variables. An enterprise customer has on average 8 use instances of the Daily Report module per week and 1.5 use instances of the Task module. However, these two variables are highly skewed; some customers had more than 1,200 use instances of the Daily Report module per week and 241 use instances of the Task module per week. Thus, we take the log transformation of the two variables in all regression analyses. On average, each enterprise customer has 64 terminals per week and stays with the first party for about seven months. Among the 4,360 enterprise customers in our sample, 17% had reached out to the first party for product support, and 26% had called for customer support; the success rates for these types of technical support are 61% and 70%, respectively.

Regarding the long-term effect of the third-party disruption, as discussed, we speculate that the key theoretical tension comes from two perspectives. On the one hand, heightened uncertainty arising from the thirdparty disruption could reduce enterprise customers use of such multivendor service, as they are often risk averse and tend to avoid unstable services in the long run. ## 4.3. The Effect on the Use of First-Party Services

The previous section establishes some basic evidence about the substitutional relationship between the disrupted Customer Visit module and the Daily Report and Task modules. In sum, these results suggest that during the thirdparty disruption, when the Customer Visit module was significantly less useful, customers switched to firstparty services that broadly serve similar purposes— that is, the ones that allow sales workers to report their work status and progress to managers in a different for mat. This finding suggests that the disruption made customers shift to services that could serve as similar goal substitutes to maintain normal business operations as much as possible in the short run. However, this positive effect of the disruption on first-party service use did not last long, as suggested by the coefficient estimate of Treated × AfterR in Table 4 as well as by Figures C2 and C3 in Online Appendix C. Collectively, the results from Tables 2 and 4 suggest that, on average, customers did not maintain an increased level of use of the first-party services, and at the same time, they decreased their use of the multivendor service that experienced the disruption in the long run. These results could indicate a likelihood for potential churn in the future in that the total IT service use (i.e., the sum of first-party service use and multivendor service use)

became somewhat lower, even after the multivendor service was fully restored.

## 5. The Roles of First-Party Technical Support During Disruption

## 5.1. The Effect on the Long-Term Use of First-Party Services

Given the transient increase in first-party service use during the disruption, an important question is whether the first party can effectively boost the longterm use of its services through effective technical support. As noted earlier, we categorize the first party’s technical support into product-related and customerrelated categories, which may have different implications for boosting the long-term use of first-party services. Based on specification (4), columns (1) and (2) in Table 5 present the results on how the first party’s technical support during the disruption for addressing product-related issues moderates the effect of the disruption on customers’ long-term use of the Daily Report module and the Task module, respectively. According to the estimate of Treated × AfterR, customers receiving low-quality product support did not increase the use of either module in the long run. However, as indicated in the last row of Table 5, customers receiving high-quality product support increased their use of the Daily Report module by 44% and their use of the Task module by 36%, even after the Customer Visit module was restored. As suggested in our data, a 10% increase in the use of the Daily Report module is associated with a 3.4% increase in contract size, and a 10% increase in the use of the Task module is associated with a 7.5% increase in contract size. The average contract size per customer is around \$2,000. Accordingly, providing high-quality product support during third-party disruptions could potentially lead to a 15% increase in contract size (\$300 in contract value per customer) for the ones interested in the Daily Report module and potentially lead to a 27% increase in contract size (\$540 in contract value per customer) for those interested in the Task module.

Columns (3) and (4) in Table 5 examine whether technical support that addresses customer-related issues had a similar effect. The coefficient estimate for High TechSupport × Treated × AfterR remains insignificant, and the estimated marginal effect for customers receiving high-quality customer support is insignificant as well. These results indicate that technical support during the disruption that gave customers instruction, helped them to configure their smartphones, or fulfilled requests for customization may not help to improve the customers’ perception about the quality of the first party’s services and maintain customers’ use of firstparty services in the long run.

As discussed earlier, unobserved factors could be correlated with getting high-quality technical support as well as with increased service use for customers in the treatment group, leading to a biased estimate of AfterR × Treated × HighTechSupport. However, because we observed a significant effect only for product support and not for customer support, this issue becomes less problematic; if an unobservable factor biases our estimates, it must be correlated with product support and customer support in a different way. Nevertheless, we next conducted a robustness check with an IV to further mitigate this concern. We first identified an exogenous variable that is correlated with HighTechSupport<sub>i</sub> for each of the two technical support categories (i.e., product support and customer support) but that is not correlated with customer i’s service use of the focal module in any other way. The variable we used is the average success rate of technical support, in the same category, that the first party’s technical support team provided to other customers during the disruption regarding modules unrelated to the three focal modules (i.e., Customer Visit, Daily Report, and Task) in terms of customer usage.

To meet the exclusion restriction requirement, the tech support quality should not be correlated with the use of a focal module in ways other than influencing the quality of tech support on the focal module. For example, if the use of a focal module is correlated with the use of another module, the tech support quality provided to the latter module might be correlated with the former (focal) module use because of the usage correlation between the two modules and thus, become no longer exogenous. Based on Table 3, the use of the three modules of interest (i.e., Customer Visit, Daily Report, and Task) is highly correlated because of the similar-goal substitute relationship but is unrelated to modules such as the Broadcast module, the Campaign module, and the Order module. ## 5.2. Plausible Mechanism: The Effect on

Customer Exploration of First-Party Services Third-party disruptions may cause customers to become very sensitive to the uncertainty of IT services offered by the first party. We argue that the first party’s technical support on product-related issues may be especially effective in improving customers’ confidence in the stability of first-party services and thus, in motivating customers to explore the first-party services. The literature suggests that this exploration is an important way for customers to develop a deeper understanding of the software product and to adapt their business process according to the complex enterprise solutions (e.g., Park et al. 2007, Albietz and Assimakopoulos 2014, Ye and Kankanhalli 2018). Thus, we speculate that highquality product support can lead to more exploration of first-party services during the disruption, thereby increasing long-term use. In this section, we provide some empirical evidence supporting this argument.

In particular, based on the sample of customers affected by the disruption, we seek to understand the relationship between the customer’s exploration behavior and technical support on product or customerrelated issues during the disruption period. Because of space limitations, the detailed empirical model, measures, and results are reported in Online Appendix F. Overall, we find that customers receiving high-quality product support explored both the Daily Report module and the Task module more than customers receiving low-quality support, but there is no strong relationship between the exploration behavior of customers and customer-related technical support. It is consistent with our speculations that high-quality product support encourages customers to explore more product features and that this exploration could lead to increased use of first-party services in the long run.

To provide some qualitative insights regarding the importance of product support, we conducted an openended interview with a product manager in the focal first-party firm.<sup>6</sup> In particular, he suggested that “if customers have any complaints about the product, the technical support team is our first contact that can calm them, help them, and guide them” and that “the technical support team can identify their needs and recommend to them the appropriate services or modules that suit their needs.” He also suggested exploration to be an important way to build long-term use of an IT module. He emphasized that “end users need to first famil iarize themselves with the functionalities of an IT module before they can establish continuous use.” These insights appear to be in line with our empirical findings.

We speculate that the effect of product support on customers’ exploration of first-party services is especially strong when it is coupled with the high uncertainty about the multivendor services that arises from the third-party disruption. In other words, during normal times, high-quality technical support may not play the same role as in the context we have observed here. To probe this possibility, we randomly selected one period before the disruption—from March 22, 2018 to June 5, 2018. For this period, we identified customers exploration activities as well as the technical support they received, and we implemented a similar analysis. The results are shown in Online Appendix F, Tables F3 and F4. Overall, we do not find that high-quality technical support led to more exploration, regardless of the specific support category (i.e., product support versus customer support). Note that this evidence does not suggest that technical support is unimportant. Instead, it highlights the importance of providing high-quality technical support, particularly on product-related issues, during the disruption. In this context, customers are more likely to be motivated to explore the firstparty services more and subsequently, to develop a long-term use habit.

## 6. Conclusion

The importance of multivendor enterprise computing services has been growing rapidly. The coexistence of a primary IT vendor (i.e., the first party) and third parties has enabled a wide range of advanced solutions for enterprise customers. However, such complex multivendor services have also presented many management challenges for primary IT vendors. Our study explores one such challenge involving the uncertainty perceived by customers because of disruptions caused by third parties. Using a third-party GPS tracking service disruption that an SaaS provider experienced, we empirically show that, on average, customers reduced their use of the disrupted multivendor service even after the service was restored. Meanwhile, customers affected by the disruption increased their use of the first-party services (i.e., the Daily Report module and the Task module), perhaps as a temporary solution during the disruption. However, they generally did not maintain this increased use in the long run, unless they received excellent technical support for product-related issues.

Our study adds to the IT services literature, which has studied the dyadic relationship between a customer and a vendor (e.g., Chen and Bharadwaj 2009, Mani et al. 2010). Our setting features a multisourcing environment in which an enterprise customer adopts a set of IT services, some of which are offered by multiple parties (e.g., Bhattacharya et al. 2018, Bapna et al. 2023). In addition to the coordination challenges emphasized in the literature (e.g., Bapna et al. 2010), enterprise customers often face greater service uncertainties because of the involvement of multiple parties. Our study shows that these uncertainties influenced their use of both the multivendor service and the first-party services both in the short run (i.e., during the disruption) and in the long run (i.e., after the service recovery). Nevertheless, first-party vendors potentially have a way to make the proverbial lemonade out of lemons. When they provide high-quality, product-related technical support, their customers may develop a more positive attitude and confidence in the first-party services and thereby, increase their exploration. Such exploration may help increase long-term use. In contrast to most studies in IT services, which focus on the IT vendor side and address issues such as monitoring, output verifiability for IT service, and the value chain in IT services (e.g., Bhattacharya et al. 2018, Gopal et al. 2020, Huang et al. 2021), we contribute by investigating the interactions between multiple IT vendors and customers.

Our study is also closely related to the service disruption and recovery literature (e.g., Maxham and Netemeyer 2002, Soares et al. 2014, Tan et al. 2016, Collier et al. 2017). These studies examine single-vendor settings (regardless of the number of services offered by the single vendor) and seek to understand how the vendor should handle the disruption and recovery to maintain or boost customer satisfaction. Meanwhile, this literature has mainly considered outcomes, such as customer satisfaction, repurchase intentions, and corporate image, using survey questions (e.g., De Matos et al. 2007). Our study represents an early attempt to analyze the effects of a disruption and recovery that are outside the primary vendor’s control in the short run. We analyzed how the disruption and recovery influence customers’ use of the disrupted (multivendor) service to understand the spillover effects on first-party services both during the disruption and after the recovery.

Third, our study adds to the body of literature on technical support in IT services. Most existing studies have focused on how to organize technical support work to improve organizational knowledge flow or productivity (e.g., Pentland 1992, Das 2003, Gray and Durcikova 2005, Durcikova et al. 2011); other work has considered learning and knowledge transfer mechanisms within the technical support team (e.g., Kim et al. 2012). Most relevant to our work is Retana et al. (2018), who study the effect of technical support on IT service use. However, in contrast to their study, we distinguish product-related technical support from customerrelated technical support and highlight that these two types of support can play different roles in shaping customers’ reactions to the uncertainty brought by thirdparty disruptions.

Our study also has several actionable implications for IT service providers and enterprise customers. For enterprise customers, they reduced their use of the multivendor service even after the service was fully restored, indicating that they may be risk averse and highly sensitive to the potential downtime caused by IT vendors. Because of the high uncertainty involved with third-party disruptions, our study stresses the importance of considering the possibility of third-party disruptions as enterprise customers negotiate service-level agreements with IT service providers. For the primary IT service provider, our results suggest that when a third-party disruption happens, if possible, it should help its customers to understand how existing IT services could serve as potential alternatives to achieve similar goals as the disrupted multivendor services. This would be a useful strategy to maintain customer satisfaction and prevent customer churn in the long run. Our study also calls for more research on appropriate contractual relationships between the first party and third parties. Meanwhile, our work highlights that technical support matters in mitigating the negative outcomes from third-party uncertainties and in motivating customers to explore new features and functionalities to meet their needs. In particular, product support efforts during third-party disruptions may reassure customers that the primary IT vendor still provides reliable products, thereby increasing long term use. Devoting enough resources to improving their technical support work could be strategically important.

We believe that these implications may be generalized to settings other than sales management. For example, in the healthcare industry, many healthcare organizations have been using a range of enterprise IT services jointly supported by the first party and third parties. Healthcare organizations may be particularly sensitive to any uncertainty brought by such multivendor arrangements, largely because of strict requirements on the security of patient data and on the stability of the IT services. As a result, we might expect any disruption in multivendor services in this setting to be more likely to motivate these organizational users to switch to first-party services that are presumably more stable and reliable.

Although we do not focus on firm structure change as the outcome variable in this current study, our results provide useful implications for a firm’s governance mode. TCE theory emphasizes that firms tend to choose the governance mode among the three that could minimize transaction costs. In industries that strongly emphasize the stability of IT services, such as financial services or healthcare providers, firms may choose to develop IT solutions in house (if they have the resources to do so) or develop IT solutions closely with a single party (i.e., the first party) to enable better coordination and joint actions. A coordinated solution with the first party could be encouraged by highquality product support, as it signals the reliability of the first party’s offerings.

One important question is whether the disruption, when combined with effective technical support, is necessary in motivating users to explore various features of first-party IT services and subsequently, to increase their use. We believe that the disruption made customers very sensitive to the uncertainty in using the IT services and that if the first party reassures customers about the stability and reliability of its own offerings, the sharp contrast can help customers to develop a more positive attitude toward the first-party service and influence use behavior. Future work might examine whether other major changes in the environment also can give the first party an opportunity to motivate customers’ deeper exploration of its IT services.

One of the important limitations of our research is our focus on a relatively long and major third-party disruption. Our results may not hold for third-party disruptions that last for only a short period of time (e.g., a few hours); in this case, customers may not have enough time or motivation to explore other available options. Understanding the effects of third-party service recovery speed on customers’ use of both multivendor services and first-party services is an interesting direction for future research. Relatedly, in our context, the third-party GPS service is one of the relatively essential features offered by the multivendor Customer Visit module. One interesting question for future research is whether customers would react similarly, regardless of the role of the third-party feature in supporting their business processes.
