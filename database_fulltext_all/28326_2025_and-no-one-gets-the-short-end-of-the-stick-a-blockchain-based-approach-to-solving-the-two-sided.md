# And No One Gets the Short End of the Stick: A Blockchain-Based Approach to Solving the Two-Sided Opportunism Problem in Interorganizational Information Sharing

Lukas Florian Bossler,<sup>a</sup> Arne Buchwald,<sup>b</sup> Kai Spohrer<sup>c,</sup>\*

Abstract. The threat of opportunistic behavior is an enduring problem in interorganizational information sharing based on sensitive data. The two sides of opportunism in interorganizational information sharing—information poaching by the information recipient and information manipulation by the information provider—may preclude beneficial information sharing relationships altogether. Previously proposed organizational and technical countermeasures against the threat of opportunism either fail to reliably prevent information poaching and manipulation or prevent only one of them at a time. To address these shortcomings, we develop three design principles for an information system that facilitates reli able information sharing based on sensitive data in interorganizational business transactions without revealing the actual data. We instantiate our design principles in a multicompany research consortium for wear-based leasing contracts for machine tools. Through in-depth interview sessions with business and technology experts, we demonstrate the efficacy of our artifact in addressing the two-sided opportunism problem. A survey-based evaluation of the artifact’s utility in the context of machine tool leasing shows that organizations are willing to engage in more interorganizational information sharing, rely more on shared information, and draw on more sensitive data when provided with our solution. The study makes three key contributions. First, it contributes to the literature on interorganizational information sharing by identifying information poaching and information manipulation as two sides of the same problem and by showing empirically that opportunities for beneficial business arise if both forms of opportunism are addressed simultaneously. Second, our design principles constitute a blueprint of a shared information system that enables reliable (i.e., verifiably truthful) information sharing between business partners without revealing the data underlying the shared information. Third, the study contributes to the literature on blockchain systems by recombining specific blockchain technology components, namely, private data collections, smart contracts, and joint governance, in a useful, novel way.

Keywords: information sharing • interorganizational information systems • opportunism • blockchain • poaching • manipulation confidentiality • design science • sensitive data • transaction cost economics

## 1. Introduction

The challenges of interorganizational information sharing have been discussed in information systems (IS) research for decades (Barrett and Konsynski 1982).

Although information sharing is widely believed to have beneficial effects (Zhou and Benton 2007, Kaipia et al. 2017, Clottey and Benton 2020), the potential for opportunistic behavior in business relationships—that is, selfish actions without considering adverse effects on other business partners (Williamson 1985)—hinders information sharing (Baiman and Rajan 2002, Lumineau and Oliveira 2020). Organizations may even refrain from beneficial business transactions that require information sharing if it is not possible or too costly to construct safeguards that protect them from opportunistic actions by business partners (Wathne and Heide 2000, Jap and Anderson 2003).

Opportunism in interorganizational information sharing generally takes two forms: information poaching, that is, the use of shared information for unintended purposes (Baiman and Rajan 2002, Clemons and Hitt 2004, Skowronski et al. 2020), and information manipulation, that is, the deliberate misrepresentation of information (Akerlof 1970, Williamson 1985). Information recipients fear information manipulation and therefore seek to prevent opportunistic actions by the information provider by examining the data underlying the shared information. Conversely, information providers fear information poaching and therefore seek to prevent opportunistic actions by the information recipient by keeping sensitive data undisclosed. Measures taken by one party to protect itself against opportunism, such as examining data to prevent information manipulation or keeping data undisclosed to prevent information poaching, are precisely what increases the risk of opportunism for the other party. Thus, when one party protects itself against opportunism, the other party can easily get the short end of the stick. Therefore, to prevent opportunistic behavior in interorganizational information sharing based on sensitive data, it is necessary to consider both sides of the twosided opportunism problem simultaneously.

Consider machine tool leasing, for example. Machine tool users often lease expensive machinery from lessors at fixed rates. Both lessors and machine tool users may be interested in more variable lease rates that adjust to the wear and tear of a machine tool as it is used. To assess the wear and tear of a machine tool, detailed usage data must be collected and analyzed. However, such usage data also provide insight into a machine tool user’s products and production processes. Consequently, the machine tool user may consider the usage data too sensitive to disclose, fearing intellectual property loss and data leaks (i.e., information poaching). Meanwhile, the lessor may consider the information about machine tool usage too unreliable, fearing that the machine tool user could manipulate the information to feign better machine conditions and obtain lower lease rates (i.e., information manipulation). Although both lessors and machine tool users could benefit from variable lease rates, their opportunism concerns may prevent the transaction. Thus, measures to facilitate the exchange would have to resolve their concerns about both information poaching and information manipulation.

Traditionally, organizational countermeasures have been used against both forms of opportunism in interorganizational relationships, including contractual governance (Dyer 1997, Lumineau et al. 2021), relational governance (Mayer et al. 1995, Lumineau et al. 2021), and trusted third-party involvement (Clemons and Hitt 2004, Du and Mao 2018). However, organizational measures are only partially effective. Although they prohibit and discourage opportunistic behavior, they cannot reliably prevent it (Lumineau et al. 2021). Scholars have therefore emphasized the need for technical measures that reliably prevent opportunistic behavior (Kumar and van Dissel 1996, Grover et al. 2002, Ackerman et al. 2016, Cho et al. 2017, Hyva¨rinen et al. 2017, Lumineau and Oliveira 2020, Lumineau et al. 2021). Prior work suggests preventing information poaching by implementing technology that hides sensitive data, enables the selective sharing of specific data, and facilitates the monitoring of data usage (Kumar and van Dissel 1996, Heller et al. 2004, Bader et al. 2018). Further, previous research suggests preventing information manipulation by implementing technology that creates data visibility, automates data exchange, and closely monitors data to be exchanged (Grover et al. 2002, Cho et al. 2017, Hyva¨rinen et al. 2017). However, automated information exchange, data visibility, and transparent data monitoring that prevent information manipulation inherently exclude simultaneous data hiding, selective data sharing, and data usage control that prevent information poaching. Thus, it seems virtually impossible to solve both sides of the opportunism problem through a simple combination of existing technical solutions for each side.

In this context, related research has recognized a need for novel technical solutions that facilitate reliable data processing without compromising confidentiality (Zyskind et al. 2015, Ackerman et al. 2016, Agahari et al. 2022). Conceptual solutions have been presented for data processing in national healthcare systems (Ackerman et al. 2016) and digital election systems (Zyskind et al. 2015), for example. However, these solutions rely on multiparty computation (e.g., Zyskind et al. 2015, Ackerman et al. 2016), which requires large network sizes and cannot be directly applied to interorganizational information sharing based on sensitive data, where networks are typically smaller and business partners are well-known (van den Broek and van Veenstra 2018, Susha et al. 2023). Thus, a solution that generally prevents information poaching and information manipulation in interorganizational information sharing based on sensitive data is still amiss. Such a solution would prevent both types of opportunism so that neither the information provider nor the information recipient would get the short end of the stick.

Against this backdrop, we investigate the following question:

How should an information system be designed to enable interorganizational information sharing based on sensitive data while simultaneously preventing both information poaching and information manipulation?

To answer this question, we employed the design science research methodology (DSRM) (Peffers et al. 2007). In particular, we designed, instantiated, demonstrated, and evaluated a blockchain (BC)-based solution with verifiable processing of confidentially stored data. Our solution enables reliable (i.e., verifiably truthful) information sharing between business partners without revealing the data that underlies the shared information.<sup>1</sup> The solution prevents both information poaching and manipulation in a shared IS. We instantiate the solution by recombining<sup>2</sup> existing blockchain technology components in novel ways—namely, private data collections (Ma et al. 2019), smart contracts (Lumineau et al. 2021), and joint governance (Beck et al. 2018).

Our study makes three key contributions. First, we contribute to the literature on interorganizational information sharing by conceptualizing information poaching and information manipulation as two sides of the same problem and by showing empirically that opportunities for beneficial business relationships arise when both are addressed together. This broadens the discourse on opportunism in interorganizational information sharing, which has treated them in isolation. The shift to a two-sided view helps to understand why some interorganizational information sharing relationships are difficult to establish or fail. It calls for new theoretica frameworks and empirical investigations to better understand their interdependence and its consequences. Second, we derive design principles for an IS that enables information sharing between business partners without the threats of information manipulation and information poaching. We demonstrate the efficacy and utility of our design principles through in-depth interviews and a survey with information providers and information recipients. The design principles go beyond existing organizational and technical measures against information poaching and manipulation in preventing both forms of opportunism at the same time. The introduction and use of IS that implement these design principles may significantly influence power dynamics in interorganizational relationships. Third, we contribute to the literature on blockchain systems by recombining private data collections, smart contracts, and joint governance in a novel, unconventional way. Our solution provides confidentiality for the information provider and verifiably correct information for the information recipient. This approach can be applied to other challenges of data confidentiality on blockchain networks and may be able to solve related problems where blockchain technology was previously considered inadequate.

## 2. Opportunism in Interorganizational Information Sharing

In the following sections, we elaborate on the forms of opportunism in interorganizational information sharing and review previously proposed organizational and technical countermeasures. By identifying the limitations of existing countermeasures, we underscore the need for a technical solution that enables reliable interorganizational information sharing based on sensitive data.

## 2.1. Opportunistic Behavior

Interorganizational information sharing is often sought to improve processes (Zhou and Benton 2007, Kaipia et al. 2017), enhance product quality (Paulraj et al. 2008), and support product development (Corsten et al. 2011). It can help reduce costs (Clottey and Benton 2020) and increase profits (Demirkan et al. 2010). However, the threat of opportunistic behavior by business partners can hinder interorganizational information sharing (Baiman and Rajan 2002, Lumineau and Oliveira 2020). Opportunism refers to “self-interest seeking with guile” (Williamson 1993, p. 97); it is a central assumption of transaction cost economics and goes beyond an economic agent’s self-interest (Lumineau and Oliveira 2020). Specifically, the notion of guile entails selfish behavior without regard to the adverse effects on business partners (Das and Rahman 2010). Opportunism takes two forms in interorganizational information sharing: information poaching (Baiman and Rajan 2002, Clemons and Hitt 2004, Skowronski et al. 2020) and information manipulation (Akerlof 1970, Williamson 1985, Wathne and Heide 2000).<sup>3</sup>

Information poaching refers to the use of received information for purposes outside the scope of the intended relationship in order to realize benefits that may come at the expense of the information provider (Clemons and Hitt 2004). An organization that provides sensitive data to a business partner exposes itself to considerable risk. Because information is a nonrival good, other organizations cannot be excluded from using it once they have access to it. Although this may not be a problem in the case of nonsensitive data, information poaching can cause serious damage in the case of sensitive, confidential information (Frazier et al. 2009). Even if the rights of information use are clearly negotiated, the information recipient may still use received information in an unauthorized manner (Mathew and Chen 2013) because misuse of information is often difficult or even impossible to monitor (Baiman and Rajan 2002).

Information manipulation refers to the “incomplete or distorted disclosure of information, especially to calculated efforts to mislead, distort, disguise, obfuscate, or otherwise confuse” (Williamson 1985, p. 47). Information manipulation includes blatantly lying about one’s capabilities or behavior in order to induce the business partner to take actions that result in self-serving benefits. It also includes both misrepresentation and withholding of information (Wathne and Heide 2000). Especially low-power parties in interorganizational relationships often resort to information manipulation because of unfavorable conditions and a lack of options for withdrawal (Frazier et al. 2009). If information is asymmetrically distributed, it can be difficult for the information recipient to assess the truthfulness of the information received. Information poaching and manipulation have often been addressed but usually in isolation from each other. ## 2.2. Mitigation Strategies

The threat of opportunism has implications for the ability of organizations to transact. Organizations that engage in interorganizational information sharing must protect themselves from opportunistic actions by other parties (Jap and Anderson 2003, Miranda and Kavan 2005). Protective measures against opportunism may be too costly and thus prevent business transactions altogether (Wathne and Heide 2000). Traditionally, opportunism has been addressed through organizational measures such as contractual governance mechanisms (Dyer 1997, Miranda and Kavan 2005, Lumineau et al. 2021), relational governance mechanisms (Mayer et al. 1995, Lumineau et al. 2021), and the inclusion of trusted third parties (Lumineau and Que´lin 2012, Du and Mao 2018). Contractual governance mechanisms aim to formalize the terms of interorganizational information sharing by explicitly specifying the conditions for information storage, processing, exchange, and use (Dyer 1997, Lumineau et al. 2021). They may include legally enforceable sanctions and penalties aimed at curbing opportunistic behavior (Lumineau and Que´lin 2012). Another way to address opportunistic behavior through contractual governance is through risk premiums that preemptively compensate for potential harm (Dyer 1997, Clarkson et al. 2007). Relational governance mechanisms emphasize the social relationships between business partners through mechanisms such as trust (Mayer et al. 1995, Huber et al. 2013), relational norms (Mathew and Chen 2013), identification with the business partner (Corsten et al. 2011), and reputation (Trkman and Desouza 2012). Relational governance mechanisms reduce opportunism concerns by focusing on good conduct and the potential value of future interactions, either with the same business partner or with other organizations in the same business network (Lumineau et al. 2021). Trusted third parties can act as a control instance or as an intermediary. As control instances, third parties can independently oversee the fulfillment of contract terms as long as the agreed outcomes are observable (Clemons and Hitt 2004, Lumineau and Que´lin 2012). As intermediaries, third parties actively participate in interorganizational relationships and facilitate the exchange of information between distrustful parties (Du and Mao 2018). A more comprehen sive review of prior studies and their mechanisms can be found in Online Appendix A.

Although some organizational measures address both information poaching and manipulation, no organizational measure can reliably prevent them (Baiman and Rajan 2002, Mathew and Chen 2013). Contractual sanctions and relational governance mechanisms can lead to punishment only after an opportunistic behavior has been detected (Baiman and Rajan 2002). Risk premiums are an inefficient hedge that increases transaction costs for the participants but cannot reliably prevent opportunism (Dyer 1997, Clarkson et al. 2007). Third parties receive information, thereby introducing the threat of information poaching themselves, and are often unable to detect opportunistic behavior when it occurs inconspicuously (Chanson et al. 2019).

In contrast to organizational mechanisms, technical solutions may be able to enforce nonopportunistic behavior from participants by providing an environment that automatically processes and manages information exchanges (Grover et al. 2002). However, previously proposed IS for interorganizational information sharing based on sensitive data can only prevent either information poaching or manipulation. On the one hand, existing technical solutions designed to prevent information manipulation rely on transparency for monitoring and automating transactions. For example, Cho et al. (2017) propose to increase information transparency in supply chain relationships by sharing key information such as production schedules, demand planning data, and inventory levels. More recently, scholars have proposed blockchain technology as an effective solution for preventing information manipulation, leveraging transparent, immutable, append-only data storage (Risius and Spohrer 2017, Mueller-Bloch et al. 2022) and a reliable computation system that automatically enforces smart contracts (Lockl et al. 2020, Beverungen et al. 2021, Lumineau et al. 2021). For example, Hyva¨rinen et al. (2017) propose to exchange tax data via a transnational blockchain to eliminate tax fraud. Although these approaches might protect an information recipient from information manipulation, their transparency and detailed monitoring enable the extraction of information and, thereby, information poaching. Prior work acknowledges that the “transparency commonly associated with blockchain is an unacceptable property in a context involving highly sensitive data” (Hyva¨rinen et al. 2017, p. 445). For example, a buyer in a transparency-focused supply chain system could use insights into a supplier’s high inventory levels as an indicator of sales difficulties and attempt to use this information.

On the other hand, existing technical solutions designed to prevent information poaching rely heavily on access controls governed by the information provider (Kumar and van Dissel 1996), so only data explicitly approved by the information provider are accessible to the information recipient, whereas all other data remain hidden (Heller et al. 2004). Although these technical approaches can prevent information poaching, they also allow the information provider to hide and manipulate data. Several approaches have been proposed using blockchain technology to keep sensitive data confidential from third parties and grant access only to legitimate business partners (e.g., Ayoade et al. 2018, Chanson et al. 2019, Lockl et al. 2020). For example, Bader et al. (2018) propose encrypting sensitive car insurance data on a blockchain system and distributing decryption keys to authorized parties such as insurers, car holders, and courts. In those approaches, however, the information recipient can still poach information once the information provider has decided to share sensitive data.

In principle, if one party can impose on the other an interorganizational IS that solves only its side of the two-sided opportunism problem, then the other party is at risk for opportunism. An IS in an interorganizational relationship is no neutral entity but a material manifestation of the existing dynamics of the interorganizational relationship, often placing control of the system in the hands of one party (Hart and Saunders 1997, Cho et al. 2017). Because the party in control of the system can change the system rules and data processing at will, perhaps even without notifying the other side, that party can act opportunistically at any time.

Multiparty computation has been mentioned as another technical approach in related data confidentiality and manipulation contexts such as elections (Zyskind et al. 2015) and healthcare (Ackerman et al. 2016). Multiparty computation (1) partitions and distributes confidential data among the actors in a network so that no actor has access to the full set of confidential data, and (2) computes aggregated results for an information recipient. Multiparty computation is naturally suited to large networks with many actors, such as a national healthcare system or digital voting systems, because the size of the network determines the amount of distrib uted confidential data stored at each individual actor. In small networks such as interorganizational networks of a few companies, the amount of sensitive data shared with the information recipient would become large and may still allow for information poaching. In addition, depending on the sensitivity of the data, some information providers may refuse to transmit and distribute their data to other actors altogether, no matter how small a fraction each actor receives (Agahari et al. 2022). Thus, multiparty computation is not viable within smal ler networks, which are characteristic of interorganizational information sharing based on sensitive data (van den Broek and van Veenstra 2018, Susha et al. 2023).

Moreover, combinations of technical and organizational measures might mitigate the two-sided problem by technically preventing either information manipulation or information poaching and organizationally disincentivizing the other. For example, the usage of a technical system may be contractually defined and technically monitored so that misbehavior can be sanctioned, or a trusted third party may run a system that prevents either information manipulation or poaching. Although such combinations of technical and organiza tional measures may address both sides of the opportunism problem to some degree, they are imperfect in that they still carry substantial threats of opportunism (e.g., potential opportunism of the third party operating a technical system) while being costly, complex, and cumbersome (Lumineau et al. 2021).

In sum, prior work has proposed several potential organizational and technical countermeasures against opportunistic behavior, but none solve both sides of the two-sided opportunism problem in interorganizational information sharing based on sensitive data. Existing technical solutions primarily address only one problem— either information poaching or information manipulation. The isolated solutions to one of the problems cannot be combined with solutions to the other because automated information exchange, data visibility, and transparent data monitoring that prevent information manipulation are inherently incompatible with data hiding, selective data sharing, and data usage control that prevent information poaching.

## 3. Method

Against this backdrop, we employed the DSRM (Peffers et al. 2007) to produce theoretically grounded and empirically validated design principles for an IS in which neither information poaching nor information manipulation constitutes a threat. Following the DSRM as specified by Peffers et al. (2007), our research process encompassed four key steps: (1) problem identification and derivation of design principles, (2) artifact instantiation in a consortium for machine tool leasing, (3) demonstration of the solution efficacy, and (4) evaluation of the solution utility (Table 2).

Drawing on the existing literature, we first identified interorganizational information sharing based on sensitive data as a two-sided opportunism problem. This perspective allowed us to see that research had suggested countermeasures to information poaching and information manipulation in interorganizational information sharing, but no solution solved both sides of the problem. Thus, we defined our theoretically and practically relevant objective as providing a blueprint of an IS that fulfills two design requirements (DRs): (1) preventing information manipulation and (2) preventing information poaching inside the system. We then derived three<sup>4</sup> design principles using the literature and intensive discussions in the author team to fulfill those requirements. In Section 4.1, we explain how these design principles prevent both information poaching and information manipulation in an IS shared between the information provider and the information recipient.

In a second step, we became part of a research consortium that engaged in the development and provision of data-driven leasing models for machine tools (Section 4.2 provides details). This consortium served as a fitting instantiation context for our design principles. Through a series of workshops and meetings, we immersed ourselves in the domain and developed an understanding of the opportunism concerns present in machine tool leasing. Specifically, meetings with machine manufacturers, machine tool users, lessors, and component manufacturers highlighted data ownership conflicts and surfaced concerns among the consortium members where one actor’s interest in owning sensitive data (e.g., sensor data about the usage, wear, and tear of machine tools) created tensions with other actors’ interest in deriving information from that data (e.g., lessor’s desire to calculate lease rates based on reliable information about the wear and tear of a machine tool). The opportunism threats described by lessors and machine tool users closely matched our design principles (DPs) (see Step 2b in Table 2). Thus, we concluded that implementing a technological instantiation of our design principles in this context would allow us to assess the efficacy and utility of our approach and solve a practically relevant problem. We developed and implemented an IS based on blockchain technology that instantiated our design principles to facilitate wear-based leasing contracts between machine tool users and lessors. In Section 4.3, we describe the implementation of the artifact and how it instantiates our design principles.

The DSRM encompasses two distinct empirical activities, demonstration and evaluation, which assess the efficacy and the utility of a proposed solution, respectively (Peffers et al. 2007, Tuunanen and Peffers 2018). Accordingly, we next demonstrated the efficacy of our artifact, showing that it works and fulfills its intended purpose in a particular situation, thereby providing a proof of concept. We assessed the efficacy of our design principles using a prototypical artifact implementation in the consortium through semistructured interviews. As shown in Section 5, interviews with domain experts from inside and outside the consortium show that our solution successfully addresses the problems of infor mation poaching and information manipulation in the context of wear-based leasing contracts. Interviews with professional blockchain experts attested to the novelty and technological validity of our solution.

In a final step, elaborated in Section 6, we evaluated the utility of our solution, showing that our design has merit across a broader range of related contexts, thereby providing a proof of value (Peffers et al. 2018, Nguyen et al. 2021). To do so, we conducted a vignettebased survey of lessors and lessees of machine tools (i.e., machine tool users) to assess the utility of our solution in the broader manufacturing industry. The results suggest that our solution (1) increases information providers’ intention to provide more sensitive data in an information sharing relationship, and (2) increases information recipients’ willingness to rely on information provided through our solution.

## 4. Design and Development

Aiming for a technical solution that prevents both information poaching and information manipulation, we followed a conceptually simple but powerful idea: we were to create an IS that allows business partners to share verifiably correct information derived from sensitive data without ever revealing the data that underlies the information (Ackerman et al. 2016).

4.1.1. Data Storage. To prevent information poaching (Clemons and Hitt 2004), the solution must guarantee that the information recipient does not have access to the raw, sensitive data of the information provider. However, hiding the data from the information recipient also prevents the recipient from verifying that the stored data are unaltered and thus introduces the possibility of information manipulation (Cho et al. 2017). To prevent information manipulation, a solution must also ensure that the information provider is not able to modify the data while it is stored in the IS (Chanson et al. 2019, Mueller-Bloch et al. 2022). Thus, a proof of integrity is required to ensure that confidentially stored sensitive data have not been manipulated. Such a proof of integrity might take the form of providing the information recipient with an automatically calculated checksum or a hash value of the confidentially stored data. Through this proof of integrity, the information recipient becomes aware of any change to the confidentially stored, sensitive data without directly accessing it (Chanson et al. 2019, Ma et al. 2019). To summarize, pro tection against information poaching and information manipulation during data storage requires that sensitive data be inaccessible to the information recipient while a reliable proof of data integrity must be provided. Thus, we derive the following design principle:

DP1. Store the sensitive data in a manipulation-resistant storage exclusively with the information provider and instantly create a manipulation-resistant proof of integrity that is available to the information recipient.

4.1.2. Data Processing. To derive meaningful information from sensitive data that can be shared with the information recipient, the confidentially stored data need to be processed. Such processing may include aggregation, reduction, or refinement of data to compute the information of interest. To prevent information poaching, the processing must not involve any operation that discloses the data to the information recipient, directly or indirectly. Thus, data processing must not be done by the information recipient. The shared informa tion should be both context-specific and abstract and should not be usable for purposes other than those agreed upon between the information provider and recipient. Furthermore, the information recipient must not be able to derive the underlying sensitive data from the computation results, that is, from the shared information. To this end, we propose the use of nonreversible one-way functions (Lamport 1979) in the computation of shared information from sensitive data.

To prevent information manipulation, a solution must also ensure that the information provider is unable to influence the data processing. The data processing therefore must be predefined by both information provider and recipient and should be executed in a deterministic manner (Beverungen et al. 2021, Lumineau et al. 2021). To this end, a solution should compute shared information from confidentially stored data in an independent, shared IS rather than on the systems of one of the actors. This shared IS needs to reliably execute fixed routines for data processing (Lumineau et al. 2021). It must be able to access and process the confidentially stored data through nonreversible functions (Lamport 1979) accepted by both parties. To summarize, achieving protection from both information poaching and information manipulation during data processing requires the computation of abstract, context-specific information from the sensitive data via predefined, deterministic functions that are executed reliably and independently of the actors. Thus, we derive the design principle:

DP2. Utilize nonreversible functions that are reliably and independently executed to compute shared information from the confidentially stored sensitive data.

4.1.3. System Governance. Moreover, no single party should be able to alter the means of doing business in the information sharing relationship by altering how the data are processed. To prevent information poaching, unilateral changes to the code that executes the nonreversible functions must be prevented because they could allow access to confidential data. To prevent information manipulation, unilateral changes to the code that executes the nonreversible functions must be prevented because they could allow altering the computed shared information. Therefore, a solution must always ensure both parties’ approval of any changes in the functions that compute shared information from confidential data (Beck et al. 2018, Bakos et al. 2021, Mueller-Bloch et al. 2022). Thus, we derive a final design principle:

DP3. Require joint approval for any changes in the mechanisms that compute shared information.

## 4.2. Instantiation Context

We instantiated our artifact in the context of leasing contracts for machine tools. We did so as part of a publicly funded research consortium that included machine tool users (lessees), machine manufacturers, lessors, tool and component manufacturers, a sensor manufacturer, a consulting agency, and two universities. The goal of the consortium was to provide machine tools and capital equipment to small- and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) at an affordable cost. Typically, SMEs do not have the financial resources to afford complex, large machine tools. As a result, they often enter into a lease agreement with a machine manufacturer or an independent financial institution. However, basic leasing contracts with a flat monthly fee incentivize machine tool users to maximize value by maximizing machine usage, which often leads to machine overuse and exponentially increased attrition. Lessors, therefore, introduce risk premiums to cover value losses resulting from excessive machine usage. Machine overuse and the corresponding reactions by lessors increase the transaction costs of leasing contracts. Although innovative leasing models, such as pay per use, have become more common in recent years, these models do not account for the complexity of machine operation. Adequate leasing models must account for the individual wear and tear of production processes. The consortium aimed to facilitate such variable leasing models by measuring machine usage with data from sensors in machine tools, enabling lessors to derive information on machine attrition to charge the according lease rates.

Within this consortium, two of the authors (as part of one of the two participating universities) were responsible for (1) recording and analyzing the stakeholders requirements from business and technology perspectives, and (2) developing and prototyping a data management solution for machine-related data between machine tool users, lessors, and machine manufac turers. Ownership conflicts and opportunism concerns between machine tool users, lessors, and machine manufacturers were abundant. On the one hand, machine tool users feared that the sensitive machine tool data would allow insights into their products and processes, potentially leading them to lose their competitive edge and intellectual property to competitors. At present, such fears already drive machine tool users to completely deny access to their machines, even for machine manufacturers during maintenance tasks. Moreover, machine tool users feared that usage data indicating poor company performance could exclude them from future leasing contracts. We found that machine tool users approved of neither the direct sharing of sensitive data (such as system and sensor data related to the tools used, feed rates, or cutting widths), which matched our DP1, nor the possibility of deriving additional insights from the information they would be willing to share, which matched our DP2. On the other hand, lessors and machine manufacturers feared that they would receive incorrect machine usage data and a lack of transparency regarding machine usage. We found that lessors required assurance that the provided data had not been altered, which matched our DP1, and certainty that they could rely on the information on machine attrition to charge according lease rates, which matched our DP2. More over, both sides wanted to take part in any decision regarding data access or lease rate calculation, fearing opportunistic actions because of power imbalances and a lack of control over the shared IS. These points matched our DP3. Overall, the concerns signaled not only the need to protect the sensitive sensor data from outside access but also that various parties in the consortium were concerned about opportunistic actions by their business partners. Thus, the case of wear-based leasing contracts for machine tools provided a promising context for instantiating our design principles in a practical context.

Our engagement with the consortium allowed us to deeply immerse ourselves within an application context in which we were able to identify an exemplary case of a two-sided opportunism problem in information sharing—namely, machine tool users fearing information poaching and lessors fearing information manipulation in wear-based leasing contracts. The rich and long-lasting cooperation with the consortium allowed us (1) to gain deep insights into the application domain, (2) to develop a proof of concept for the consortium, and (3) to have our developed proof of concept verified by domain experts who were intimately familiar with the problem and our proposed solution. Since the consortium’s inception in July 2018, one and sometimes two authors attended consortium status meetings every fortnight and multiple full consortium meetings spanning a full day every three months. More than 40 meetings were held with individual consortium partners between 2018 and 2021. Overall, this resulted in privileged access to an application domain where we could instantiate our design principles in an industry context.

## 4.3. Artifact Instantiation

Our artifact instantiated the design principles to facilitate information sharing between machine tool users and lessors based on confidential sensor data and derived information about machine tool wear and tear. Investigating potential technologies to implement our design principles, blockchain technology surfaced as a key technology with great potential because prior research emphasizes several characteristics of blockchain systems that resonate with our design principles. Blockchain systems feature manipulation-resistant storage (relating to DP1) (Chanson et al. 2019), allow for the deployment of smart contracts that establish fixed routines for data processing in the form of reliably executed programs (relating to DP2) (Lumineau et al. 2021), and are governed by their network members (relating to DP3) (Mueller-Bloch et al. 2022).

We decided to use Hyperledger Fabric 2.0 as the technological foundation because of its advanced confidentiality features, which provide granular access control (Siegfried et al. 2020). We evaluated existing confidentiality mechanisms of blockchain systems that could potentially help protect sensitive data and allow us to implement our design principles. We found that none of the established mechanisms supported our design principles without modification. Online Appendix B provides details on this analysis.

To achieve our goals, we recombined private data collections, smart contracts, and joint governance in a novel and unconventional way. We first set up a Hyperledger Fabric network on which the machine tool user and the lessor each operated a peer node. These peer nodes were responsible for executing and validating transactions, including smart contracts, as well as maintaining the distributed ledger and the world state (Guggenber ger et al. 2020). Furthermore, we set up orderer nodes that ordered network transactions and performed access control duties for channels. Using the channel architecture, we created a channel called StressChannel between the peer nodes of the machine tool user (acting as information provider) and the lessor (acting as information recipient) that facilitated data exchange between the channel members and provided a private ledger to store the exchanged data. We then set up a web server to provide a prototypical application programming interface for sensors and front-end components, utiliz ing the gRPC remote procedure call protocol to connect to the blockchain.

On top of this network structure, we instantiated our design principles, as summarized in Table 3. Addressing DP1, we created a private data collection and modified its usage by restricting the access rights exclusively to the information provider and a smart contract. Previously, private data collections have only been used as joint data storages for multiple members of a channel, that is, for data sharing between some but not all parties in the blockchain system (Ma et al. 2019, Guggenberger et al. 2020). Intrinsically, private data collections store a public hash of the private data on-chain. As soon as sensor data were stored in the private data collection, a hash of the data was generated and shared as a continuous proof of integrity. If the privately stored data were to be altered, this would result in an endorsement failure based on the public hash available to the information recipient. Thus, the information recipient would immediately be informed in case the data were tampered with. This speci fic way of using private data collections consequently granted protection against information manipulation as well as information poaching.

Addressing DP2, we deployed a smart contract to the shared channel. This smart contract established a fixed routine on how to calculate a context-specific piece of information. Specifically, other consortium members (the other university and the machine manufacturers) worked on defining a nonreversible function that calculated a so-called stress factor from the sensor data, an aggregate metric to adequately judge machine tool wear and tear for wear-based leasing contracts. In our prototype, we used a simplified formula to calculate the stress factor. The smart contract read the sensitive sensor data from the private data collection, processed it according to the formula that had been approved by both the machine tool user and the lessor, and stored the calculated stress factor in the ledger of the shared channel so that it became available to both parties.

Adding to this, the interviews with blockchain experts substantiated the novelty and validity of our technological solution.

## 6. Evaluation: Proof of Value

To evaluate the utility of our solution in a wider range of contexts (Nunamaker et al. 2015, Peffers et al. 2018, Tuunanen et al. 2024), we conducted a vignette-based survey in the broader manufacturing industry with machine tool users and lessors. Drawing on two vignettes in the context of wear-based leasing contracts for machine tools, we evaluated whether access to an IS that instantiates our design principles facilitates new information sharing relationships by reducing machine tool users’ precautions taken against information poaching and lessors’ precautions taken against information manipulation. To do so, we examined their information sharing intentions, specifically (1) machine tool users’ willingness to share more sensitive data, and (2) lessors’ reliance on the accuracy of the information provided. This wider-range study with independent practitioners allowed us to collect evidence for the utility of our solution in bilateral information exchanges in machine tool leasing.

Following Venable et al. (2016), we favored this artificial evaluation strategy over naturalistic evaluation strategies for two primary reasons. First, our artifact is primarily a technical solution that does not rely heavily on user interactions. Therefore, it does not necessarily require a naturalistic evaluation with end users. Second, artifacts that instantiate our design principles cannot be implemented quickly in many different contexts. Instead, the implementation of our solution depends on the strategic business decision to introduce such a system for sharing information derived from sensitive data (e.g., machine tool data) and on the interdependent decision of a business partner to provide business services based on such information (e.g., wear-based leasing contracts). Thus, implementations require decisions at the executive level as well as interorganizational implementation projects. Access to potential naturalistic evaluation environments is, therefore, very limited. In this case, an artificial evaluation strategy is appropriate (Venable et al. 2016). A vignette-based survey allowed us to reach many different companies to gain insight into diverse contexts. The survey respondents complemented the feedback gathered from the consortium members during the demonstration phase with their different backgrounds and organizational contexts.

## 6.1. Vignettes and Procedure

The survey started with a baseline vignette that introduced the respondents to the concept of wear-based leasing contracts (Online Appendix E). Following this vignette, the respondents answered questions about the general attractiveness of this type of business transaction for their company. Next, a vignette presented a more detailed wear-based leasing scenario in which the respondent’s company could potentially face threats of opportunism from the business partner in the form of information poaching or information manipulation (scenario 1). Respondents then answered questions about their information sharing intentions (information providers) and information reliance intentions (information recipients) based on this scenario. Next, a vignette presented a scenario of wear-based leasing facilitated by an IS that implements our design principles (scenario 2). We included DP1 to DP3 in the scenario description, explaining that (1) machine-generated data are directly stored in the blockchain system but not accessible to the lessor, (2) the lessor receives reliably calculated information based on the data, and (3) any changes to the blockchain system would require joint approval. Respondents then answered questions about their information sharing and information reliance intentions based on this scenario, as well as controls and demographics. Online Appendix E displays the survey instrument, including vignettes and measurement items.

## 6.2. Participants

Given the strategic importance of sensitive data, the technical specificity of machine tool data, and the financial intricacies of lease agreements, a very limited set of decision makers constituted appropriate respondents for our survey. Respondents needed technical expertise in machine tools as well as executive business knowledge about finance and interorganizational information sharing in their organization. Because we aimed for reliable statements about how organizations would alter their information sharing and information reliance behavior with or without a system implementing our design principles, high-level executives were, in most cases, the best possible respondents who could credibly speak for their entire organization. Our sampling frames consisted of the 419 organizations registered as machine tool users on a major manufacturing platform for Austria, Germany, and Switzerland in 2022 and the 92 member organizations of the Association of German Leasing Companies registered as lessors related to machine tools. For each organization, we searched for online profiles of executives on the organization’s website, the web, and two professional social networks (LinkedIn, Xing). For lessors, we identified those executive profiles that appeared to have the most influence on the organization’s leasing business models (especially managing directors and board members) and could influence strategic interactions of the lessor with machine tool users (especially department managers, sales managers, key account managers focused on the manufacturing industry). For machine tool users, we identified those executive profiles that appeared to have the most business insight into machine tool financing while being aware of the data sensitivity of production processes (especially production department/division managers, managing directors, and board members). We emailed the executives and invited them to participate in our vignette-based online survey. Where possible, we followed up the invitation by phone and mail. In our communications, we emphasized that respondents needed to be able to discuss machine tool financing and machine tool wear data

We discarded 22 responses from respondents who did not complete the survey or completed it in a very short time. The exclusions resulted in a sample of 85 machine tool users and 77 lessors. Table 5 shows the occupational distribution of the respondents in the final sample. The respondents had substantial professional experience as evidenced by their years of tenure in their company (mean: 13.8, standard deviation (SD): 9.5 for lessors; mean: 15.2, SD: 10.7 for machine tool users) and industry (mean: 22.2, SD: 9.3 for lessors; mean: 20.5, SD: 9.8 for machine tool users), allowing them to make credible statements about their companies’ most likely actions. Substantial proportions of the machine tool users worked in metal fabrication (44%), machinery and equipment manufacturing (14%), and motor vehicle manufacturing (11%). No other industry accounted for more than 6% of the sample. In spite of this imbalance, tests for industrylevel effects in our results remained insignificant.

## 6.3. Measurement and Analysis

Where possible, we relied on established and validated scales to measure constructs, modifying them only where necessary to fit the specific research context. The measurement scales can be found in Online Appendix E. The survey was administered in German. All measurement items and vignettes were translated into German using DeepL,<sup>5</sup> with one author leading and the other authors reverse translating and checking for consistency over multiple rounds. Where not indicated otherwise, items were measured with multiple items on a seven-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree).

6.3.1. Information Sharing Intentions. For information providers (i.e., machine tool users in the context of our study), we measured how willingly they intended to share machine tool data with the business partner or the blockchain system, differentiating by the amount of shared data and the sensitivity of the data. To properly capture data sensitivity, we consulted with two experi enced sensor data experts from the research consortium—a data expert from a sensor manufacturer and a mechanical engineering researcher from a technical university. We pretested the instrument qualitatively in 13 interviews within the consortium and found it adequately captured the sensitivity as perceived by the interviewees. We arrived at 12 types of machine tool data that belong to two classes of data sensitivity (six types each, see Online Appendix E). Low-sensitivity data (e.g., operating hours and temperature) reveal little information about the parts produced and the operations performed. However, it is possible to capture roughly how much and how intensively the machine is used. High-sensitivity data (e.g., CNC code, feed rate, and workpiece material) allow for a more accurate assessment of machine tool wear and tear but potentially reveal details about the production processes and the goods produced. Large collections of high-sensitivity data may ultimately serve to reengineer production processes.

6.3.2. Information Reliance Intentions. For information recipients (i.e., lessors and machine manufacturers in the context of our study), we adapted a scale from Dennis (1996) and Wixom and Todd (2005) to measure how willingly information recipients relied on the accuracy of the information provided to them. Upon interview-based pretesting of the scale, we adapted the general phrasing of the original items (“the information others contributed”) to a more vignette-specific version (“the stress factor provided by our lessee”). The final construct measured how likely lessors were to rely on the stress factor provided to them. We compared lessors’ reliance intentions in case the information was provided by (1) the machine tool user, or (2) an IS that implements our design principles. The comparison allowed us to understand their intentions to engage in an information sharing relationship with a business partner under (1) provision of the information by the business partner versus (2) provision of the information by an IS that implements our design principles.

6.3.3. Controls. To ensure that it is indeed the design of our solution that leads to information sharing and reliance on shared information rather than the feasibility of wear-based leasing contracts or undue trust in blockchain technology, we controlled for relevant variables. Specifically, we controlled for the general attractiveness of wear-based leasing contracts as a business transaction (adapted from Nicolaou and McKnight 2006) and the perceived competence of an organization’s IT department (adapted from Lu and Ramamurthy 2011). We also controlled for overly high (i.e., hyped) expectations of blockchain technology. We developed the latter scale in an iterative process among the authors, based on anecdotal reports of overly high expectations about the impact and applicability of blockchain technology. We pretested the scale with 13 interviewees from the consortium.

The correlations and significance levels remained stable. ## 6.4. Results

Tables 7 and 8 display the results regarding the effects of an IS that implements our design principles on the information sharing intention of machine tool users (Table 7) and the information reliance intention of lessors (Table 8). The results show that the availability of a system that implements our design principles increases the information sharing intentions of machine tool users, both for low-sensitivity data and high-sensitivity data. In fact, the effect on respondents’ willingness to share high-sensitivity data (main effect model HS3: p � 0.002; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.13, 0.59) is more pronounced than the effect on low-sensitivity data (main effect model LS3: p � 0.026; 95% CI: 0.02, 0.35).

Likewise, lessors were more willing to rely on the accuracy of the provided information when receiving the information from an IS that implemented our design principles (main effect model IRI3: p < 0.001; 95% CI: 0.53, 1.04). The AIC and BIC values show that including the presence of an IS that implements our design principles in the analysis improves the explanatory quality of the models and reduces model deviance in all analyses. Thus, our survey results suggest that access to an IS that instantiates our design principles facilitates information sharing relationships. In particular, it reduced machine tool users’ precautions taken against information poach ing (in the form of not sharing sensitive data) and lessors precautions taken against information manipulation (in the form of not relying on provided information). Overall, these results demonstrate the utility of our solution in a variety of organizational contexts. More generally, they suggest that opportunities for beneficial business relationships arise when information manipulation and information poaching are addressed simultaneously.

Finally, examining the robustness of our results, we followed Frank et al. (2013) and Venkatesh et al. (2020, 2023) to calculate the impact threshold of a confounding variable (ITCV) and the robustness of the inferences to replacement. To invalidate our analysis of the effects of a system implementing our design principles, 37.95% of the observations in information providers and 67.49% of the observations in information recipients would have to be replaced with cases for which the effect is zero. This lends support to the robustness of our results.

## 7. Discussion

In this study, we designed, instantiated, demonstrated, and evaluated an IS that prevents both information poaching and information manipulation in interorganizational information sharing. The results have implications for the research on interorganizational information sharing, opportunism, and blockchain systems. In the following, we elaborate on the key contributions of this study as well as its limitations.

## 7.1. Contribution

The study provides three key contributions. First, at a conceptual level, we contribute to the literature on interorganizational information sharing by identifying information poaching and information manipulation as two sides of the same problem and showing empirically that opportunities for beneficial business arise if both forms of opportunism are addressed simultaneously. In doing so, we broaden the discourse on opportunism in interorganizational information sharing, which has long emphasized the importance of each side of opportunism in isolation (Williamson 1985, Clemons and Hitt 2004, Lumineau and Que´lin 2012, Skowronski and Benton 2018) but has not considered them interdependently. Our study provided empirical evidence that interorganizational information sharing relationships can be subject to two-sided opportunism concerns. We showed that information providers are willing to provide more sensitive data if their information poaching concerns are addressed, whereas information recipients are more willing to rely on provided information if their information manipulation concerns are addressed.

Thus, countermeasures that prevent both forms of opportunism simultaneously enable new interorganizational information sharing relationships based on data that would otherwise be considered too sensitive to share or too untrustworthy to rely on. If only one side of the two-sided opportunism problem is addressed, one party must always take precautions against the potential opportunism of the other, such as not sharing sensitive data or not relying on received information. As a result, business opportunities may remain unexploited. As Williamson (1993, p. 97) observes: “Huge numbers of interesting problems of economic organization are missed or misconstrued if opportunism is ignored or suppressed.”

By uncovering the two-sided nature of opportunism in information sharing, we add to prior research aimed at understanding the triggers for and safeguards against information poaching (Skowronski and Benton 2018, Skowronski et al. 2020) and information manipulation (Lumineau and Que´lin 2012). We emphasize the duality of these threats and the need to consider them simulta neously, challenging the prevailing approach of investigating opportunism in interorganizational information sharing solely as either information poaching or information manipulation. Shifting from a one-sided to a two-sided view, it becomes easier to understand why some interorganizational information sharing relationships are difficult to establish or fail. According to transaction cost economics, business relationships become increasingly risky and costly as the threat of opportun ism grows. Because of the duality of information poach ing and manipulation, the risks encountered by both parties may influence and even exacerbate one another. Therefore, a two-sided view calls for new theoretical frameworks and empirical investigations to better understand this interdependence of information poaching and manipulation as well as its consequences.

A second conceptual contribution to the literature on interorganizational information sharing is our set of design principles for a shared IS that enables reliable (i.e., verifiably truthful) information sharing between business partners without revealing the data underlying the shared information. The design principles constitute a generic approach to solving the two-sided opportunism problem in interorganizational information sharing Although we have designed a blockchain-based system to implement the design principles in a technical solu tion, there may also be other ways to implement the design principles based on different underlying technologies. In fact, blockchain technology has several properties that are often considered challenging (Beck et al. 2018, Siegfried et al. 2020, Beverungen et al. 2021), and implementations of our design principles based on other technologies could potentially have technologica advantages. Nonetheless, our solution goes beyond existing organizational measures, such as contractua governance (Dyer 1997, Clarkson et al. 2007), relational governance (Mayer et al. 1995), and third-party involvement (Lumineau and Que´lin 2012, Du and Mao 2018) Specifically, our solution prevents opportunistic actions ex ante, whereas existing organizational measures disin centivize and discourage such actions or sanction them ex post. Our work also goes beyond existing technica solutions proposed for interorganizational information sharing by simultaneously addressing both information poaching and information manipulation. Although various technical approaches have been proposed to address either side (e.g., Kumar and van Dissel 1996, Grover et al. 2002, Heller et al. 2004, Hyva¨rinen et al. 2017), virtually all existing technical approaches that prevent information poaching are incompatible with those that prevent information manipulation. Our solution also complements the idea of multiparty computation, which avoids this incompatibility. Whereas some conceptual papers (e.g., Zyskind et al. 2015, Ackerman et al. 2016) suggest that multiparty computation could prevent both information poaching and information manipulation in sufficiently large networks, our solution is applicable even in networks as small as two organizations sharing information. The implementation of IS based on our design principles can therefore impact interorganizational relationships involving few or many parties. Given that opportunistic actions such as information manipulation and poaching are closely tied to the power distribution in interorganizational relationships (Frazier et al. 2009, Cho et al. 2017), the introduction of a new IS implementing our design principles may lead to significant shifts in power dynamics and potentially trigger evasive maneuvers—an area that requires thorough investigation in future research.

Third, at a technological level, we contribute to the literature on blockchain systems by recombining specific blockchain technology components—private data collections, smart contracts, and joint governance—in a new way to achieve novel results. Prior work in industry and academia has used private data collections to share data among some actors in a larger network, effectively hiding data from illegitimate access by third parties but sharing it with legitimate parties (e.g., Gug genberger et al. 2020, Amend et al. 2021). In contrast, we utilize private data collections as a truly private data store that keeps sensitive data completely confidentia to the information provider while performing prespeci fied computations on the data through smart contracts. The latter have been used to automate conditional trans actions based on publicly available data on a blockchain (Beck et al. 2018, Lumineau et al. 2021). In contrast, we combine a specific type of smart contract (i.e., smart contracts with nonreversible functions) with private data collections to compute and share reliably truthful infor mation derived from undisclosed, confidential data. Furthermore, we combine private data collections and smart contracts with joint governance mechanisms of blockchain systems to prevent information providers and information recipients from unilaterally changing the computation logic or access rights to information. Thus, we recombine existing technologies in an uncon ventional way and show that there is value in doing so. Our demonstration with professional blockchain and business experts attests to the efficacy and novelty of this solution. The specific recombination of existing blockchain technology components guarantees the confidentiality of sensitive data for the information pro vider and verifiably correct information for the informa tion recipient. By offering this recombination, our study bridges two distinct strands of research on block chain systems: managerial blockchain literature, which explores the effects of blockchain’s transparency and manipulation resistance on transaction costs and poten tial opportunism (e.g., Lumineau et al. 2021), and techni cal blockchain literature, which examines the feasibility of data confidentiality in blockchain networks (e.g. Chanson et al. 2019, Ma et al. 2019). Our study draws attention to the issue of information poaching, which has largely been overlooked in the managerial blockchain lit erature, and emphasizes the need to address information poaching and manipulation together—an aspect not ye considered in the technical blockchain literature. In doing so, our study may foster cross-fertilization between these strands of research and stimulate the development of alternative technical solutions and new managerial appli cations. Our specific recombination of blockchain tech nology components may also be able to solve related problems where blockchain technology was previously considered inadequate. For example, although block chain technology has been proposed to combat tax fraud in prior work, its inherent transparency was considered an “unacceptable property” (Hyva¨rinen et al. 2017, p. 445). In this case, our recombination of private data col lections, smart contracts, and joint governance could potentially be used to make a verifiably correct claim about tax payments without sharing unwanted details about the taxpayer.

Relatedly, from a practical perspective, we envision the transfer of our conceptual and technological solutions to areas beyond the bilateral information exchange in machine tool leasing. For example, another application case can be found in supply chain management, which is an active area of research on opportunism (Lumineau and Oliveira 2020, Skowronski et al. 2020). With growing pressure to ensure and certify compliance with human rights regulations throughout the entire supply chain (Hofmann et al. 2018), downstream vendors are demanding increasingly detailed information from suppliers several levels upstream. However, suppliers may be reluctant to provide detailed information about their own supply chain because of concerns over the downstream vendors’ potential for backward integration and encroachment into their business operations (Lin et al. 2014). Our design principles offer a blueprint for developing a shared IS that enables suppliers to provide reliable attestations regarding specific process and delivery characteristics to downstream vendors without revealing more information than necessary. Similarly, other domains of interorganizational relationships, such as IT outsourcing (Huber et al. 2013, Mathew and Chen 2013), coopetition (Wiener and Saunders 2014), and stra tegic alliances (Das and Rahman 2010), could benefit from our two-sided view of opportunism and the solutions we propose. In IT outsourcing relationships, clients may manipulate information by setting unrealistic targets for vendors, whereas vendors may engage in information poaching by using confidential client data for their own purposes (Mathew and Chen 2013). In coopetition settings, competing service providers who are required to cooperate may fear for their own information while simultaneously seeking to exploit the information shared by their peers (Wiener and Saunders 2014) In strategic alliances where independent organizations collaborate in one business area while competing in others, information exchange and data confidentiality are particularly challenging issues (Das and Rahman 2010). Our two-sided view of opportunism, along with our conceptual solutions, may support the development of multifaceted governance frameworks and information systems that address opportunism from both sides

## 7.2. Limitations

Our study has several limitations and boundary conditions. First, our solution is most effective when both the information provider and the information recipient fear opportunism at the same time. If only one party fears opportunistic behavior, then simpler technical solutions than ours may be sufficient to address the one-sided threat. In addition, our solution requires that both the information provider and the information recipient have the necessary technical expertise to effectively perform their roles in defining appropriate nonreversible functions (DP2) and in governing and operating the system (DP3).

Second, jointly defining and approving appropriate nonreversible functions can be time-consuming and costly. Therefore, our solution can be expected to perform better under relatively stable conditions with infrequent changes to the nonreversible functions and the logic of the smart contract. Appropriate nonreversible functions must be found for each application context. We invite researchers to work on such context-specific metrics that derive meaningful and instrumental information from the underlying data without revealing it.

Third, our solution relies on the integrity of the underlying blockchain network. Previous research has shown that the integrity of blockchain networks can be compromised by various attacks (Mueller-Bloch et al. 2022). Our solution for interorganizational information sharing is designed for relatively closed networks of relatively well-known participants who wish to share information based on sensitive data for business transactions, and it is built on a permissioned blockchain. Although these characteristics should make our solution less susceptible to many of the threats prevalent in open, permissionless blockchain networks (Bakos et al. 2021), the integrity of the blockchain network must be monitored and enforced.

Fourth, our solution depends on the provision of correct and unaltered data to the shared IS. Technologi cally, our solution is subject to the “first mile problem” of blockchain systems (Reyna et al. 2018, Lumineau et al. 2021), as preceding systems and their components (e.g., at the hardware level) could be targeted for manipulation before the data enter the shared IS. Put differently, our solution can only prevent both forms of opportunism—information poaching and information manipulation—once the data have entered the IS. Blockchain-enabled hardware that combines sensing and transaction management could help overcome this limitation, but it is still under development (Chanson et al. 2019).

Finally, we evaluated the utility of our solution using an artificial, rather than a naturalistic, evaluation strategy. A vignette-based survey with business experts allowed us to examine the solution’s utility in the diverse contexts of many organizations. Although DSRM evaluation guidelines recommend such artificial evaluation strategies for systems with the technical characteristics of our solution and similarly long and complex implementation cycles in practice (Venable et al. 2016), artificial evaluation strategies have limitations. For example, the business experts in our survey may not have been intimately familiar with the inner workings of our solution, which could have biased their utility assessments. Similarly, the selection process focused on business experts whose profiles could be found online. Business experts without online profiles may evaluate the utility of our solution differently. Thus, future work should include additional naturalistic utility evaluations.

## 8. Conclusion

The threat of opportunistic behavior in the form of information poaching and information manipulation is a persistent problem for interorganizational information sharing. We conducted a design science study to address the two-sided problem of opportunism. We derived three design principles for IS that compute shared information from sensitive data in a confidential and manipulation-resistant manner, enabling the reliable use of sensitive data for information sharing without revealing the data. We instantiated, demonstrated, and evaluated our design principles using blockchain technology in the domain of wear-based leasing contracts for machine tools, leveraging access to a multifirm research consortium. Our study is one of the first to address the need for technical countermeasures against the potential opportunism of both information providers and information recipients in interorganizational information sharing based on sensitive data, thereby providing a springboard for future research in the domains of interorganizational information sharing, opportunism, and blockchain systems.
