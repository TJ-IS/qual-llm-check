# Noisebnb: An Empirical Analysis of Home-Sharing Platforms and Residential Noise Complaints

Abstract. Societal externalities stemming from digital multisided platforms on nonparticipants have received increasing attention from scholars in recent years. In this paper, we investigate one such spillover, how the emergence of home-sharing platforms (viz., Airbnb) affects noise complaints in densely populated urban areas. Urban noise is a stalwart social problem in urban living, and anecdotal evidence has suggested that the entry of home-sharing platforms has significantly exacerbated urban noise. We empirically examine the relationship between the entry of Airbnb and noise complaints in New York City (NYC). Leveraging the phased expansion of Airbnb into different locations at different times and a difference-in-differences approach, we find that the arrival of Airbnb is associated with a significant decrease in the rate at which city residents file residential noise complaints. Furthermore, we find that the decrease in noise complaints is intensified (i) in locations where more tourist attractions exist and (ii) when occupancy rates of rentals are lower. This suggests that the effect is driven by limited physical occupancy, via either tourist stays or unmet capacity. Our work contributes to broader scholarship by addressing the myth and merit of Airbnb disturbance and informing regulators who are considering the cumulative impact of home-sharing platforms on quality of life in urban areas.

## Introduction

The social implications of digital multisided platforms have received increased attention from information systems researchers in recent years. This stream of scholarship has examined a plethora of diverse topics with significant social ramifications, ranging from the prevalence of sexually transmitted diseases to drunk driving, charitable giving, personal bankruptcy, and even crimes and sexual assault (Chan and Ghose 2014, Chan et al. 2016, Greenwood and Agarwal 2016, Martin-Buck 2016, Greenwood and Wattal 2017, Burtch and Chan 2019, Park et al. 2021, Han et al. 2022, Wang and Overby 2022). In doing so, scholars note that digital platforms facilitate transactions by simultaneously increasing the efficiency of search and reducing costs (Parker and Van Alstyne 2005), leading to striking shifts in individual behavior and social outcomes (Bapna et al. 2016, Greenwood and Wattal 2017, Wang and Overby 2022). And as this work has matured, there has been increasing attention paid to the effects of platform entry on platform nonparticipants, that is, non-transacting parties. This trend is critical because these negative externalities can be troubling if unaccounted for, notably when plat forms engage in regulatory arbitrage during their expansion to sidestep regulations (Malhotra and Van Alstyne 2014).

In this paper, we investigate one such negative externality by examining a particularly insidious nuisance of urban living: noise. Defined as “unwanted and highdecibel sound with an adverse effect on health,” noise is a leading scourge of urban living (Hartig and Kahn 2016). Exposure to noise in urban settings has been shown to cause both physiological and psychological damage (e.g., hearing impairment, anxiety, depression, hypertension, tachycardia, increased cortisol release) (Passchier-Vermeer and Passchier 2000, Muzet 2007, Seidman and Standring 2010, Hartig and Kahn 2016). And although urban noise can come from many sources (e.g., traffic, construction, aviation; see Dratva et al. 2010, Shepherd et al. 2010, Botteldooren et al. 2011), a significant and consistent source is neighbors and the sound-producing devices they use. Indeed, prior work emphasizes that this noise can reduce the quality of life in built-up urban areas (Bronzaft and Wallerstein 2006,

Stokoe 2006), with specific deleterious effects on the socioeconomic lower class (Riedel et al. 2014). Therefore, it is not surprising that most residential and municipal authorities include statutory restrictions on the extent of noise permissible under local law in residential areas.

In locations where noise pollution has become even more severe, municipal governments have begun to act beyond encouraging residents to file complaints with law enforcement. Instead, these cities spend untold resources investigating and mitigating the sources of urban noise. In New York City (NYC), the focus of this work, immense resources have been invested to resolve the issue of urban noise. In addition to helplines and law enforcement, the city has created health platforms dedicated to noise and partnered with numerous academic bodies to abate the problem (e.g., New York University’s (NYU) Center for Urban Science + Progress, NYU’s Tandon School of Engineering, and Ohio State Engineering).<sup>1</sup> Above and beyond financial and human capital investments, NYC has devoted considerable legislative capacity to finding a lasting solution to urban noise, leading to sweeping reforms of the municipal noise code.<sup>2</sup> Yet, in spite of these efforts, noise persists as the number one reason why residents call the city’s nonemergency 311 number and the leading quality of life complaint from city residents (dramatically outstripping complaints regarding the cost of living and even crime).<sup>3</sup> Taken together, this anecdotal and scholarly evidence underscores the deleterious sociological and economic impact of urban noise pollution on urban residents.

All the while, the advent of home-sharing platforms has added yet another variable to the mix in an already complicated policy quagmire. Anecdotal evidence surrounding Airbnb indicates that an increase in unwelcome noise is likely a result of platform entry. Airbnb enables homeowners to rent their homes, or part of their homes, when the properties are not used to full capacity. The decision to rent is made exclusively by owners, thereby allowing the owner to generate revenue from an underutilized resource.<sup>4</sup> From a theoretical perspective, such rentals create a moral hazard problem. Although owners generate revenue from rentals, renters have little incentive to adhere to prevailing community norms, and one way in which these inadequate incentives may manifest in pernicious renter behavior is noise. Whereas the owners and their neighbors depend on compliance with social and regulatory norms within the community, the incentives for renters are weaker. Moreover, the negative externalities of noise are borne by the community at large, rather than the owners. Thus, there exists a tradeoff in this setting between the private good for the owners, who might profit from renting their unutilized space, versus the public costs borne by others in the form of increased urban noise.

Yet although the theoretical connection between renting and increased noise may appear simple, recent work also raises the possibility that noise complaints might fall. Scholars have argued that the corpus of regulatory mechanisms platforms use (e.g., online reviews, community building) may be sufficient to address the issues caused by moral hazard in this context (Thierer et al. 2016). Furthermore, there are pragmatic reasons for why noise complaints may fall when home rental platforms enter a neighborhood. There is a rising trend in properties in places like New York City being run by professional hosts rather than resident homeowners looking to rent underused capacity; that is, entire apartments are rented rather than individual bedrooms. This suggests that properties may remain unoccupied for longer periods of time relative to other residences. There is also the fact that short-term renters are often tourists and vacationers, meaning they tend to spend significant time outside the property experiencing local attractions (e.g., museums, historical sites, cultural markers). In both scenarios, the actual property is left unoccupied for protracted periods of time compared with when it is occupied by the owner or a full-time renter. This would suggest that noise complaints will not rise, and may instead fall, after the arrival of home rental platforms.

Given this tension and the criticality of such questions to policymakers, we ask: What is the effect of home rental platform (Airbnb) entry on noise-related complaints in urban areas? To determine the size of any such effect, we exploit the phased expansion of Airbnb into different parts of New York City (NYC) between 2004 and 2019 using a two-way fixed-effects approach. This context is an ideal case for examining the effects of home-sharing on noise in urban areas. Airbnb is the largest home rental platform globally, with more than 660,000 listings in the United States in 2020. Similarly, NYC is the largest urban market for Airbnb in the United States, and the rich administrative data captured by the city allows for a level of visibility into the downstream effects of homesharing that is hard to match in other urban areas. Our data set is drawn in part from the Airbnb listings in NYC, the NYC Open Data portal offered by the Department of IT and Telecom, as well as the U.S. Census Bureau. Because the availability of rental offerings, caused by landlords selecting into the market, oc curs at different times across zip codes, we execute a difference-in-differences (DD) estimation wherein we compare the change in noise complaints for treated areas, that is, those with an Airbnb rental, with those that have yet to display available rental properties. This approach offers a simple yet compelling strategy to identify the effect of home rentals on noise complaints.

The results indicate two main findings. First, the entry of Airbnb is associated with a reduction in residential noise complaints. Economically, the effect corresponds to a decrease of 2.77 noise complaints made per zip code month, or a 5.1% decrease based on the 2019 estimates (3.6% based on 2018). Second, we find a greater reduction in residential noise complaints when occupancy rates (i.e., the percentage of days a rental is occupied by renters) of Airbnb rentals are lower and where outside attractions are more prevalent. This is evidenced by (i) fewer noise complaints when the property’s calendar indicates it is less often rented and (ii) fewer noise complaints near areas with a significant number of restaurants, bars, and other tourist attractions. In auxiliary analyses, we also find the entry of Airbnb associated with fewer owner-occupied units within NYC. These results, viewed in summary, suggest that Airbnb has largely decreased noise levels within the community and highlights the extent to which a material proportion of Airbnb guests are tourists or business travelers who spend time away from the rental (if and when the rental is occupied). These findings are consistent with recent work showing that Airbnb guests are indeed sensitive to noise (Cheng and Jin 2019), contradicting claims that Airbnb serves as a public nuisance by creating “party houses” (McNamara 2014). Probing further, we also find a significant increase in street noise complaints following Airbnb’s entry, providing support for the idea that Airbnb renters tend to physically move noise outside of rental properties and toward tourist activity rather than noise within residential premises.

Two primary contributions stem from this work. First, we contribute to the literature on platforms by extending the body of work on their societal implications, particularly to persons who have no direct interaction with a platform participant. We are thus able to add to the increasingly rich mosaic of scholarship (Greenwood and Wattal 2017, Wang and Overby 2022) that discusses the downstream social effects of digital platforms. In this case, we are examining the effect of two nonparticipant entities—residents who file noise complaints and municipal authorities—and how they react to the entry of a platform. Understanding such spillovers on nonparticipants is important, notably as platforms continue to attract attention from policy makers and regulatory bodies, because such scholarship is needed to fully explicate their social and economic implications for all persons affected. In addressing an important quality-of-life factor, that is, noise (Hartig and Kahn 2016), we provide empirical evidence that has been thus far missing in this literature.

Second, we contribute to the ongoing policy discussion surrounding home-sharing rentals. As many scholars have noted, although home sharing has the capacity to create significant efficiencies through the reuse of existing resources, it can also impose significant externalities (Quattroneet al. 2016, Filippas and Horton 2017, Gurran and Phibbs 2017, Coles et al. 2018). The negative externalities imposed by home rentals have remained a topic of considerable debate between those who focus on the need for top-down regulation and others who believe that a community-based approach that emphasizes self-regulation can counter such nega tive externalities (Cohen and Sundararajan 2015). Still, empirical work on the subject remains limited. We address this gap through a focus on noise complaints. Although policy prescriptions for home sharing in a city will necessarily involve a plethora of considerations, including traffic, crime, rents, and real estate prices, noise remains an important part of this complex mix. We believe our work will help a more holistic evaluation of the benefits and costs imposed by homesharing platforms on densely populated urban areas such as NYC.

## Background and Theory

Digital platforms create markets by facilitating transactions between buyers and sellers. In doing so, platforms charge participants a fee for coordinating the transaction but do not take ownership of the exchanged goods during the transaction (Rochet and Tirole 2003, 2004; Rysman 2009; Hagiu and Wright 2015). They are thus able to reduce market inefficiencies for participants by decreasing information asymmetries, transaction costs, and expediting the matching process between economic actors (Bakos 1997, Brynjolfsson and Smith 2000, Rysman 2009). More recently, sharing platforms have emerged, which allow individuals to rent underutilized resources such as housing and transportation. This is collectively referred to as the “sharing economy” (Sundararajan 2016). In contrast to platforms like Amazon and eBay, the sharing economy represents a specific case of peer-to-peer (P2P) transactions conducted on platforms (Greenwood et al. 2017). Firms like Uber and Airbnb are representative (Filippas and Horton 2017, Greenwood et al. 2017, Zervas et al. 2017) because they provide markets wherein individuals can offer short term “rentals” of their vehicles or residences. The rise of these platforms has not been without controversy, because they are clearly disruptive to incumbents. The valuation of taxicab medallions, for instance, has plummeted with the entry of ridesharing. Similarly, the hotel industry’s revenues have been undercut by low-cost rentals (Zervas et al. 2017, Farronato and Fradkin 2018), also stunting the supply of rental units (Barron et al. 2021).

And whereas early research on digital platforms focused on their economic benefits through the reduction of market frictions and enhanced efficiencies (Bakos 1997, Brynjolfsson and Smith 2000, Brynjolfsson et al. 2003, Rysman 2009), more recent work has begun to consider the social implications that arise from the advent of platforms. This has become increasingly important as platforms become ubiquitous in domains that are underserved by traditional firms (e.g., dating, food delivery, point-to-point transit). In the case of sharing platforms, extant research has shown a diverse set of social implications spanning charitable giving, traffic, drunk driving, HIV rates, and bankruptcy (Greenwood and Agarwal 2016, Burtch et al. 2018, Erhardt et al. 2019, Wang and Overby 2022). Yet despite the rapid expansion of research on the social implications of platforms, structural gaps persist in our understanding of how they affect the social environment in which they are embedded.

One area that remains less developed pertains to how the behavior of sharing platform participants may spill over onto platform nonparticipants, although work in this area is growing. Early scholarship, for example, examined the effect of platforms on the proliferation of sexually transmitted diseases (Chan and Ghose 2014, Greenwood and Agarwal 2016), wherein the disease could eventually spread beyond the platform. More recent work has also addressed broader questions pertaining to platform entry, such as the effect of ridesharing platforms on traffic congestion in cities (Babar and Burtch 2020, Li et al. 2022). Building on this small but influential literature, in this paper we address the following question: What happens when platforms cause changes in the lives of individuals who neither meaningfully participate on the platform nor directly transact with platform participants? These social and economic spillover effects are important, because platforms can simultaneously (i) impose costs on the immediate social environment and (ii) facilitate economic growth (Horton and Zeckhauser 2016). More importantly, policymakers need to be informed of these effects when evaluating the competing tradeoffs that stem from digital platforms. We discuss these tradeoffs next.

## Home Rental Platforms

Home rental platforms such as Airbnb and Vrbo offer homeowners an opportunity to rent their properties to guests when those properties are not fully utilized. As of March 2022, Airbnb had six million active listings in more than 220 countries.<sup>5</sup> As with other gig-economy platforms, Airbnb serves as a coordinating mechanism between owners and renters by generating additional revenue for owners with excess housing. Unsurprisingly, this increase in short-term accommodation has yielded material increases in economic activity, especially in densely populated, urban areas where they are available (for a review, see Horton and Zeckhauser 2016). When viewed through this lens, (i) the ability to use underutilized residential space, (ii) the ability to generate additional homeowner revenue, and (iii) the ability to stimulate local economic activity, all indications suggest that the overall impact of platforms like Airbnb may be largely positive.

Despite these positives, such platforms have also yielded negative effects (Malhotra and Van Alstyne 2014, Slee 2017). These include, but are not limited to, higher rents as a result of a reduced residential housing supply, reduced affordable housing, loss of tax revenue to local governments, and job losses in the hospitality sector (Lee 2016, Horn and Merante 2017, Bivens 2019, Barron et al. 2021).<sup>6</sup> These issues are particularly acute in densely populated cities and urban areas where real estate is already at a premium. Indeed, municipal gov ernments across the globe have taken measures to rein in the use of such rental platforms by imposing strict conditions under which they may operate.<sup>7</sup> Cities including Austin, San Francisco, Los Angeles, Amsterdam, Vancou ver, and Paris have all enacted policies restricting home rentals, and many are directly engaged in litigation to either curb or eliminate Airbnb’s operation entirely.<sup>8</sup> In 2018, for example, Palma de Mallorca became the first city to ban Airbnb outright. New York City, the context of this work, has been battling Airbnb since 2013 over regulations that prohibit the use of residential areas as vacation rentals when the homeowners do not cohabitate the property with guests.<sup>9</sup> As expected, the litigious nature of the relationship between Airbnb and local governments has yielded a host of research aimed at informing both policymakers and platform owners about how this contentious relationship might be governed (Guttentag 2015, Filippas and Horton 2017, Gurran and Phibbs 2017, Coles et al. 2018).

Beyond policymakers and local hotel operators, the entry of Airbnb can give local residents cause for concern, bringing to the fore issues about the scarcity of research addressing the implications for platform nonparticipants. Most Airbnb rentals are in residentially zoned areas. To the extent that Airbnb rentals are com mercial, rather than residential, municipal governments have argued that they are illegal.<sup>10</sup> Moreover, allowing short-term rentals in residential areas can cause significant negative externalities that affect platform participants and nonparticipants alike. These include crime, infrastructure problems, ballooning rents, excess garbage, traffic, water and power usage, and more (see, e.g., Sheppard and Udell 2016, Barron et al. 2021, Han et al. 2021, Ke et al. 2021, and Alyakoob and Rahman 2022). Furthermore, because short-term renters have no ties to the community and limited expectations of future interaction, moral hazard issues stemming from weak incentives to either obey social norms or self-regulate behavior can arise, imposing costs on the local community. Although hosts operating on home rental platforms can defray some of these costs through increased revenues, others in the community are not compensated for any short-term costs that renters may inflict. One outcome that may result from these weak incentives is noise, which we discuss next.

## Noise and Urban Quality of Life

Although seemingly trite, urban noise is a wellrecognized scourge of urban living. Scholarly efforts to measure its effect indicate the ruinous effect that noise can have on quality of life, especially within large and densely populated urban areas such as New York City and other similarly populated cities around the world (Stansfeld et al. 2000, Bello et al. 2019, Mucci et al. 2020). Noise is both an economic issue (Lam and Chung 2012) as well as a medically sensitive issue (Moudon 2009). Exposure to noise from traffic, construction, and nearby aviation (Dratva et al. 2010, Shepherd et al. 2010, Botteldooren et al. 2011) can cause serious physiological and psychological harm (Passchier-Vermeer and Passchier 2000, Muzet 2007, Seidman and Standring 2010, Hartig and Kahn 2016). Furthermore, recent work has found that noise and the deleterious effects of noise in urban areas disproportionately harm the lower class, who typically have fewer “outside” options to deal with the pernicious effects of urban noise (Riedel et al. 2014).

Anecdotal accounts about noise in the press corroborate this view about its pollutant effects. In our context, New York, urban noise is often cited as a significant problem with tremendous economic and social costs.<sup>11</sup> In fact, New Yorkers make more calls to the city’s nonemergency 311 line to complain about noise than they do for any other reason (77% of calls), all of which leads to noise being the number one quality of life complaint in NYC,<sup>12</sup> outstripping both crime and traffic. Indeed, the city reports that 20% of New Yorkers are frequently disturbed by noise at home.<sup>13</sup> More to the point, and relevant to our work, a leading source of residential noise complaints is neighbors (Bronzaft and Wallerstein 2006, Stokoe 2006). To deal with this issue, both city authorities and nonprofit institutions have tried to find ways to alleviate the costs of noise pollution within the city. Examples are easy to find; NYC has introduced health platforms dedicated to noise, and the city is actively collaborating with academic institutions to resolve the issue. These include, but are not limited to, NYU’s Center for Urban Science + Progress, NYU’s Tandon School of Engineering, and the Ohio State University’s School of Engineering. 14 Furthermore, the New York City Council is acting to limit the extent to which urban noise remains an issue within the city, with the local government as well as the state government looking to toughen restrictions put in place by the city’s noise code.<sup>15</sup> Taken together, this body of work underscores the degree to which urban noise is a material issue influencing quality of life in the city and that factors that exacerbate urban noise are ripe for policy interventions. One such factor that has arisen in recent years is the advent of home-sharing platforms.

## Home Rental Platforms and Noise Complaints

As discussed, noise is often cited in discussions of home rental platforms in both the scholarly work and the popular press. However, the effect of home-sharing platforms on local noise complaints has not been addressed in any rigorous empirical manner beyond descriptive analysis and anecdotal narratives (see, e.g., McNamara 2014, Khadem 2016, Sheppard and Udell 2016, Binzer 2017, Filippas and Horton 2017, Gurran and Phibbs 2017, Coles et al. 2018, Bivens 2019). Furthermore, the a priori relationship between home-sharing and noise is theoretically ambiguous. On the one hand, anecdotal evidence and the relatively small body of research addressing the issue (listed above) suggests that Airbnb’s entry into a community may be associated with an increase in noise complaints. The stereotypical “party house” rented on Airbnb has been vividly portrayed in the popular press, leading the platform to ban to “open invite” parties as well as large parties at apartment buildings and condos.<sup>16</sup> Indeed, economic theory suggests that short-term renters are more likely to flout social norms and be less mindful of the noneconomic costs incurred by their neighbors because they do not bear the costs of such behavior (Lambert 1983, Schneider et al. 2010). Thus, they are more likely to display behavior consistent with moral hazard because they do not possess the same incentives to adhere to norms as the owners of the property do (Bhattacharyya and Lafontaine 1995). This perspective would indicate that, on average, the entry of Airbnb is likely to yield significantly more noise complaints in local communities.

This perspective notwithstanding, in 2019 alone more than 41 million guests used the platform, up from 33 million guests in 2017, indicating robust growth (Statista 2020). Because rampant opportunistic behavior by renters would result in systematic curtailing of rental agreements, the question of why noise stemming from Airbnb rentals may not be an issue is naturally raised. Prior work studying sharing-based platforms argues that governance mechanisms on platforms are different and that the risk of moral hazard in these markets can be ameliorated by alternate mechanisms based on online trust, social norms, and reputational systems that reward good behavior (Thierer et al. 2016). The intuition is simple; by creating norms of behavior and communal mechanisms for disciplining unacceptable behavior (such as online ratings and quick feedback mechanisms), moral hazard might be ameliorated in online platforms. Cohen and Sundararajan (2015), for example, described how a regime of self-regulation on sharing platforms, based on reputation systems like online reviews, transparency, and norms that reward good behavior, can address moral hazard. Airbnb fits this mold because it possesses community-based norms and clear reputation systems (Brandt and Sigmund 2005).

Pragmatic reasons also exist. For example, we might expect noise complaints to fall when home-rental platforms enter a neighborhood as a result of the rise of “professional” hosts on Airbnb (Malhotra and Van

Alstyne 2014). Contrary to the narrative of hosts leasing out spare or underutilized resources, many individuals choose to lease entire apartments on the platform, essentially transitioning from being a “host” to a “landlord.” This reflects a level of professionalism entering the homesharing domain. One recent suit filed by NYC documented more than 100 host accounts and 18 corporations that acquired and converted residential properties into Airbnb rentals, subsequently using them like hotel space since at least 2012.<sup>17</sup> Moreover, because home-rental properties are popular with vacationers and experience average occupancy rates of 36% to 51%, it is likely that the entities offering these rentals are not traditional homeowners leveraging existing capacity<sup>18</sup> but are instead professional “hosts” who operate more like traditional hotels. And although exact numbers of professional hosts versus those who genuinely share excess capacity are hard to come by, because most of these are private entities, recent work suggests that these trends are partly responsible for putting upward pressure on housing prices (Lee 2016), specifically in NYC (Sheppard and Udell 2016).

What are the implications of these trends? First, to the extent that full-residence rental models depend on excess capacity, there are likely to be periods where the property remains unoccupied so that additional rents can be charged during periods of high use (such as weekends or vacation seasons). Because hosts in such a model do not live in these properties, it is intuitive that the number of noise complaints emerging from such properties will be lower because they are often unoccupied relative to homes that are occupied by owners on a full-time basis. Second, even when these properties are occupied by guests, notably in large cities like NYC, these guests are likely to be tourists who spend time outside the property. In contrast to residents, guests are likely to spend time outside the property visiting restaurants, bars, museums, and shops or patronizing local tourist attractions. Of course, these dynamics are less likely in smaller cities and towns or places that are not as densely populated. However, noise complaints associated with home-rental properties tend to be focused on large cities rather than in smaller or sparsely populated regions. In cities like NYC (and other large cities like Tokyo, Amsterdam, or London), the presence of guests who spend less time in the property relative to full-time residents should significantly reduce the amount of noise generated therein.

In summary, there exists considerable ambiguity in how the entry of Airbnb may affect noise complaints. On the one hand, moral hazard would indicate an increase in noise complaints, whereas arguments based on self-regulation, professionalism, and transient guests suggest no such effect, or even a net reduction in noise. In lieu of formal hypotheses, we allow our empirical analysis to provide guidance.

## Data and Analysis Setting and Data

To test the effect of home rental platform entry on noise complaints, we leverage the phased expansion of Airbnb in different zip codes in New York City (NYC). Taking such an approach offers two benefits. First, Airbnb is the largest accommodation provider in the world, Marriott being a distant second, offering only a third of Airbnb’s accommodation capacity (Haywood et al. 2017). This ensures that we are capturing the first entry of a home-rental platform in any given area. It further ensures visibility of the outcome because the size of the treatment effect is sufficiently large to plausibly impact the level of noise. Second, we can treat the phased expansion as quasi-experimental, allowing us to execute a difference-in-differences design. We can do so because Airbnb enters different locations at different times, creating treatment and control groups. Figure 1 shows the spaced and staggered entry of Airbnb in a cumulative plot of the number of zip codes with Airbnb listings between 2007 and 2019.

To execute the difference in differences, we draw on multiple sources of data. Data on Airbnb listings in NYC are drawn directly from Airbnb. We focus on active Airbnb rentals (rather than listings) by crosschecking listing data with the guest reviews posted for each property. Data on noise complaints are drawn from NYC’s Open Data portal provided by the New York Department of IT and Telecom. Zip code level Airbnb and noise data are aggregated monthly to observe heterogeneous effects attributable to zip-specific differences. The range of the analysis is from April 2004 to August 2019. Controls (e.g., housing units, median income) are drawn from the U.S. Census Bureau. The unit of analysis is the zip code month.

## Variable Definitions

Dependent Variable. The primary dependent variable for the analysis is the number of residential noise complaints received by the NYC municipal government both online and via phone at the zip code month level.<sup>19</sup> The number of housing units is set as an exposure variable. Thus, the dependent variable should be interpreted as the rate of noise complaints rather than the number of complaints.

Independent Variable. The independent variable of interest is a Boolean indicator, which captures the first active Airbnb listing in zip code i at time t (1 if present, 0 otherwise), as crosschecked by guest reviews. To execute this crosscheck, we ensure that there is an actual review from guests who stayed at the listing. This ensures that guests are actually using the rental rather than simply checking the listings that may be or may not be rented.<sup>20</sup> Note that we refer to our focal independent variable as “entry” to remain consistent with prior work (Zervas et al. 2017, Farronato and Fradkin 2018, Zhang et al. 2018). In practice, Airbnb’s appearance in zip codes is based on the individual listing of residential properties, hence indicating selfselection on the part of the host. This is different from the standard operationalization of entry in other studies wherein the platform (e.g., Uber, Craigslist) is the one selecting into operations in a local area. In the case of Airbnb, the platform already exists within the municipality, but the actual observation of a rental and review within a zip code thus represents “entry” as we operationalize $\mathrm { i t } ^ { 2 1 }$ We discuss the implications of host selfselection in our analysis and offer a series of robustness checks to ensure that it does not bias our results.

Controls. Because we use a two-way fixed-effect estimation, location (zip code) and time (month) fixed effects are included. These should absorb any timeinvariant endogenous process that might be correlated with both Airbnb entry and noise complaints in a zip code. Moreover, time fixed effects should account for any time-varying changes that occur across NYC overall, such as changes to noise ordinances implemented during the sample. We further control for housing market indicators, financial factors, and demographic factors, including the median household income, the number of housing units, the number of units that are occupied by their renters, the number of housing units that are vacant, unemployment, education, age, and population at zip code year level.<sup>22</sup> Housing market indicators help us control for time-variant changes across zip codes in housing stocks. Demographics, which change slowly year on year from one zip code to the next, are included to control for the rapid gentrification of large cities observed over the course of the sample. Finally, we control for the number of restaurants to capture ongoing service-based economic activity in the zip code. ## Estimation Procedure

As discussed, we use a difference-in-differences (DD) approach to identify the effect of Airbnb entry on noise. The DD approach has been widely used in prior work on multisided platforms by exploiting the phased nature of platform entry (Greenwood and Wattal 2017, Burtch et al. 2018, Wang and Overby 2022), thereby simulating an experimental design using observational data. This is achieved by casting locations where the platform has entered as treatment and locations where the platform has yet to enter as control. Using location fixed effects to control for time-invariant zip code heterogeneity and time fixed effects to account for trends in noise complaints over time, the effect of platform entry is identified by comparing the change in the DV for the treated and untreated locations. Formally,

$$
Y _ {i t} = \beta_ {1} A I R _ {i t} + T _ {t} + S _ {i} + \gamma^ {\prime} X _ {i t} + \varepsilon_ {i t},
$$

Before discussing any results, it is important to acknowledge and test the assumptions that DD estimations are based on, the most important of which is parallel pretreatment trends. Parallel trends mandate that the movement of the dependent variable is similar for treated and untreated locations prior to treatment (Bertrand et al. 2004). The absence of parallel pretreatment trends weakens the argument that changes in the dependent variable are a result of platform entry and suggests that the control may be an invalid counterfactual. We use several tests, including a leads/lags model and a Bayesian analysis to test the parallel trends assumption and robustness of the estimation. These are described in the robustness section below.

To assess the validity of the parallel trends assumption, we replicate a common variant of the Autor (2003) leads and lags model by interacting the time fixed effects with an indicator of a zip code receiving the treatment. In doing so, we capture the trend in the effect pre- and posttreatment by modeling the effect semiparametrically. Our expectation is to obtain a significant effect post-treatment, with no pretreatment differences. Formally, we estimate that

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & Y _ {i t} = \sum_ {j} \tau_ {j} P r e - E n t r y _ {i t} (j) + \sum_ {k} \omega_ {k} P o s t - E n t r y _ {i t} (k) + T _ {t} \\ & \qquad + S _ {i} + \gamma^ {\prime} X _ {i t} + \varepsilon_ {i t}, \end{array}

As can be seen in both the tabular and graphic results, in the months prior to entry the semiparametrically estimated relative time indicators are insignificant and center around zero. Furthermore, roughly three months after the entry of Airbnb into a zip code, the estimated coefficients become both negative and significant. Such a delay in the effect is intuitive given the network effects that digital platforms like Airbnb rely on. Taken in sum, these effects corroborate the earlier estimations showing that Airbnb is associated with a significant decrease in noise complaints and are consis tent with the main model in terms of direction and magnitude.

Goodman-Bacon Decomposition. This is because the calculation of the overall treatment effect coefficient is a weighted comparison of those groups that are treated earlier versus later, treated versus never-treated groups, and so forth. Bayesian Analysis. Results presented this far show that, on average, the entry of Airbnbs into a zip code leads to a drop in noise complaints. However, this average effect can conceal the distribution of the effect size that would otherwise demonstrate the heterogeneity of the effect across the zip codes. Bayesian analyses provide a simple, data-driven way to estimate these heterogeneities across location by calculating the complete distribution rather than the sampling distributions to achieve a point estimate (Gelman et al. 2013, Cumming 2014). Thus, in addition to the frequentist analyses presented above, we also conduct a Bayesian analysis of the effect. These analyses further provide us with the ability to examine whether the estimated effects from the Poisson Quasi-Maximum Likelihood estimation are robust to alternative statistical techniques and serve as a corroborative tool to the estimation of the treatment effects discussed earlier. In particular, estimating heterogeneities can be useful if the distribution is skewed, even if the overall effect is negative, for obvious policy reasons.

To prevent any bias from the selection of priors, we use flat and weakly informative priors (a half Student-t prior with three degrees of freedom) to compute posterior sampling distributions using a Hamiltonian Monte

Carlo Method (No U-Turn Sampler (NUTS)). The model assumes a Poisson distribution and uses a log link function for unbounded count data. We control for time and include zip code-level fixed effects. After we compute the posterior samples, we report the model summary in which every parameter is summarized using the mean and the standard deviation of the posterior distribution and two-sided 95% credible intervals (l-95% CI and u-95% CI) based on quantiles. Results are in Table 11. Because of computational limitations, the model is run at the zip code year level and matches the analysis reported in Table 5.

Results consistently show that entry is associated with an approximately 2% decrease in the IRR number of noise complaints (comparable to the 2.5% decrease presented in Column 2 of Table 5). The distribution of the effect is shown in Figure 3. In this figure, the distribution of the posterior samples is concentrated in the lower end, indicating that the modal effect is one of a reduction in noise complaints. The 95% credible interval and highest posterior density interval for the posterior lie below zero, indicating that the effect of Airbnb’s entry on noise complaints is significantly distinct from zero and negative. In terms of the magnitude and direction of effects, the Bayesian results corroborate those obtained from frequentist analyses and show support for the finding that home share platforms appear to reduce noise complaints.

Other Types of Noise. To the extent that our theory relates to the effect of home-sharing platforms on residential noise as opposed to other forms of urban noise, administrative data from NYC also offer us a powerful placebo test in the form of nonresidential noise complaints. Therefore, we replicate our analysis using two additional sets of noise complaints: those originating from collection truck noise (sanitation) and those coming from street and sidewalk noise, rather than residential noise complaints. Any decrease in these forms of noise would undercut our argument that the entry of Airbnb yields lower levels of residential noise complaints. An increase in the latter would corroborate our argument that the observed changes in noise might be attributed to tourists.

Logit Hazard Model. A further open question pertains to whether Airbnb hosts are directly sampling on changes in the level of noise before listing their properties, raising the issue of reverse causality. If potential hosts observe that extant levels of noise complaints (or noise generally) are increasing, then they may be more or less likely to list the property based on the clientele they believe they can attract. Although the relative time model provides a powerful test against this, directly examining the antecedents of “entry” directly is worthwhile. Therefore, we estimate a logit hazard model and examine whether changes in the number of noise complaints in a zip code explain Airbnb’s entry into that zip code subsequently (Singer and Willett 2003). In doing so, we regress our main independent variable, Airbnb entry, on the number of monthly noise complaints as well as the suite of controls. Zip code fixed effects are not included because each zip code is only initially treated a single time (i.e., monotonically increasing following Airbnb’s entry). After a zip code observes the entry, it is removed from the panel. Results in Table 14 indicate no significant relationship between noise and entry, as would be expected, suggesting that changes in the level of noise ex ante are not a significant predictor of entry.

Treatment Intensity. Finally, we test whether the treatment intensity, as measured by the number of Airbnb listings in a zip code, has an effect on the number of noise complaints. The intuition is straightforward; if Airbnb in fact reduces residential noise complaints, rather than increasing noise complaints, then the number of complaints should continue to fall as the number of listings increases. Consistent with our main analysis, the relationship is negative and statistically significant. Broadly, this suggests that as treatment intensity rises $( \mathrm { i . e . } ,$ , as the number of Airbnbs in a zip code increases), the number of noise complaints drops even further.<sup>34</sup>

## Testing for Mechanisms and Sources of Heterogeneity

Thus far, our results show a negative effect of Airbnb entry on residential noise complaints. However, it is interesting to consider the possibility of heterogeneity in these effects, notably as such heterogeneity may allow us to tease out the mechanisms by which the effect manifests. We explore these in this section.

Occupancy Rate. Recall our arguments for why Airbnb may yield a decrease in noise complaints; (i) governance mechanisms instituted by platforms might be able to redress the issues of weak renter incentives, and (ii) Airbnb rentals may be under capacity and not be fully occupied. The former explanation about platform governance remains untested in the literature, although scholars have argued for its value (Sundararajan 2016). However, we can test for the second mechanism by examining whether the treatment effect intensifies as occupancy rates of rentals change, that is, testing for the moderating effect of occupancy. Our expectation would be that when occupancy is lower, that is, the rental is not occupied by guests, the associated noise complaints will be lower. Conversely, as occupancy rises, the odds of noise complaints being generated will increase.

Accommodation Capacity. In addition to whether properties in a zip code experience high occupancy in a given month, it is also worth considering the effect of accommodation capacity, that is, the total number of guests who are permitted to stay in a property. When viewed in aggregate, zip codes that have rental properties that have high accommodation capacities are likely to see a different effect in terms of noise complaints. We thus test for a moderation effect of the average rental capacity at the level of the zip code. To do so, we consider the average number of guests that can be hosted in a property per zip code and split the sample at the median. Based on this split, zip codes are assigned to the high-occupancy or low-occupancy subsamples across the full data set.<sup>37</sup> We then conduct the splitsample analysis shown in Columns 1 and 2 of Table 17.

The results show that noise complaints are negatively associated with Airbnb’s entry in the zip codes where Airbnb rentals accommodate fewer guests, whereas the negative effect disappears in the zip codes where Airbnb rentals accommodate more guests, on the margin. This result provides evidence that the less crowded the properties are on average, the fewer the noise complaints that are issued. On the other hand, Airbnb properties accommodating larger groups may see an increase in noise complaints. This significant positive effect shows up when we focus on the fourth quartile for accommodation levels in subsample analysis, as shown in Column 3 of Table 17 (using the same split criterion described above). From a regulatory perspective, this is worth noting because hotels are regulated in terms of how many guests are allowed to stay in each unit. No such regulations exist for Airbnb, even though the platform imposes its own advisories on how many guests are ideal for a given property. It is largely up to the hosts whether they are willing to adhere to such guidance from the platform. We observe that the expected negative externality on noise complaints shows up in the relatively rare cases of large group rentals rather than the modal rental that serves smaller groups.

## Discussion

The advent of home-sharing platforms like Airbnb has led to significant speculation in the academic community and popular press about the ostensible effects on the economy, businesses, and individuals. These include a variety of outcomes at the macro, mezzo, and micro levels, including economic activity, restaurant business, traffic, crime, rent, and home prices. However, one negative externality that has been associated with the entry of Airbnb but has not received significant empirical attention is noise pollution. In this paper, we attempt to fill this gap by studying the relationship between Airbnb entry and noise complaints in New York City. In doing so, we add to the broader corpus of work examining the implications of digital platforms by studying the effect on nonparticipants, that is, those who do not participate on platforms but are nevertheless affected by them. As platforms become more pervasive in society and more social activities become digitally mediated, it is important to consider a more expansive view of the implications of platforms for at least two reasons. First, from a managerial perspective, it is useful to understand how platforms affect outcomes in the ecosystem that they operate in, whether these outcomes are economic or social in nature (Chan and Ghose 2014, Babar and Burtch 2020, Mejia and Parker 2021). Second, and equally important, platforms raise important regu latory questions about how they affect local businesses and communities, particularly when they are viewed as being disruptive to incumbent industries (Filippas and Horton 2017). Our work addresses both goals by pro viding rigorous analysis to decision makers on the prag matic and localized effects of Airbnb’s entry, rather than relying on anecdotal accounts or press reports (see, e.g., McNamara 2014 and Lobel 2016), thereby allowing for a more holistic and informed decision process.

The common narrative surrounding the entry of Airbnb and residential noise has been a deleterious one; that is, home rentals were assumed to lead to greater noise (see, e.g., McNamara 2014, Khadem 2016, Shep pard and Udell 2016, Binzer 2017, Filippas and Horton 2017, Gurran and Phibbs 2017, Coles et al. 2018, Bivens 2019). Noise affects not only those who choose to rent their properties but also others in the community. More to the point, noise remains a critical factor for quality of life that has been important enough for city and municipal authorities to spend considerable resources in tackling noise complaints and instituting noise codes with city ordinances. To the extent that noise is clearly associated with home-sharing platforms, it would require a regulatory response from policymakers, notably if incentives to adhere to social norms are weak (Lambert 1983, Quattrone et al. 2016). However, there exists a clear gap between what is assumed in the press and what is empirically tested from public data in terms of the relationship between home sharing and noise. Our work addresses this gap, wherein strikingly, we find that noise complaints decrease significantly once Airbnb is available in a localized market.

We offer two potential explanations for such a result. On the one hand, platform-based incentives might mitigate any such spillover effects. On the other hand, it is possible that the fact that the premises remain unoccupied for significant amounts of time, either because of their remaining empty by design or because they are occupied by absent tourists, leads to fewer noise complaints, all else being equal. When probing these mechanisms, we consider four direct sources of variation: the presence of restaurants, bars, and performance arts establishments within the zip code, the number of days the property is rented, the extent to which the rental is occupied by a large number of guests, and the rate of owner occupancy in residential units. In each case, the results suggest that when the premises are unoccupied or unoccupied for longer parts of the day (in the case of tourist stays, for example), the associated noise complaints decline. Interestingly, we also observe that Airbnb’s entry is associated with a reduction in the number of owner-occupied units in the city, which is suggestive of more apartments or residences being leased on Airbnb as opposed to unused bedrooms per se. A series of robustness tests confirm these findings, providing strength to the inferences that we draw.

Our work contributes to the literature on platforms in two specific ways. First, our work contributes to a growing stream of research that addresses nonpecuniary outcomes associated with the introduction of platforms in existing economic areas. As discussed earlier, we study how platforms influence outcomes that reflect quality of life or social costs that may otherwise be glossed over in the pursuit of economic benefits. Although prior work has addressed diverse social outcomes such as drunk driving, dating, and the spread of disease, we add to this work by considering urban noise pollution as a relevant and important outcome. Within this stream of research, our work focuses on platform nonparticipants in a particularly interesting context, where neighbors in a local area may be troubled by the presence of Airbnb rentals in their community. We show that a muchposited relationship, which has been widely discussed anecdotally, does not appear when subjected to empirical scrutiny. Rather, the opposite effect appears to dominate, where noise tends to be reduced when home sharing enters specific communities.

A second contribution of our work is to a broader but no less important audience: policymakers who are interested in finding ways to regulate platform entrants in sensitive sectors, without curbing the natural benefits that they provide to society and individuals (Sundararajan 2016). Given the power of platforms and their ability to catalyze change in the sectors in which they operate, policies that either allow them free hand or apply regulatory controls require two inputs: first, a clear theoretical sense of how any regulatory decisions may influence behavior on the platform (Cusumano et al. 2021); and second, clear evidence of how platforms have affected existing economic sectors (Foerderer et al. 2018). What might policymakers do with the information in this paper? As is evident from the falling rates of noise complaints after Airbnb entry, the primary policy implication surrounds what policymakers should likely avoid doing. Our work suggests that targeting home-sharing platforms like Airbnb as a way to reduce noise within NYC may be unnecessary. Because resources available to the city authorities are limited, their allocation toward combating noise as an urban nuisance may be better directed toward other obvious sources of noise pollution (traffic, construction, and so forth) rather than toward home-sharing networks. Of course, our work cannot speak to other reasons to regulate home-sharing networks, such as traffic, crime, and home rents. To the extent that noise is a factor, our work indicates limited value in using this as a regulatory lever.

A second and equally important policy implication of our work deals with our results showing that Airbnb is associated with an increase in residences that are not occupied by their owners. In general, there is a preference for residential units to be occupied by their owners within the city from a social welfare viewpoint. Identifying the socially optimal level of homeowners who stay in their homes within NYC remains out of the scope of our work, but if Airbnb’s entry has lowered the occu pancy rates by homeowners under the socially optimal level, then policymakers may need to consider remedies. The literature on sharing economy models offers several such options, including, but not limited to, mandatory minimum occupancy rates, more aggressive enforcement of extant policy (such as the one host, one property policy adopted by Airbnb), or the implementation of licensure requirements for prospective hosts. In essence, these would increase the costs associated with operating on the platform, whether for hosts or renters, thereby reducing their attractiveness. Again, these costs would need to be balanced against the value and benefits offered by these platforms. Such a policy oriented cost-benefit analysis is independent of the issue of noise, which remains our focus, but represents an area of importance in terms of future research.

At a managerial level, our work informs Airbnb managers and hosts about first, the potential for noise emerging from residences with high occupancies; and second, aggregate evidence from urban cities like NYC showing that, on a systematic level, noise may not be as compelling a problem as first imagined. An interesting implication to our work here, even if not directly addressed through our data, pertains to the notion that noise complaints may fall simply because properties are unoccupied for longer periods of time (whether because they are empty or due to renters who are not there for much of the day). This suggests that the common narrative of renting a spare bedroom or a mattress is simply not true in aggregate. Rather, these properties are likely to be investment properties, as evidenced by the decline in owner-occupied units that we see in our analysis. The strategy of acquiring investment properties in order to rent them out through sharing platforms is not new; prior work has shown this to be the case in the case of Uber, for instance (Gong et al. 2017). These hosts, if indeed present in large numbers, represent a potential challenge to Airbnb as they bring the platform services much closer in nature to the traditional hospitality sector. Airbnb enjoys regulatory advantages over traditional hotels who are more clearly regulated as a service provider (Malhotra and Van Alstyne 2014), but if a large number of hosts are making strategic investments in real estate, then this raises additional regulatory risks for the platform. We acknowledge that we cannot identify these trends clearly in our data and leave this to future research to fully establish the role of professional hosts in this context.

Our work is subject to certain limitations. First, we consider noise complaints at the zip code level, which is a level of aggregation based on data availability. We acknowledge that noise complaints at the level of the street or immediate neighborhood would be ideal, but data are not available at this level. Second, we account for occupancy using data gathered from the calendar provided by Airbnb as well as limits on the number of guests allowed in each rental, but we do not have specific information on actual guest stays, which can be obtained only from Airbnb itself. Our attempts at contacting Airbnb for this information have been unsuccessful. This remains a limitation, although any nonavailability stemming from the property not being on the market would be subject to the same theoretical mechanisms as a guest not occupying the space. Third, although we run a series of falsification and robustness tests to establish the validity of our results, there may be other unobserved factors that could introduce bias into our estimations. Although we can establish the critical role of occupancy in terms of its effects on noise, we cannot rule out other mechanisms that might concomitantly be at play (e.g., improved data-based governance (Cohen and Sundararajan 2015) or changing relationships between tenants and renters). We leave this for future research. Finally, although there is no indication that noise complaints are a significant leading indicator of Airbnb entry, we cannot empirically rule out the possibility that local residents, who might be more prone to complain about noise, constitute those who are more likely to become Airbnb hosts. As a result, it is possible that Airbnb results in a selection of residents who are more noise sensitive out of locations where Airbnb enters, thereby causing noise complaints to fall. We thank an anonymous referee for raising this limitation.

Our work also opens paths for future work. The need to provide an appropriate regulatory framework for sharing-based platforms and their role within the economy requires significant amounts of empirical and theoretical research (Cusumano et al. 2021), all of which represent opportunities for actionable and impactful work for future scholars. Furthermore, as digital platforms enter highly social and institutionalized sectors, more research is needed to examine their social impact as well as their effects on community and social welfare. Finally, platform governance itself remains an interesting area for future research. In our own context, we are not able to explicitly account for Airbnb’s guid ance provided to guests about noise, for instance, but it is worth considering how influential the platform itself is in terms of generating prosocial outcomes and reducing negative spillover effects (Lobel 2016). Not all participants are obliged to adhere to these guidelines, but to the extent that digital platforms allow greater visibility into participant behavior through IT, negative externalities can be reduced while nurturing positive effects, especially on outcomes like noise where objective measurement may be more feasible. Empirically, the models in our work here can be extended by using individual, residential-level data in collaboration with the company (Airbnb) to generate further insights into the heteroge neity of effects we observe at the aggregate level. These questions fall directly into extant research and represent interesting areas of inquiry.
