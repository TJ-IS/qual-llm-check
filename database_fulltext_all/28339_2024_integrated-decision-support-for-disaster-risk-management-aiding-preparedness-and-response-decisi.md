Abstract. A central challenge in disaster risk management (DRM) is that there are key dependencies and uncertainty between the decisions made at the mitigation, preparedness, response, and recovery stages. Evaluating the impact of strategic decisions on the decisions and outcomes of subsequent stages is paramount to determine informed risk management policies (e.g., estimating the risk reduction that may be achieved by a mitigation strategy relative to its cost). Performing such analyses is difficult, not only because of the uncertainty inherent to disastrous events but because it implies integrating the logic and data of different processes that occur at each stage. Comprehensive decision support systems for disaster management and thus require information systems that allow timely and reliable integration of data sources from different domains, including information on hazards and vulnerabilities for risk analysis and organizational and logistical information for decision analysis. We propose an analytics-centered framework that integrates predictive and pre scriptive models responding to unique characteristics of DRM. The framework relies on probabilistic risk assessment and uses optimization-based simulation of the response phase as a means to inform decisions at the preparedness stage. This paper presents a case study regarding the analysis of preparedness and response decisions for wildfire control in Uruguay. Numerical results illustrate the insights that can be derived from the integration of data and models at multiple stages. Specifically, in the Uruguay case, slight reductions in the preparedness budget can lead to disproportionate losses during the response stage, whereas slight increases have little effect unless explicitly directed to control high consequence scenarios. Motivated by a real-world problem, this case study emphasizes the challenges for integrated information systems that enable the potential of analytical deci sion support frameworks for DRM.

## 1. Introduction

Disaster risk management (DRM) is a complex problem that involves the interaction of interrelated systems, including the natural environment (e.g., earth and atmospheric phenomena), the built environment (e.g., infrastructure and technological systems), and organizational systems $( \mathrm { e . g . , }$ , communities, authorities, responders), which add a layer of socio-cultural, economic, and political aspects that shape DRM decisions.

We will use the term system-of-systems (SoS) referring to this characteristic, especially including cyber-physicalsocial systems (CPSS), which explicitly account for the pervasiveness of information systems. At the core, DRM deals with high uncertainty and low-probability, highconsequence events that challenge the business-as-usual intuition. Risk-informed decision making is thus a necessary frame to ponder the value of efforts toward resilience of the overall SoS.

DRM involves different stages before and after a disruptive event. Figure 1 outlines typical decisions in the mitigation, preparedness, response, and recovery stages of DRM. These questions (further explored in Section 2) cover strategic, tactical, and operational actions around a potentially (even if unlikely) major disruptive event, providing a sense of the multiple time scales, uncertainties, interests, and dependencies of different decision problems. A key challenge of DRM is the need to rationally evaluate tradeoffs and efficiently execute courses of action to manage risk at a societal level. Particularly, pre-event decisions are crucial, as they have the potential to influence the impact of an eventual disruption and the capacity to adequately react to it.

A central challenge in DRM is to make long-term, highcost decisions under uncertainty, rationally weighing tradeoffs to justify and communicate risk management decisions. It is as difficult as it is paramount to assess the “adequate effort” that ought to be invested in the present to avoid or control consequences that may only materialize far in the future, even raising issues of sustainability and resilience (MacKenzie et al. 2014). On the one hand, the relative low frequency of catastrophic events can induce decision makers to prioritize current, more tangible needs than risk prevention. On the other hand, because the outcome of successful risk prevention actions is that risks do not materialize (i.e., nothing happens), preemptive actions become further disincentivized. Unless the reduced harm of risk management actions is credibly communicated, tangible investments (e.g., a new road) may lead to opportunity costs that do not favor prevention.

One of the roles of information systems (IS), and particularly decisions support systems (DSS), in DRM is to assist high-ranking officials with evidence to support decisions for which they are accountable and which may have profound social, environmental, political, and economic consequences. For instance, IS/DSS can guide officials to take controversial decisions, such as funding disaster preparedness programs instead of investing in new infrastructure or mandating preven tive lockdowns at the expense of economic activity. IS and DSS can support DRM as long as they can identify and respond to key stakeholders’ questions by making expert knowledge and tools accessible and usable for decision makers. DRM demands the articulation of knowledge from a wide spectrum of disciplines, broadly including engineering and natural and social sciences, thus making it inherently chal lenging to develop unified frameworks toward holistic IS/DSS for DRM. Design science provides a usefu paradigm to ideate, prototype, and validate IS/DSS artifacts (Gregor and Hevner 2013, Abbasi et al. 2016) that fulfill stakeholder needs and user requirements based on domain-specific toolboxes from the disciplines involved in DRM.

The use of data and quantitative analyses to inform decisions (nowadays popularized as analytics) provides a discipline-agnostic framework to address stakeholder questions in DRM processes and articulate expert knowledge from the disciplines involved in different stages and layers of the problem (e.g., natural, physical, operational, social, cyber). Moreover, the impact of analytics across industries has made IS/DSS increasingly appealing and accessible for organizations, providing an opportunity to reach decision makers in DRM. However, successful analytics solutions require the incorporation of domain-specific rules. In this sense, an analytics approach to DRM should articulate information about: how hazards may realize and evolve; how the natural and built environment may be affected by hazards; how different actors value the assets, systems, and communities exposed to hazards; and how authorities and communities implement policies and actions to avoid and mitigate risk, as well as prepare, respond, and recover from disruptions. The focus on stakeholder questions is a virtue of analytics that, paired with domainspecific expertise, can enable a meaningful integration of knowledge for decision makers in a problem as complex as DRM.

We address the problem of assessing the impact of pre-event decisions on postevent consequences in DRM by using an analytics framework to integrate elements from different facets (and disciplines) of DRM. The problem framing phase is fed with concepts, processes, and metrics from risk and resilience of complex systems, which guide and provide meaning to the dataand model-related tasks. Based on this, we exploit the parallel of the risk assessment and risk management processes in risk analysis with predictive and prescriptive analytics techniques. Although predictive analytics seeks to estimate what could happen in the future, prescriptive analytics deals with determining the most adequate course of action.

The interplay between predictive and prescriptive techniques is common in the stochastic optimization field, where simulation and optimization models are integrated to assess the downstream effects of decisions throughout future scenarios. We emphasize the importance of integrating predictive and prescriptive models for multistage decisions in DRM under the lens of the unified framework for stochastic optimization proposed in Powell (2019). Particularly, we rely on the notion of lookahead approximation, where we assess the goodness of the current decision by modeling the decisions that would follow at future stages (and their outcomes). Our DRM analytics framework relies on probabilistic hazard assessment models (a standard practice in the risk engineering community) as a predictive module that informs about the occurrence and evolution of a hazard of interest; modeling the interrelated DRM processes and dynamics at stages of interest; and two-stage decision problem structures, as a prescriptive module, measuring the quality of current decisions based on estimations of their downstream effects.

Specifically, we instantiate the proposed framework in the context of a wildfire management problem in Uruguay (South America), in which we seek to determine the impact of decisions at the preparedness stage on the overall performance of the response stage. We analyze how the location of facilities and allocation of resources for wildfire response affect suppression activities, considering the time to contain fires and the costs associated with operations and damage. Following the proposed framework, we model the ignition and propagation of wildfires to generate scenarios of wildfire seasons and estimate the number of resources and demand sites that must be reached to contain the fires (predictive module). Then, based on the idea of forecasting decisions through scenarios, we build a model that articulates decisions at preparedness and response stages of wildfire management. The proposed approach integrates descriptive, predictive, and prescriptive modules throughout the processes, time scales, and scenarios relevant to the problem. As a result, we formulate a risk-informed optimization problem to evaluate how location and allocation decisions impact the step-bystep process of suppression as wildfires propagate (prescriptive module).

We validate our instantiation based on the use-case of the Uruguay wildfire management problem, evaluating how the overall framework fulfills a set of requirements and how the implemented modules add to the decision support capabilities. The proposed instantiation demonstrates the suitability of the proposed framework to address questions regarding tradeoffs between different levels of investment in preparedness and their associated consequences at the response stage, considering budget constraints and risk control features (i.e., how costly it is to avoid certain loss). For instance, we are able to determine whether additional investments are worthwhile in terms of risk reduction, or conversely, whether part of the budget can be directed elsewhere while maintaining an acceptable risk.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 provides an overview of the research relevant to the design of DSS for DRM, focusing on the elements that allow the development of a specialized DRM analytics framework. Section 3 describes the design of the proposed framework, whereas 4 defines a specific instantiation to assist coupled decisions at the preparedness and response stages of wildfire management. Section 5 presents a validation exercise for the proposed instantiation based on a case study of wildfire suppression in Uruguay. Section 6 concludes and presents a discussion of limitations and future work.

## 2. Related Work

Disaster risk management implies making decisions under uncertainty, urgency, limited resources, and potentially overwhelming demand (Holgu´ın-Veras et al. 2012), let alone that operations often must be carried out amidst the disruption of essential services. The lack of adequate response capacity when disasters strike may lead to significant losses, thus putting pressure on pre-event decisions and calling for integrated decision support throughout DRM stages. In this section, we provide an overview of risk and resilience of SoS as an area that provides domain-specific knowledge about DRM due to its focus on disruptions at a societal level (Section 2.1). We also review efforts in the design and development of IS/DSSs in the context of DRM to extract design principles that inform the development of a framework to address DRM (Section 2.2). Finally, we review analytics-related techniques available to support decisions in wildfire management (Section 2.3). As a result, we identify ideas developed in different domains dealing with several aspects of DRM, including modeling hazards and systems, managing DRM operations, and the design of IS/DSS, which we integrate in the framework proposed in Section 3.

## 2.1. Risk and Resilience of Complex Systems

Disasters are of foremost importance for those who build and operate large-scale systems that provide essential services for society (e.g., transportation or energy networks). Potentially major disruptions must be considered in their design and operation, as they are meant to endure throughout large life cycles. The ideas within risk analysis, life cycle analysis, and (more recently) interdependent networks have shaped risk engineering research to the point where the notion of sustainability and resilience of cyber-physical-social systems (Fiksel 2003, Frangopol et al. 2012, Ellingwood et al. 2016, Barker et al. 2017) is becoming an umbrella for the several communities concerned with the impact of disruptions of essential systems in an interconnected world. Sustainability, in the context of disaster, implies being able to adapt to a changing environment, not only avoiding or containing hazards, but coevolving with them as an act of responsibility with future generations (Gillespie-Marthaler et al. 2019).

Understanding how hazards occur and evolve and how they may impact systems of interest is at the core of DRM. Probabilistic risk assessment has been traditionally used in engineering systems (Faber and Stewart 2003), integrating physical and statistical models of hazards with physics- and network-based models of the exposed facilities or components to understand the likelihood and extent of a range of consequences. In this sense, risk analysis constitutes a suitable framework for DRM, as it articulates concepts and processes of risk assessment, management, and communication, which use the knowledge on the hazard and the system to guide organizations in addressing risk. DRM inevitably involves risk-informed decision making as a means of dealing with high uncertainty (Pate´-Cornell 2012, Shortridge et al. 2017), scarce resources (Dillon et al. 2003), and complex interactions between natural and technological systems (Steinberg et al. 2008).

Addressing risks at the societal level has led to a focus on large-scale interdependent systems, such as infrastructure (Duen˜ as-Osorio et al. 2007, Barker and Haimes 2009) and global supply chains (MacKenzie et al. 2014), focusing on their long-term capacity to withstand and quickly recover from disruptions, as they support communities, businesses, and government (Ouyang et al. 2012, McAllister et al. 2019). Designing for the life cycle of these systems implies considering the occurrence of, and capacity to respond to, rare events (Dillon et al. 2003), anticipating how the actions required at different stages of DRM are to be implemented. Complex logistical challenges arise because planning must be made under wide uncertainty (at mitigation and pre paredness stages), and execution occurs amidst disruption (at response and recovery stages). A few examples include prioritizing mitigation actions under uncertainty (Gomez and Baker 2019), allocating resources for disaster response against competing interests (Chen et al. 2008), supporting preparation and training to provide timely response and relief amidst the disruption (Boin and McConnell 2007), and recovering essential services efficiently (Gonza´lez et al. 2016, Gomez et al. 2019).

Barker et al. (2017) captures a compelling trend toward resilience analytics, discussing the use of analytical models in pursuing the resilience of interdepen dent cyber-physical-social systems. Although a variety of models have proven to be useful in specific problems in DRM, challenges remain in terms of data integration and organizationally friendly solutions. Translating the use of data and models into actionable insight about genuine stakeholders’ concerns is the essence of analytics solutions but requires thorough consideration of domain-specific logic. Ouyang et al. (2012) provide key definitions of the stages involved in resilience, whereas Hosseini et al. (2016) provide a comprehensive review of resilience measures, which can inform the design of IS/DSS for DRM. For instance, measures such as the costs associated with loss of service or time to system restoration can be drivers of DRM analyses.

## 2.2. Decision Support Systems in DRM

Efforts in the design of information and decision support systems for different DRM stages shed light about design principles toward actionable analytics approaches, as called for by Barker et al. (2017) in the resilience community and Thompson et al. (2019) in the wildfire management domain. Sakurai and Murayama (2019) review the application of information technologies in DRM, highlighting the essential role they play in recording, exchanging, and processing information, and emphasizing the need for integration of information systems and decision support tools across different stages of DRM to enable the coordination of involved actors. In a similar direction, Horita et al. (2017) identify the challenge of mapping the key decision tasks of DRM with available data sources, adopting an observationaware decision model and notation approach to support the decisions of a disaster management authority. Othman and Beydoun (2013) identify a range of models that are relevant in DRM and propose a metamodel that provides a unified view of common concepts and actions applied in various disasters. Yang et al. (2012) discuss the design of an information platform for emergency response, identifying design principles that include assuring end-user involvement, providing a servicecentered system architecture, adopting component-based design, implementing simultaneous prototyping and evaluation, and conceiving visualization for situation awareness.

Thompson et al. (2019) discuss the parallel between risk analysis processes and the use of descriptive, predictive, and prescriptive analytical models, providing a semantic ground for end-users to understand models in terms of the questions “what did happen,” “what could happen,” and “what should happen,” with examples of problems of each type in wildfire management. Martell (2015) provides a comprehensive review on DSS for wildfire management, with Ro¨nnqvist et al. (2023, 2015) emphasizing the body of work on operations research models and techniques proposed at the stages of prevention, detection, suppression, and large fire management. Mavsar et al. (2013) discusses the economic models used for assigning resources for wildfire management, arguing that DSS must include the negative and positive socioeconomic impacts of fire, which is often absent in practice due to unavailable data and ecosystemic studies. Dunn et al. (2017) provide a comprehensive discussion of the objectives, decision variables, and constraints used for a variety of decision problems in wildfire management, as they propose a decision support framework building on the literature on operations research techniques for different fire management problems. Besides acknowledging the latent potential for DRM analytics, two common issues in these works are as follows: the need to adopt integral approaches to fire management (beyond the emphasis on suppression), adequately weighing the benefits and harms of fires by valuing nonmarket assets and the ecosystemic impact of fires, and the need to provide decision makers with tools that articulate decisions at different stages of DRM.

The work of Noble and Paveglio (2020) is particularly important as it discusses the adoption of DSS for wildfire management from the perspective of end users, allowing to draw lessons from practitioners that can be used as design principles, such as pursuing timeliness and ease of use and implementation, not overriding the experience and expertise of managers, and relying on the support of the management and training of personnel. Va´squez et al. (2021) review an extensive base of DSSs for wildfire management, identifying that most works are on detection, monitoring, and prediction, with opportunity for contribution on simulation, prevention, and management, and an overarching need for user-friendly DSSs and visualizations to make the trend on the use of big data accessible for decision makers. As examples of these principles, Lourenc¸o et al. (2021) propose a DSS that emphasizes visualization and situationawareness based on Geographic Information System to provide in-field information almost in real time; Gonzalez-Mathiesen et al. (2021) review Australian systems that support wildfire management from a perspective of spatial planning; Inglis and Vukomanovic (2020) focus on visualization of complex data for wildfires, producing a dashboard that processes data about fire generation from different sources in nearly real time; and Chen et al. (2013) developed a systematic data model that captures and standardizes responsecritical information, highlighting information sharing and data consistency for effective emergency response.

## 2.3. Techniques for (Wildfire) DRM

Abundant research works have focused on problems regarding strategic, operational, and tactical aspects related to the implementation of actions at all stages of DRM, including resource allocation, scheduling and planning, and humanitarian and recovery logistics, among others (Altay and Green 2006, Galindo and Batta 2013). Wildfires pose a distinctive challenge due to the particular urgency of dealing with fire, given the dynamic and continuously threatening nature of the propagation process, and the specific techniques devised to counter the fire. Because of such specificity, much of the literature on wildfire control focuses on postevent tactical aspects in the response phase, often leaving long-term decisions and investments such as the (re)-location of fire stations out of scope. Although these assumptions make sense for the intended purpose (e.g., aiding firefighting units), broader and coupled decision problems arise when dealing with risk management at a strategic level (e.g., can a region become more resilient to wildfires by investing in smarter configurations of response units?).

Several techniques have been applied to address specific challenges related to wildfire risk management drawing from operations research (Minas et al. 2012, Martell 2015). Simulation and mathematical programming are among the most common techniques applied to support decisions that minimize operation costs, response time, or both. Some of these models focus on strategic decisions, seeking to preposition resources at existing (rather than new or modified) facilities, whereas others focus on tactical decisions involving the distribution of resources or the deployment of firelines for wildfire control. Research on wildfire control can be grouped into several categories according to the choice of solution technique (e.g., simulation, optimization), the type of decision of interest (e.g., prepositioning, facility location, distribution), the decision scope (e.g., strategic, tactical), and other problem considerations (e.g., whether geographic or fire propagation conditions are accounted for). In Online Appendix A (particularly A.2), we present a brief taxonomy of models used in the wildfire literature, including additional references for the interested reader. Henceforth, we focus on a selection of works most closely related to the instantiation proposed in Section 5.

A common technique for wildfire control are the firelines that stop fire propagation. A major research area is devoted to models that assist explicit fireline construction. Wei et al. (2008) and Belval et al. (2016) develop models to support decisions about where to intervene the forest to minimize propagation. Mees et al. (1994), Martin-Ferna´ndez et al. (2002), Hu and Ntaimo (2009), and Homchaudhuri et al. (2013) propose methodologies for placing firelines to stop the wildfire, thus minimizing the affected area. These methodologies include the use of simulation and optimization, allowing achievement of nonlinear geometries for firelines due to the versatility provided by heuristic algorithms. These works are relevant to our approach, as fireline construction is one of the aspects that determine the type and number of necessary resources to respond to wildfires.

A variety of models have been proposed to address problems at the preparedness and response phases in wildfire management. With regards to response decisions, Pappis and Rachaniotis (2010a, b) propose a scheduling problem to control a set of wildfires with the objective of maximizing the forest areas prevented from burning. In terms of preparedness, Dimopoulou and Giannikos (2001) propose a model based on deploying fire-fighting resources in critical locations to control wildfire as soon as possible. They use a maximal covering model to select the best locations for resources, considering a classification of subregions depending on the type of slope, and the type of fuel located in the area of interest, to evaluate the terrain and the level of coverage needed. Zhang et al. (2020) propose a model to decide how to allocate resources, based on the propagation speed of wildfires, using a vehicle routing problem (VRP) to schedule wildfire attention. Although in conjunction these models address preparedness and response, no articulation is made between the two problems at once.

Some authors have focused on solving the resource location problem by using covering models. The work developed by Zeferino (2020) uses maximum expected covering to choose the best locations for aerial resources considering available airbases and reachable locations from each of those bases. Sakellariou et al. (2020) propose a methodology of simulation and optimization to estimate the wildfire probabilities and the spatial planning of fire agency forces, respectively. These works include the planning of facility locations, but they do not include the time to control wildfire, that is, the time from the facility to the propagating wildfire. Some works in the wildfire literature incorporate postevent considerations in preparedness decisions. Haight and

Fried (2007) simultaneously minimize the resources assigned at the beginning of the planning horizon and the number of wildfires that do not receive standard response (i.e., wildfires that exceed the demand for prepositioned resources). Lee et al. (2013) develop an optimization model with stochastic simulation for prepositioning of inventory for wildfire control, taking into account budget constraints and the capacity of facilities. In contrast to the approach proposed in this paper, these works do not incorporate costs related to the response phase, such as transportation or inventory costs, nor do they account for wildfire dynamics.

## 3. Framework for DRM Analytics

The adoption of analytics approaches to DRM is a compelling idea emerging in parallel communities (Barker et al. 2017, Thompson et al. 2019) and requires embracing a set of domain-specific processes, requirements, and metrics that guide the use of analytical models to support decisions in a relevant way. Figure 2 illustrates a parallel between core processes of analytics and risk analysis. Although methodologies such as Analytics Solutions Unified Method for Data Mining (Ange´e et al. 2018) describe analytics processes in more detail, we use a broad scheme to focus on how core concepts in risk analysis can inform the analytics problem framing stages to provide the domain specificity necessary for DRM (Section 3.1). Specifically, the interactive processes of risk assessment and management provide a way of articulating different types of models according to how they contribute to DRM (Section 3.2). Finally, the deployment and adoption of analytics solutions must account for issues such as risk communication and cognitive biases on top of considerations related to technology and usability (Section 3.3).

## 3.1. Framing the Analytics Problem for DRM

Risk analysis processes, such as system and hazard identification, risk assessment, management, and communication are ineludible components for any IS/DSS for DRM. Also, the implementation of DRM actions implies organizational and logistical decision problems at strategic, operational, and tactical levels. Although mathematical and computational models can significantly expand risk analysis, true impact on DRM practice requires the design of decision support tools that capture stakeholders’ needs. In the same line, the adoption of IS/DSS for DRM requires that end users can see direct applicability and credibly consume insights in a context in which biases and uncertainty can undermine adequate risk communication. These considerations shape the design of the proposed framework for DRM analytics at a general level.

Risk management actions include attempting to reduce exposure or vulnerability (and then accept or transfer residual risk) or to prepare for when (not if) risks do materialize. Figure 1 provides concrete examples in line with these actions and the questions stated previously. Before the disruptive event, questions revolve around whether to invest in reducing exposure (e.g., relocate communities), reducing vulnerability (e.g., strengthen systems’ components), or preparing for postevent activities (e.g., training personnel, prepositioning resources). After the disruptive event, priorities focus on humanitarian aspects (i.e., disaster relief), along with restoring essential services as soon as possible, posing complex operational problems that heavily rely on pre-event decisions. Adequately addressing these questions relies on information on hazard-related and other uncertainties, which makes risk assessment a crucial component of DRM.

The purpose and challenges of DRM decision problems change substantially by stage and so should the corresponding models and metrics. Pre-event problems are difficult mostly due to the uncertainty about the moment, location, and magnitude associated with hazard realizations (i.e., planning for the unknown). In that case, models must exploit available data (e.g., weather) to reduce uncertainty in characterizing the hazard and its potential effects and consequently may rely on metrics such as expected loss, conditional value at risk, or exceedance curves, which capture the effect of hazard-related uncertainty and how it may be altered with mitigation and preparedness actions. Postevent decisions face enormous pressure to act quickly (e.g., to save lives and restore essential services) amidst disruption-induced constraints and uncertainties. In that case, models must address operational and tactical aspects of response and recovery; thus, data and models should pursue situation awareness and key metrics include time-to-recover, service interruption cost, and operational and loss-related costs to account for the effect of disruptions on communities and the natural and built environments.

Problem framing implies identifying requirements in the form of problems or questions that stakeholders need to solve. According to Section 2, a set of core ques tions for DRM are as follows: (i) how hazards may real ize and evolve?; (ii) how natural and built systems may be affected by hazards?; (iii) how different actors value the assets, systems, and communities exposed to hazards?; and (iv) how authorities and communities plan and implement policies and actions to avoid and mitigate risk, as well as prepare for, respond to, and recover from disruptions? Addressing these questions from an analytics perspective implies putting together models that address hazard assessment, system performance, economic valuation, and logistics in a way that makes sense for stakeholders and end users of the analytics solution. The discussion thus far allows to define design principles that guide our proposal of a decision analytics framework for DRM, echoing similar efforts in formalizing design artifacts (Yang et al. 2012, Seidel et al. 2018). Consequently, IS/DSS for DRM must

• Recognize the significant impact of hazard-related uncertainty, considering the probabilistic nature of the tradeoffs between prevention efforts and observed consequences;

• Incorporate the dependence between pre- and postevent decisions, which demands evaluating the downstream effects of decisions through DRM stages at strategic and operational levels;

• Propose choices of models, techniques, and user interfaces which are aligned with genuine stakeholders questions guiding situated requirements for each DRM stage (as exemplified in Figure 1); and

• Adopt validation and socialization exercises that honor risk communication and risk-informed decision support principles (e.g., in terms of high-percentiles or tails of distributions rather than analysis based on expected values, as the interest is in preparing for lowprobability high-consequence scenarios rather than doing well on average).

## 3.2. Model Selection and Implementation

Models are at the core of analytics approaches. In DRM, the role of descriptive models is to characterize the situation, for instance, in terms of current hazard imminency (when applicable), asset and community exposure, current state of systems of interest, available resources, and crew location and status, among others. Beyond descriptive aspects, we exploit the parallel of the risk assessment and risk management processes in risk analysis with the purpose of predictive and prescriptive analytics techniques, respectively. Although the former intends to estimate what could happen in the future, the latter deals with determining the most adequate course of action. DRM analytics thus can leverage predictive and prescriptive models to implement the risk assessment and management processes by seeking the most adequate courses of action under uncertainty. The term “most adequate” is used deliberately to denote several ways in which decisions could be valued in risk-informed decision making. The integration of predictive and prescriptive techniques is prevalent in stochastic optimization communities, achieving breakthroughs across industries by using probabilistic and statistical/ machine-learning models along with simulation to interactively inform optimization problems about the downstream effects of decisions throughout future scenarios. In particular, we rely on the notion of lookahead approximation (Powell 2019), conveying the idea of modeling the decisions that would be made at future stages (and their outcomes) as a means to approximately assess the goodness of the current decision (as is common in approximate dynamic programming).

Following these principles, our framework uses probabilistic hazard assessment models assembled as a predictive module that estimates the potential occurrence and evolution of a hazard of interest. Then, we model key processes of the DRM stages of interest to articulate the decision problems stated in requirements, namely, whether the problem is to prioritize retrofits of a water supply network, or to deploy disaster relief supplies; this leads to modeling different processes and uncertainties under different goals, decisions, and metrics, thus inducing different model choices. Finally, we adopt a two-stage decision problem structure, as a prescriptive module that suits the dynamics of preand postevent stages of DRM, measuring the quality of current decisions based on estimations of their downstream effects and incorporating communicable insights about nonintuitive, risk-related tradeoffs. These components capture the risk-informed, multistage nature of DRM. Formulating decision problems across

DRM stages is challenging as there are multiple time scales and sources of uncertainty. Mitigation actions (e.g., building landslide barriers) may take weeks or months and occur under hazard-related uncertainty, whereas response time frames can be as critical as minutes (e.g., in rescue activities) and occur under disruptionrelated constraints and uncertainty (e.g., failures in communication and transportation systems, rapid changes, unreliable data sources).

## 3.3. Deployment and Life Cycle Management

Models alone rarely fulfill the requirements of analytics projects because data readiness and user adoption issues are challenging. The spread nature of DRMrelevant data sources makes it challenging to conceive solutions with streamed data pipelines enabling realtime decision support. This has been identified as a primary bottleneck for DRM analytics solutions (Barker et al. 2017, Thompson et al. 2019), with data often being disparate in terms of completeness, accuracy, consistency, validity, uniqueness, and integrity. Fulfilling the established requirements would imply the capacity to access and integrate data concerning environmental phenomena, real-time monitoring of critical systems, economic information about exposure and coverage of assets, and managerial and logistical information about response capacity, among other types of data.

A central issue when deploying analytics solutions is adoption. A necessary (albeit not sufficient) condition for IS/DSS to generate impact in practice is that their form and function resonate with end user needs. As highlighted in the literature (Dunn et al. 2017, Noble and Paveglio 2020), successful adoption of DSS for DRM relies on tangible evidence of the practical value or applicability of the solution; demonstration of the credibility of the solution (mostly when sensible decisions are at stake); and overall ease of implementation, in terms of both technological burden and staff training effort for organizations.

The specific case of DRM calls for thorough treatment of risk communication issues, considering that probabilistic thinking is challenging for humans and affected by cognitive biases (Kahneman and Tversky 1996), mostly when considering rare events (Taleb 2007). The way information is presented can have a tremendous impact on sensible decisions under uncertainty (Alexander 2014), for instance, by misrepresenting the odds of events, their magnitudes (e.g., logarithmic scales are common in DRM) or obscuring the interpretation of concepts like discount factors, return periods, exceedance curves, confidence intervals, and epistemic uncertainties. DRM relies on people (e.g., high-ranking officials, emergency managers) making, and being accountable for, hard choices. DSS must provide evi dence and tools, not only to pursue desirable outcomes but to support decision rationales even when outcomes are (inevitably) bad.

## 4. Instantiation of the Framework in Wildfire Management

We instantiate the proposed framework in the context of wildfire management problems in Uruguay (South America), seeking to determine the impact of decisions at the preparedness stage on the performance of the response stage. Specifically, we analyze how the location and allocation of facilities and resources for wildfire response affect suppression activities, considering the time to contain fires along with the overall cost of operations and damage. The remaining of this section discuss how each component of the proposed framework is specialized for the wildfire management problem and provides details of the information used as input for the Uruguayan use-case.

## 4.1. Framing the Analytics Problem

Wildfires represent a threat to human settlements and biodiversity, and the fact that fires propagate rapidly motivates DRM actions aimed to contain damage effectively. Australia’s 2019/2020 fire season burnt almost 19 million hectares, destroyed more than 3,000 houses, and killed 33 people (Filkov et al. 2020). The Camp Fire in California 2018 caused 85 fatalities and nearly 19,000 structures were destroyed (Brown et al. 2020). Moreover, loss of forest areas compromises human sources of clean air and other important resources (World Wildlife Fund 2017). Far from being exceptional events, wildfires are constantly burning across the globe. According to Brushlinsky et al. (2018), a total of 485,265 wildfire events were reported in 2016 alone for a group of 20 countries. This work is part of ongoing collaborative efforts with risk management practitioners and local authorities in pursuit of a framework to support comprehensive decisions in wildfire management.

Wildfire control is a process in which firefighters stop fire propagation by means of indirect or direct attacks. Once a wildfire event occurs, prepositioned resources such as water, hand tools, fire trucks, or retardant chemicals are deployed from storage facilities to the wildfire location to start a direct or indirect attack. Because of fire propagation, short response time is critical to successful wildfire control; thus, the location and supply of storage facilities becomes a key driver of response effectiveness. The definition of such strategic locations is challenging, as it depends on uncertain information about wildfire ignition sites and propagation dynamics, which must be integrated into the evaluation of pre-event resource allocation schemes that enable an adequate postevent response. In practice, the information systems that enable hazard and risk assessment are rarely integrated with the organizational, economic, and logistical information on which authorities rely for adequate response. Moreover, information systems for disaster management are often incipient and not interoperable in parts of the developing world where high exposure and vulnerabilities to hazards should call for the opposite (Altay and Labonte 2014).

Uruguay has an extension of $\overline { { 1 7 6 , 2 1 5 } } \mathrm { k m } ^ { 2 }$ and wildfire seasons ranging from late November to mid-April. The number of wildfires is around 835 per season (Global Fire Monitoring Center 2016), and although most are relatively easy to contain, we focus on some that are of special attention due to their magnitude. Considering our focus on assessing the impact of decisions at the preparedness stage on the performance of the response stage (rather than supporting actual response decisions), the general DRM requirements (end user questions) can be specialized for the case of wildfire management as follows. (i) What fires are we expected to deal with? (ii) How do wildfires propagate and how to contain them accordingly? (iii) How much resource needs to be mobilized to attend a given fire season? (iv) What would be an efficient yet responsible deployment of resources to prepare for wildfire response? (v) How effective would the response be for a given wildfire season under a given allocation of resources? (vi) How costly could it be to prepare for high-consequence scenarios (or conversely, to cut costs in prevention)?

## 4.2. Selection and Implementation of Analytical Models

Although most of the stated questions involve the use of a mix of descriptive, predictive, and prescriptive models, the nature of questions (i), (ii), and (iii) is to estimate what could happen (i.e., predictive analysis), whereas questions (iv), (v), and (vi) evaluate possible courses of action (i.e., prescriptive analysis). Other requirements are the ease of use and sensible risk-communication capabilities, as well as data- and resource-constrained development. We propose a predictive module (Section 4.2.1) with three components, addressing questions (i), (ii), and (iii), respectively: wildfire simulation, fireline estimation, and demand estimation. The prescriptive module (Section 4.2.2) evaluates how location/allocation decisions impact suppression activities based on three components, addressing questions (iv), (v), and (vi), respectively: lookahead approximation for response, two-stage resource allocation, and risk control. Figure 3 synthesizes the elements that comprise the predictive and prescriptive mod ules in this instantiation.

## 4.2.1. Predictive Module.

4.2.1.1. Wildfire Simulation. We determine the ignition and propagation for each wildfire and build a set of fires that are representative of a wildfire season for the area of interest. This estimation is achieved by using the wildfire module of Next Generation CAPRA to generate a probabilistic hazard assessment (Bernal and Cardona 2018). This approach consists of assessing the potential of occurrence, spread, and extinction of wildfires in multiple climates and regions considering the geographical distribution of vegetation fuel and moisture, terrain characteristics, and wind fields. The model randomly simulates thousands of nonpreviously observed wildfires to build a probability domain for the impact. Details of the wildfire generation procedure are presented in Online Appendix B.1. The output of this component is a collection of simulated wildfires, including their ignition site and propagation polygons throughout a set of discrete time steps. These wildfires are used to estimate the size of the firelines and, in turn, the number of resources that would be needed to contain the fire at any time step: This will inform prescriptive models about the consequences of reaching fires at different time steps.

For the case of Uruguay, we generate a collection of wildfire realizations that is consistent with the climate and topographical conditions of the region. The simulation considers the topography, the type of vegetation, an ignition risk map, and climate conditions of Uruguay as the primary input. From the Ministry of Housing, Territorial Planning and Environment of Uruguay (MVOTMA), we gathered vegetation cover data. 4.2.1.2. Fireline and Demand Estimation. One tactic to control wildfires is the construction of firelines (i.e., barriers where the fuel is removed to prevent the propagation of a fire). We adopt the model proposed by Homchaudhuri et al. (2013) as one of several available choices to determine the fireline shapes, mainly due to its straightforward logic. Their model (a genetic algorithm) receives a wildfire propagation pattern through time and the number of crews working on the fireline. The output is the fireline shape for each time step at which each wildfire could be reached by the crews. This component serves a lookahead approximation purpose as it provides a reasonable forecast of a future decision (i.e., a good fireline geometry per wildfire per time step) allowing to estimate resource allocation needs at the cur rent stage (preparedness). Details of the fireline estimation procedure are presented in Online Appendix B.2.

For the case of Uruguay, we determine the number of resources and locations where they would be needed for each wildfire and scenario and for the different time steps at which fires may be reached. Our adaptation of the genetic algorithm in Homchaudhuri et al. (2013) provides fireline shapes for all wildfires and time steps. We considered five parallel crews $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ five demand points can be attended at once), but this parameter is meant to be adjusted by decision makers when planning for investments. The size of the crew is defined dynamically depending on wildfire size as follows: First, we estimate the approximate length of the complete fireline; then, we estimate the size of the crew by using the fireline production rates available in USDA (2011) (although these are in the context of the United States, we use them for Uruguay due to lack of a better source). We start with a 10-person crew and keep adding firefighters until the time to complete the fireline is less than 12 hours. For the genetic algorithm, we used 20 generations for each wildfire, with a population size of 101 individuals, and six time steps of 1.25 hours each; we adjusted the parameters from Homchaudhuri et al. (2013) and chose values for population and generations which enabled convergence.

4.2.1.3. Scenario Generation. Preparedness decisions ought to consider the actions required to respond to a wildfire season rather than an individual fire. Hence, we define a scenario as a realization of an entire wildfire season, defined by a probabilistic selection of individual wildfires from the wildfire simulation, providing a collection of ignition sites and propagation patterns per scenario. The underlying assumption is that simulated wildfires are a representative sample of real wildfires.

For the case of Uruguay, the scenario definition phase led to 1,000 wildfire seasons, with an average of 40 wildfires per season. The probability of choosing a single scenario was fixed at 40 of 139, because we aim at a mean of 40 wildfires per scenario from a pool of 139 representative realizations of Uruguayan wildfires. The main limitation in this regard is that these realizations are conceived as independent, which is likely not the case; the generation of time-space–dependent wildfires is left for future work.

## 4.2.2. Prescriptive Module

4.2.2.1. Lookahead Approximation for Response. We propose a graph that allows modeling wildfire propagation and flows of resources to reach fireline construction sites throughout a set of locations, time steps, and scenarios (a time-scenario expanded graph). Although this approach may not offer the granularity required to support actual response operations, it enables a wellinformed estimation of resource utilization and logistic performance during suppression activities, providing evidence for preparedness decisions in a scalable way, as it allows to embed key aspects of response within the optimization model that supports preparedness actions.

A time-expanded graph is one in which physical nodes are replicated over several time steps to capture time-evolving attributes; in this case, the evolution of the fire spread (see Online Appendix B.3 for the mathematical formulation). We include potential facility locations as source nodes and potential fireline construction sites as demand nodes; these physical nodes are repli cated in the graph throughout time steps and scenarios to account for the dynamic and stochastic nature of propagation and suppression. Prepositioned resources may not always be enough to control the wildfire (e.g., due to poor planning or unusually large wildfires); hence, we represent unmet demand by adding penalized sink nodes (i.e., nodes where demand can be artifi cially satisfied at a large cost). Time steps represent a discretization of the elapsed time relative to each wildfire’s ignition and not the beginning of the wildfire season. This means that, in the model, the initial time step for all fires is zero, even if they occur at different moments in practice. The underlying assumption is that individual wildfires could be handled independently even if they occur simultaneously, which is not necessarily the case, as the availability or cost of personnel may vary in such cases, and fires may have complex correlated dynamics.

Figure 6 shows a graph for an instance with a set of nine wildfires (events), four scenarios (Thick frames), two facilities (Filled triangles), two time steps (at time 0.75 and 1.5), and two crews. To build the graph, we add one time step at time 0 to represent departures from the installed facilities, and one time step at the end to represent the sink nodes for any unmet demand. Filled circles represent demand points at different moments, Filled triangles represent potential facilities, and empty circles and empty triangles represent transition nodes. Demand nodes have different spatial locations (represented vertically) for time 0.75 and 1.5 due to the propagation of wildfires. Solid arrows represent holdover arcs, whereas dotted arcs represent movement arcs between two adjacent time steps (i.e., movements that take less than 0.75 hours), and dashed arcs represent movements that take between 0.75 and 1.5 hours to complete. For simplicity, just a subset of arcs is shown. We further expand the graph by replicating it for each simulated wildfire season, as preparedness decisions must account for not one but many possible response scenarios. At a given time step, several demand nodes represent the demand at that time with respect to the ignition of the wildfire. Time steps represent the time with respect to the ignition of each event, and they are not simultaneous since each wildfire could ignite at different time (day) of the wildfire season. The extra graph layers stacked behind represent how wildfire propagation graphs must be replicated per scenario. The number of wildfires per scenario may not always be the same; the potential locations for facilities (filled triangles) do not belong to any scenario, as this decision is made before their realization and is shared across scenarios.

For the case of Uruguay, we selected 34 potential places for facilities based on the main 34 cities/towns in the country (see triangles on Figure 4). We used the Open Source Routing Machine (Luxen and Vetter 2011) to calculate driving times from these locations to potential demand points and built a time-scenario–expanded graph (280,386 nodes; 5,804,521 arcs) with the estimated times and transportation costs between points of interest.

4.2.2.2. Two-Stage Decision Problem. We propose a two-stage stochastic optimization model to decide the number and location of facilities and their corresponding number of resources, responding to fireline construction needs throughout scenarios (based on the wildfire propagation provided by the time-scenario–expanded graph model). The objective function minimizes first-stage costs that include installing the facilities and prepositioning the initial inventory at each of them and the expected cost of controlling each scenario (second stage cost), including the cost of distributing resources, the excess costs (i.e., cost of maintaining inventory), and the shortage costs and the burnt area cost, depending on the time step at which the wildfire is reached. The complete formulation of the mathematical model is presented in Online Appendix B.4, which includes the described objective function (Equation (1)) and constraints that enact the following conditions (all equations refer to the online appendix).

• Allocated resources must not exceed the capacity of facilities; in case a facility is not installed, no resources can be prepositioned at that node (Equation (2))

• At most one type of facility can be installed at any node (Equation (3)).

• Flow balance must be honored throughout the timescenario–expanded graph (Equations (4), (5), and (6)), including flow balance at facilities that remain usable (not burned) under each scenario (Equation (4)), and modeling flow from facility or transition nodes to demand nodes (Equation (5)), and flow to sink nodes (Equation (6)).

• For a wildfire to be considered as controlled at a specific time step, all demand nodes from that time step must be reached (Equations (7) and (8)).

We used the proposed optimization model to determine facility and resource distribution schemes that balance preparedness and response costs, which include the consequences of not controlling fires effectively. The full set of parameters is presented in Online Appendix C.1, Table 3. We considered two types of facilities, small and large, with capacities of 2,500 and 6,500 units and installation costs of 30,000 and 60,000, respectively. These values were adequate for the demands estimated for the proposed instance, but more types and casespecific values can be considered. According to estimates made with local firefighters, we set the excess and shortage cost to be a quarter and four times the purchase cost, respectively, and we set the burnt forest cost as $\$ 1.2/\mathrm { m } ^ { 2 ^ { \prime } }$ (based on estimates with local authorities). These are critical parameters, as different stakeholders might offer different valuation methodologies depending on social, economic, and environmental considerations; higher cost estimations for burnt forest may direct the model toward larger investments in preparedness, thus being a sensible aspect when analyzing risk management policies.

4.2.2.3. Risk Control. One key notion from risk analysis is that the expected value of consequences does not necessarily reflect decision maker preferences or risk profiles because DRM may require risk-averse approaches (i.e., safeguarding communities and critical assets), specifically for officials under public scrutiny. We incorporate constraints that control intolerable deviations (i.e., extreme losses) throughout scenarios. The stochastic program in Online Appendix B.4 is a risk-neutral model, as it focuses on the expected value of costs throughout wildfire seasons. However, specific scenarios may lead to significant consequences that may be unacceptable to stakeholders, even if these occur with low probability. We introduce constraints to control a risk measure that captures scenarios in which tolerable losses are exceeded. Hence, the optimization model is forced to consider more conservative actions to prevent any scenario (even those with low probability) from having extreme wildfire season control costs. We adopt expected regret (ER), a coherent risk measure (Szego ¨ 2002) that defines the average cost of exceeding a fixed tolerable target throughout wildfire seasons. A risk-averse version of our proposed optimization model should include such constraints, as detailed in Online Appendix B.5.

For the case of Uruguay, decision maker preferences, particularly risk aversion, is accounted for in the analysis. We propose a risk-neutral approach based on the model in Equations (1)–(15) (Online Appendix B.4) and a risk-averse approach in which a loss threshold should not be exceeded in the second stage (i.e., a model with Equations (1)–(18)).

In this section, we present computational experiments to test the implementation of the proposed framework for the case of wildfire management in Uruguay. ## 5.1. Comparison of Artifact Against Baseline

The proposed framework articulates predictive and prescriptive modules, producing a forecast of decisions and outcomes throughout a set of estimated scenarios of the response stage (lookahead approximation) to inform decisions at the preparedness stage. For wildfire management, this strategy is implemented into two models, as shown in Section 4.2.2: a risk-neutral model (Equations (1)–(15)), which minimizes expected costs throughout response scenarios, and a risk-averse model that includes risk-control constraints (Equations (1)–(18)). We evalu ate how these models perform against the following baseline approaches: first, a deterministic model that corresponds to a simplification of the previous risk-neutral model, in which there is only one scenario: the expected value realization (hence, no use of the predictive mod ule’s scenarios); second, a constrained model that corresponds to a simplification of the previous risk-neutral model, in which the preparedness budget is deliberately constrained to be at most 95% of the risk-neutral model’s first stage cost (hence, no use of the prescriptive module’s “optimal” preparedness budget); third, a naïve-conservative model that corresponds to a simplifica tion of the previous risk-averse model, in which an attempt to be conservative is pursued by forcing the deterministic model to spend at least as much as the risk-averse model in the preparedness phase but with out the scenario information (hence, no use of scenarios or risk-control constraints).

Figure 7 presents the locations of installed facilities for each of the proposed cases, with most facilities installed in the south, where wildfire density is the highest. The risk-neutral model installed eight small facilities and one large facility, resulting in an acceptable second-stage cost ranking as the second best. In contrast, the risk-averse model installed fewer facilities (two large and six small) but allocated additional resources leading to a reduction in the expected shortage costs. The constrained model installed even fewer facilities (due to the first-stage budget), leading high transportation and shortage costs. The deterministic model installed the fewest facilities and prepositioned the least resources, thus obtaining the worst secondstage performance. The naïve-conservative model prepositioned a small number of resources while opening the largest number of facilities, which is explained by the focus on the expected value as a single scenario; as a result, demand is smoothed, leading to high shortage costs when the model was evaluated with the complete set of scenarios.

## 5.2. Contribution of Artifact Components

The techniques used to implement the framework modules are meant to be interchangeable, depending on specific context needs. However, we provide alternative implementations for the predictive and prescriptive modules to analyze how the techniques proposed in Section 4 compare with simplified approaches based on rules-of-thumb or heuristics, closer to what may be used in practice. For a replacement of the proposed predictive module, we implement a kernel density estimation (KDE) approach in which we fit the wildfire locations and demands to a distribution based on Gaussian kernels. This allows us to generate random variates of wildfires outside the original set of 139. These variates consist of locations and demands but lack the propagation and fireline information discussed in Section 4.2.1. For a replacement of the prescriptive module, we implement a heuristic that builds a clustering of the wildfire observations and associates each obtained cluster with the closest potential facility location; facilities and resources are then assigned following a greedy procedure based on the clusters’ demand. The details of these implementations are presented in Online Appendix C.3.

Figure 9 summarizes the proposed comparison based on a 10-fold cross-validation exercise, in which the models are run (“trained”) with 900 scenarios and then tested with the remaining 100 (based on n � 1,000 scenarios from Section 4). Figure 9(a) focuses on shortage costs, with each plot providing side-by-side comparisons of the exact (risk) and heuristic approaches for the 10 folds; the upper plot displays results based on the wildfire simulation from Section 4.2.1, whereas the lower plot displays results based on the KDE procedure.

The 10 folds provide a sense of how different wildfire seasons may look like. For instance, folds 3, 4, and 8 may represent mild scenarios in which none of the models show large shortage costs (i.e., unattended fires) or scenarios with little content in the tails, as in folds 1 and 2. Folds 5, 6, 7, 9, and 10, conversely, may correspond to cases in which the location and magni tude of realized fires lead to significant tails in shortage for the heuristic model. The exact risk-averse optimization has the advantage of explicitly considering scenarios and aiming to control tails, such that large shortages never occur, even when faced with wildfire seasons not considered when producing the solutions (suggesting that the model captures significant variability). The lower plot in Figure 9(a) (i.e., KDE) follows a similar pattern, but distributions are sparser, due to the variability induced by generating new wildfires from the fitted distribution (interpolating beyond the original 139). The tail content for the heuristic model becomes more distributed albeit high shortage costs remain, whereas the risk-averse model consistently controls shortage within a much lower limit.

Figure 9(b) compares total second-stage costs for the exact and heuristic approaches throughout the 10 folds (upper part for wildfire simulation and lower part for KDE). This allows conclusions to be drawn beyond just the shortage costs, observing how the exact optimization may incur in slight extra costs to achieve the risk control features observed, which is precisely a key tradeoff we aim to make evident to stakeholders and which is tied to their valuation of losses, as discussed previously.

Overall, we observe a more drastic reduction in solution quality when using simplified predictive approaches than when using simplified prescriptive approaches. The predictive component is certainly paramount, not because the prescriptive one is less important, but because they work together: The optimization relies on the generated scenarios; hence, poorly constructed scenarios will invariably lead to bad decisions (whereas simpler decision rules may do well under well-informed scenarios).

## 6. Conclusion

We developed and instantiated a decision support framework for DRM considering a use-case in the context of wildfire management in Uruguay (South America). Given the challenge of assisting decision makers in problems characterized by high complexity, uncertainty, and the potential to trigger profound socio-economic disruptions, we articulate an analytical artifact based on design principles from a range of fields relevant to DRM: from hazard assessment, to risk analysis, to resilience of cyber-physical-social systems. These fields provide domain-specific requirements, particularly in the form of questions at different DRM stages, which can be addressed with the support of analytical models. As a result, we enable riskinformed decision support, seeking to evaluate and unveil tradeoffs between efforts in prevention and their impact on risk reduction. We argue that information systems can enhance risk management and communication by offering decision makers traceability and credible evidence to support the tough choices for which they are accountable and scrutinized. For instance, addressing dilemmas between being the official who over-spent for a disaster that “never” materialized and the one who could be seen as complicit in a major disruption for their community.

A central aspect of the proposed framework is the notion of lookahead approximation, which responds to an identified need to integrate decisions throughout DRM stages by forecasting decisions at the response stage (via simulation) to determine the adequacy of decisions at the preparedness stage (via optimization). In the proposed wildfire use-case, this allows to compare how different approaches to pre-event decisions lead to different probabilistic distributions of consequences; for instance, with individual fires taking longer to be controlled, or the overall burnt area being larger throughout scenarios (thus, producing longer tails in cost distributions). We validated our proposed artifacts (framework and instantiation) through the implementation of models that respond to the identified requirements in key decision problems at DRM stages of interest (in particular, for wildfire management). Particularly, we tested the articulation of predictive and prescriptive models by comparing our solutions against approaches that do not use the lookahead approximation provided by the stochastic optimization (i.e., making preparedness decisions based on their estimated outcomes at the response stage). The inclusion of scenario information and risk-control constraints not only leads to better numerical results but also enables a richer discussion regarding the tradeoffs between efforts in preparedness and the probabilistic behavior of consequences (i.e., tails representing low-probability high-consequence scenarios). Furthermore, we compared the quality and decision support capabilities of different ways to implement our framework modules based on alternative decision rules, evidencing not only the modularity of the framework but also the adequacy of the chosen techniques.

The attempt at building an end-to-end decision support tool for our DRM use-case exposes the challenges in integrating concepts and models used at different facets of the complex DRM problem: characterizing hazards; evaluating risks from social, economic, and environmental perspectives; planning and executing the operations that implement risk management actions; and designing information and decision support systems that are sufficiently applicable and accessible to be adopted. The main challenges for the adoption of decision support systems in DRM remain data availability and integration, user-centered design, and issues of organizational training and coordination. The actual benefit of analytics-based approaches depends on the quality of the data to estimate future scenarios to enable subsequent prescriptive analyses. For this study, we include cross-validation to evaluate how well the models respond to unseen scenarios (with satisfactory results), but deeper questions on how to adequately deal with uncertainty remain.
