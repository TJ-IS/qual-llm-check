# Speak with One Voice? Examining Content Coordination and Social Media Engagement During Disasters

Abstract. Disaster relief organizations (DROs) utilize social media to provide information rapidly and broadly. Many DROs post content via multiple social media accounts within a single platform, on which each account represents a distinct entity (e.g., national headquarters, local branch). According to crisis communication theory, an organization’s communication channels can coordinate by “speaking with one voice.” In a disaster context, this can be facilitated by accounts within the same DRO producing convergent content through the match of their content creation decisions (e.g., targeting the same audience). Using Twitter data collected in partnership with the Canadian Red Cross, we show that content creation decisions by accounts within the same DRO are intertwined, unveiling at least an implicit level of coordination. Our results also indicate that engagement improves by 4.3% from a match only when uncertainty and urgency are at their highest (i.e., the response phase). Strikingly, a divergence of content creation decisions, or mismatch, yields 29.6% more engagement when uncertainty subsides (i.e., the recovery phase). We, thus, reveal that the recommended coordination strategy from crisis communication theory does not uniformly hold across the disaster management cycle. Moreover, we demonstrate that the effect magnitudes of a match versus a mismatch on engagement vary between accounts representing national and local level entities. Our findings extend crisis communication theory and pro vide actionable guidelines for DROs’ internal coordination of social media content generation, which, ultimately, help DROs meet the needs of those affected by disasters.

## 1. Introduction

In the last 20 years, disasters claimed 1.23 million lives and caused \$2.97 trillion in economic losses worldwide (United Nations Office for Disaster Risk Reduction 2020). Once disasters strike, disaster relief organizations (DROs), such as the national societies in the International Federation of Red Cross and Red Crescent Societies, mobilize critical resources to help impacted communities. Relief resources include physical ones (e.g., food, water) and, equally importantly, information (Meier 2015). Among information technologies used by DROs to distribute information efficiently, social media has risen to the forefront. Because social media enables DROs to disseminate information instantly, broadly, and freely, DROs are universally active on social media (Department of Homeland Security 2012).

As do most organizations, DROs aim to create social media content that draws user engagement. Higher engagement is listed as the top measure of success for DROs’ social media usage (International Committee of the Red Cross, International Federation of Red Cross and Red Crescent Societies, and Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs 2017). This is because engagement catalyzes the fast and wide diffusion of DROs’ content, thereby improving DROs’ ability to reach targeted audiences and respond to disasters (Yoo et al. 2016). To illustrate, Figure 1 shows two tweets from the 2016 Fort McMurray wildfire in Canada. Despite social media’s importance to DROs, research on the strategy for DROs to engage users effectively is scarce.

DROs often maintain multiple accounts within a single social media platform. Each account represents a unique operational entity of a DRO. For instance, as shown in Figure 1, the CRC runs one account for the national headquarters of Red Cross operations in Canada (i.e., national account), running other accounts for its local branches in each Canadian province (i.e., local accounts). When social media accounts sharing an association, such as representing the same DRO, coordinate content production, engagement is higher (Mallipeddi et al. 2021). To manage coordination, crisis communication theory prescribes that an organization’s channels “speak with one voice” and generate convergent content (Coombs 1999). This is crucial to reduce rampant uncertainty during crises (Seeger 2006). For accounts within the same DRO to produce convergent content, they need to align their decisions about content creation and design (e.g., audience, topic). Therefore, coordination between a DRO’s accounts can entail the convergence (i.e., match) of their content creation decisions.

Critically, crisis communication theory adopts a static viewpoint by implicitly assuming that uncertainty is always high during crises, such as disasters. However, uncertainty levels in disasters vary across the phases of the disaster management cycle (Van Wassenhove 2006). The response phase (i.e., immediately after a disaster when DROs mobilize emergency resources) is characterized by urgency and extreme uncertainty. The recovery phase (i.e., when DROs work toward restoration of impacted communities) is characterized by the need to reduce human suffering and lower uncertainty (Holgu´ın-Veras et al. 2012). Because disasters are evolving situations with shifting uncertainty levels, crisis communication’s recommended coordination strategy of convergence may not always hold for DROs with multiple accounts. In fact, previous studies on coordination suggest that coordination does not have to be necessarily equated with convergent decisions (Bramoulle ´ 2007, Stasser and Abele 2020). Rather, another valid coordination strategy for a DRO’s accounts may involve the divergence (i.e., mismatch) of their content creation decisions. Accordingly, our first research question asks, how do DROs with multiple accounts currently coordinate (i.e., match or mismatch) their content creation?

Whereas coordination can be facilitated in more ways than one, the most effective choice of coordination strategy for increasing engagement is far from obvious, especially when recognizing disasters as dynamic events with distinct phases. This leads us to our second research question: to increase social media engagement during the disaster response and recovery phases, should a DRO’s accounts match or mismatch their content creation decisions? On top of the disaster management phase, the level of an account (i.e., national versus local) may affect how the type of coordination strategy drives engagement. This is because a DRO’s national and local accounts may have different characteristics and areas of expertise even though they represent the same organization. To shed light on this, our third research question asks, how does the effect on engagement from a match or mismatch differ by the level of a DRO’s accounts in the disaster response and recovery phases?

To study coordination strategies for DROs with multiple accounts on the same social media platform empirically, we partnered with the CRC to collect Twitter data from the 2016 Fort McMurray wildfire. We measure engagement as the total number of retweets, likes, replies, and clicks. We formulate two empirical models to evaluate how a DRO’s accounts should coordinate their production of social media content to increase engagement. In these models, we focus on the strategic content creation decision of audience. DROs design social media content for two key audiences: disaster victims and supporters (e.g., donors, volunteers) (International Committee of the Red Cross, International Federation of Red Cross and Red Crescent Societies, and Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs 2017, United Nations Volunteers 2018, Yan and Pedraza-Martinez 2019).

Our results indicate that content creation decisions by one account are shaped by those made by another account within the same DRO. Furthermore, we find that the coordination strategy that is most conducive to increasing engagement depends on when content is created and by whom. During the response phase, engagement improves from a match of content creation decisions. Strikingly, a match suppresses engagement during the recovery phase. Our results also demonstrate that the magnitudes of these effects on engagement vary between national accounts and local accounts.

Our contribution to theory is threefold. First, we challenge the conventional wisdom from crisis communication theory that organizations should unilaterally “speak with one voice” by publishing convergent content across all channels when responding to crises. Our results indicate that this advice holds in the response phase, in which engagement is 4.3% higher under a match of content creation decisions rather than a mismatch. In contrast, in the recovery phase, a match reduces engagement by 29.6%. This reveals that a mismatch of content creation decisions is more beneficial for engagement during the recovery phase. By considering the disaster management cycle, we add a dynamic perspective to the crisis communication literature.

Second, we extend the extant research on coordination for DROs. This literature largely focuses on interorganizational coordination. However, intraorganizational coordination is crucial too, especially because DROs commonly operate multiple entities at the national and local levels. Our study helps fill this gap. A lack of clarity about the roles and responsibilities of each entity within the same DRO inhibits rapid action, good decision making, and aid delivery (Webster and Walker 2009). DROs with poor intraorganizational coordination also incur higher costs (Pedraza-Martinez et al. 2020), which matters because DROs often operate in resource-constrained settings. In the context of social media, we find that content creation decisions by accounts within the same DRO are interdependent, suggesting at least an implicit degree of intraorganizational coordination. Our results also demonstrate that how DROs internally coordinate their social media accounts materially affects engagement. Given that prior work concentrates on how DROs should coordinate the distribution of physical resources during disasters, our contribution lies in investigating intraorganizational coordination strategies for information resources.

Third, we identify that the impact on engagement from how content creation decisions are coordinated depends on the level of an account. For example, our results indicate that, in the recovery phase, engagement decreases from a match of content creation decisions compared with a mismatch by 36.2% for a national account and by 26.8% for a local account. This finding runs counter to the mainstream claim that the internet and digitalization have made the world flatter and more even. Rather, our results suggest that DROs’ organizational hierarchy may carry implications for coordination strategies not only in the off-line world, but also in the online world.

We also offer practical guidance for managers of DROs’ social media accounts. Our results suggest that DROs with multiple accounts on the same platform should adopt a nuanced coordination strategy for content production to improve engagement. During the response phase, this strategy should involve accounts within the same DRO making the same content creation decisions to release content with similar characteristics. During the recovery phase, DROs with multiple accounts should pivot their coordination strategy to involve a mismatch of content creation decisions.

## 2. Research Background and Related Literature

This section first presents the background of our research with a description of coordination for DROs in the disaster management context. We then review the relevant extant literature and establish the theoretical foundation for our study.

## 2.1. Research Background

As a central part of their mission, DROs mobilize resources to respond to two types of disasters. Slow-onset disasters (e.g., floods) have a longer warning time and buildup, whereas sudden-onset disasters (e.g., wildfires, earthquakes) transpire quickly and without warning (Holgu´ın-Veras et al. 2012). Our study involves a suddenonset disaster. In addition, DROs work throughout four different phases of the disaster management cycle. The first two, mitigation and preparedness, precede a disaster event. The latter two, response and recovery, follow a disaster event. The response phase covers the immediate aftermath of a disaster when DROs activate their emergency operations and work urgently to address disaster victims’ needs. The recovery phase follows the response phase and covers the period in which DROs work toward stabilizing the affected communities (Van Wassenhove and Pedraza Martinez 2012). This phase can be divided into short- and long-term recovery. We study short-term recovery (hereafter referred to simply as recovery), which involves activities such as managing donations and volunteers, cleaning debris, and securing temporary housing (Holgu´ın-Veras et al. 2012).

Because these two phases are characterized by volatile and disrupted environments, information is needed just as much as physical resources such as food and water (International Committee of the Red Cross and International Federation of Red Cross and Red Crescent Societies 2016). DROs have been leveraging social media to disseminate information quickly and broadly (Yoo et al. 2016). Whereas DROs can freely utilize social media, they must allocate time and resources toward producing content for their followers and users at large. When creating social media content, DROs make a series of decisions about its design. A key decision is the audience or for whom the content is created (International Committee of the Red Cross, International Federation of Red Cross and Red Crescent Societies, and Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs 2017, United Nations Volunteers 2018). DROs primarily provide information for two audiences once a disaster occurs: disaster victims and supporters (United Nations Volunteers 2018, Yan and Pedraza-Martinez 2019). Disaster victims are those whose homes are directly impacted by disasters. Supporters encompass those from the general public who are interested in or committed to contributing to DROs’ response efforts, such as current and potential donors, funding institutions, and volunteers as well as victims’ family members.

To ensure that disaster victims and supporters receive the information they need, DROs need to coordinate the delivery of social media content to these audiences. However, coordination is one of the most significant challenges that DROs face (Kova´cs and Spens 2009). In the disaster management context, coordination refers to how organizations work together to respond to an emergency and determines when a specific organization is responsible for providing a specific resource (Van Wassenhove 2006). Because disaster response involves a multitude of actors (e.g., DROs, military, government), DROs need to manage interorganizational coordination (Holgu´ın-Veras et al. 2012). At an intraorganizational level, most DROs, especially large ones, run and coordinate multiple operational entities at the national (e.g., national headquarters) and local (e.g., local chapters) levels. This allows DROs to contribute to disaster response operations on both a national and local scale (Van Wassenhove and Pedraza Martinez 2012). Prior literature has conducted in-depth investigations of interorganizational coordination for disaster management (e.g., Balcik et al. 2010, Ergun et al. 2014, Ruesch et al. 2022), but intraorganizational coordination for DROs is largely unexplored.

Besides managing multiple physical entities, DROs must manage multiple online presences. A DRO can be represented on a single social media platform by more than one account, and each account represents a distinct entity of the DRO. This means that DROs maintain national accounts in conjunction with local accounts on the same social media platform. Figure 2 depicts examples of the national and local accounts on Twitter for the CRC along with its corresponding entities. As such, DROs need to devise coordination strategies between their national and local representations in the off-line world as well as the online world.

To resolve the internal coordination issue in the offline world, DROs rigidly control how their national and local entities work together based on the underlying hierarchical structure. A DRO’s initial response to a disaster is conducted by the entities closer to the disaster, that is, its local entities. If the disaster exceeds the capacity of local entities, then the DRO’s national entity assumes responsibility and administers the response (American Red Cross 2012). Whereas insights and policies have been developed for DROs’ coordination of physical resources, little guidance is available for how DROs can or should coordinate the distribution of information. We aim to address this gap by examining how DROs with multiple social media accounts should coordinate content generation.

## 2.2. Related Literature

Our study relates to two research streams primarily, which we review in the following sections.

2.2.1. Social Media and Disaster Management. Despite social media being recognized as a valuable communication channel during emergencies, research on this technology and its impact on disaster management is still an emerging area. One strand within this literature focuses on how individual users utilize social media (e.g., Oh et al. 2013, Nan and Lu 2014). Another strand examines how DROs use social media (e.g., Yoo et al. 2016, Yan and Pedraza-Martinez 2019). Because of our focus on DROs’ coordination strategies, our study is situated within the second strand.

An essential aspect of DROs’ social media usage is content creation, especially because DROs aim to post content that drives engagement. Research on the creation of social media content by DROs is nascent but indicates that the type of information impacts engagement. For instance, Yan and Pedraza-Martinez (2019) find that engagement in the form of comments depends on whether DROs publish information that is actionable and holds practical value (e.g., instructions for how to donate or find shelter). Yoo et al. (2020) further discovered that users’ propensity to engage with and follow DROs is also affected by the extent to which DROs’ content contains actionable information. Additionally, the novelty of DROs’ social media content matters because engagement declines when redundant information is posted (Yoo et al. 2019).

An underlying assumption of the aforementioned research is that each DRO is represented on a social media platform by a single account. In reality, however, DROs often have more than one account on the same social media platform to represent their different entities. Because Twitter content is usually public, one account’s content creation decisions may exert social influence on another’s (Burtch et al. 2013). Indeed, prior work examining the behavior of social media content creators models their decisions as a function of other creators’ decisions (Tang et al. 2012, Iyer and Katona 2016). Consequently, we unpack this assumption and examine how content creation decisions by accounts belonging to the same DRO affect each other and, ultimately, engagement.

2.2.2. Social Media Engagement. Engagement has been defined as the “behavioral manifestations” between organizations and their stakeholders (Van Doorn et al. 2010). Social media platforms enable organizations to communicate with their stakeholders more directly and, thereby, elicit engagement more easily. Examples of social media engagement include liking, sharing, or commenting on posts. For-profit firms especially value social media engagement because it is shown to impact economic performance measures such as sales (Goh et al. 2013), product adoption (Aral and Walker 2011), and market value (Luo et al. 2013). As a result, researchers analyze how firms can induce greater engagement on social media. According to extant literature, engagement with a firm’s social media content is driven by content characteristics, such as emotional valence (Berger and Milkman 2012, Yang et al. 2019), informativeness (Lee et al. 2018, Bapna et al. 2019), and the use of hashtags (Kumar et al. 2022) along with creator characteristics, such as the prestige of the firm’s social media account (Susarla et al. 2012).

Within this research stream, a handful of studies examine how engagement with an organization’s account is influenced by the content creation decisions of other related accounts. Borah and Tellis (2016) argue that mental linkages exist between content published by accounts representing organizations that share an association. Consequently, social media engagement for a focal firm is a function of that for its competitors (Borah and Tellis 2016, Chae et al. 2017). Additionally, Krijestorac et al. (2020) find that a firm posting the same content via its accounts on different social media platforms boosts awareness of the content and, as a result, lifts engagement. Finally, Mallipeddi et al. (2021) propose that, when accounts representing a political candidate and the party to which the candidate belongs publish social media content with a similar tone, users view this as a consensus being formed. This leads to users perceiving the information to be more credible and meaningful, which increases their engagement with the candidate’s content.

Whereas the extant literature has made significant progress in understanding social media engagement, it focuses primarily on the commercial setting during times of normalcy. We contribute to this literature by investigating the disaster setting, which is distinct for several reasons. First, engagement is important to for-profit firms because of its economic impact. DROs’ main objective, however, is to reduce human suffering (Holgu´ın-Veras et al. 2013). Thus, DROs value engagement as a means to share information about available resources and elicit donations (Yan and Pedraza-Martinez 2019). Second, information is an extremely perishable resource because disasters wreak havoc and uncertainty (Yoo et al. 2020). By disseminating social media content, engagement helps ensure that the content reaches those who need it before it expires. We further contribute to this research stream by adding nuance as to how engagement for an account is affected by the content creation decisions made by other accounts that share an affiliation. We demonstrate that this effect depends on the level of an account (i.e., national or local).

## 2.3. Theoretical Foundation for Content Coordination

Major DROs and government organizations, such as the Federal Emergency Management Agency and the

Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), have integrated crisis communication theory into their communication strategies (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 2018). Inspired by this real-world adoption, we utilize crisis communication theory as the core part of our theoretical foundation. This theory investigates how organizations should communicate during a crisis, which is defined as “an event that is an unpredictable, major threat that can have a negative effect on the organization, industry, or stakeholders if handled improperly” (Coombs 1999, p. 2).

Originally, crisis communication theory focused on managing stakeholder relationships and repairing reputations following crises for which organizations are responsible (e.g., product recalls because of human error). This theory has evolved to inform communication strategies for a broader set of crises, including unintended events that are outside of organizations’ control (e.g., natural disasters) (Coombs and Holladay 2002). The CDC’s Crisis and Emergency Risk Communication Manual, which uses crisis communication theory to guide the organization’s actions in response to such crises, is a testament to this evolution (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 2018). Crisis communication theory also now recognizes social media as an official communication channel that is vital for crisis management because many stakeholders use social media during crises (Veil et al. 2011). The evolution of this theory along these fronts highlights its relevance for our study.

The goals of crisis communication are to lessen the negative impacts of a crisis on the organization and its stakeholders and reduce any threats to public safety (Coombs 1999). Crisis communication is relevant not only to the acute period following a crisis, but also when organizations work toward recovery and returning to normalcy (Fink 1986). For public service organizations, such as DROs, crisis communication is less focused on addressing responsibility for crises and reputation repair. Instead, the priorities are to disseminate accurate, timely, and useful information, provide aid to victims, and restore order (Seeger 2006). DROs can achieve this on social media through engagement. Accordingly, we lean on crisis communication theory to illuminate how DROs can increase engagement through the coordination of their social media accounts.

Coordination among communication channels belonging to the same organization is cited as a best practice of crisis communication (Seeger 2006). Regarding how coordination should be executed, extant literature advocates for an organization’s channels to speak with one voice and release content that is consistent and has convergent characteristics (Coombs 1999, Barton 2001). This implies that when channels belonging to the same organization create content and make decisions about its design, these decisions should converge and match. The idea that coordination is facilitated by a match in decisions is also in line with how coordination is typically framed in the economics literature (e.g., Rubinstein 1989, Mehta et al. 1994).

Convergence of content across channels is recommended because it builds credibility and legitimacy (Coombs 1999, Massey 2001), which are vital for reducing confusion and combating uncertainty inherent in crises (Seeger 2006). Coombs and Holladay (2012) even encourage an organization’s channels reaching overlapping stakeholders to generate convergent content because redundancy can further heighten exposure and credibility. Therefore, under a coordination strategy in which content creation decisions by accounts within the same DRO match, content appears more credible and meaningful. As a result, more user engagement should occur.

On the other hand, convergence across a DRO’s accounts may lead to lower engagement. Differential attention theory argues that attention to content declines as it is repeated (Unnava and Burnkrant 1991). This can be attributed to individuals realizing that they have already processed the content and, subsequently, lowering their motivation to process the content again. When accounts match their content creation decisions, they post content with redundant characteristics. Following the logic of differential attention theory, convergence has an adverse effect on engagement. Prior studies on coordination from economics and psychology indicate that a match in decisions does not always yield the best outcome. Coordination may alternatively be facilitated by a mismatch wherein decisions do not conform to each other (Bramoulle ´ 2007, Stasser and Abele 2020). Hence, engagement may benefit from a coordination strategy that involves a mismatch of content creation decisions.

Moreover, whether DROs should coordinate their accounts through a match or a mismatch in order to raise engagement may depend on the phase of the disaster management cycle. This study focuses on the response and recovery phases to assess coordination in a postdisaster setting. The response phase is characterized by extreme levels of uncertainty and more pressing needs for information and other resources (Holgu´ın-Veras et al. 2012). To manage the uncertainty and stress associated with this phase, users actively seek information and place high priority on finding trustworthy and accurate information (Sutton et al. 2008). Accordingly, users may be less opposed to the redundancy from a DRO’s accounts posting convergent content and, instead, place greater value on the resulting boost to the credibility of the information. This leads to higher engagement (Mallipeddi et al. 2021). Therefore, in order to improve engagement in the response phase, a DRO’s accounts should coordinate with a match of its content creation decisions.

As emergency operations in the response phase transition to rehabilitation efforts in the recovery phase, the situation is still critical, but uncertainty levels tend to be lower (Holgu´ın-Veras et al. 2012, Yan and Pedraza-Martinez 2019). This leads to a shift in information preferences (Yan and Pedraza-Martinez 2019). Whereas users are receptive to receiving more information in the response phase, they are averse to increases in information processing costs outside of this phase (Yoo et al. 2020). Because of this, in the recovery phase, users may be more prone to recognizing the redundant nature of convergent content and that they have previously processed it. Per differential attention theory, users then withdraw their engagement (Unnava and Burnkrant 1991). Thus, whereas crisis communication research unilaterally suggests that a match of content creation decisions by a DRO’s accounts is advantageous for engagement, this may not apply to the recovery phase. Rather, engagement should increase when DROs adopt a coordination strategy that involves a mismatch of content creation decisions.

In addition to the phase of the disaster management cycle, whether a match or a mismatch of content creation decisions is better for engagement may vary by the level of DROs’ accounts (i.e., national or local). Although they belong to the same DRO, national and local accounts may have different focuses and areas of expertise. This is reminiscent of how a firm’s national and local advertising serve distinct purposes (brand building and shortterm promotions, respectively) (Sridhar et al. 2016). Furthermore, the underlying hierarchical structure may also play a role because national accounts represent entities higher in the organizational hierarchy. As such, how a DRO coordinates content production across its social media accounts may be contingent on their levels.

Our study expands extant theory by integrating arguments for coordination as a match or a mismatch of content creation decisions. In contrast to the crisis communication literature, we propose that a one-size-fits-all approach may not be appropriate for DROs that need to manage the generation of social media content across multiple accounts. Instead, there may be conditions that call for a match of content creation decisions and others that call for a mismatch. We, therefore, qualify crisis communication theory by presenting situations in which organizations’ communication channels should aim to create divergent information.

## 3. Research Context and Data

We begin this section by describing the DRO and disaster event we investigate. We next discuss the data and topic modeling approach for identifying tweets’ audience. We use Twitter data as Twitter is recognized as the most effective platform for disaster management (Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs 2014).

## 3.1. Focal DRO and Disaster Event

The CRC is one of the largest DROs in Canada. Whereas the CRC runs a variety of initiatives to help those in need, a core part of its work involves responding to and helping individuals and communities recover from disasters. According to its 2019–2020 annual report, the CRC responded to 17 large-scale disasters and supplied assistance to more than 67,000 victims (Canadian Red Cross 2020). The CRC provides services across all of Canada as a national level entity and operates local level enti ties, such as branches for each Canadian province.

The disaster event we examine is the 2016 Fort McMurray wildfire, which forced the largest wildfire evacuation in Alberta of more than 80,000 people and became the costliest Canadian natural disaster with estimated insured losses at \$3.7 billion (MNP 2017). The wildfire began on May 1, 2016, in a forested area outside Fort McMurray in the province of Alberta. On May 3, the wildfire entered Fort McMurray and destroyed nearby communities, oil sands facilities, and important infrastructures, such as water systems. The following day, the Government of Alberta declared a provincial state of emergency. The fire spread rapidly because of good burning conditions until it reached a size of 483,084 hectares on May 18 (MNP 2017). The wildfire continued to grow sporadically after this point until it was finally declared under control on July 4.

Because of the scope of the wildfire, the CRC was activated swiftly at a national level. The local branch that primarily supported the national operations was the CRC in Alberta (i.e., CRC-A). Besides supplying and distributing physical resources, the CRC and the CRC-A actively pushed out information on Twitter. The CRC was represented by a national account (i.e., @redcrosscanada), and the CRC-A was represented separately by a local account (i.e., @RedCrossAB). The two accounts were managed by independent social media teams. These accounts also share a significant number of followers as evidenced by 56% of the CRC-A’s followers also following the CRC. The overlap between followers is important as this group of users is most likely to be exposed to the content creation decisions of both accounts, which may influence their engagement.

## 3.2. Data

Our sample consists of tweets posted by the CRC and the CRC-A from May 1 through August 4, 2016, which covers the beginning of the wildfire through one month after the fire was declared under control. As such, our data set spans the response and recovery phases of the wildfire. The two accounts posted 1,790 tweets in total during the data collection period. Because tweets conveying gratitude are noninformative (Yan and Pedraza-Martinez 2019), we removed 227 tweets containing fewer than three words and the word “thank” (Yang et al. 2019, Ammann and Schaub 2021). Thus, our final sample size is 1,563 tweets. Of these, the CRC posted 934 tweets (59.8% of total tweets), and the CRC-A posted 629 tweets (40.2% of total tweets).

The main data for our empirical analyses come from two of Twitter’s enterprise application programming interfaces (APIs). The first is the Historical PowerTrack API, through which we extracted the entire archive of tweets posted by the CRC and the CRC-A. Online Appendix A provides a more detailed description of this data set. The second is the Engagement API. Through our partnership, the CRC granted the required authentication to use this API to collect engagement data for tweets by the CRC and the CRC-A. The engagement data include metrics such as retweets, likes, and replies. Within our study’s timeline, the CRC’s tweets received 33,861 retweets and 34,722 likes, whereas the CRC-A’s tweets garnered 4,802 retweets and 2,862 likes.

## 3.3. Identification of Audience

To study the coordination of social media content creation by a DRO’s accounts, we focus on the key decision of for whom content is created. Therefore, our study investigates when a DRO’s coordination strategy should involve a match in decisions as manifested by generating content for the same audience or a mismatch under which content is produced for different audiences. Recall that DROs’ two main audiences consist of disaster victims and supporters.

Given our focus on the decision of audience, a critical part of our analysis is identifying for whom tweets were designed. In line with prior literature (e.g., Lappas et al. 2016, Shi et al. 2016, Han et al. 2020), our process involves topic modeling to discover latent topics and then categorizing them into higher level dimensions (i.e., audience of disaster victims or supporters) using expert domain knowledge. We use the Gibbs sampling Dirichlet multinomial mixture (GSDMM) model to discover distinct topics in the tweets published by the CRC and the CRC-A (Yin and Wang 2014). Traditional long text topic modeling algorithms, such as latent Dirichlet allocation (Blei et al. 2003), assume each text is generated by multiple topics. However, GSDMM assumes each document is generated by one topic, which is more suitable for short texts (Qiang et al. 2020).<sup>1</sup> Based on the degree of semantic similarity or coherence among the top features within each topic, we conclude that three is the ideal number of topics discussed among the tweets in our sample. We also rely on expert domain knowledge to corroborate the topic labels. Through this process, the three topics identified are updates on the disaster and recovery process, donation appeals, and instructions for registering for and receiving aid. Please refer to Online Appendix B for additional details on our topic modeling approach.

The GSDMM algorithm assigns a topic for each tweet. We then classify each topic and thereby each tweet as targeting victims or supporters. Because the first and second topics provide general information about the disaster and include donation appeals, we set the audience as supporters. The audience for the third topic is victims as it deals with their needs. Table 1 lists the number of tweets for each audience along with an example tweet from our data.

## 4. Content Creation Decisions Model

We develop an empirical model to identify whether content creation decisions by one account affect those of another. This analysis enables us to establish if matching or mismatching these decisions is an actual behavior. In doing so, we generate insight into the current decisionmaking process behind content creation by a DRO’s accounts and answer our first research question.

## 4.1. Model Formulation

Our study’s context involves two Twitter accounts that represent the CRC (i.e., one national and one local account). Therefore, a focal account, i, has one other counterpart account that belongs to the same DRO (hereafter referred to as an affiliated account), j. Our model specification builds upon previous literature that models online content generation as a product of multiple decisions (e.g., Moe and Schweidel 2012, Ho et al. 2017). In line with these studies, we model a focal account’s content creation behavior using two distinct but related subprocesses: (i) whether to post a tweet and (ii) for whom to post.

We use an hour as the unit of time because it is a critical time measure for disaster response (Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs 2017) and prior research establishes that an hourly analysis is needed to capture dynamics on social media during disasters at a fine-grained level (Nan and Lu 2014). Table 2 presents the variables for the content creation decisions model and their descriptive statistics.

4.1.1. Whether to Post a Tweet. We model i’s decision of whether to post a tweet in t as a function of three components: (I) whether i posted a tweet in t – 1, (II) whether j posted a tweet in t – 1, and (III) the organizational level (i.e., national or local) of i. The first component tests whether a focal account tends to publish content frequently (International Committee of the Red Cross, International Federation of Red Cross and Red Crescent Societies, and Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs 2017) or refrains from constantly posting on social media to avoid user wear-out (Anderson and De Palma 2013). The second component investigates to what extent an affiliated account’s decision to post affects a focal account’s decision to post. Because social media content is usually public, a focal account may consider the content creation decisions made by other accounts (Burtch et al. 2013). This may then shape how the focal account produces content (Asvanund et al. 2004, Tang et al. 2012, Iyer and Katona 2016). Such social influence may be more salient between a focal and affiliated account because they represent an identical DRO and have a shared mission and purpose (Susarla et al. 2012, Wang et al. 2018). However, the direction of this influence is unclear because prior literature identifies complementary effects (Xia et al. 2012, Bapna and Umyarov 2015) as well as substitutive effects (Asvanund et al. 2004, Burtch et al. 2013). The third component evaluates how the underlying organizational structure of a DRO impacts a focal account’s decision-making process for content creation.

4.1.2. For Whom to Post. Once i decides to post content in t, for whom to post is another decision that i must make. The latter decision is conditional on the previous decision of whether to post content. We model the decision of audience to depend on three components: (I) for whom i posted in t – 1, (II) for whom affiliated account j posted in $t - 1$ , and (III) the organizational level of i. As in the whether-to-post model, the first component evaluates whether a focal account posts consistently (Anderson and De Palma 2013; International Committee of the Red Cross, International Federation of Red Cross and Red Crescent Societies, and Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs 2017). The second component measures how $j ^ { \prime } \mathrm { s }$ audience decision may exert social influence on i’s corresponding decision (Asvanund et al. 2004, Tang et al. 2012, Iyer and Katona 2016). Finally, the third component assesses the influence of organizational hierarchy.

The dependent variable for this model is defined as a binary variable that is one if content is posted for disaster victims and zero if for supporters. Thus, conditional on content being posted, the probability that i posts a tweet for victims in t is given by the following probit specification:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \operatorname * {P r} (V i c t i m _ {i, t} = 1 \mid P o s t _ {i, t} = 1) = \\ \Phi (\beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} V i c t i m _ {i, t - 1} + \beta_ {2} V i c t i m _ {j, t - 1} + \beta_ {3} S u p p o r t _ {i, t - 1} \\ + \beta_ {4} S u p p o r t _ {j, t - 1} + \beta_ {5} N a t i o n a l _ {i} \\ + \beta_ {6} V i c t i m _ {j, t - 1} \times N a t i o n a l _ {i} + \beta_ {7} S u p p o r t _ {j, t - 1} \times N a t i o n a l _ {i} \\ + \boldsymbol {\eta} \mathbf {X} _ {i, t} + \boldsymbol {\theta} \mathbf {W} _ {t}), \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

where Φ again represents the standard normal CDF. The first and second modeling components are reflected by the following four variables: $\hat { V i c t i m _ { i , t - 1 } }$ and $V i c t i m _ { j , t - 1 }$ are binary variables that equal one if i and j posted content for victims in $t - 1$ , respectively; $S u p p o r t _ { i , t - 1 }$ and $S u p p o r t _ { j , t - 1 }$ are binary variables that capture whether i and j posted content for supporters in $t - 1$ , respectively. We include variables related to both audiences because I and j may have posted tweets addressing victims in conjunction with tweets addressing supporters or may not have posted any tweets in t – 1. Of particular interest to us are the coefficients for $V i c t i m _ { j , t - 1 }$ and $S u p p o r t _ { j , t - 1 }$ . The former shows i’s propensity to post content for the same audience as $j ,$ whereas the latter shows its propensity to post content for a different audience.

As in Equation (1), we represent the third modeling component with National<sub>i</sub>. We also include interaction terms $V i c t i m _ { j , t - 1 } * N a t i o n a l _ { i }$ and $S u p p o r t _ { j , t - 1 } * N a t i o n a l _ { i }$ to allow for $i \prime \mathrm { s }$ propensity to match its audience decision to depend on its level. As in the whether-to -post model, we include control variables for the demand for conten by the focal account, the public’s interest in the wildfire, and the evolution of the disaster as well as hourly fixed effects.

## 4.2. Estimation

The following likelihood function provides the joint likelihood of observing the vector of i’s decisions to post $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ $P o s t _ { i , \cdot } )$ and the vector of i’s decisions of for whom to post $( \mathrm { i . e . , } V i c t i m _ { i , \cdot } ) ;$

$$
\begin{array}{l} L (P o s t _ {i, \cdot}, V i c t i m _ {i, \cdot}) = \\ \prod_ {t \in (P o s t _ {i, t} = 1)} \operatorname * {P r} (V i c t i m _ {i, t} \mid P o s t _ {i, t} = 1) \operatorname * {P r} (P o s t _ {i, t} = 1) \\ \prod_ {t \in (P o s t _ {i, t} = 0)} (1 - \operatorname * {P r} (P o s t _ {i, t} = 1)), \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

where $\mathrm { P r } ( P o s t _ { i , t } = 1 )$ is given by Equation (1) and $\mathrm { P r } ( V i c t i m _ { i , t } \mid P o s t _ { i , t } = 1 )$ is given by Equation (2) and its complement.<sup>2</sup> For those incidences in which a tweet is posted $( \mathrm { i . e . , } P o s t _ { i , t } = 1 )$ ), the likelihood is the product of the likelihood that a tweet is posted (from the whetherto-post model) and the likelihood that a tweet is directed toward a particular audience (from the for-whom-topost model). For those incidences in which a tweet is not posted $( \mathrm { i . e . , } P o s t _ { i , t } = 0 )$ , we only consider the likelihood associated with the whether-to-post model.

We fit the proposed joint likelihood model consisting of the product of probit densities using maximum likelihood estimation. The log-likelihood function is maxi mized with respect to the coefficients presented in Equations (1) and (2). Because the first order conditions arising from logged Equation (3) are nonlinear, we use numerical optimization methods to obtain the estimates. We set the starting values as the estimates of the individual models. In order to avoid local maxima, we repeat the estimation process using a number of different sets of starting values.

## 5. Engagement Model

The preceding analysis indicates that a DRO’s accounts favor matching their content creation decisions. Next, we answer our second and third research questions by assessing how engagement is impacted by a match versus a mismatch of content creation decisions by a DRO’s national and local accounts.

## 5.1. Model Formulation and Estimation

Our results in the previous section empirically confirm that the content production process across multiple accounts belonging to the same DRO is not independent. We build on this by considering that the outcome of one’s decision is also a function of the decisions of others in many scenarios (Rubinstein 1989, Mehta et al. 1994). We adopt this reasoning when examining the effect of coordination between a DRO’s accounts on engagement. Therefore, we test how engagement for a focal account is impacted by the content creation decisions made by itself and an affiliated account.

Consistent with the prior analysis, we focus on whe ther a match or a mismatch exists for the audience deci sion. We again denote a focal account with i and an affiliated account with j. The unit of analysis is also at the account-hourly level for the same reasons as stated earlier and because engagement for social media content can change rapidly from hour to hour (Wu and Huberman 2007, Goel et al. 2016). Table 4 provides the list of variables for the engagement model along with their measurements and descriptive statistics.

We define the dependent variable of Engagements as the total number of retweets, likes, replies, link clicks, media clicks, and hashtag clicks for tweets by focal account i in hour t. These metrics are commonly used by extant literature to measure engagement on Twitter $( \mathrm { e . g . } ,$ Lambrecht et al. 2018, Mallipeddi et al. 2021, Kumar et al. 2022). The number of engagements for i in t is specified using the following model:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \text { Engagements } _ {i, t} & = \mu_ {0} + \mu_ {1} \text { AudienceMatch } _ {i, t} + \mu_ {2} \text { Response } _ {t} \\ & \quad + \mu_ {3} \text { AudienceMatch } _ {i, t} \times \text { Response } _ {t} \\ & \quad + \boldsymbol {\phi} \mathbf {Z} _ {i, t} + \boldsymbol {\rho} \mathbf {W} _ {t} + \epsilon_ {i, t}, \end{array} \tag {4}\tag{4}
$$

where AudienceMatch<sub>i,t</sub> is a binary variable equal to one if a match exists between the audiences targeted by i in t and $j$ in t � 1 and zero if a mismatch exists. We determine audience with the results for whether a tweet was designed for disaster victims or supporters from our audience identification process presented in Section 3.3. The audience for i in t is one of four potential audiences: Victims, Supporters, Both, and None. The audience is set to Victims (Supporters) when tweets by i in t only targeted victims (supporters). If i generated at least one tweet for victims as well as supporters in $t ,$ the audience is set to Both. If i did not post any tweets in $t ,$ the audience is set to None. We evaluate the audience targeted by j in t � 1 similarly. Note that we use the audience of j in t � 1 instead of in t to avoid estimation issues arising from focal and affiliated accounts simultaneously influencing each other’s behavior, which is known as the reflection problem (Manski 1993).

The binary variable Response indicates if t occurs during the response or recovery phase. As in the content creation decisions model, we determine the response phase to end at 19:08 local time on May 18. Accordingly, Response takes on a value of one when t is before this date and time and zero if after. Moreover, we incorporate the interaction term, AudienceMatch × Response . The coefficient for this interaction term informs us how the effect of matching audiences on engagement is moderated by the phase of the disaster management cycle.

We use $\mathbf { Z } _ { i , t }$ to represent the vector of control variables. The control variables include the number of tweets aimed at disaster victims and supporters along with the number of tweets containing interactive elements, such as links, media (i.e., pictures, video), and hashtags. Additionally, we measure the demand for content published by an account with its change in followers and number of mentions. For the aforementioned control variables, we include measurements for i in addition to $j .$ This is because prior research documents that the engagement of one account may be impacted by the activity of similar or related accounts $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , Mallipeddi et al. 2021). As we do for the content creation decisions model, we control for the public’s interest in the wildfire on Twitter with the volume of disaster-related Twitter activity and outside of Twitter with Google Trends data. We also control for the evolution of the disaster with the count of hours elapsed since the start of the fire. Finally, we incorporate hourly fixed effects, which are denoted as $\mathbf { } { \mathbf { } } \mathbf { W } _ { t } ,$ to control for hourly usage patterns.

## 5.2. Results

The estimation results are presented in Table 5. The overall sample results are displayed under M3. The results from the national and local subsamples are shown in M4 and M5, respectively. We focus the discussion of our results on those of greatest interest to our study. These are the coefficients for $A u d i e n c e M a t c h _ { i , t }$ and the interaction term between $A u d i e n c e M a t c h _ { i , t }$ and Response<sub>t</sub>. We interpret the coefficients on a multiplicative scale because we use a negative binomial model, which is a nonlinear model (Buis 2010).

As shown in Table 5, the estimate for the main effect of $A u d i e n c e M a t c h _ { i , t }$ is consistently negative and significant in the overall sample $( - 0 . 3 5 1 , \bar { p } < \bar { 0 } . 0 1 )$ as well as in the national $( - 0 . 4 4 9 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ and the local $( - 0 . 3 1 2 , p < 0 . 1 0 )$ subsamples. This result suggests that, during the recovery phase, engagement for a focal account declines when its audience matches the audience of an affiliated account. The $A u d i e n c e M a t c h _ { i , t }$ estimate shown in M3 from the overall sample reveals that this decline is substantial. Holding all else equal, engagement for a focal account decreases by $2 9 . 6 \% \overline { { ( = \exp ( - 0 . 3 5 1 ) - 1 ) } }$ when its coordination strategy in the recovery phase involves convergence. Put differently, our results show that, during this phase, engagement is expected to be 29.6% higher when accounts within the same DRO do not speak with one voice but, rather, coordinate by mismatching their content creation decisions. Therefore, in support of differential attention theory (Unnava and Burnkrant 1991), we find that users are more sensitive to redundancy across a

DRO’s accounts in the recovery phase when uncertainty and urgency are lower. This behavior can be ascribed to users’ changing information needs and preferences to avoid incurring information processing costs in the recovery phase (Yan and Pedraza-Martinez 2019, Yoo et al. 2019).

The coefficient of the interaction term between $A u d i e n c e M a t c h _ { i , t }$ and Response<sub>t</sub> is consistently positive and significant (0.393, $p { < } 0 . 0 1$ for the overall sample; $0 . 3 8 5 , p < 0 . 0 1$ for the national subsample; $0 . 5 5 9 , p < 0 . 0 5$ for the local subsample). From the overall sample in M3, the estimate of 0.393 for this interaction indicates that the effect of $A u d i e n c e M a t c h _ { i , i }$ <sub>t</sub> becomes less negative by a factor of 1.481 $( = \exp ( 0 . 3 9 3 ) )$ in the response phase. This translates to a 4.3% lift in engagement for a focal account when it matches instead of mismatches the audience of its content with that of an affiliated account during this phase. As such, engagement for a focal account is decidedly higher in the response phase under a coordination strategy wherein a DRO’s accounts post convergent content. A potential explanation for this behavior is that such consistency raises the legitimacy and credibility of a DRO’s accounts (Coombs 1999, Massey 2001), to which users respond positively in order to cope with the stress and uncertainty inherent to the response phase.

These results indicate that the improvement to engagement from a match in the audience decision during the response phase instead of a mismatch is stronger for a local account than a national account. Hence, we find that the amount by which engagement increases from a match in content creation decisions depends on whether an account is at the national or local level.

In summary, our results demonstrate how engagement is impacted by whether a DRO’s accounts match their content creation decisions, such as for whom to produce content. We show that a match is better for engagement only in the immediate aftermath of a disaster (i.e., the response phase) and that this effect is more pronounced for a local account than a national account. As disaster response efforts shift to the recovery phase, engagement experiences a downturn when content creation decisions by a DRO’s accounts match. We further detect that a match in the recovery phase is worse for the engagement of a national account compared with a local account.

## 6. Robustness Checks

We verify that our results are not driven by modeling choices or variable measurements using an extensive battery of robustness checks. We describe the robustness checks in this section and provide the results in Online Appendix C. Reassuringly, the results of all robustness checks are qualitatively consistent with the original results.

## 6.1. Alternative Topic Modeling Approach

We first ensure that our models are not sensitive to our topic modeling approach. Instead of GSDMM, we identify the audience of tweets using a latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA) model (Blei et al. 2003), which is another prominent topic modeling approach. LDA has also been used to discover topics for tweets (e.g., Han et al. 2020) and is shown to perform well relative to GSDMM (Qiang et al. 2020). In addition, we reconsider how we assign tweets based on their topics to an audience. One of the topics identified by the GSDMM model pertains to updates about the disaster’s progression and response efforts. We originally allocate this topic to the audience of supporters, but this information may be relevant to victims as well. As such, we rerun our analyses with the tweets about updates assigned to victims instead of supporters. The full set of results from the alternative topic modeling approach is reported in Online Appendix C.1.

## 6.2. Alternative Specifications

We also test multiple alternative specifications to our models. First, we check that our results are not affected by the main variables of interest involving lags of one period. We extend our model to include additional lags through t – 3. Second, to ensure that our findings are not determined by the response phase cutoff, we use a different date to mark the end of the response phase and the start of the recovery phase. We choose May 24 because this is when reentry into impacted areas began, signaling the initiation of stabilization efforts (KPMG 2017). Third, we include the wind speed in the affected region as another proxy for interest in the wildfire. Wind speed is critical to the spread of wildfires, and disasters of greater size tend to attract greater media attention and public interest (Aflaki and Pedraza-Martinez 2016). Fourth, it is possible that, when responding to a disaster, DROs post social media content that is not related to the disaster but is still relevant to their mission and work. We drop 190 tweets (10.6% of the total tweets in our sample) that are not related to the wildfire in our study. Fifth, we reestimate the content creation decisions model using a binary logit specification and the engagement model using a Poisson specification. We report the estimation results from the aforementioned alternative specifications in Online Appendix C.1.

## 6.3. Robustness Checks for the Engagement Model

We run additional robustness checks for the specification of the engagement model only. Recall that the dependent variable is measured as the sum of retweets, likes, replies, link clicks, media clicks, and hashtag clicks. We make sure that our results are robust to the dependent variable being redefined as each of these metrics individually. Additionally, we address the potential concern that engagement is an autocorrelated variable by controlling for its lagged value (i.e., $E n g a g e m e n t _ { i , t - 1 } )$ . We also reestimate the engagement model with robust clustered standard errors at the account level to account for autocorrelation over time (Bertrand et al. 2004).

Finally, we verify that our results are not driven by the cases in which $A u d i e n c e M a t c h _ { i , t }$ equals one but the accounts did not post (i.e., audience for i in t and j in t – 1 is None). We include a binary variable $( B o t h T w e e t e d _ { i , t } )$ that equals one when i posted in t and j posted in t – 1 and equals zero otherwise. This allows us to separate the cases in which the targeted audience of both accounts was None instead of Victims, Supporters, or Both. The results of these robustness checks can be found in Online Appendix C.2.

## 6.4. Second Test Bed

To demonstrate further that our results are not sensitive to the focal disaster event in our study (i.e., the 2016 Fort McMurray wildfire), we assemble a second test bed. We collected Twitter data from the 2017 British Columbia (BC) wildfires in Canada. This disaster started with a two-hectare fire on July 6, 2017, that aggressively grew to 4,000 hectares in three days (Culbert et al. 2017). As a result, a provincial state of emergency was declared for the BC province, and more than 65,000 residents were evacuated. As with the 2016 Fort McMurray wildfire, the 2017 BC wildfire season was record-breaking in size and necessitated operations by the CRC at local and national levels.

Given the national and local involvement of the CRC for the 2017 BC wildfires, we collected data on the Twitter activity by the CRC and the CRC’s local account for the BC province (i.e., CRC-BC). This data set, although obtained for a different disaster event, is structurally analogous to our conceptual framework in that it contains multiple accounts representing the same DRO within a single social media platform. The data collection period for the second test bed begins with the start of the disaster on July 7 and ends on September 15, one month after the provincial state of emergency was lifted. This timeline spans the response and recovery phases, and its start and end parameters along with its length parallel that of the original data set. We set the conclusion of the response phase as July 24 when thousands of evacuees were allowed to return to their homes (Kane 2017). During this data collection period, the CRC and the CRC-BC posted 638 tweets.

We estimate the content creation decisions model from Equations (1) and (2) and the engagement model from Equation (4) using the second test bed. The results are reported in Online Appendix C.3 and are qualitatively consistent with our original set of results. This increases our confidence in our results and reduces concerns that they are derived from the original context. The fact that our findings are replicated in the second test bed also helps establish the generalizability of our results.

## 7. Discussion and Conclusions

Social media is an essential communication tool for DROs to disseminate critical and potentially life-saving information. Typically, large DROs, such as the CRC, provide information via more than one channel within the same social media platform by maintaining multiple accounts. Each account represents a different entity of the DRO. Our study proposes that engagement improves when a DRO’s accounts coordinate the generation of their social media content. Inspired by crisis communication theory, the coordination strategy may involve a match or a mismatch of content creation decisions, depending on the phase of the disaster management cycle.

Through data analysis, we find that content creation decisions made by a DRO’s accounts are not independent of each other and tend to match their content creation decisions. Our results also indicate that engagement is 4.3% higher with a match of content creation decisions during the response phase rather than a mismatch. Thus, we provide strong evidence for engagement increasing when accounts within the same DRO produce convergent content in the response phase. On the other hand, a mismatch of content creation decisions is 29.6% more effective for engagement during the recovery phase. We further find that the changes to engagement from matching content creation decisions are unequal between national and local accounts.

Our research unveils several significant insights and makes the following contributions to extant literature. First, we demonstrate that convergence across a DRO’s accounts does not always lead to the best outcome. Consequently, we show that there are exceptions to the standard recommendation from crisis communication theory that an organization’s channels should speak with one voice (Coombs 1999). We also extend the literature on coordination for DROs. To the best of our knowledge, we are among the first to examine how DROs should internally coordinate the distribution of information resources. Finally, our results highlight that the impact on engagement from a match of content creation decisions varies between national and local accounts. Contrary to claims that the internet has made the world flatter, we demonstrate that organizationa hierarchy transcends the off-line world and creates differences in the online world.

We also prescribe specific guidelines as to how national and local accounts within the same DRO should coordinate the production of their social media content. We find that a match of content creation decisions during the response phase is more effective at increasing engagement for local accounts than national accounts. Local accounts, therefore, should prioritize matching their content creation decisions with those made by national accounts. This suggests that local accounts should follow the lead of national accounts regarding the supply of information in the response phase. Such an order of operations is aligned with how physical resources are distributed by a DRO’s national and local entities (American Red Cross 2012). During the recovery phase, we show that, whereas both national and local accounts should avoid matching content creation decisions to enhance engagement, it is more imperative for national accounts to do so. Whereas our findings and implications are obtained from the context of disaster management, the framework of coordinating content production within a hierarchical structure can be applied to a broader context involving any organizations or firms that provide information through multiple chan nels on a single platform.

We conclude with a discussion of directions for expanding the generalizability of our study. We collected data from catastrophic wildfires, which are classified as sudden-onset disasters. However, DROs also need to supply information when responding to slow-onset disasters. Therefore, one avenue for future research is to test our findings on slow-onset disasters. One critical difference between slow- and sudden-onset disasters is that the latter category takes some time to develop and, thus, involves a preparedness phase. Because preparedness is an integral part of the disaster management cycle, a natural extension of our research is to investigate how DROs with multiple accounts should coordinate the production of their social media content during this phase. Another extension is to examine the coordination of a DRO’s accounts in long-term recovery because we focus on short-term recovery. We also encourage future work to shed light on the consistency of our findings in the con text of disasters with prolonged durations, such as the COVID-19 pandemic.

Second, we call on future research to examine if our results generalize beyond the context of intraorganizational coordination for DROs. Whereas prior research has thoroughly studied the interorganizational coordination of physical resources during disasters, there is an opportunity to study this type of coordination for information. Future research can assess how DROs should coordinate social media content production with other organizations in order to enhance engagement. Additionally, future studies can model the changes to engagement when coordination is facilitated between more than two accounts.

Finally, we call on researchers to apply crisis communication theory to their work. Crisis communication studies typically focus on for-profit firms and their communication strategies following crises for which they are responsible. However, this theory has grown to inform how organizations should communicate in the face of crises outside of their control and identifies additional crisis communi cation objectives beyond reputation repair. This is exemplified by DROs adopting the crisis communication framework. Furthermore, we encourage further evaluations of how social media engagement is affected by accounts within the same organization speaking with one voice. We investigate convergence of the audience decision, but future research can study the match of other content creation decisions, such as the actual message. Given that engagement can benefit disaster response efforts, such investigations will carry academic and practical value.
