# Routine and Innovative Use of Enterprise System: Intricacy of Change Management Levers, System Characteristics, and Regulatory Focus

Abstract. The success of enterprise systems (ESs) hinges on employees’ ability to integrate these systems into their daily tasks, encompassing both routine and innovative uses. This study proposes a model based on the context-specific theorization framework that examines the interplay between usage context (leadership styles and support structures), system characteristics (modularity and complexity), and user characteristics (situational regulatory focus) in shaping employees’ ES usage (routine and innovative uses). Using longitudinal field survey data from employees and managers at a large company implementing a customer-relationship-centric ES, we identify three key findings. First, distinct paths emerge from leadership styles (transactional versus transformational) and support structures (impersonal versus personal) that influence ES use through situational regulatory focus (prevention versus promotion). Second, system characteristics exhibit varying mod erating effects: system modularity strengthens the relationship between prevention focus and routine ES use. However, it has no significant effect on the relationship between promotion focus and innovative ES use. In contrast, system complexity weakens the relation ship between prevention focus and routine ES use while strengthening the relationship between promotion focus and innovative ES use. Third, routine and innovative ES use differentially influence job outcomes, including leader-rated job performance and employee self-evaluated job satisfaction. In summary, organizations should tailor change management levers, consider system design nuances, and acknowledge individual regulatory focus to maximize ES potential and improve employee outcomes. Theoretical contributions and managerial implications are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

Enterprise systems (ESs), such as customer relationship management (CRM), resource planning, and collaborative work systems (Sykes and Venkatesh 2017, Zhang and Venkatesh 2017, Sun et al. 2019), are crucial for digitalizing organizational operations<sup>1</sup> and empowering employees.<sup>2</sup> Despite their importance, many ES implementations fail to deliver the expected benefits (Karimi et al. 2007, Sykes et al. 2009). Notable examples include Hewlett-Packard’s \$160 million ES implementation failure, which dented its continuous effort to acquire information technology (IT) capability, whereas Rich-Con Steele’s bankruptcy was due to an unsuccessful implementation (Sykes 2015). A key insight is that employees, the primary users of these systems, significantly influence the outcomes of ES implementations (Jasperson et al. 2005, Hsieh et al. 2011, Lauterbach et al. 2020).

Employees’ system use can differ based on whether it is routine or innovative (Jasperson et al. 2005, Hsieh et al. 2011, Li et al. 2013, Schmitz et al. 2016, Sun et al. 2019). Routine use involves standardized interactions with the

ES to support job tasks (Sundaram et al. 2007, Li et al. 2013), which is an exploitative behavior (Maier et al. 2021). In contrast, innovative use entails exploring new ways to utilize the system (Ke et al. 2013, Li et al. 2013, Liang et al. 2015), which is an exploratory behavior (Maier et al. 2021). Both behaviors are essential for maximizing the benefits of ES (Li et al. 2013, Tong et al. 2015, Maier et al. 2021). Management’s challenge is how to encourage both types of use, given their different motivational drivers and mechanisms.

Our literature review reveals that we have a plethora of studies investigating how to motivate employees intrinsically (Li et al. 2013) or train them to be competent in using the system (Bala and Venkatesh 2015) (see Table A1 in Online Appendix A for the review summary). However, there is a lack of systematic examination of how to lead employees to conduct routine and innovative use, specifically with the prospect of affecting employees’ job outcomes. With an intent to fill in this knowledge gap and in response to the call for taking the context into greater consideration to develop more comprehensive insights into technology use (Hong et al. 2014, Ho et al. 2020, Venkatesh et al. 2023), we seek to answer the following research questions:

Research Question 1. What are (and how do) the change management levers influence ES routine and innovative use?

Research Question 2. How are the above influences contingent on the technological characteristics of the system?

Research Question 3. What are the consequences of ES routine and innovative use on employees’ job outcomes?

We develop our research model using the contextspecific theorization framework (Hong et al. 2014) and extend prior work in change management (Tsui et al. 1997, Shin et al. 2012) and regulatory focus (Higgins 1998). This framework emphasizes the “interplay between the characteristics of technologies, users, and usage contexts” (Hong et al. 2014, p. 132). We propose two change management levers, namely leadership styles (transactional and transformational) and support structures (personal and impersonal) (Armenakis et al. 1993, Kotter 1995, Sharma and Yetton 2003), to shape employees’ attitudes and behaviors in response to ES implementation, which alters job tasks (Jarvenpaa and Stoddard 1998, Sharma and Yetton 2003, Kim and Kankanhalli 2009, Morris and Venkatesh 2010). Different types of ES use (routine versus innovative) require different leadership and support approaches: leadership helps employees understand why to use the system, whereas support structures help with how to use it (Thong et al. 1997, Sharma and Yetton 2003, Sykes 2020). We also explore how change management levers activate self-regulatory goal orientations, such as prevention (focused on avoiding negative outcomes) and promotion (focused on growth) (Higgins 1998, Liang et al. 2013, Sassenberg and Hamstra 2017). Lastly, we consider system modularity and complexity (Liang et al. 2015, Peng and Guo 2019) as key technological characteristics that interact with regulatory focus to shape ES use: Modularity emphasizes the separability and recombination of ES modules and sets of functions; in contrast, system complexity stresses the difficulty in understanding and using an ES (Liang et al. 2015, Peng and Guo 2019).

We empirically test our research model using data from a cross-level longitudinal survey of employees and managers in a large electricity company implementing a CRM system. The current research enriches our understanding by unraveling the mechanisms lead ing to different types of ES use and offers four main contributions to the literature. First, it extends existing research by exploring how team-level change management (leadership styles and support structures) influences individual-level ES use, moving beyond technical characteristics and personal motivation (Ke et al. 2013, Li et al. 2013). We identify two distinct effectual paths link ing leadership (transactional versus transformational) and support structures (personal versus impersonal) to situational regulatory focus (prevention versus promotion) and ES use (routine versus innovative), thus providing a granular, targeted change management strategy. Second, we demonstrate how situational regu latory focus mediates the influence of change management levers on routine and innovative ES use. The unique underlying mechanism unveiled provides finegrained insights into the intricacy of shaping employee ES usage behaviors by the management. Third, as a response to the appeal of contextualization in theory development, our research work enriches the understanding of systematic factors driving different types of ES use behaviors (Hong et al. 2014, Te’eni 2015, Ho et al. 2020). By considering the moderating roles of system modularity and system complexity, we offer nuanced insights into how different technological characteristics of the system shape the relationship between situational regulatory focus and employees’ ES use. Finally, our results reveal that, although both ES uses could yield positive effects on job performance, only innovative use positively influences job satisfaction, which highlights the importance yet difference between routine and innovative use in shaping employees’ different job outcomes.

## 2. Theoretical Background

As introduced at the onset, we anchor on the contextspecific theorization framework to guide our theoretical development. The framework advocates the concurrent consideration of factors related to technology, user, and usage context to understand users’ beliefs and behaviors (Hong et al. 2014). This section is organized in tandem with this purview by diving into the literature on

ES use, change management (i.e., usage context), regulatory focus (i.e., user characteristics), and system modularity and system complexity (i.e., technological characteristics), which we synthesize to inform our theoretical model in Section 3.

## 2.1. Routine and Innovative ES Uses

In the postacceptance stage, ES implementation disrupts the work environment, requiring employees to adapt and integrate the system into their job tasks (Jasperson et al. 2005, Bala and Venkatesh 2015). Employees typically begin by applying limited system functions prescribed by the management or learned in training, progressing to routine use as they become familiar (Cooper and Zmud 1990, Hsieh et al. 2011). Routine use is defined as using ES in a routine and standardized way to support job tasks (Hsieh and Wang 2007, Thatcher et al. 2011). Also, employees can go beyond the prescribed usage by exploring new system functions and applying them creatively (Cooper and Zmud 1990, Hsieh et al. 2011). Innovative use refers to using ES in an innovative and novel manner to support job tasks (Jasperson et al. 2005, Hsieh and Wang 2007).

Routine use aligns with exploitation, focusing on stability and efficiency, whereas innovative use mirrors exploration, emphasizing creativity and adaptability (Faems et al. 2005, Li et al. 2013, Maier et al. 2021). Routine use helps employees master prescribed functions, whereas innovative use enhances effectiveness through experimentation (Hsieh and Wang 2007, Bala and Venkatesh 2015). Routine use differs as employees exhibit significant variance in standardization and integration of ES use in their work, and innovative use differs at employees’ discretion. Thus, both routine and innovative use vary across different employees (Li et al. 2013).

There could be three potential approaches in which routine and innovative uses are measured. The first approach is to obtain objective data, such as the system log of features used. Other than the practical reason for the difficulty in obtaining such data due to the company’s privacy and security concerns, there is an issue with the match between conceptualization and operationalization. Specifically, the data on the features used would not be able to draw inferences on the routine and innovative uses as such uses concern individual employees’ work. The second approach requires the immediate supervisor to rate their subordinates’ use behaviors. As Li et al. (2013) argue, “whether a behavior (which is hard to observe by a third party) is routine or innovative is in the beholder’s eyes” (p. 661). As such, the immediate supervisors may not be able to fully monitor and observe each of the subordinates’ usage of the ES and then reflect on the extent of their routine and innovative uses. Additionally, biases due to favoritism and degree of work coupling could confound the supervisors’ assessments. The third approach is to have the employees report their assessment of routine and innovative uses of ES. Routine and innovative uses require a reflection of one’s work, which can be hard to observe directly. In this study, we closely follow the operationalizations of the two constructs by Li et al. (2013) and set employees as the frame of reference for evaluating the nature of routine and innovative use. This is aligned with several individual-level survey studies with respondents to self-evaluate or report their behaviors (Li et al. 2013, Chen et al. 2022, Venkatesh et al. 2023).

## 2.2. Usage Context: Change Management Levers

Change entails altering organizational routines and strategies, as well as disrupting the work system (Kotter 1995, Sharma and Yetton 2003). Such an organizational change can interrupt employees’ work routines, leading to stressful demands, such as increasing workloads and adapting to new work relationships (Fugate et al. 2008). Similarly, when a new ES is implemented, drastic changes in business processes and workflow can result in entirely new jobs, creating stress for related employees (Sykes and Venkatesh 2017, Ke et al. 2021). In such situations, employees can become change-averse and reluctant to enact behaviors that are conducive to implementing change (Kiefer 2005). Effecting a change would thus necessitate measures to help employees cope (Tsui et al. 1997, Shin et al. 2012), which requires the right leadership and provision of needed support structures (Sharma and Yetton 2003, Herold et al. 2007, Hom et al. 2009).

2.2.1. Leadership Styles. Effective leadership influences employees (Sharma and Yetton 2003, Kark and Dijk 2007, Kark et al. 2018) by clarifying required behaviors, administering rewards and punishments, communicating job expectations, and creating working conditions that affect employees’ beliefs of a system (Dong et al. 2007, Liang et al. 2007) and their system usage behaviors (Armstrong and Sambamurthy 1999, Li and Hsieh 2007, Liang et al. 2013, Zhang 2017) (see Table B1 in Online Appendix B for the review summary). Leadership styles can be transactional or transformational. Transactional leaders motivate employees by rewarding them for performing the required activities. They direct employees through a material exchange when employees follow rules, procedures, and normative beliefs. In contrast, transformational leaders inspire employees by meeting employees’ intrinsic needs for growth (Grant 2012). These two leadership styles are not mutually exclusive; leaders frequently display both behaviors (Alexander and van Knippenberg 2014). In the current study, we focus on leadership exercised by the team leader as it reflects stimuli perceived by all team members, which is particularly useful during times of change (Sharma and Yetton 2003, Herold et al. 2008). Also, given their proximity to employees, the team leader can influence employees more effectively than the leaders at higher organizational levels (Maruping and Magni 2015).

2.2.2. Support Structures. Support structures, such as documentation, online helpdesk support, and peer advice networks (Sykes 2015, 2020), help employees cope with change demands and reduce system-related stress (Sykes et al. 2009, Bala and Venkatesh 2015, Ke et al. 2021). These structures enhance employees’ competence and empower them to use the system effectively (Maruping and Magni 2015, Ke et al. 2021). Based on resource customization and distribution, support structures can be categorized as impersonal or personal (Tong et al. 2015). Impersonal support consists of standardized resources, such as system documentation and user manuals, accessible to all users for consistent guidance (Beaudry and Pinsonneault 2010). In contrast, personal support provides tailored assistance through information systems (IS) specialists, peer user support, and peer champions (Sykes et al. 2014), addressing employees’ unique needs. It is important to note that, although personal guidance often features in training, user training typically occurs before actual system usage and is not considered a support structure (Tong et al. 2015). Because support resources vary across organizational units and employees rely on proximal networks (Liang et al. 2010), we conceptualize support structures at the team level, following Ke et al. (2021).

In the view that context effects are typically defined as “the set of factors surrounding a phenomenon that exert some direct or indirect influence on it-also characterized as explanatory factors associated with higher levels of analysis than those expressly under investigation” (Whetten et al. 2009, p. 31), we treat change management levers in terms of leadership styles and support structure as variables characterizing the usage context. Also, by adopting a cross-level perspective, we examine how they affect and interact with other aspects of the phenomenon, that is, user and technological characteristics (Hong et al. 2014).

## 2.3. User Characteristics: Regulatory Focus

Regulatory focus theory suggests two types of motivational orientation, namely prevention focus and promotion focus (Higgins 1998), which can affect individuals behaviors. Prevention focuses on vigilance, fear of failure, and pursuit of duties, obligations, and responsibilities (Higgins 1997, 1998). Prevention-focused individuals tend to focus on how to avoid risk and loss (Arazy and Gellatly 2012). Promotion focus involves an eager approach toward advancement, growth, and accomplishment. People with such a regulatory focus tend to be motivated and persistent to pursue success and gains (Higgins et al. 1994). Thus, individuals with a prevention focus try to become the “ought self” by assuming duties and obligations. In contrast, people with a promotion focus try to become the “ideal self” by fulfilling accomplishments and aspirations (Liang et al. 2013). Although regulatory focus can be chronic (i.e., stable over a relatively long time), it can also be situational, that is, a state of individuals. Thus, contextual factors can induce it (Yeo and Park 2006, Weber and Mayer 2011).

Given that we intend to study how management activates employees’ motivation orientation, we focus on situational regulatory focus in the current study and examine its mediating effects on the relationship between change management levers and ES use. According to Kark and Van Dijk (2019), the differentiation between chronic and situational regulatory focus “is crucial in leader-follower regulatory-focus studies because it affects the type of study and role that regulatory focus will play in the research model” (p. 512). Indeed, prior IS studies have examined chronic regulatory focus and found support for its moderating effects on the relationship between management tactics and employees’ ES use and compliance (Ke et al. 2013, Liang et al. 2013). As such, we consider chronic regulatory focus as a control variable to recognize the dual nature of regulatory focus.

## 2.4. Technological Characteristics: System Modularity and Complexity

The context-specific theorization underscores the significance of considering the specificity of technology characteristics and emphasizes the interaction between technology attributes and individual factors (Hong et al. 2014). As such, we reviewed the literature and found that system modularity and system complexity are recognized as the inherent technological characteristics of ES, such as CRM (Liang et al. 2015, Peng and Guo 2019). Complexity describes many parts or elements that make an ES difficult to understand. In contrast, modularity describes the tight coupling between an ES’s compo nents or modules that can be designed independently yet function together as a holistic system (Xue et al. 2013, Vickery et al. 2016).

System modularity refers to “the extent to which the modules of an implemented ES can be separated and recombined” (Peng and Guo 2019, p. 442). High system modularity enables structural focus in configuring systems and mixing and matching modules and components. Also, it induces structural constraints that limit the scope of the entire system accessible to employees (Xue et al. 2013). The current ES designs often use the module-based approach, in which each module relates to certain business-related tasks. The implemented ES with high system modularity can provide employees with reusable functions that can be flexibly applied to changing task domains (Peng and Guo 2019). A CRM with high system modularity allows employees to seamlessly integrate the customer service module with the customer profile management module. For example, as a lead progresses, it triggers automated actions in the customer profile management module, such as creation and updates.

System complexity refers to the extent to which an ES is difficult to understand and use. A system is complex when “it is made up of a large number of parts that interact in a nonsimple way” (Liang et al. 2015, p. 328). Contemporary ES, such as CRM, is usually complex, with many interrelated components arranged deliberately to help support various business functions (Bala and Venkatesh 2013). Some researchers regard system complexity as the opposite of perceived ease of use (Davis 1989, Liang et al. 2015). Although system complexity can hinder individuals’ use of systems in general, it offers a wealth of functions to explore diverse ways to achieve business goals (Jasperson et al. 2005, Liang et al. 2015).

Prior studies have examined how these two system characteristics individually affect user behaviors. For example, Peng and collaborators study the direct effect of system modularity on employees’ exploration and extended use of a system (Peng et al. 2018, Peng and Guo 2019). Liang et al. (2015) investigate how system complexity moderates the effects of job autonomy and task variety on system exploration. Upgrading, reconfiguration, and adaptation can change system modularity and complexity. Thus, employees’ perceptions of system modularity and system complexity vary due to different trainings received and learning abilities (Liang et al. 2015). As system modularity and system complexity impose different demands on employees and provide room for exploration in different manners, they may interact with different situational regulatory focuses, thereby affecting employees’ ES usage behavior.

## 3. Research Model and Hypotheses

Figure 1 depicts our research model. We propose a set of intertwined relationships among change management levers (as manifested in leadership styles and support structures) as antecedents, the ES characteristics (i.e., system modularity and system complexity) as moderators, and the user’s situational regulatory focus (i.e., prevention and promotion) as mediators. Finally, for completeness, we consider the consequence of ES use on job outcomes measured by leader-rated job performance and job satisfaction.

## 3.1. Change Management Levers and Situational Regulatory Focus

3.1.1. Leadership Styles. Leadership behaviors guide employees’ sense-making of the change (i.e., seeing “why” change is needed) and mobilize them to apply self-regulatory strategies consistent with the context (Kark and Dijk 2007, Sassenberg and Hamstra 2017). With a formal system of rewards and punishments, transactional leadership signals to employees the organization’s expectations for individual employees’ behaviors; that is, following rules and applying the system will allow employees to receive positive reinforcement and avoid negative consequences (Dong et al. 2007, Ke and Wei 2008, Liang et al. 2013, Kark et al. 2018). Also, it creates an exchange-based relationship by clarifying the rules and routine issues regarding ES use, which emphasizes employees’ obligation to apply the system (Pieterse et al. 2009, Luo et al. 2015). Hence, transactional leadership motivates employees to execute their work at a level that meets the management’s expectations and fulfills their job responsibilities, thus activating situational prevention focus among employees when using the ES (Kark et al. 2018).

In contrast, transformational leadership induces a situational promotion focus among employees when using the ES. By communicating the vision of ES implementation, transformational leaders paint a vivid and bright future, which inspires employees to seek advancement with the system (Li and Hsieh 2007). Also, with the focus on transforming the existing state of affairs (Bass 1985, Pieterse et al. 2009), transformational leaders encourage followers to think outside the box, emphasize divergent thinking, and develop a sense of self-efficacy (Sassenberg and Hamstra 2017, Kark et al. 2018). Furthermore, they energize employees to strive for intrinsic capability development and stimulate the desire to pursue the “ideal self” (Kark et al. 2015, 2018).

Hypothesis 1a (b). Transactional (transformational) leadership positively relates to situational prevention (promotion) focus.

3.1.2. Support Structures. Support structures, comprising impersonal and personal support, help employees adapt to shifting demands by assisting them in grasping the “how” required to use the system effectively (Bala and Venkatesh 2015, Sykes 2020, Ke et al. 2021). Impersonal support includes standardized resources such as user manuals, brochures, and multimedia files, which provide detailed instructions for workflows and task responsibilities (Beaudry and Pinsonneault 2010). These materials are rule-based and accessible to all employees, helping them avoid errors by adhering to prescribed procedures. Employees with situational prevention focus, characterized by a desire to avoid mistakes, mitigate risks, and follow established rules, value this structured guidance, as it aligns with their need for stability and error avoidance. Hence, impersonal support activates situational prevention focus by offering clear, reliable direction.

Personal support, in contrast, is a customized resource that facilitates employees to resolve ad hoc issues and use system functionalities in innovative ways (Ke et al. 2021). This type of support provide specific, relevant advice for emerging problems, making it dynamic and interactive (Tong et al. 2015). Thus, personal support aligns well with the goal of promotion focus and would activate employees’ situationa promotion focus.

Hypothesis 2a (b). Impersonal (personal) support positively relates to situational prevention (promotion) focus.

## 3.2. Situational Regulatory Focus and ES Use

As a self-regulatory behavior (Jasperson et al. 2005), ES is used by employees to align with their goals, such as avoiding risk and seeking gains (Brockner and Higgins 2001). Although routine and innovative ES use coexist (Li et al. 2013), they have different underlying motivational orientations. Specifically, with a situational prevention focus, employees will be risk averse and choose to avoid negative consequences by following rules. In routine use, employees comply with the pre scribed system application, which allows them to avoid making mistakes and thus reduce the risk of being punished by the management (Li et al. 2013, Liang et al. 2013). Also, situational prevention focus drives employees to assume their duty. Since routine use of the system enables employees to become experienced enough to perform job tasks efficiently (Sykes and Venkatesh 2017), it allows employees to fulfill their obligations to avoid possible losses (Pieterse et al. 2009, Luo et al. 2015). Hence, we conjecture that situational prevention focus would drive employees to conduct routine use.

In contrast, with situational promotion focus, employees endeavor to change the existing state of affairs (Bass 1985, Pieterse et al. 2009), thinking divergently and developing a sense of self-efficacy (Sassenberg and Hamstra 2017, Kark et al. 2018). Individuals with such a regulatory focus tend to exhibit an explor atory orientation (Friedman and Fo¨rster 2001). Applying this notion to the current research, we expect that situational promotion focus would motivate employees to experiment with the system proactively to find functions different from what the management prescribes (Li and Hsieh 2007, Pieterse et al. 2009). Also, promotion focused individuals pursue gains and growth (Higgins et al. 1994). Through innovative use, employees will find more effective ways of applying the system, including discovering new and combining functions that can best support their job task accomplishment in a novel way (Sun 2012, Bala and Venkatesh 2015). It allows employees to use the system to its fullest potential and thus maximize the gains from the system (Chen et al. 2022). As such, the focus on situational promotion would lead employees to use innovative methods of the ES.

Hypothesis 3a (b). Situational prevention (promotion) focus positively relates to routine (innovative) ES use.

## 3.3. System Modularity and System Complexity as Moderators

Regulatory fit theory suggests that individuals feel right when the means to attain the goal sustains their regulatory focus, which motivates them to expend efforts on related tasks (Higgins 2005, Hamstra et al. 2011, Liang et al. 2013). Applying this notion, we expect ES characteristics, that is, system modularity and system complexity, to moderate how situational regulatory focus affects employees’ ES use. Specifically, system modularity and system complexity provide different scopes of the system for use and impose different demands for employee effort. They align with the goals of different types of situational regulatory focus (Higgins 2000, 2005). The resulting alignment and misalignment will cause differences in regulatory fit experienced by employees when using the system. Hence, we expect the technological characteristics to moderate the relationships between situational regulatory focus and employees’ ES use in different ways.

3.3.1. System Modularity. System modularity enables the decomposition and recombination of system components, breaking applications into smaller, independent parts (Peng and Guo 2019). This compartmentalization simplifies understanding and navigation, allows localized error resolution, and minimizes risks of system-wide failure. Employees perceiving high modularity experience greater structural control and reduced cognitive effort, aligning with the prevention focus’ emphasis on avoiding risks and minimizing errors (Ke et al. 2013, Liang et al. 2013). Also, system modularity allows users to employ a “divide and conquer” approach, enhancing manageability and predictability, which suits preventionfocused employees’ preference for stability and certainty (Benjamin and Flynn 2006, Hamstra et al. 2011). Consequently, system modularity interacts with situational prevention focus and enables employees to sense a regulatory fit, which sustains employees’ effort in ES routine use and thus strengthens the effect of situational prevention focus.

In contrast, system modularity can hinder the innovative ES use of situational promotion-focused employees. Although system modularity enables component flexibility (Peng et al. 2018), it introduces structural constraints, creating boundaries between modules that restrict cross-module exploration. For example, marketing employees may be limited to functions within their module, curbing opportunities to explore other modules and find functions that can better support cross-functional tasks. These constraints conflict with promotion-focused employees’ goals of seeking opportunities and achieving growth, leading to tunnel vision and reduced system-wide understanding (Ke et al. 2021). Without a holistic view, promotion-focused employees face barriers to collaboration and novel application of the system, weakening their regulatory fit and reducing their effort in engaging innovative ES use.

Hypothesis 4a (b). System modularity positively (negatively) moderates the relationship between situational prevention (promotion) focus and routine (innovative) ES use.

3.3.2. System Complexity. System complexity challenges employees with a situational prevention focus, who prioritize risk avoidance and clean processes. Complex systems, such as CRM, involve numerous features, functions, and interfaces, heightening the likelihood of errors and system failures (Liang et al. 2015). These systems, with their intricate interactions among components, demand significant cognitive effort to understand and navigate (Chen et al. 2021). It creates uncertainty for employees with a situational prevention focus about their ability to use the complex system effectively without undue risk or effort. This misalignment with their preference for straightforward and predictable workflows discourages engagement in routine ES use. Moreover, the cognitive load and potential for mistakes associated with system complexity exacerbate stress, reducing their regulatory fit and making sustained routine use difficult (Ke et al. 2013, Liang et al. 2013).

In contrast, system complexity can motivate employ ees with a situational promotion focus, who thrive on growth and innovation. Complex systems provide rich opportunities for exploration and the discovery of novel applications to enhance job performance (Liang et al. 2015). Promotion-focused individuals view complexity as an avenue for learning and creativity, aligning with their drive to challenge the status quo and pursue personal development (Benjamin and Flynn 2006, Hamstra et al. 2011). The intricate interactions with a complex system encourage diverse perspectives and problemsolving approaches, fostering regulatory fit for employees with a situational promotion focus (Higgins 2005). By leveraging these opportunities, they are motivated to engage deeply in innovative ES use, aligning system capabilities with their aspirations for growth and collaboration (Liang et al. 2013, Hamstra et al. 2014).

Hypothesis 5a (b). System complexity positively (negatively) moderates the relationship between situational pro motion (prevention) focus and innovative (routine) ES use.

## 3.4. ES Use and Job Outcomes

The existing literature suggests ES users’ routine and innovative use behaviors can improve their job outcomes (Li et al. 2013, Bala and Venkatesh 2015). We include such consideration in our research not only due to the pursuit of complete theorization, spanning from antecedents through behaviors to outcomes, but also to fill in a gap in prior research that has not investigated their effects on job outcomes in a single model. We propose that routine and innovative use improve job performance and satisfaction.

Routine use stresses that employees use the prescribed functions of the ES in their work context (Li et al. 2013). Employees performing routine use would regard ES as a regular part of their work and apply ES functions in standardized work processes (Saga and Zmud 1994). Such repetitive use of ES helps employees become familiar with a set of predefined rules and procedures embedded in ES and thus enables employees to better integrate their ES use with their job tasks (Luo et al. 2015). In this view, routine use will help employees perform routine and standardized job tasks more efficiently, leading to higher job performance (Li et al. 2013, Luo et al. 2015).

Routine use can also positively impact job satisfaction (Moqbel et al. 2013, Bala and Venkatesh 2015). Routine use typically involves minimal adaptation because employees leverage a set of ES functions prescribed by the management or learned during the training to perform daily organizational tasks (Bala and Venkatesh 2015). By performing limited adaptation to meet their job expectations, employees would “relax and enjoy life” (Beaudry and Pinsonneault 2005, p. 501). In particular, employees can develop a sense of job enrichment and transformation by leveraging a few core functions of the ES that are consistent with the management’s intention (Bala and Venkatesh 2015).

Hypothesis 6. Routine ES use positively relates to (a) job performance and (b) job satisfaction.

Innovative use represents the employees’ creative usage of ES in their organizational work contexts. By applying ES innovatively, employees will take advantage of ES functions not identified by designers or implementing teams but can better support their job tasks (Jasperson et al. 2005). Thus, innovative use helps employees better use the systems and makes them more competitive, enhancing job performance beyond what routine use enables (Burton-Jones and Grange 2013, Bala and Venkatesh 2015, Liang et al. 2015).

Furthermore, innovative use enables employees to explore and experiment with ES functions to leverage the potential of ES to a higher level; it also makes the ES more flexible and appropriate for addressing ad hoc challenging tasks (Ke et al. 2013, Li et al. 2013, Luo et al. 2015). For example, when employees explore the ES functions through different paths, they feel empowered and believe the ES enriches their jobs (Bala and Venkatesh 2015). More importantly, employees may find job activities exciting and engaging by proactively exploring the ES functions. Employees will develop a sense of job enrichment and transformation in this exploration process. Accordingly, we propose that innovative use helps improve both job performance and satisfaction.

Hypothesis 7. Innovative ES use positively relates to (a) job performance and (b) job satisfaction.

## 4. Research Method

## 4.1. Research Site and Sample

We tested the hypotheses using longitudinal survey data from a Fortune 500 electricity organization in China. The study focused on a provincial subsidiary managing 110 customer service groups, each compris ing 4–10 employees, which had implemented a CRM system to streamline customer service. The CRM includes five core modules: customer service, customer profile management, electricity charge management, electric energy metering, and system support. These modules enable employees to manage customer data, address electricity demand, and predict consumption trends (see Figure C1 in Online Appendix C for a highlevel architecture of the CRM system). At the time of our data collection, the provincial subsidiary organization implemented the CRM system across different customer service groups for about 14 months, which is beyond the typical 8- to 12-month acceptance timeframe for major IS implementations (Gattiker and Goodhue 2005, Morris and Venkatesh 2010, Li et al. 2013). During this implementation stage, employees usually become familiar enough with the system and extend their system function usage. Thus, it is an appropriate timeframe to examine employees’ routine and innovative use behaviors of the system at this stage (Ke et al. 2013, Li et al. 2013, Liang et al. 2015).

We surveyed 800 customer service employees who used the CRM system daily as part of their jobs. Corresponding to our expectations, these customer service employees can conduct routine and innovative CRM to support their tasks. For example, our interviews indicated that customer service employees, on the one hand, can use CRM to process some basic transactions on a routine basis. On the other hand, they can also use CRM data to segment customers based on their energy usage patterns and preferences and then offer personalized recommendations to help them optimize their energy consumption and reduce costs. Moreover, our interview with the customer service employees and their group managers indicated no explicit rewards for routine use or innovative use. The customer service employees had significant discretion in engaging in routine and/or innovative use of the CRM.

The data were collected through three waves. In the first wave, we sent a cover letter explaining the research objectives of our study. Employees provided information on leadership styles, support structures, situational regulatory focus, system characteristics (modularity and complexity), and demographic and control variables (e.g., computer self-efficacy, personal innovativeness with IT, intrinsic motivation, creativity and chronic regulatory focus). The respondents were required to generate their participation codes through a coding system, which enabled us to link their responses with those in the subsequent stages of the survey. A total of 504 returned questionnaires were collected, representing a 63% response rate. Two months later, in the second wave, employees reported on their routine and innovative use of the CRM, resulting in 375 matched responses from 92 teams. In the third wave, two months after the second data collection, employees reported job satisfaction and group leaders rated their team members’ performance. The two-month lag in time avoided potential noise in the change management–ES use–job outcomes relationship and has been commonly used in the longitudinal design for ES use studies (Hsieh et al. 2011). However, the leaders were informed that their members’ ratings would be confidential, and they did not know whether these members participated in the survey. Similarly, the members had no information about their leader-rated performance data.

To ensure that the employees had been working under the same leadership, we used a specific question to determine whether they had left the company, changed to another department, or worked under different direct leaders. If the answer was yes, they were excused from responding to the second or third stage of the survey. After linking the responses received in the third wave of the survey with those in the second wave, we finally had 365 pairs of valid returned questionnaires from 91 teams, with a total response rate of 45.6%. The number of respondents in a team ranged from three to seven, with an average of four among all the teams. Table 1 shows the demographic information of the respondents. We compared the $\chi ^ { 2 } \mathbf { s }$ from respondents to nonrespondents to examine the possible nonresponse bias. Based on their demographic data, we found no sig nificant difference between these two groups.

## 4.2. Measurements

We first developed an English version of the questionnaire by adopting/adapting previously validated measures from the existing literature. We used seven-point Likert scales, with options ranging from one (strongly disagree) to seven (strongly agree) to measure the items. Given that this study was conducted in China, four bilingual researchers who were knowledgeable about the subject matter first translated the English version of the questionnaire into Chinese independently and then discussed their translation results to achieve a consensus (Van de Vijver and Leung 1997). Following the conventional back-translation method suggested by Brislin (1980), we subsequently asked a translator unaware of our research context to back-translate the Chinese version of the questionnaire into English. Finally, the two English questionnaires were compared and modified to ensure the Chinese version was equivalent to the original English version. All the measurement items are listed in Table D1 (Online Appendix D).

4.2.1. Change Management Levers. Following prior studies (Kark et al. 2015, Zhang 2017, Kark et al. 2018), we measured transformational leadership with 16 items of four subscales assessing idealized influence, inspirational motivation, intellectual stimulation, and individ ualized consideration. The transactional leadership scale included transactional active management and transactional passive management. We modeled transformational leadership as a second-order formative construct with reflective items for each of the four dimensions and transactional leadership as a secondorder formative construct with reflective items for each of the two dimensions. The three items used to measure personal support and four used to measure impersonal support were adapted from Tong et al. (2015).

4.2.2. Regulatory Focus. We measured chronic prevention and promotion focus by adopting Haws et al. (2010) with five items for each. These measures were also used in the recent IS context and reported high reliability and validity (Wu et al. 2019). We measured situational regulatory focus by adapting the scale from Haws et al. (2010) into the IS context, which is more specifically directed to ES use.

## 4.3. Common Method Bias and Multicollinearity Test

Several measures addressed the potential common method bias (Podsakoff et al. 2003, MacKenzie et al. 2011). First, we conducted a longitudinal design using three waves of data (Podsakoff et al. 2003). The items were randomized within blocks and anonymously collected, which has been suggested as an effective way to reduce common method bias (Podsakoff et al. 2003, MacKenzie et al. 2011). Second, we applied the marker variable technique to examine the common method bias (Lindell and Whitney 2001). We used the respondents agreement on the statement “I hope my next car is blue” as the marker variable (Carter et al. 2020), which was unlikely to be theoretically related to our model’s constructs. Based on the method advocated by Lindell and Whitney (2001), we used the lowest positive correlation $( r = 0 . 0 0 3 \cdot$ : the correlation between the marker variable and impersonal support) to correct the correlations among the constructs. As shown in Table 2, the adjusted correlations indicated that none of the insignificant correlations became significant. All the significant correlations remained significant, indicating common method bias was unlikely to be a major issue with the data set.

We also conducted a multicollinearity test by assessing the variance inflation factor (VIF) and tolerance values. Generally, multicollinearity is indicated by a VIF value higher than 10.0 or a tolerance value less than 0.1 (Mason and Perreault 1991). We tested these values and found that the highest VIF and the lowest tolerance values were 4.54 and 0.22, respectively. This result indicated that multicollinearity was not a significant issue in this study.

## 4.4. Assessing the Measurement Mode

We evaluated the measurement model by assessing the constructs’ reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity. Table E1 (Online Appendix E) showed that composite reliability ranged from 0.90 to 0.98, and Cronbach’s alpha ranged from 0.87 to 0.98. Both were higher than 0.70, indicating good reliability of the measurements (Fornell and Larcker 1981). We further tested convergent and discriminant validity. The convergent validity was tested based on the value of loading and average variance extracted (AVE). Table E1 (Online Appendix E) shows that the loadings of all items were higher than 0.70 (except INS1, which was deleted for further analysis because of low loading), and the tvalues were significant at the $p < 0 . 0 0 1$ level. The AVE values ranged from 0.66 to 0.94, above the 0.50 recommended level (Koufteros 1999). These results suggested that our measures had good convergent validity.

To assess the discriminant validity, we calculated the square roots of the AVE of each construct and compared them with the correlations among constructs. Table 2 shows that the square roots of AVEs for all constructs were more significant than the correlations between constructs, confirming the discriminant validity (Koufteros 1999). Furthermore, we followed Gerbing and Anderson (1988) and performed two separate confirmatory factor analysis models (i.e., unconstrained and constrained) for all possible pairs of constructs to assess discriminant validity further. The results showed that all the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ differences were statistically different at $p <$ 0.001 level, which provided sufficient discriminant validity of the constructs.

## 4.5. Multilevel Validity Testing

Our model included individual-level (i.e., situational regulatory focus) and team-level constructs (i.e., leadership styles and support structures). To test whether considering leadership styles and support structures as team-level variables is appropriate, we assessed withinand between-group homogeneity to determine whether aggregation is viable within naturally occurring groups in two ways (Bliese 2000). First, we assessed withingroup homogeneity using the $r _ { w g \ ( j ) }$ statistic. The mean $r _ { w g \ ( j ) }$ of transactional and transformational leadership are 0.83 and 0.89, respectively. The mean $r _ { w g \ ( j ) }$ of personal and impersonal support are 0.87 and 0.89, respectively. James et al. (1984) state that the within-group agreement is sufficient if $r _ { w g \ ( j ) }$ equals or exceeds 0.70. Second, we computed an analysis of variance and associated intraclass correlation coefficients $( \mathrm { I C C s } ) ,$ , justifying the aggregation of individual-level scores to the team level for leadership and support structures. The ICC(1) value reflects between-team variance in individual responses, and ICC(2) estimates the reliability of the team-level means (Raudenbush and Bryk 2002). Therefore, ICC(1) values greater than 0.12 and ICC(2) values greater than 0.50 are acceptable (Liao and Rupp 2005). The results of a one-way analysis of variance indicated significant differences across teams for transactional leadership $( F _ { 9 0 , 2 7 4 } = 9 . 0 2 0 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , transformational leadership $( F _ { 9 0 , 2 7 4 } = 5 . 6 4 8 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , personal support $( F _ { 9 0 , 2 7 4 } = 4 . 8 1 8 , ~ p ~ < ~ 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , and impersonal support $( F _ { 9 0 , 2 7 4 } = 3 . 5 9 3 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . ICC(1) and ICC(2) were 0.67 and 0.89 for transactional leadership, 0.54 and 0.82 for transformational leadership, 0.49 and 0.79 for the personal support structure, and 0.39 and 0.72 for the impersonal support structure, respectively. Thus, aggregating the individual scores for transactional leadership, transformational leadership, personal support, and impersonal support is appropriate.

## 4.6. Results

4.6.1. Change Management Levers and Situationa Regulatory Focus. To test change management levers multilevel effects on employees’ situational regulatory focus, we employed hierarchical linear modeling (HLM) (Kang et al. 2011). Given that transformational and transactional leadership are second-order formative constructs, we modeled the paths from the lower-order to the higher-order construct following Chin et al. (2003) and Edwards (2011). We then used the scores of the latent variables from the PLS analysis as the formative measures for transformational and transactional leadership. To minimize the possibility of multicollinearity, we grouped mean-centered level 1 predictors and grand-mean-centered level 2 predictors (Hofmann and Gavin 2016). Before testing our hypotheses, we tested whether there was substantial between-group variation in individuals’ situational regulatory focus by setting a null model. The results reveal that 43.36% of the variance needed to be explained by the between-group variance for situational prevention focus $( \chi ^ { 2 } ( 9 1 ) = 6 0 . { \overset { \cdot } { 9 } } 8 , p <$ 0.001) and 50.43% of the variance needed to be explained by the between-group variance for situational promotion focus $( \chi ^ { 2 } ( 9 1 ) = \stackrel { \smile } { 5 } 4 . 8 \dot { 2 } , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ). ## 4.7. Additional Analyses

We performed several additional analyses.

First, although we did not explicitly propose hypotheses about the mediating roles of situational regulatory focus in the relationship between change management levers and ES use, we utilized bootstrapping procedures to test potential cross-level mediation (Preacher and Hayes 2008). ## 5. Discussion

In the context of large-scale system implementations, ES plays a pivotal role. These systems demand employees adaptation and seamless integration into their daily tasks. The effectiveness of this integration is reflected in both routine and innovative uses of the ES. By anchoring on the context-specific theorization framework (Hong et al. 2014) and extending the extant works in change management (Tsui et al. 1997, Shin et al. 2012) and regulatory focus (Higgins 1998), we theorize and empirically examine how change management levers affect employees’ different ES uses $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ routine and innovative uses) as mediated by situational regulatory focus at the postacceptance stage. Our empirical investigation reveals several major insights we discuss in detail and perspectives when highlighting the study’s contributions.

## 5.1. Theoretical Contributions

The current study offers four main contributions to the theory. First, it contributes significantly to the theoretical discourse on ES adoption by focusing on change management levers, specifically leadership styles and support structures. Although prior research has primarily examined individual or technical factors (Ke et al. 2013, Li et al. 2013, Bala and Venkatesh 2015), as summa rized in Table A1 (Online Appendix A), our work transcends these boundaries. By exploring the “why” and “how” of system use (Thong et al. 1997, Sharma and Yetton 2003, Sykes 2020), we investigate how manage ment can inspire diverse ES usage behaviors among employees. We shed light on the effectiveness of leadership styles (transactional versus transformational) and support structures (impersonal versus personal) in this process (Li et al. 2013, Maier et al. 2021). Elaborately, we have unveiled two distinct effectual paths of leadership styles (i.e., transactional versus transformational) and support structures (i.e., impersonal versus personal) on an employee’s ES use (routine versus innovative), thus suggesting granular, targeted change management strategies to achieve desired ES utilization.

Second, our study significantly extends the situational regulatory focus literature by providing nuanced insights into how different situational regulatory focus channels distinct change management levers’ effects on specific ES usage behaviors during the postacceptance stage. Our findings underscore the importance of internalizing organizational measures to shape employee behavior. Building on prior research (Ke et al. 2013, Maruping and Magni 2015), we go beyond by suggesting that the effectiveness of internalization may vary based on the specific measures employed and the situational regulatory focus at play. To this end, previous ES studies have predominantly examined chronic regulatory focus (Ke et al. 2013, Liang et al. 2013). However, we delve into situational regulatory focus, recognizing its adaptability to context. By contextualizing situational regulatory focus within the context of ES use, we enrich the existing literature, aligning well with the assertion of Kark and Van Dijk (2019) that distinguishing between chronic and situational focus is essential for advancing research in this field.

Third, our research advances the understanding of the technological intricacies inherent in ES adoption. Specifically, we dissect how system modularity and complexity intersect with situational regulatory focus, shedding light on the dynamics of employees’ ES usage behaviors. Concerning system modularity, our results corroborate the prevailing notion that modularity fosters better adaptation to diverse functions (Pil and Cohen 2006). We observe that when prevention-focused employees encounter a modular system, routine use flourishes. Surprisingly, however, modularity does not significantly impact the relationship between situational promotion focus and innovative use. This suggests that the drivers of innovative use may differ from those influencing routine use. Furthermore, our examination of system complexity contributes to the nuanced discourse on its paradoxical effects (Jasperson et al. 2005, Liang et al. 2015). Our findings reveal a dualistic impact: while system complexity tends to discourage routine use among situationally prevention-focused employees, it energizes situationally promotion-focused employees to engage in innovative use of the system. This dichotomy underscores the contrasting roles of system modularity and complexity, suggesting that each technologica characteristic engages different aspects of situational regulatory focus in different manners. This observation thus adds to the empirical evidence that the interaction between individual and technological characteristics critically affects employee ES use (Peng et al. 2018, Chen et al. 2022).

We also explore the cross effects of system complexity on innovative use among prevention-focused employees and routine use among promotion-focused employees. We find that the interaction term of system complexity and situation promotion focus on routine use and the interaction term of system complexity and situational prevention focus on innovation use are not significant. These findings confirm our theoretical alignment between situational regulatory focus (prevention or promotion) and the corresponding system use (routine or innovative). For example, users with a promotion focus may not prioritize routine use, whereas users with a prevention focus may not prioritize innovative use. Particularly with a highly complex system, users may respond to the complexity of the system itself rather than aligning their behavior with their situational regulatory focus. The marginally significant direct effect of system complexity on routine use also suggests that users tend to engage in routine use when faced with a complex system.

Last, our study unravels the intricate relationship between ES use and employee outcomes, investigating the distinct impacts of routine and innovative uses on job performance and satisfaction (Schmitz et al. 2010).

Our findings confirm the research by Bala and Venkatesh (2015), substantiating the positive effects of both ES uses on job performance. Engagement with the system, whether through routine or innovative activities, consistently enhances employee performance. ES becomes a catalyst for productivity. However, our results diverge from Bala and Venkatesh (2015) regarding the effect of routine use on job satisfaction, which we found to be insignificant. Thus, the unexpected outcome may be attributed to the lack of fulfillment of the fundamental need for autonomy (Deci and Ryan 1987). Further post hoc analyses reveal intriguing complexities. Although a combined use of routine and innovative uses enhances job performance, it does not significantly elevate satisfaction. Strikingly, a balanced integration of both routine and innovative efforts appears to diminish satisfaction, highlighting the elusive equilibrium between these usage types. This raises critical questions: How do different management levers influence these combinations of use? What organizational factors orchestrate this intricate interplay? The answers lie beyond our current theoretical grasp, beckoning further investigation. Nonetheless, our study reveals that ES use is a multifaceted voyage, intricately linked not only to performance enhancements but also to nuanced impacts on employee satisfaction and broader organizational dynamics.

## 5.2. Managerial Implications

Our research yields practical implications for managing ESs during the postacceptance stage. First, our findings help managers realize that routine and innovative ES use differ. Both routine use and innovative use can help employees achieve better job performance. Promoting employees’ routine and innovative use is crucial to pro mote better performance in their organizational work contexts. For job satisfaction, managers need to note that employees’ routine use could not improve their job satisfaction. Managers should also be cautious when encouraging employees to use combinations of routine and innovative methods (e.g., combined and balanced use). Thus, understanding the nuances of ES use empowers managers to optimize performance and satisfaction in organizational contexts.

Second, our findings suggest that team managers leadership styles and support structures available at the teams are essential in shaping employees’ ES use behavior. This study corroborates the suggestion provided by Maruping and Magni (2015) that managerial interventions directed at teams can effectively promote employees’ ES use. Our study further suggests that leadership styles and support structures differ in driving different patterns of ES use. For example, managers can adopt the transactional leadership style and afford impersonal support to induce employees’ situational prevention focus, reinforcing the routine use of ES. Conversely, adopting a transformational leadership style and affording personal support can induce employees’ situational promotion focus, leading to more innovative use of ES. Training and development programs can be provided to orient the managers on specific leadership styles and support structures depending on their bearings on specific desired ES use.

Third, managers need to note that system modularity and complexity can influence employees’ induced situational regulatory focus in engaging in certain ES uses. Specifically, managers should be aware that system complexity can promote situationally promotionfocused employees to conduct more innovative use. In contrast, high system modularity can facilitate situationally prevention-focused employees to conduct more routine use. As such, managers can also pay more attention to the specific technological characteristics of the system when seeking to promote employees’ certain ES use behavior.

Finally, management may need to cultivate transactional and transformational leadership because they influence routine use and innovative use. This finding is consistent with Vera and Crossan (2004), who argued that strategic leaders must be “ambidextrous” by involving transactional and transformational behaviors. In the view that transactional and transformational leadership induce different kinds of regulatory focus among employees and uniquely motivate specific types of ES use, leaders can be trained to exhibit different blends of leadership behaviors depending on the situation. Moreover, our further analyses also indicate that leadership styles and support structures can complement or substitute each other in inducing certain situational regulatory focus. Therefore, managers need to exercise caution when implementing leadership of different styles and providing different support structures simultaneously.

## 5.3. Limitations and Future Research

Despite its contributions to theory and practice, we acknowledge the caveats of the current research, which suggest directions for future works. First, the current study theorized two distinct paths of leadership styles (i.e., transactional versus transformational) and support structures (i.e., impersonal versus personal) on an employee’s situational regulatory focus (i.e., prevention versus promotion) and ES use (routine versus innovative). However, the “dark side” of transformational leadership may exist, as well as the “bright side” of transactional leadership (Liang et al. 2021). Our post hoc analysis indicated that ambidextrous leadership had varying effects on different types of ES use (see Table G1 in Online Appendix G). In addition, our post hoc analysis shown in Table H1 (Online Appendix H) also found some significant interactions between leadership styles and support structures on certain situational regulatory focus and ES use. The leaders’ goals, such as ES use goal priority, may influence their employees’ specific ES use behavior. Thus, we call for a further study integrating the bright and dark sides of leadership and/or the leaders’ goals to examine their possible effects on employees’ ES use.

Second, the current study only focused on the situational regulatory focus that mediates the change management levers’ influence on ES use. Although we controlled several individual factors (e.g., employee creativity, chronic regulatory focus, and intrinsic motivation) in our model, future research could consider other individual factors, such as individual risk propensity, psychological empowerment (Zhang and Bartol 2010), and psychological needs (Karahanna et al. 2018), which may also influence the employees’ use at the postacceptance stage.

Third, except for the leader-rated job performance, the employees were the only source of information in the field study. Future studies may consider collecting more data from leaders or organizational archival data, which could provide further insights. For example, leaders’ scores on the employees’ ES use could provide data triangulation and help reduce the common method bias. Future research could use archival data on employees’ ES use, such as the specific function frequencies and duration each employee used, to objectively measure employees’ different ES uses.

Fourth, like any other empirical work, the generalization of findings could be contingent on the data collected. We collected our data from an organization in one country. Therefore, researchers and practitioners should exercise caution when applying the current study’s findings to other contexts. Future studies should consider cultural factors because employees in different contexts respond to change management levers differently (Ke et al. 2013). In particular, we found some high correlation between individual- and team-level variables (e.g., the correlation between situational promotion focus and transformational leadership). This might be because participants in our study may share common organizational or cultural backgrounds that influence their perceptions of leadership and motivational factors. Future research could explore the possible effects of cul tural factors (e.g., individualism/collectivism, uncertainty avoidance, and power distance) that influence the relationship among change management levers, regulatory focus, and ES use.

Finally, although we believe that personal support is more likely to enable employees’ situational promotion focus (and also did not find a significant relationship between personal support and situational prevention focus in our study), personal support could also constrain ES use by establishing boundaries for acceptable use, potentially leading to a situational prevention focus. Future studies can use the data on the specific interactions between employees and their personal support providers and further examine the effect of personal support under varying contexts.

## 6. Conclusion

The quest to digitally charge an organization often entails implementing enterprise information systems. Yet, the true essence lies not in their mere existence but in how employees wield them. Our study, guided by a context-specific theorization framework, unravels this intricate dance—a choreography of change management, individual focus, and technological nuances. This study illuminates the path, but the journey continues. Many questions remain to be answered in this workspace. We hope that this study paves the way for future research to further explore the connections between change management strategies, employees’ reactions, and the appropriate usage behaviors needed for the widespread adoption of ESs.
