# The Impact of “Retail Media” on Online Marketplaces: Insights from a Field Experiment

Vibhanshu Abhishek,<sup>a</sup> Kinshuk Jerath,<sup>b</sup> Siddhartha Sharma<sup>c,</sup>\*

Abstract. Advertising on e-commerce marketplaces, wherein sponsored product listings are interleaved with organic product listings in the search results, is a large and growing phenomenon that falls under the umbrella of “retail media.” In this paper, taking the perspective of the marketplace, we obtain insights into the impact of sponsored listings being shown at the most salient positions in the list of results. To do so, we analyze data from a large-scale field experiment at Flipkart, a leading online marketplace in India. We find nuanced results that substantially vary across categories. In the electronics category, the sponsored listings perform worse (in terms of clicks and conversions) than the organic listings that they replace, whereas the organic listings in the neighborhood of the sponsored listings perform better than in the absence of the sponsored listings. Surprisingly, these effects are reversed in the clothing and footwear categories, in which the ads perform better than the displaced organic listings, suggesting that sponsored listings might help the platform identify new high-relevance products and improve search rankings for these categories. However, at the search level, because of the countervailing impacts on sponsored listings and neighboring organic listings (even though the directions of these effects are different for different categories), we find that increasing the fraction of sponsored listings (by about 10% points) does not affect clicks and conversions in any product category. This implies that ads bring in additional revenue for the marketplace yet do not hurt overall consumer response (in the short run). We theorize that the variation across categories occurs because of differing degrees of information asymmetry on product relevance to a query between the marketplace and the independent sellers of listed products and provide supporting evidence for this mechanism.

## 1. Introduction

E-commerce marketplaces, such as Amazon, Alibaba, and Flipkart, enable third-party sellers and consumers to transact online. These leading marketplaces have hundreds of thousands of products available in most product categories and typically charge a commission to the third-party sellers for the transactions taking place on the marketplace. In response to a user search query for a product (such as “white top” or “wireless headphone”), the marketplace presents a list of organic results, relying on product features and historical performance data on products. More recently, there has been a massive rise in retail media networks in which a retailer provides digital space on its platform to thirdparty sellers for advertising.<sup>1</sup> As a part of retail media, e-marketplaces also allow sellers to promote their listings in search results in exchange for a fee (Borison 2015, Shields 2017); these promoted listings are referred to as sponsored product listings (ads). Sponsored listings appear interleaved with organic listings in the ladder of results and are identified as such (e.g., with a “sponsored” identifier on Amazon).<sup>2</sup> The proportion of sponsored slots in the search results is typically around 20% across marketplaces. The motive behind enabling these ads is to give sellers and products more visibility, creating an additional source of revenue for the plat forms in addition to the sales commission that they charge (Abhishek et al. 2016). Selling advertisements is a lucrative business for marketplaces (Economist 2018); in 2021, Amazon made more than US\$31 billion from its advertising business (which is greater than the total annual global ad spend on newspapers) (Kaye 2022), whereas Alibaba’s marketplace is known to charge a very small sales commission rate and to make most of its money through ad sales. More broadly, “retail media,” which refers to ads shown on retailers’ websites, is projected to be a market worth more than US\$100 billion by 2026 (Weiner et al. 2022).

However, whereas sponsored listings create a new source of revenue for marketplaces, they can be a double-edged sword. Sponsored listings allow thirdparty sellers to artificially inflate their positions on the search results page, which might dilute the quality of the search results as organic listings in some of the most valued top positions might now be substituted with less relevant or lower quality listings. This, in turn, can adversely affect the user experience and the probability of purchase on these platforms.<sup>3</sup> As sponsored listings on marketplaces become an increasingly pervasive phenomenon, we ask the following key questions in this paper, all from the perspective of the marketplace: How do sponsored listings perform in terms of clicks and conversions compared with the organic listings that they displace (i.e., the organic listings that would have appeared had there been no ads) at the most salient slots? How does the presence of sponsored listings at the top positions affect the clicks and conversions for the organic slots in the neighborhood of the sponsored listings? What is the effect of increasing sponsored listings on clicks and conversions at the search level, and what do the results imply for the strategy of the marketplace regarding displaying sponsored listings as part of the search results?

To answer these questions, we conduct an empirical investigation using data from a large-scale randomized field experiment on the mobile app of Flipkart, which is a leading e-commerce marketplace in India. In this experiment, which was run for eight days, approximately 1.2 million users were randomly divided into different groups that were served varying numbers of ads at different positions, and their click and conversion responses were recorded. The exogenous variation in the positions and the numbers of ads across the different groups allow us to identify the causal effects of sponsored listings. In our analysis, we consider searches from several product verticals that belong to the most popular product categories, namely, electronics, clothing, and footwear, which together account for more than 80% of Flipkart’s revenues.

First, we look at outcomes for sponsored and organic slots separately within a search, that is, at the impression level, and find nuanced patterns in the outcomes. In searches related to the electronics category compared with the organic listings that they displace, both the clicks and conversions of sponsored listings are significantly lower, statistically and economically. This result is in accordance with our aforementioned intuition that ads perform worse than organic results as typically lower ranked sellers have the incentive to advertise and artificially inflate their rankings. Further, in this category, the presence of sponsored listings leads to significantly higher clicks and conversions for organic slots in the neighborhood of the sponsored slots; that is, there is a positive spillover from the sponsored to the organic listings at neighboring positions. Surprisingly, however, in the footwear and clothing categories, this result is reversed, and compared with the displaced organic listings, both the clicks and conver sions of sponsored listings are significantly higher, statistically and economically. In other words, in this case, ads perform better than organic listings. This suggests that sponsored listings can be utilized by the platform to learn about new good products in these categories. Furthermore, in footwear or clothing, for organic listings in the neighborhood of a sponsored slot, the presence of the sponsored listing does not significantly impact clicks or conversions.

Next, we conduct the analysis at the level of a user search. We find no average effect of changing the fraction of sponsored listings (such that ad exposure by impressions varies between approximately 16% and 26%) on clicks or conversions at the search level in any category. Therefore, an important finding that we obtain is that, whereas showing ads brings in additional revenue for the marketplace, at least in the short run, it does not hurt consumer engagement and conversions. Therefore, on average, it is a profitable strategy for the marketplace to increase (within reason) the fraction of sponsored listings.

To summarize, we find that ads might worsen the set of listings displayed in response to a search query as observed for electronics, but in categories such as clothing, they can improve the set of listings. In aggregate, the countervailing spillover effect on the organic slots cancels out the effect on the sponsored slots, and increasing the fraction of ads (within reason) does not affect clicks or conversions at the level of a search.

We theorize that the following mechanism could be driving the differences between the categories. Specifi cally, there may exist information asymmetry between the platforms and the sellers of products with sellers holding some private information about the products they sell. When a user queries, a marketplace faces the daunting task of matching and ranking a few hundred of the most relevant product listings from scores of thousands of product listings. Online marketplaces typically rely on product features and historical performance data to perform this matching. However, independent sellers, especially of new products, may have some private information about the relevance of their product that the platform does not have, and for different queries, the marketplace has different levels of information asymmetry on product relevance, which might systematically differ across product categories.

We note that this assumption of asymmetric information between the platform and third-party sellers also appears in previous work (Jiang et al. 2011, Long et al. 2022, Yang et al. 2023).

Because products in the electronics category are generally easier to characterize in an online setting compared with products in the clothing or footwear categories because they have a larger share of digital attributes (Lal and Sarvary 1999), we expect that information asymmetry between the third-party sellers and the platform is lower in the electronics category than in the clothing and footwear categories. This implies that the platform finds it easier to identify high-relevance sellers in electronics than in clothing and footwear. Because sellers who can gain from advertising but otherwise will not be displayed prominently have a greater incentive to advertise, low-relevance sellers are more likely to advertise in electronics, whereas highrelevance sellers are more likely to advertise in clothing and footwear. This explanation, which is consistent with our empirical results, also resonated with the managers at Flipkart. However, we emphasize that whereas this theory is compelling, further research is needed to test it formally.

At a time when retail media and advertising on marketplaces are getting increasingly popular, an important implication of our results for marketplace managers is that, in the short run, the advertising fee from sponsored listings either adds to (or does not hurt) the platform’s revenue from transaction commissions. Therefore, increasing sponsored listings weakly increases the revenue of the marketplace. Further, the fact that, in certain categories, sponsored listings can outperform the organic listings (that would have appeared otherwise) can be utilized by the platform to identify new highrelevance products and improve organic search rankings (e.g., Long et al. 2022, Yang et al. 2023). From the point of view of sellers, our results indicate that highrelevance sellers placed in sponsored (organic) listings attract clicks and conversions away from nearby organic (sponsored) listings if they are of lower relevance. However, all sellers obtain, on average, greater clicks and conversions from advertising if this helps them get placed in much higher positions.

In Section 6, we present and discuss our key results. In Section 7, we present additional empirical analyses that give further supportive evidence for our theoretical argument that the result is driven by asymmetric information. ## 2. Related Literature

Our paper contributes to the nascent literature on the role of search advertising on e-marketplaces. Choi and Mela (2019) develop a joint model of consumer and seller behavior on an online marketplace of handmade goods to find the optimal ad ranking and pricing mechanism. A main theme in their research is that the platform trades off fees from advertising with commissions from product sales. We find that, in certain situations, showing sponsored listings may actually increase conversions; that is, there may not always be a trade-of between advertising and sales revenues. In a recent paper, Moshary (2024) also studies the impact of sponsored listings on an e-commerce marketplace at the plat form level, but not the individual position level. Whereas we find no significant effect, they find that the purchase probability on the platform decreases by 0.5% with more sponsored listings. These contrasting results could be attributed to two major contextual differences. First, whereas our context is of a mobile application, theirs is of a desktop site. Second, in our experiment design, the proportion of ads increased on the margin (from 16% to 26%), whereas in Moshary (2024) it increased from 0% to about 12%. Long et al. (2022) conduct a theoretical study to determine how a marketplace that wants to maximize total profit from ad fees and sales can use information learned from sponsored listing auctions to determine the optimal ordering of the joint list of organic and sponsored results and other parameters, such as the commission rate. Relatedly, Yang et al. (2023) empirically find that using advertising information of sellers in ranking search results can improve sales on an e-commerce marketplace. Broadly speaking, these papers fall under the umbrella of retail media.

Our paper is also broadly related to the literature on sponsored search advertising on internet search engines, which has primarily taken the perspective of advertisers. A large stream of work focuses on studying the impact of position on click-through rates (CTRs) and conversion rates (Ghose and Yang 2009, Jerath et al. 2011, Narayanan and Kalyanam 2015). Other studies look at the effect of competition from organic (Agarwal et al. 2015) and other sponsored links (Jeziorski and Segal 2015, Agarwal and Mukhopadhyay 2016) on the performance of the focal sponsored link. The latter studies generally conclude that high-quality ads hurt the performance of listings at succeeding positions because of information satiation. In this paper, taking the perspective of the platform, we focus on how sponsored listings perform compared with the organic listings that would have appeared at those positions had there been no ads and how their presence affects the performance of the organic listings in the neighboring positions. To the best of our knowledge, ours is the first paper to analyze the difference in the performances of sponsored and organic listings in any context and, in particular, how it varies across different product categories.

In the past decade, researchers have used large-scale randomized field experiments to analyze the impact of paid search on advertisers (Blake et al. 2015, Johnson et al. 2017) or the spillover effects of display advertisements on competitors (Sahni 2016) on internet search engines. Experiments are also used to measure consumer sensitivity to sponsored search advertising on internet search engines (Sahni and Zhang 2024) or audio advertising on internet radio (Huang et al. 2018). Similar to our result, Sahni and Zhang (2024, p. 63) find that making ads more prominent on an internet search engine does not significantly impact total clicks though it increases the number of searches by a user. As with our theory, they propose that “advertising compensates for important information gaps in organic listings: it conveys relevant new information, which is … missed by the organic listings algorithm.” However, there seems to be no empirical work that examines how sponsored search advertising impacts the incentives of e-commerce marketplaces, particularly for different product categories.

Our paper also sheds light on the information asymmetry problems in two-sided digital markets. Most of the work in this literature focuses on the information asymmetry between consumers and sellers and explores the signaling aspect of sponsored search advertising (Animesh et al. 2010, Feng and Xie 2012, Sahni and Nair 2020). However, we, as do Liu and Viswanathan (2014), discuss the information asymmetry between the platform and the sellers and propose that sponsored search advertising can serve as a screening mechanism for the platform. Finally, our work also adds to the growing literature on e-commerce marketplaces (Jiang et al. 2011; Hagiu and Wright 2015; Chu et al. 2020; Sun et al. 2020, 2021; Zhang et al. 2020).

## 3. Theoretical Arguments

Our aim is to study the impact of sponsored listings on clicks and conversions by consumers on the set of organic and sponsored results presented to them in response to a query. To anticipate this impact, we present the following theoretical arguments.

We assume that there is asymmetric information between the platform and the third-party sellers wherein the third-party sellers have some private information on their products that is relevant for ranking the products, but the platform does not have this information. As mentioned earlier, this assumption has appeared in several papers before (Jiang et al. 2011,

Long et al. 2022, Yang et al. 2023). For different categories, the degree of this asymmetric information is different. For instance, the degree of asymmetric information is small for a category such as electronics in which products can generally be described comprehensively through their technical specifications and, therefore, the platform and a seller work with similar ranking relevant information given a query. On the other hand, the degree of asymmetric information is large for a category such as clothing or footwear in which products can generally not be described comprehensively through their specifications, and therefore, sellers can be expected to have ranking-relevant information that the platform does not have. This implies that, in the presence of information asymmetry, the ranking algorithm performs worse (along any metric of comparison) in a condition when there is no information asymmetry.

Consider a category in which the degree of asymmetric information is small, such as electronics. In this case, in response to a query, the platform can rank sellers quite well in the organic results. Therefore, highrelevance sellers feature at the top of the ladder of organic results, and if sponsored slots are available, low-relevance sellers, who otherwise will not be featured prominently, want to be shown in these slots. In this case, therefore, sponsored results are, on average, worse than organic results.

Now, consider a category in which the degree of asymmetric information is large, such as clothing or footwear. In this case, in response to a query, the platform cannot rank sellers well by relevance in the organic results. Therefore, high-relevance sellers are likely to be left out of the top of the ladder of organic results, and if sponsored slots are available, these high-relevance sellers want to be shown in these slots. In this case, therefore, sponsored results are, on average, better than organic results.

Essentially, our theoretical argument is that, if the platform is not able to generate high-relevance organic results in response to a query, which happens in situa tions with high information asymmetry, then sponsored results are, on average, better than organic results and vice versa.

Next, we present a stylized model of marketplace advertising with information asymmetry that develops this intuition.<sup>4</sup> Consider a marketplace that ranks three listings in the search results in response to a query by a representative consumer. We assume that one of the listings (denoted by H) is of high relevance to consumers and the other two (denoted by $L _ { 1 }$ and $L _ { 2 } )$ are of lower relevance to the consumers; this reflects the reality that, typically, there are more low-relevance products than high-relevance ones. Higher relevance here implies that, ceteris paribus, the consumer has a higher likelihood of clicking and purchasing after viewing H than after viewing $L _ { 1 }$ or $L _ { 2 } .$ We assume that the consumer always views (though does not necessarily click) the listing at the first position but, because of search costs, views the second listing with a probability of less than one and never views the third listing. After viewing the listings, the consumer decides to either click one of the listings (and purchase it with some nonzero probability) or exit the search. These factors make the ranking of search results an important problem for the platform.

Generalizing from the stylized model, we can state that, in the case of no or low information asymmetry (e.g., for a category such as electronics), products featured in ads can be expected to be worse than products featured in organic positions that they replace; on the other hand, when there is significant information asymmetry (e.g., for categories such as clothing and footwear), products featured in ads can be expected to be better than products featured in organic positions that they replace. Based on this, we can expect that, for a category such as electronics, if sponsored ads are introduced at prominent slots in the ladder of results, then these ads should have lower clicks and conversions than the organic results that they replace. However, because consumers are in search mode, we can expect the organic results in the neighborhood of the ads to have higher clicks and conversions than if the sponsored ads are not there; that is, there would be a countervailing effect in the neighborhood of the ads. For categories such as clothing and footwear, we can expect the opposite results. This intuition is formalized in Online Section 2.

Next, we go to our empirical study armed with the insights that we develop in this section. We emphasize that we are not formally testing the theory, and we are using the theory only to provide us guidance in our empirical study.

## 4. Empirical Setting

For our empirical study, we collaborate with Flipkart, a leading e-commerce marketplace operating in India (Shelley 2019). We conduct our study using data from Flipkart’s Android mobile app. Our data are from the year 2016; mobile commerce accounted for more than 65% of all e-commerce activity in India around this time period (Keelery 2017).

At the top of the Flipkart app’s homepage, a user sees a search tab to type in a search query. Just below the search tab, Flipkart lists different product categories such as electronics, fashion, home, etc., to aid consumer search. In this paper, we focus on sessions in which a user voluntarily searches for something. We define a search response or, simply, a search as the ladder of listings that get created in response to a query by a user and an impression as the displaying of one listing by the platform in a search; that is, a search consists of multiple impressions. Since May 2015, Flipkart started showing sponsored listings (product listings ads) in the search results. Around 20% of the listings in a search are sponsored and appear at fixed positions in the ladder, and the rest are organic. Users can browse through the list ings by scrolling through them. The maximum position viewed by the user during a search determines its depth.

Typical features or information available on the search results page regarding a listing are the name of the product, an image, its price, any discount being offered, its average rating, the number of ratings, and an $^ { \prime \prime } { \mathrm { A D } } ^ { \prime \prime }$ identifier if the listing is sponsored. Based on this information, a user can click (tap) on the listing to enter the specific product’s page to learn more details, such as more images, seller details, reviews, etc. On this page, a user can either convert by tapping on “buy now,” “add to cart,” or “add to wishlist” or navigate back to the search results page or to another page.

Flipkart charges sellers a fixed price per click on sponsored listings. Given the ad price, sellers can choose the products that they want to advertise, their total budget, and the campaign duration. We note that the ad price is set at the subcategory level (e.g., mobile phones, headphones, etc., in electronics and shirts, tops, etc., in fashion) and not at the product level or the query level. Note that sellers cannot bid for ads by position. At the time of the experiment, about 15% of all the sellers used product listing ads for more than 50,000 products.

When a user enters a search query, using its internal algorithms, Flipkart ranks all eligible sponsored listings in decreasing order of relevance and places them starting from the top slot reserved for sponsored listings.<sup>6</sup> Note that, irrespective of the position in which a sponsored listing shows up, the seller advertising that listing is charged the same price per click; therefore, the payments across positions only vary because of different numbers of clicks not because of the price per click. Flipkart also ranks all the organic listings in decreasing order of relevance and places them starting from the top slot reserved for organic listings.<sup>7</sup> If the number of listings available for sponsored slots (i.e., the number of ads) is lower than the number of sponsored slots, then some sponsored slots are not utilized and are simply used for organic listings. It is Flipkart’s policy to not have the sponsored listing reappear as an organic listing in the search results.<sup>8</sup>

## 4.1. Field Experiment Details

A randomized experiment was run at Flipkart with approximately 1.2 million mobile app users for eight days in February 2016. In this experiment, consumers arriving at Flipkart were randomly allocated to one of three buckets. The consumers, once assigned to a bucket, remained in that bucket for the duration of the experiment. Neither the consumers nor the sellers were aware of this experiment. In bucket 1, ads start at position 8 and, after that, occur at intervals of 4, 6, 4, 6, … ; in bucket 2, ads start at position 2 and occur at intervals of 4, 4, 4, 4, … ; and in bucket 3, ads start at position 3 and occur at intervals of 4, 6, 4, 6, … (see Figure 3 for a graphic depiction). For brevity, we sometimes denote buckets 1, 2, and 3 by B1, B2, and B3, respectively. Note that a higher starting position of ads and/or a lower gap between two sponsored slots lead to a higher ad exposure in buckets 2 and 3 than in bucket 1.

The exogenous variation in the positions of ads across different experimental buckets crucially allows us to observe, for the top few positions, the performance of both the sponsored listings and the counterfactual organic listings (i.e., those that would have appeared) at the same position. For instance, the organic listing in position 2 in B1 is the counterfactual for the sponsored listing in position 2 in B2. Similarly, the organic listing in position 3 in B1 is the counterfactual for the sponsored listing in position 3 in B3. Further, the experiment design causes exogenous variation in the proportion of ads users see in a search and helps us identify the causal effect of serving more sponsored listings across various outcomes of interest at the search level. To obtain a sense of the difference in ad exposure across buckets, in Table 1, we present the average ad exposure to users in different buckets weighted by viewed impressions, that is, impressions viewed by users (which is determined to happen if a user scrolls down to that impression).<sup>9</sup> In Table A.1, we provide the average weighted ad exposure for different categories. We see that the differences in ad exposure across buckets are quite significant, ranging from 14% in the lightest treatment (in bucket 1) to 26% in the heaviest treatment (in bucket 2). As bucket 1 users do not get served any ads in the top seven positions and have the lowest average ad exposure, we can use this bucket as our control group and the other two buckets as treatment groups, in which the treatment is a higher intensity of ads. Although it would have been preferable to have a control group with no ads, Flipkart did not want to lose a significant amount of ad revenues during the experiment and did not construct such a group.

## 5. Empirical Methodology

In this section, we present the empirical model for our analysis. From the perspective of the marketplace, the effect of displaying a sponsored listing instead of an organic listing in a search result slot can be decomposed into two effects. First, it is determined by the effect that the sponsored listing has on the performance (in terms of metrics such as click-through and conversion rates) of the slot in which it is placed, that is, the difference in the performances of the sponsored listing and the organic listing that would have appeared at the same position had the ad been absent, which we term the effect on the sponsored position. Second, it is also determined by the effect the sponsored listing has on the performance of organic listings at the neighboring positions, which can be thought of as the difference in the performances of the organic listings that are in the neighborhood of the position of the sponsored listing when an ad is shown in this position versus not. We term this the effect on the neighboring positions. The aggregation of these two effects determines the extent of the effect of displaying sponsored listings at the search level, that is, the total effect. In Figure 4, we illustrate this with a stylized scenario in which there is an ad in the first slot of the ladder of results (whereas there would be an organic listing in this slot if ads were not shown). Accordingly, in the subsequent discussion, we present the models to estimate the effect on the sponsored position, the effect on the neighboring organic positions, and the total effect.

## 5.1. Effect on the Position with the Sponsored Listing

In this section, we describe our methodology to investigate whether and how outcomes change at a position if it is populated by a sponsored listing instead of an organic listing. Our goal is to estimate the average treatment effect, $\mathbb { E } [ Y _ { i } ( 1 ) - Y _ { i } ( 0 ) ]$ , where $Y _ { i } ( 1 )$ is the outcome (click or conversion) when a search i displays a sponsored listing at a given position and $Y _ { i } ( 0 )$ is the outcome when it displays an organic listing at the same position. Note that this effect is a result of the difference in characteristics (e.g., relevance, price, rating, AD identifier on the sponsored listing, etc.) between the sponsored and corresponding organic listings. To estimate this effect, we compare buckets 2 and $^ { 3 , }$ which serve as the treatment arms, with bucket 1, which serves as the control arm. Recall that, in bucket 1, position 2 is an organic listing, whereas in bucket 2, position 2 is a sponsored listing. Similarly, in bucket 1, position 3 is an organic listing, and in bucket 3, position 3 is a sponsored listing. Furthermore, the allocation of a consumer into a bucket is randomized. Therefore, comparing the outcomes for position 2 in bucket 2 (position 3 in bucket 3) with the outcomes for position 2 in bucket 1 (position 3 in bucket 1) across searches gives the average impact of replacing an organic listing at position 2 (position 3) with a sponsored listing.

Specifically, we estimate the following linear regression model using impression-level data from (i) position 2 of bucket 2, (ii) position 3 of bucket 3, and (iii) positions 2 and 3 of bucket 1:

$$
Y _ {i k} = \gamma + \delta \cdot \text { Sponsored } _ {i} + \alpha_ {k} + \xi_ {i k},\tag{1}
$$

where i denotes a search, k the position of the impression in the search, Sponsored is a binary indicator for bucket 2 (or bucket 3) versus bucket 1, α<sub>k</sub> captures position fixed effects, and $Y _ { i k }$ is the binary outcome variable that equals one if the listing at position k is clicked or converted during search i. The coefficient δ then measures the impact on the probability of click and conversion as the case may be on position 2 by virtue of being in bucket 2 (in which case, position 2 has a sponsored listing) versus being in bucket 1 (in which case position 2 has an organic listing). Note that as Sponsored does not vary between buckets 2 and $^ { 3 , }$ including position fixed effects ensures that δ is estimated by comparing the outcomes between buckets 2 and 1 at position 2 and between buckets 3 and 1 at position 3.

There are two things to note about this strategy. First, as Sponsored varies exogenously across buckets, all search-level characteristics (e.g., query, timing, etc.), say $X _ { i } ,$ are similarly distributed across buckets; that is, there is no selection or omitted variable bias $( X _ { i } \bot \mathrm { S p o n s o r e d } _ { i } )$ As we only exploit the cross-bucket variation in the treatment variable for our identification, the timing variation will not affect this estimation. In particular, if we examine Equation (1), the timing, $X _ { i } ,$ is subsumed in the error term $\xi _ { i k } ,$ and because the bucket assignment is completely random, the distribution of $\xi _ { i k }$ is the same across buckets. Thus, we need not control for any search level attribute in our specification.

Second, any treatment effect (<sup>ˆ</sup>δ) we estimate is driven by the difference in several observed or unobserved characteristics between the sponsored listings and corresponding organic listings. In other words, these listinglevel characteristics, say $Z _ { i k } ,$ are the mediators (in the causal path) between the treatment and the outcome. Therefore, we need not control for listing-level covariates (e.g., price, rating, brand, etc.) because they may absorb some of the effects from the main effect that we intend to estimate (Cinelli et al. 2024). Nevertheless, we control for some of the listing-level characteristics later in Section 7.2 to explore the mechanisms behind the results we obtain in Section 6.1.

## 5.2. Effect on the Neighborhood Positions of the Sponsored Listing

In this section, we describe our methodology to investigate whether and how outcomes change in the neighborhood of a position if it contains a sponsored listing instead of an organic listing. Bucket 2 contains organic listings at positions 1, 3, 4, and 5, whereas in bucket 1, positions 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 are all organic listings. Similarly, in bucket $^ { 3 , }$ positions 1, 2, 4, and 5 are organic listings. We first define positions 1, 3, 4, and 5 (positions 1, 2, 4, and 5) as being in the neighborhood of position 2 (position 3).<sup>12</sup> We then compare the average outcomes in terms of clicks and conversions for positions 1, 3, 4, and 5 between buckets 2 and 1 and for positions 1, 2, 4, and 5 between buckets 3 and 1.<sup>13</sup> Because the allocation of a consumer after a search into bucket 1 or bucket 2 is randomized, comparing these outcomes gives the average impact of having a sponsored listing on the neighboring organic positions.

We estimate the following using impression-level data from (i) positions 1, 3, 4, and 5 of bucket 2; (ii) positions 1, 2, 4, and 5 of bucket 3; and (iii) positions 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 of bucket 1:

$$
Y _ {i k} = \gamma^ {\prime} + \delta^ {\prime} \cdot \mathrm{Sponsored} _ {i} + \alpha_ {k} ^ {\prime} + \xi_ {i k} ^ {\prime},\tag{2}
$$

where the coefficient $\delta ^ { \prime }$ then measures the impact on the probability of click and conversion, as the case may be, on positions $1 , 3 , 4 ,$ and 5 by virtue of being in bucket 2 (in which case, position 2 has a sponsored listing) versus bucket 1 (in which case, position 2 has an organic listing). Again, including position fixed effects ensures that δ<sup>′</sup> is estimated by comparing outcomes between buckets 2 and 1 at positions 1, 3, 4, and 5 and between buckets 3 and 1 at positions 1, 2, 4, and 5.

## 5.3. Effect on Search-Level Outcomes

In this section, we describe our methodology to investigate whether and how search-level outcomes change if the intensity of sponsored listings is varied across searches.<sup>14</sup> Consider the following simple linear regression model:

$$
Y _ {i} = \alpha + \beta \cdot \mathrm{AdExposure} _ {i} + \epsilon_ {i},\tag{3}
$$

where i denotes a search; $Y _ { i }$ denotes the outcome variable, which is the total clicks after search i or the total conversions after search i; and AdExposure denotes the degree of exposure to ads to the consumer conducting search i. AdExposure is computed as follows. For search i, denote by $d _ { i }$ the depth of search (i.e., how many total listings were viewed by the consumer). For this depth, denote by $n _ { s i }$ the number of ads (sponsored listings) and by $n _ { o i }$ the number of organic listings, where $n _ { s i } + n _ { o i } = d _ { i }$ . Then, AdExposur $\underline { { \underline { { \mathbf { \Pi } } } } } _ { i } = n _ { s i } / d _ { i }$ , that is, the fraction of all listings seen that are sponsored listings.<sup>15</sup> We are interested in the coefficient $\hat { \boldsymbol { \beta } }$ that measures the impact of ad exposures on the outcome measures.

However, AdExposure is endogenous because search depth i is endogenous as it might depend on the number of ads seen by a consumer.<sup>16</sup> Therefore, we use bucket membership as an instrumental variable and conduct a two-stage analysis.<sup>17</sup> We can see from Table 1 that the exposure to ads differs between searches in buckets 1 and 2 or between buckets 1 and 3. In the first stage, we regress AdExposure on Bucket<sub>i</sub>, where search i can be in bucket 1, 2, or 3 as follows:

$$
\mathrm{AdExposure} _ {i} = \alpha_ {1} + \beta_ {1} \cdot \mathrm{Bucket} _ {i} + \epsilon_ {1 i}.\tag{4}
$$

In the second stage, we regress $Y _ { i }$ on the values of AdEx<sup>d</sup>posure obtained from (4), as follows:

$$
Y _ {i} = \alpha_ {2} + \beta_ {\mathrm{IV}} \cdot \widehat {\mathrm{AdExposure}} _ {i} + \epsilon_ {2 i}.\tag{5}
$$

Then, $\beta _ { \mathrm { { I V } } }$ is the coefficient that measures the impact of an increase in ad exposure on different outcomes.

## 7. Additional Analyses

Our empirical results align with what we would expect based on our theoretical arguments in Section 3, which provides support for the asymmetric information theory. In this section, we conduct additional analyses that lend further support to this theory. Specifically, in Section 7.1, we conduct subcategory-level analyses and find results consistent with the theory, and in Section 7.2, we conduct analyses to rule out some potential alternative explanations.

## 7.1. Subcategory-Level Analyses

In this section, we perform additional analyses at the subcategory level, that is, at a more granular level, to check if the results we obtain align with the asymmetric information theory in Section 3. The subcategories in electronics include mobiles, headphones, computers, television, and speakers, and we consider them low information asymmetry subcategories, whereas those in footwear and clothing include shoes, sarees (a popular Indian dress), fabric, shirts, tops, T-shirts, dresses, and jeans, and we consider them high information asymmetry subcategories.

Whereas it is difficult to determine the level of information asymmetry between the platform and the sellers for different categories, one can try to compare it across categories through factors that are directly observed and are potentially influenced by the level of information asymmetry. One such factor is search ranking performance, that is, how well the platform ranks the products in its search results across different categories. To analyze this, we use data for a random sample of about 250,000 users who were not part of the experiment and, therefore, continued to see sponsored listings in the search results in the business-as-usual (i.e., the pre-experiment) slots during the experiment.<sup>23</sup> Using this data, we plot the cumulative distribution of clicks and conversions across positions in the two categories and their subcategories in Figure 5. The probabilities indicate how much consumers search in a category, that is, the amount of search for a consumer to obtain enough information to make a choice (which may be to not make a purchase). We find that 75% of all clicks or conversions from the top 50 positions in electronicsrelated searches take place in the top 10 slots, whereas the same proportion of clicks or conversions take place over more than 25 positions in footwear- or clothing related searches (left panel of Figure 5). In fact, the domi nance of electronics search rankings holds for all the subcategories (right panel of Figure 5). This highlights the fact that it is easier for a platform to display relevant products in electronics than in clothing or footwear, suggesting that it has more information regarding the true relevance of products in electronics than for products in clothing and footwear.

Furthermore, for each subcategory, we determine the effect on clicks for the position with the sponsored listing. (We do not conduct the analysis for conversion as we do not have sufficient power because of less data at the subcategory level.) The results of these analyses are presented in Table A.8, in which we also present the mean search depth and its standard deviation for reference. We observe that our main finding holds; that is, in low information asymmetry subcategories (i.e., subcategories under electronics) sponsored listings receive fewer clicks than the organic listings that they displace, whereas in high information asymmetry subcategories (i.e., subcategories under clothing and footwear) sponsored listings receive more clicks than the organic listings that they displace.<sup>24</sup> This is illustrated in Figure 6(a). Furthermore, we examine the correlation at the subcategory level between average search depth (as cal culated from the business-as-usual condition) and the effect on clicks at the sponsored position. This correlation is positive and has a value of 0.87 (see Figure 6(b)), which implies that the performance of ads relative to the displaced organic listings improves in subcategories in which users search more, which is in accordance with the implication of our proposed theory.

We emphasize that these findings only provide suggestive rather than conclusive or causal evidence for the argument that information asymmetry drives the heterogeneous effect of sponsored ads across the categories. There can be factors other than the information asymmetry that might lead to these differences across the product categories; we try to rule out some of the major alternative explanations by performing various robustness checks that are detailed in Section 7.2. Practical considerations can make it difficult to identify the causal impact of information asymmetry as this is a characteristic of the product category, and information asymmetry essentially cannot be manipulated directly by the researcher or the platform. Furthermore, there are typically just a few product categories on any platform that generate a majority of the sales (e.g., clothing and electronics on Flipkart). As a result, one needs to perform the analysis at the subcategory level to find sufficient variation in the level of information asymmetry, which already leads to underpowered tests even with more than a million users (as in our case). Furthermore, in our setting, Flipkart places limitations such that advertisers can only express their bids at the granularity of the subcategory, so we cannot run our analysis at a finer granularity level (e.g., at the product level). Therefore, whereas we cannot explicitly test our theory, given the various constraints, we strongly believe that the analysis that we present here is a step in the right direction in understanding the effect of sponsored listings on marketplaces and how this effect is mediated by information asymmetry, which is a major issue for online marketplaces.

## 7.2. Alternative Explanations

In this section, we discuss phenomena other than infor mation asymmetry between the platform and the sellers, which can possibly explain our results. We find that these alternative explanations are unlikely to explain our main results.

7.2.1. Consumer Heterogeneity. It is plausible that the heterogeneous effects that we find across the product categories in Section 5 are due to consumer heterogeneity. This could be especially true if users who browse clothing products are very different from those who buy electronic products. To gain some insight into this, we repeat our analysis with only those users who browsed both electronics and clothing products during the experiment period; the results are available in Table A.9. We also repeat the analysis with only those users who browsed both electronics and footwear products; the results are available in Table A.10. As for our main analysis, we find that the effect on the sponsored position is significantly negative for electronics in terms of both clicks and conversion and is significantly positive for clothing and footwear in terms of clicks. Therefore, consumer heterogeneity across the categories is unlikely to explain our main result.

7.2.2. Role of Observable Features. Here, we investigate whether quantifiable features such as prices or average ratings can explain the differences in the performances of the sponsored and the displaced organic listings. We estimate the model in (1), controlling for listing characteristics, such as the average rating, price, percentage discount, and the number of ratings. Table A.11 presents the results for the effect on click-through rate. We again find that the difference between the click rates of the sponsored and displaced organic listings is negative and statistically significant in the electronics category and is positive and statistically significant in the clothing category. The results imply that sponsored listings do not perform better or worse than the substituted organic listings only because of observable and quantifiable features.

7.2.3. Specificity of Search Queries. It is possible that the difference we observe in the relative performance of ads between electronics and footwear or clothing is because search queries in electronics may be more specific (though this could itself be another source of information asymmetry between the platform and the sellers). Higher specificity of search queries increases the likelihood of sponsored listings being less relevant compared with organic listings as the pool of sponsored listings becomes smaller. However, Flipkart does not serve ads if the relevance of the listings in the sponsored pool does not cross a threshold relevance, and we only analyze the searches in which ads were served. To further make sure that our results are not driven by the specificity of search queries, we analyze the data from searches with only less specific search queries in electronics (i.e., more generic search queries, specifically, nonbranded and nondescriptive search queries that have at least 200 searches) to see if the poor performance of ads still persists. Our main results continue to hold in this situation (see Table A.12).

7.2.4. Variety Seeking and Less Risk Aversion in Clothing. It is plausible that the difference we observe in the relative performance of ads between electronics and footwear or clothing is because users searching for clothing products are more variety-seeking (though this could itself be another source of information asymmetry between the platform and the sellers) and less riskaverse than users searching for electronics. If the sponsored products are relatively new, the variety-seeking behavior of users in the clothing category could lead them to click more on sponsored listings. However, based on our analysis including control variables earlie in Table A.11, we find that, even after accounting for the number of ratings (a good indicator of the recency of listings), the sponsored position effect remains positive for clothing and negative for electronics. It is also worth noting that, consistent with our theory, the varietyseeking behavior of consumers in clothing actually makes it harder for the platform to identify high relevance products, thus increasing the information asymmetry with the sellers.

One more difference between clothing/footwear and electronics categories that could potentially explain our main results is that users buying electronics might be more risk-averse because of a higher price point of electronic products and, hence, are less likely to click on new products that are advertised at the sponsored posi tions. However, based on our subcategory analysis in Section 7.1, we find that, even for subcategories such as headphones and speakers, which are of similar price points as clothing/footwear subcategories, the effect at the sponsored position is negative. This suggests that the contrasting results we observe across the categories are unlikely driven by differences in average price points.

## 8. Conclusions

We study how the presence of sponsored listings in an e-commerce marketplace impacts clicks and conversions in the ladder of search results presented to consumers. Using unique data from a large-scale field experiment at Flipkart, one of the largest online marketplaces in India, we uncover the key insight that, in different contexts, sponsored listings may obtain more or fewer clicks and conversions than the organic links that they displace. However, there is a countervailing or null effect on the neighboring organic listings such that, in aggregate, there is no significant effect.

Sponsored search advertising on e-commerce platforms is an important component of the rising phenomenon of retail media, and our research has important implications for practice. From the point of view of the platform, our finding that in categories such as clothing sponsored listings can feature better products than the displaced organic listings can help platforms improve future search rankings by discovering high-relevance products (Long et al. 2022, Yang et al. 2023). Further, our findings imply that including more sponsored listings (in our case, including more ads such that weighted ad exposure increases from approximately 14% to approximately 26%, which is an increase in ad exposure of approximately 60%) is beneficial; they bring in additional revenue without impacting overall engagement or purchases (at least in the short run). In fact, sponsored listings have the potential to increase the average quality of listings, and even when they do not, consumers search multiple product listings and are able to find well-matching products essentially to the degree that they would have been able to with fewer ads. From the point of view of sellers, our results indicate that highrelevance sellers placed in organic listings benefit from a positive spillover if nearby sponsored listings are of lower relevance, whereas high-relevance sellers placed in sponsored listings attract clicks and conversions away from nearby organic listings if they are of lower relevance. (Nevertheless, low-relevance as well as high-relevance sellers obtain greater clicks and conversions from advertising if this helps them to be placed in higher positions.) We also observe that there is a marked difference in how sponsored listings perform relative to the displaced organic listings across major product categories: clothing/footwear and electronics. We theorize and provide suggestive evidence that this effect arises because of the information asymmetry regarding product relevance between the platform and the third-party sellers.

We note that our theoretical model assumes that the consumer does not react differently to a listing if it is marked as sponsored (AD) or shown as a regular organic listing. This is because we assume that observed and unobserved product characteristics determine the inherent relevance of a product listing. An important point here is that the products shown in sponsored listings are from the same overall pool of products (which would generally be true for most platforms) as the ones shown in organic listings with the only difference being that sponsored listings may be more or less relevant depending on the category as explained in our theory. However, in situations in which there may be reason to believe that organic and sponsored listings come from sets of products with different distributions of product characteristics, then, on top of relevance, the fact that a listing is sponsored or organic may influence the reaction of a consumer $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ there may be an effect of the AD identifier). Then, the manner in which product characteristics determine how the organic and sponsored pools of products are different can mediate the response of a consumer to these listings beyond relevance.

Our paper has several limitations that can be addressed in future research. For instance, we look at only the short-term impact of showing ads in the search results. It will be interesting to examine how sponsored listings impact user behavior in the long term. Further, at Flipkart, ad price is set at the subcategory level (e.g., tops, headphones). This might limit the ability of sellers to reveal their private information compared with the sellers on platforms that use auctions to show sponsored listings (e.g., Amazon). Nevertheless, it is worth noting that the monopoly price of ads set by Flipkart creates similar incentives as implied by an auction. This is because a seller that does not value the sponsored slot much is unlikely to opt in for advertising and pay a high price per click. Furthermore, we could strengthen our empirical analysis if we were able to obtain data on more subcategories and brands of the product listings. Finally, our data only comes from the mobile app interface; it could be useful to analyze the impact in the con text of the desktop web interface.
