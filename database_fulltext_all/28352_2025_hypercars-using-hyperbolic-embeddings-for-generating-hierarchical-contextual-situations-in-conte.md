# HyperCARS: Using Hyperbolic Embeddings for Generating Hierarchical Contextual Situations in Context-Aware Recommender Systems

Abstract. Contextual situations, such as having dinner at a restaurant on Friday with the spouse, became a useful mechanism to represent context in context-aware recommender systems (CARS). Prior research has shown important advantages of using latent embed ding representation approaches to model contextual information in the Euclidean space leading to better recommendations. However, these traditional approaches have major challenges with construction of proper embeddings of hierarchical structures of contextual information, as well as with interpretations of the obtained representations that would be useful for managers. To address these problems, we propose the HyperCARS method that models hierarchical contextual situations in the latent hyperbolic space. HyperCARS combines hyperbolic embeddings with hierarchical clustering to construct contextual situations, which allows to loosely couple the contextual modeling component with recommendation algorithms and therefore provides flexibility to use a broad range of previously developed recommendation algorithms. We demonstrate empirically that the proposed hyperbolic embedding approach better captures the hierarchical nature of context than its Euclidean counterpart and produces hierarchical contextual situations that are more distinct and better separated at multiple hierarchical levels. We also demonstrate that hyperbolic contextual situations lead to better context-aware recommendations in terms of standard recommendation metrics and to better interpretability of the resulting hierarchical contextual situations. Because hyperbolic embeddings can also be used in many other applications besides CARS, in this paper, we propose the latent embeddings representation framework that systematically classifies prior work on embeddings and identifies novel research streams for hyperbolic embeddings across information systems applications.

## 1. Introduction

Context is an important, powerful, and multifaceted concept in computer and data science (Bella and Bouquet 2019, del Carmen Rodr´ıguez-Herna´ndez and Ilarri 2021), and it has been extensively studied in the field of recommender systems within the context-aware recommender systems (CARS) framework (Adomavicius et al. 2021) for the past 20 years. Furthermore, most of the leading companies, including Netflix, Spotify, LinkedIn, and Google, incorporated contextual information into their recommendation engines and leveraged context to its full advantage (Hastings 2012, Beutel et al. 2018, Baltrunas 2019, Hansen et al. 2020).

Within the CARS area, the focus has shifted over the last few years from modeling context in the explicit feature spaces to doing it in the latent spaces within the framework of deep learning (DL) and DL-based approaches to recommender systems (Haruna et al. 2017, Adomavicius et al. 2021). In these DL-based approaches, the contextual information is modeled with latent contextual embeddings that represent different types of compressed multidimensional contextual information in the latent Euclidean space (Unger et al. 2016, Wu et al. 2017, Ding et al. 2019, Okawa et al. 2019, Xin et al. 2019). In addition to using powerful mathematical properties of representing different types of heterogeneous contexts as n-dimensional vectors in the Euclidean space, this approach naturally supports the concept of a contextual situation that represents a complex interplay among various combinations of different types of context, such as reading this article as a CARS scholar on Thursday evening because it can be relevant for your current research project. These contextual situations have been introduced in Unger and Tuzhilin (2020) within the DL-based latent framework, and they enjoy several important advantages for providing better context-aware recommendations. Furthermore, hierarchical contextual embeddings and hierarchical contextual situations have been proposed in Unger et al. (2020), where it was shown that the hierarchical latent methods outperform nonhierarchical ones and lead to better recommendations.

All the previously proposed approaches to building latent representations of contextual information follow the traditional embedding-based methods and represent it as unstructured vectors in the Euclidean space, and thus leverage strong theoretical developments (Aggarwal 2018) and numerous successful industrial deployments (Zhang et al. 2019). Although modeling hierarchical Euclidean contextual embeddings in the latent space has various important advantages leading to better recommendations for the users, they also have the following two problems. First, it is challenging to construct these hierarchical contextual embeddings properly in the Euclidean space because they suffer from a high distortion when embedding scalefree and hierarchical data (Chami et al. 2019). Second, they cannot be easily interpreted, which significantly reduces their usefulness in many business settings where it is crucial for managers to understand the correct contexts for providing particular recommendations to mitigate potential risks and adverse effects of poor recommendations. Therefore, without proper support for hierarchies and interpretability, this useful concept of latent contextual situations does not work well in practice.

To address these problems of suboptimal contextual hierarchies and the lack of interpretability of the Euclidean-based embeddings, we propose to construct embeddings of contextual information in the hyperbolic space. One of the main motivations for this idea is that contextual information is often hierarchical in nature (Palmisano et al. 2008), and hyperbolic embeddings are well suited for modeling hierarchies, as was demonstrated across various types of machine learning applications, such as natural language (Tifrea et al. 2018) and image processing (Khrulkov et al. 2020).

Although using hyperbolic spaces for modeling contextual information in CARS is the focus of this paper, the use of hyperbolic embeddings goes well beyond the CARS applications because they can be deployed across a broad set of other problems in information systems (IS). To understand how this can be done in a systematic manner, in this paper, we propose the latent embedding representation framework that classifies various IS applications using a two-dimensional matrix where one dimension corresponds to the types of geometric spaces used for embedding representations (hyperbolic versus Euclidean) and the other dimension classifies various methods used for processing the obtained embeddings (e.g., nongrouped versus grouped methods). Besides providing a classification of prior work on embeddings using the framework’s matrix and a better understanding of how they can be used in IS, the proposed framework also paves the way to identifying novel research streams exploring the benefits of hyperbolic spaces across IS applications.

The constructed contextual embeddings are subsequently used in CARS systems, which can be done in either a loosely or a tightly coupled manner. The loosely coupled approach processes the contextual information independently from recommendations to construct proper representations of context (e.g., contextual embeddings) and subsequently uses them together with the user and item information as inputs into various types of contextaware recommendation methods. In contrast, the tightly coupled approach uniquely integrates the contextual information with the user and item representations within the same model (e.g., takes context, user, and item information as inputs into the model that produces appropriate context-aware recommendations). In this paper, we focus on the loosely coupled approach because it provides several significant advantages that are extensively explained in Section 4. Therefore, our guiding research question is the following.

Research Question. (a) How to properly model contextual information with hyperbolic embeddings and (b) how to incorporate it into a recommender system in a loosely coupled manner, so that it would outperform the state-of-the-art CARS-based recommendation methods?

To address this question, we propose a new Hyper-CARS method that captures the hierarchical structure of contextual information by representing it using a combination of hyperbolic embeddings and hierarchical clustering. The resulting hierarchical contextual situations are represented in the form of vectors of clusters’ IDs, and therefore, they can be loosely cou pled with a broad range of recommendation methods, not only in the hyperbolic but also in the Euclidean space. This approach provides a new perspective on how to better take advantage of hyperbolic embeddings for modeling hierarchical contextual information and, at the same time, provides flexibility to use a broad range of previously developed recommendation algorithms. Furthermore, HyperCARS uses the attention mechanism to automatically select the most valuable hierarchical levels in terms of recommendation performance. In this paper, we also demonstrate that the HyperCARS approach dominates Euclidean-based contextual embeddings in terms of better representing complex hierarchical contextual situations, resulting in better context-aware recommendations. Moreover, the proposed hyperbolic approach also demonstrates better interpretability of contextual situations than its Euclidean counterpart, thus making contextual situations more practical and useful for modeling in contextrich applications.

In this paper, we make the following contributions. First, we propose the latent embedding representation framework that systematizes research on latent data representations according to the utilized geometric spaces (Euclidean versus hyperbolic) and the methods used to process the obtained embeddings (e.g., nongrouped versus grouped). Second, we introduce the concept of hyperbolic embeddings to the IS literature and position it within the proposed framework. Moreover, we examine various roles that hyperbolic embeddings can play in the IS field, thus paving the way to new streams of research exploring the advantages of using hyperbolic spaces across different IS applications. Third, in reference to CARS, we propose to model hierarchical structures of contextual information with hyperbolic latent embeddings and introduce the concept of hierarchical hyperbolic contextual situations that help to provide better context-aware recommendations. Fourth, we propose a novel method for building hierarchical hyperbolic contextual situations that combines hyperbolic embeddings and hierarchical clustering. We empirically demonstrate that hyperbolic contextual embeddings outperform the traditional Euclidean-based contextual embeddings in terms of better partitioning quality, recommendation performance, and interpretability, thus making the case for using hyperbolic embeddings in CARS.

## 2. Background and Related Work

Hyperbolic geometry is a non-Euclidean geometry that satisfies all of Euclid’s postulates except the parallel postulate. Hyperbolic n-dimensional space $H ^ { n }$ is defined as a homogeneous, simply connected, ndimensional Riemannian manifold with a constant negative sectional curvature (i.e., the surface curves away from the tangent space in different directions) (Greenberg 1993). Although the hyperbolic space $H ^ { n }$ is diffeomorphic (i.e., can be mapped) to the Euclidean space $R ^ { n }$ (Greenberg 1993), it has very different geometric properties because of constant negative curvature. This property of hyperbolic space implies that the hyperbolic circle area and length grow exponentially with its radius (Nickel and Kiela 2017), which is key to finding good continuous embeddings of hierarchical structures. This is the case because, in hierarchical structures, as you move away from the parent node, you often encounter additional child nodes that are supposed to be close to their parent nodes but further away from each other. Similar embeddings in the two-dimensional Euclidean space are impossible because the circle length and the area grow only linearly and quadratically with its radius. Intuitively, hyperbolic spaces can be thought of as smooth versions of trees abstracting their hierarchical organization. A more detailed description of hyperbolic spaces and the Poincare ´ ball model used in this paper is provided in Online Appendix A.

CARS literature has shown that automatic latent modeling of contextual information can be useful to capture its richness, including implicit relationships between various types of contexts (Unger et al. 2020). Recent studies suggest representing contextual information by using latent embeddings modeled as unstructured numeric vectors (Smirnova and Vasile 2017, Wu et al. 2017). An alternative way to model it is by extracting contextual situations that are composed of groups of similar latent contextual vectors extracted in the Euclidean space (Unger and Tuzhilin 2020, Unge et al. 2020). For example, if a latent embedding represents explicit contextual values of “Saturday,” “evening,” “loud,” and “location � (Broadway, NYC),” this set of contexts collectively defines the contextual situation of attending a show on weekend. Specifically, the authors proposed to represent latent contexts as hierarchical contextual situations, defined as sets of contextual situations specified at different granularity levels. For example, granular contextual situations in a restaurant application, such as “Saturday evening with the spouse” and “Sunday morning with the fiance” ´ can be aggregated to a higher-level contextual situation of “weekend with a partner” that is less granular than the two previous contextual situations. By building a tree structure using hierarchical agglomerative clustering for representing hierarchical contextual situations, Unge and Tuzhilin (2020) showed that such an approach can better represent contextual situations in the Euclidear space at different granularity levels and improve recommendation performance. However, recent work of Schmeier et al. (2019) suggested that hyperbolic embeddings are better suited for representing hierarchies across a broad range of domains, such as text mining (Dhingra et al. 2018), link prediction, and node classifica tion (Chami et al. 2019).

In this paper, we go beyond these Euclidean-based contextual hierarchies and propose a new approach called HyperCARS to represent latent contextual information in the hyperbolic space since it is better suited for modeling hierarchical data due to certain properties of this space (Mathieu et al. 2019). HyperCARS combines hyperbolic embeddings with hierarchical clustering for building context representations in the form of hierarchical hyperbolic contextual situations. We show that this approach to representing context generates better-separated and more interpretable contextual situations than the traditional Euclidean-based method, which results in higher recommendation performance and, thus, makes the case for using hyperbolic embeddings in CARS.

## 2.1. Literature Review

An extensive amount of work has been done on recommender systems (RSes) over the past 20 years, covering a broad range of topics and fields (Ricci et al. 2022). In the IS field, research has predominantly studied the impact of RSes on various business measures, such as sales diversity (Lee and Hosanagar 2019) or product consumption levels (Li et al. 2020). There are also IS works that focus exclusively on context-aware recommender systems. Panniello et al. (2016) ran an experiment with customers and showed that using contextual information affects business-related performance measures, such as company sales, by improving accuracy and diversification of recommendations. Also, Bauman and Tuzhilin (2022) studied the ways contextual information is expressed in user reviews and proposed a context parsing method for extracting contextual information from user-generated reviews. Their analysis showed the importance of discovered contextual information for recommendation purposes.

2.1.1. Hyperbolic Embeddings. In recent years, representation learning in hyperbolic spaces has attracted an increasing amount of attention with applications across various fields, including natural language (Tifrea et al. 2018) and image processing (Khrulkov et al. 2020). For example, Khrulkov et al. (2020) demonstrated that in many cases, such as image classification, hyperbolic embeddings provide a better alternative than Euclidean and spherical embeddings across various performance metrics. Similarly, Nickel and Kiela (2017) introduces an approach for learning hierarchical data by embedding it into n-dimensional Poincare ´ ball and shows that hyperbolic embeddings can outperform Euclidean embeddings in terms of both representation capacity and generalization ability. The key difference between our work and the aforementioned research is that we model context, such as time and weather, in a hyperbolic space to reveal hierarchical contextual situations of users and provide recommendations based on these contextual situations.

Some recent studies have applied hyperbolic representation learning to recommendation tasks (Sun et al. 2021). Vinh et al. (2018) suggests learning user and item hyperbolic representations and leverage Bayesian personalized ranking for recommendation purposes, and Feng et al. (2020) proposes the hyperbolic metric embedding (HME) model, which projects the check-in data into a hyperbolic space. HME jointly captures sequential transitions, user preferences, and content information by learning their embeddings in a shared hyperbolic space. Also, Vinh Tran et al. (2020) studies the connection between metric learning in hyperbolic space and collaborative filtering (CF) by exploring Mo¨bius gyrovector spaces to better model the distance in user-item pairs. Furthermore, Mirvakhabova et al. (2020) introduces a simple autoencoder based on hyperbolic geometry for solving standard CF problems. However, all these papers are different from our approach, as they do not consider contextual information. They focus on the representations of users and items, while we focus on modeling contextual information in the hyperbolic space to find the most important contextual situations and thus enhance the representational capabilities of CARS.

2.1.2. Latent Embeddings in CARSs. Recent studies proposed to use latent, rather than explicit, contextual information in CARSs that is usually modeled using latent embeddings (Ding et al. 2019, Okawa et al. 2019, Xin et al. 2019), and it has been shown that this approach significantly improves the performance of CARS. However, the previously proposed methods modeled latent context as high-dimensional vectors in the Euclidean space and, thus, ignored the hierarchical structure of contextual variables and the semantically meaningful interrelationships among them (Pathak et al. 2016, Nweke et al. 2018). As shown in Nickel and Kiela (2017) and Chami et al. (2019), the ability of embedding methods to model complex patterns is inherently bound by the dimensionality of the Euclidean space, and therefore, Euclidean embeddings suffer from high distortion when embedding scale-free and hierarchical data. In contrast, we propose to model contextual information in the hyperbolic space and thus better represent its hierarchical structure for recommendation and interpretation purposes.

## 3. Latent Embedding Representation Framework

The problem of representing data in IS goes back to the 1960s and even earlier, including the work of the group of French mathematicians under the pseudonym Nicolas Bourbaki when they introduced the concept of structured set theory in Bourbaki (1954). There have been numerous approaches developed to represent information and knowledge within the field of knowledge representation since that time (Russell 2010, Gelfond and Kahl 2014), with Unified Modeling Language being one of the prominent and widely accepted practical approaches to data modeling (Booch et al. 1996). A common characteristic of these different methods is that they represent knowledge in the feature space where various objects, such as products and customers, are represented using their characteristics (features), such as product name, date when it was produced, its weight, size, color, and so on. Considered objects usually have certain relationships among them, such as connections between products and customers. At the same time, knowledge about these objects, as well as used features, usually has structure, such as hierarchical organization of product classification. As demonstrated in Table 1, IS literature has studied numerous knowledge representation applications where the data has a hierarchical organization by nature. For example, Goes et al. (2016) analyzed learning incentive hierarchies, Du et al. (2017) and Gong et al. (2018) studied the hierarchy of keywords in sponsored search advertising, Adjerid et al. (2018) modeled a quasi-hierarchy of health information exchanges, and Ho et al. (2020) studied local competition based on the hierarchy constructed using geographical distances. Langer et al. (2020), Pu et al. (2022), and Petryk et al. (2022) studied issues associated with corporate hierarchies.

Although widely adopted and useful, the featurebased data representation has a problem with measuring “closeness” (distances) between objects, such as how close is movie “Casablanca” is to the movie “North by Northwest” or how similar customer A is to customer B. For this and various other reasons, there has been a more recent trend to represent data, not in the feature, but in the latent space in the form of object embeddings, where an embedding constitutes a vector in an n-dimensional latent space capturing the “essence” of an object (e.g., movie “Casablanca”), originally defined in the feature space, through some encoding methods (Jian et al. 2018, Xin et al. 2019, Dong et al. 2020). For example, the movie “Casablanca” can be “encoded” in the latent space based on its characteristics (e.g., director, leading actors, budget, year, awards, reviews, etc.) that are defined in the feature space. A number of IS papers relied on embeddings to represent data in latent spaces. For instance, Nie et al. (2020) constructed embeddings of questions and predefined topics from Q&A platforms to annotate a newly posted question with topic tags automatically, and Jian et al. (2018) constructed flexible categorical data embeddings by enhancing the coupling relationships between heterogeneous feature values. Yang et al. (2022) proposed a word-embedding method that helps to learn semantic relationships among words in firms’ reports for better volatility prediction. A recent MIS Quarterly editorial on computationally intensive theory construction (CITC) (Miranda et al. 2022) mentions Natural Language Processing (NLP) and network embeddings as important examples of methods that extract latent categories and associations from data.<sup>1</sup>

• Non-Euclidean Spaces. In addition to the Euclidean space, there has been growing interest in mapping objects from the feature space into embeddings defined in hyperbolic spaces (Chami et al. 2019) (Section 2). As explained in Section 1, hyperbolic embeddings have several natural advantages over their Euclidean counterparts and, therefore, provide a complementary method to modeling embeddings. It is worth noting that besides hyperbolic geometry with constant negative curvature, there are other examples of non-Euclidean geometries with constant positive curvature, such as elliptic geometry. However, these types of spaces are less suitable for embeddings of hierarchical structures as positive curvature causes points in a hierarchy to spread apart at an accelerating rate as you move away from a reference point (e.g., a parent node embedding), making it less effective for representing hierarchical data where preserving the relative distances between nodes is crucial for understanding their relationships effectively.

These two major thrusts of better latent embedding representations give rise to the following 2 × 3 framework presented in Figure 1, where the rows of the matrix represent different types of geometric spaces (Euclidean versus hyperbolic) and the columns represent specific ways to organize latent embeddings, such as “flat”/nongrouped versus grouped, where the latter can be classified into nonhierarchical (clusterbased) versus hierarchical methods. The traditional embedding-based approaches, corresponding to the vector-based nongrouped Euclidean embeddings, fit into the low-left corner of the matrix in Figure 1. Because they have been studied extensively over the last 10 years, this approach is well understood, and the low-left quadrant of the $2 \times 3$ matrix constitutes a set of mature technologies at this point.

In contrast to this quadrant, the upper-left quadrant explores various ways to work with unstructured (“flat”) hyperbolic embeddings, and there has been sig nificant work done on this topic in the literature over the last few years in various applications, including recommender systems (Feng et al. 2020, Mirvakhabova et al. 2020, Wang et al. 2021), natural language (Tifrea et al. 2018), and image processing (Khrulkov et al. 2020). It has been demonstrated in Ghadimi Atigh et al. (2021) that switching from Euclidean to hyperbolic embed dings provides various advantages for modeling hierarchical data in latent spaces, including more efficient representation in terms of dimensionality and interpretability of the hyperbolic embeddings for multiple prediction tasks. The issue with this class of modeling approaches lies in that the embeddings are unstructured (nongrouped) in that class, and this creates the following problems. First, modeling embeddings in the form of “flat” vectors ignores certain structures of the features. In particular, embedding-based generation techniques, although trying to extract embeddings and reduce the dimensionality of the original feature space, do not take into account the structure of latent variables and the semantically meaningful interrelationships among them (Pathak et al. 2016, Nweke et al. 2018). Second, the representation of unstructured embeddings ignores the patterns of latent information, that is, latent trends and anomalies, that are useful for prediction purposes and for the conceptual representation and understanding of embeddings. Finally, optimizing the size and quality of unstructured (nongrouped) embeddings can be a complex task, whereas grouping them can help to maintain their consistency, reduce their dimensional ity, and overcome the major sparsity problem across various applications, including recommender systems (Haruna et al. 2017, Adomavicius et al. 2021).

To address these issues, there has been some work done over the last few years to go from the individual vector embeddings to the grouped representations, both in the hyperbolic and Euclidean spaces (arrows (1) and (2) in Figure 1). For example, Sun et al. (2022) showed that clustering hyperbolic embeddings of hyperspectra images leads to better classification results in a wide range of remote sensing applications. Jagabathula et al. (2018) proposed a customer segmentation technique that includes constructing embeddings in Euclidean space and clustering of those embeddings. Wang et al. (2018) clustered mobile apps based on embeddings of their descriptions to identify original ones and their copycats. Bauman and Tuzhilin (2022) applied clustering to the word2vec embeddings of contextual phrases forming the groups representing contextual variables.

Furthermore, because the grouping of latent embeddings can be done either in a hierarchical or nonhierarchical manner, we also juxtapose the two rightmost columns in Figure 1 (the nonhierarchical versus hierarchical cases). There has been some recent work that proposed the concept of hierarchical embeddings and contextual situations in the Euclidean space (Unger and Tuzhilin 2020, Unger et al. 2020), where latent hierarchical contextual situations were proposed to organize compressed latent contextual vectors into groups of implicit contextual situations. By modeling latent contextual vectors in a structured manner from nonhierarchical latent contextual information, they enriched recommendation algorithms with new types of contextual patterns that are compact and better reflect user context. It has been demonstrated in Unger and Tuzhilin (2020) that such a shift from nonhierarchical to hierarchical embeddings in the Euclidean space (corresponding to arrow (4) in Figure 1) provides the following advantages. First, hierarchical embeddings are more compact than nonhierarchical ones and can help overcome the sparsity problem of context-aware recommender systems. Second, it is capable of capturing complex and meaningful recurrent patterns in the latent space that relate to complex contexts of customer actions and situations at different granularity levels that are useful for the conceptual understanding of embeddings. Last, these hierarchical embeddings can be used by data scientists to develop better predictive models.

Based on the conceptual framework presented in Figure 1 and the review of the related literature, we can make the following conclusions:

• There has been no prior work in the IS and the recommender systems literature that focused on the upper-right quadrant in Figure 1, that is, how to use latent hyperbolic embeddings organized in a hierarchical manner in various applications, including recommender systems. This is unfortunate because, as we argue in this paper, the upper right quadrant provides several advantages over the alternative approaches (e.g., hierarchical Euclidean and nonhierarchical hyperbolic cases), including better representations of hierarchical data leading to higher interpretability and better performance in the subsequent prediction tasks. Therefore, shifting the focus of research toward representing hierarchical data using hyperbolic embeddings and hierarchical grouping methods (along arrows (3) and (5) in Figure 1) is important across relevant IS appli cations, including recommender systems. ## 4. HyperCARS Hierarchical Contextual Situations Model

In this section, we present the proposed HyperCARS method of constructing hierarchical contextual situations in the hyperbolic space and using them in CARS. Our method uses recommendation ratings accompanied by contextual information, such as the time and location of a transaction, that is specified by a group of contextual variables (Adomavicius et al. 2021) and represented by vector $x _ { j } .$ Although these original contextual vectors $x _ { j }$ could be directly used in CARS models, representing these data in a latent space has certain significant advantages (Ding et al. 2019, Okawa et al. 2019, Xin et al. 2019). CARS literature has already highlighted the hierarchical nature of contextual information (Adomavicius et al. 2021), and many CARS papers follow Palmisano et al. (2008) and define each contextual dimension K by a set of q attributes $\boldsymbol { K } = ( K ^ { 1 } , \ldots , K ^ { q } )$ having a hierarchical structure and capturing a particular type of context, such as Time or Intent of Purchase. The values of attribute K<sup>q</sup> define more granular levels, whereas $K ^ { 1 }$ define coarser (less granular) levels of contextual knowledge.

For example, Figure 2(a) presents a three-level hierarchical structure of contextual variable Intent of Purchase specifying the reason for shopping in an e-retailer application (Palmisano et al. 2008). The first level is defined by $K ^ { 1 } = \{ P e r s o n a l , ~ G i f t \} .$ , where the “Personal” value is further subdivided into work-related or othe purposes defining categories of the $K ^ { 2 }$ level. Similarly, the “Gift” value for $K ^ { 1 }$ can be subdivided into a gift for a partner or a friend and a gift for parents or others.

We present a new HyperCARS method that constructs latent embeddings of the original high-dimensional contextual vectors in hyperbolic space groups them into the hierarchical contextual situations and incorporates those situations in a recommendation model. Although contextual situations were studied before, to the best of our knowledge, we are the first to use hyperbolic space to model contextual information. We argue that this is an important contribution because, as explained previously, contextual information is often hierarchical in nature, and hyperbolic space can represent such complex structures well and more effectively than traditional latent Euclidean embeddings (Mathieu et al. 2019). As demonstrated in Section $^ { 6 , }$ this advantage leads to better recommendation performance and better interpretability of the constructed hierarchical contextual situations in terms of the original contextual variables.

After the contextual information is defined, it is subsequently used in CARS systems for providing context-aware recommendations. Construction of proper representation and incorporation of this contextual information in recommendations can be done using either a loose coupling or a tight coupling approach. Loosely coupled methods (Nalepa et al. 2019, Unger et al. 2020) process the contextual information independently from recommendations to extract contextual embeddings and subsequently use this processed contextual representation together with the user and item information for providing contextual recommendations. In contrast to this, tightly coupled methods (Liu et al. 2022) integrate the contextual information together with the user and item representations in the same combined model for providing context-aware recommendations. More specifically, this approach allows us to synergistically integrate the hyperbolic embeddings of the user, item, and contextual information into one DL-based model that predicts unknown ratings. For instance, we can follow (Feng et al. 2020, Vinh Tran et al. 2020), rely on metric learning to construct hyperbolic embeddings of users and context-item pairs, and provide recommendation by either searching for nearby items (Schmeier et al. 2019, Wang et al. 2021) or use Einstein midpoint aggregation as it was done in Chamberlain et al. (2019), Feng et al. (2020). Alternatively, the hyperbolic embeddings of context, users, and items can be integrated within a hyperbolic neural network (Ganea et al. 2018). This tight coupling approach provides a promising research direction because it synergistically integrates three different types of hyperbolic embeddings into one cohesive system and thus focuses directly on optimizing recommendation performance.

Nevertheless, we decided to use the loose coupling approach in the HyperCARS method for the following reasons. First, because contextual information does not necessarily have the same characteristics as the user and item information, modeling it independently provides more freedom to choose a context representation method regardless of how users and items are modeled. It means that, within the loose coupling paradigm, we can take advantage of using hyperbolic space to model hierarchical contextual information but, at the same time, model the user and item components within the standard Euclidean paradigm. Therefore, the loose coupling approach to modeling context in a hyperbolic space provides great flexibility to use a broad range of previously developed recommendation algorithms, and not only in the hyperbolic but also in the Euclidean space. Second, unlike tight coupling, the loose coupling approach allows the managers to inspect and assess the quality and interpretability of the obtained contextual situations before using them to provide recommenda tions. Furthermore, loose coupling is more conducive to better interpretations of contextual representations in terms of the original contextual variables (motivated in Section 1) because the obtained representations are not affected by other external modeling constructs, such as users and items (which is the case for the tight cou pling approach). Third, the loosely coupled approach simplifies the overall recommendation engine development process by separately implementing the contextual and recommendation components in a modular fashion. Fourth, the proposed hyperbolic embedding approach to modeling contextual information requires additional calculations, as shown in Section 5.1, which, unlike the tight coupling case, can be done only once within the loose coupling paradigm, as we explain in Section 7. To summarize the loose versus tight coupling discussion, Table B1 from Online Appendix B presents a detailed comparison between these two approaches to system design in terms of the comparison criteria dis cussed in this section. In conclusion, the loosely coupled approach provides several important advantages when designing CARS, and therefore, we have chosen it in our HyperCARS method. This design choice is in line with the argument made by the authors of the paper “Loose Coupling: An Invisible Thread in the History of Technology” (Ma¨mmela ¨ et al. 2023), where it was argued that the loosely coupled systems provide important benefits across various applications vis-a´-vis the tightly coupled method.

The general structure of the HyperCARS method is shown in Figure 3 and consists of three main steps. We start by collecting contextual variables $x _ { j }$ and constructing latent contextual embeddings $c _ { j }$ in the hyperbolic space in Step 1. In Step 2, we apply hierarchical clustering techniques to extract hierarchical contextual situations hcs from those hyperbolic embeddings. In Step $^ { 3 , }$ we use hcs to provide context-aware recommendations using ratings and other pertinent information besides these contextual situations. We describe the specifics of each of these steps.

## 4.1. Step 1: Constructing Latent Embeddings of Contextual Information in the Hyperbolic Space

In this step, we reduce high-dimensional contextual vectors $x _ { j }$ defined by the original set of contextual variables to their latent vector representations (embeddings) $c _ { j }$ in a low-dimensional hyperbolic space by using variational auto-encoders (VAE) (Kingma and Welling 2014) in the hyperbolic space.

Figure 3. (Color online) Structure of the Proposed HyperCARS Method for Constructing Hierarchical Contextual Situations in Hyperbolic Space and Using Them in CARS  
![](/api/attachments/F9K54BPW/fulltext/images/65313ac2095d3be6036ce7a3917ae2cd4818a17b3953f7aa7db437d42bb290de.jpg)

The structure of VAE (Figure C2 in Online Appendix C) consists of an encoder and a decoder that are trained to minimize the reconstruction error between the decoded data $\hat { x _ { j } }$ and the original data $x _ { j } .$ Different from a standard autoencoder, VAE is regularized by encoding a distribution over the latent space instead of encoding $x _ { j }$ as single points and minimizing the following loss function:

$$
\mathcal {L} = \left\| x _ {j} - \hat {x _ {j}} \right\| ^ {2} + K L (N (\mu_ {x}, \sigma_ {x}), N (0, I)),\tag{1}
$$

where KL(·) is a Kulback-Leibler divergence (Kingma and Welling 2014), $\mu _ { x }$ and $\sigma _ { x }$ are the mean and standard deviation of the latent distribution, and I is an identity matrix.

We selected to use VAE in the proposed HyperCARS method for the following reasons. First, this approach ensures that produced embeddings $c _ { j }$ in the compressed layer have useful properties in the latent space (Bengio et al. 2013), such as continuity, meaning that any two contextual embeddings $c _ { i }$ and $c _ { j }$ that are close in the latent space refer to vectors $x _ { i }$ and $x _ { j }$ that are close in the original space of contextual variables (Kingma and Welling 2014) and therefore can be clustered in the second step of the HyperCARS method. Second, Mathieu et al. (2019) demonstrated that VAE yields better, more generalizable, and more interpretable embeddings, which leads to more interpretable contextual situations produced by the proposed HyperCARS in our case. Moreover, prior works have successfully used this method across different domains, including speech generation (Chung et al. 2015), image segmentation (Kohl et al. 2018), reinforcement learning (Hafner et al. 2019), and music creation (Dhariwal et al. 2020).

HyperCARS method constructs hyperbolic latent contextual vectors $c _ { j }$ on the Poincare ´ ball model (Online Appendix A). We use the VAE implementation developed by Mathieu et al. (2019), who adjusted VAE to the specifics of the hyperbolic geometry. They proposed efficient and reparametrisable sampling schemes for two canonical Gaussian generalizations defined on the Poincare ´ ball used in the loss function (Equation (1)) and a decoder architecture that explicitly takes into account the hyperbolic geometry. The distance between the produced latent contextual vectors $c _ { 1 }$ and $c _ { 2 }$ in the compressed layer of VAE (Figure C2) is calculated according to Equation (A1). As explained in Section 2, this distance measure allows constructing continuous embeddings of hierarchical structures. With this approach, we aim to obtain a hierarchical organization of latent contextual embedding vectors $c _ { j }$ in the hyperbolic space similar to the one presented in Figure A1b (Online Appendix A).

As an illustration, Figure 2(b) shows the embeddings of contextual variable “Intent of Purchase” (Figure 2(a)) on the Poincare ´ ball model where the most granular values appear as points. Because we use the VAE embeddings, close contextual values, such as

Friend and Partner, should appear next to each other inside the ball, as shown in Figure 2(b). Furthermore, the produced latent contextual vectors can be grouped further into more general clusters representing the hierarchical structure of the contextual variable “Intent of Purchase,” as shown by colors in Figure 2(b).

## 4.2. Step 2: Constructing Hierarchical Contextua Situations in Hyperbolic Space

The contextual situation is defined as a set of latent contextual vectors $c _ { j }$ grouped together based on their proximity, and the hierarchical contextual situation is defined as a set of contextual situations specified at different granularity levels (Section 2). To better represent the hierarchical nature of contextual information and, at the same time, implement a loose coupling approach, we propose to use hierarchical clustering to convert each latent contextual vector $c _ { j }$ to hierarchical contextual situation hcs . We start by constructing clustering tree hierarchies based on latent contextual embeddings in hyperbolic space using two hierarchical clustering techniques: agglomerative hierarchical clustering (AHC) (Davidson and Ravi 2005) and hierarchical density-based spatial clustering of applications with noise (HDBSCAN) (Campello et al. 2013). We propose two approaches to select levels from the obtained clustering tree and use those levels to construct $h c s _ { j }$ as a path of clusters’ IDs containing contextual vector $c _ { j }$ at those hierarchical levels (Figure 4).

4.2.1. Using AHC for Constructing Clustering Hierarchy. AHC is a “bottom-up” approach to building a hierarchy, where initially, each latent contextual embedding $c _ { j }$ starts in its own cluster, and then pairs of the nearest clusters are merged as we move up in the hierarchy. By design, AHC constructs a complete tree hierarchy for N observations, where the top level has only one cluster with all the observations, and the lowest level has N clusters with a single element in each.

4.2.2. Using HDBSCAN for Constructing Clustering Hierarchy. HDBSCAN (Campello et al. 2013) is a density-based hierarchical clustering method that extends the DBSCAN (Ester et al. 1996) approach and produces a clustering hierarchy. HDBSCAN selects the appropriate number of clusters based on the density of the latent contextual vectors $c _ { j }$ data and produces only several hierarchical clustering levels based on these criteria. HDBSCAN uses a single parameter for training: the minimum number of elements in each of the resulting clusters. Therefore, HDBSCAN builds clustering hierarchy by running this method with various values of this parameter.

Once the clustering tree hierarchy is constructed by either of the considered methods (AHC or HDBSCAN), we select certain levels $\mathcal { H } = \{ h _ { 1 } , h _ { 2 } , \ldots , h _ { n } \}$ from it using one of the proposed approaches. The first “Complete-Tree” approach uses all levels of the complete clustering tree hierarchy. The second “Selected-Levels” approach analyzes the tree and selects only the most important levels H based on the following “level-selection” criteria. First, these selected levels should demonstrate good partitioning quality of contextual situations in terms of the standard clustering measures, such as silhouette measure (Rousseeuw 1987) described in Section 5.3. Second, they should have very distinct levels of granularity to represent a diverse set of contexts that capture the important information from the complex representation of context in the tree structure. Intuitively, the final hierarchy of contextual situations should include levels with high granularity to provide a more detailed representation of contextual information, which could lead to better recommendation performance. To address the described criteria, we propose the following tree levels selection steps: (1) start with the level containing X number of contextual situations (clusters), and then (2) select the next level from the clustering hierarchy containing about two times $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , 2 * X )$ more contextual situations than the previous one. The exact number of situations in each level is selected based on the highest partitioning quality. The iterative process of moving down the hierarchy stops once the next level would no longer demonstrate sufficient partitioning quality.

## 4.3. Step 3: Using Hierarchical Contextual Situations in CARS

In the final step of the HyperCARS method (Figure 3), we integrate the constructed hyperbolic hierarchical contextual situations hcs into the recommendation process. As explained previously, HyperCARS loosely couples these hyperbolic context representations with the recommendation process, which allows us to use a broad range of previously developed recommendation algorithms, not only in the hyperbolic but also in Euclidean space. This flexibility is achieved by using a combination of hyperbolic embeddings and hierarchical clustering described in the previous steps. More specifically, we use hyperbolic hierarchical contextual situations hcs<sub>j</sub> along with the user ratings to train the context-aware recommendation model. We propose to use a modification of the CARS model described in Unger et al. (2020) that extends the Euclidean-based neural matrix factorization (NeuMF) method. This method uses DL-based embeddings of users and items combined with latent contextual embeddings to provide context-aware recommendations. Specifically, we modify this model to include the following inputs: user ID one-hot vector, item ID one-hot vector, and the hyper bolic hierarchical contextual situations path hcs<sub>j</sub>.

To produce recommendations, HyperCARS concatenates hierarchical contextual situations hcs with the user u and item i vectors to learn a scoring function $f ( u , i , h c s _ { j } )$ that represents nonlinear correlations between all the three inputs $u , i ,$ and hcs . The scoring function is constructed using two main DL components that are concatenated to predict the final rating: generalized matrix factorization (GMF) and multilayer perceptron (MLP). The GMF uses a linear function that is learned by embeddings of one-hot vectors of users and items. The second MLP component learns the interactions between the user, item, and hyperbolic hierarchical contextual situations. Two approaches to select certain levels H from the clustering tree hierarchy presented in Section 4.2 imply two versions of the final recommendation model. The $H y p e r C A R S _ { S e l e c t e d - L e v e l s }$ version uses only levels selected according to the “levelselection” criteria, and it is structured as explained above. The $H y p e r C A R S _ { C o m p l e t e - T r e e }$ version uses all levels of the complete clustering tree hierarchy, and in addition, it implements the attention mechanism (Vaswani et al. 2017) to automatically select the most important levels of that tree in terms of the recommendation performance metrics. In particular, the input to the attention layer is a set of sequential vectors, each of which represents contextual situations from the same level of the tree, and its output is an embedding representing the most important contextual situations in an implicit manner.

HyperCARS is an instance of the contextual modeling approach (Adomavicius et al. 2021) that considers the constructed hyperbolic hierarchical contextual situations as a part of the DL-based recommendation model to learn the influence of contextual information on the overall prediction target. In the rating prediction task, the target is the user rating $y _ { u , i , h c s _ { j } }$ . In the top-N recommendation task, the target is a latent score $y _ { u , i , h c s _ { j } }$ that is used to rank items in the descending order for each user u and recommend top-N items from that list.

Furthermore, to analyze the contribution of each selected level of the constructed contextual hierarchy used in $H y p e r C A R S _ { S e l e c t e d - L e v e l s }$ to its overall recommendation performance, we propose a variation of that model that considers only the subpath of $h c s _ { j } .$ . We denote these models as $H y p e r C A R S  – \bar { H } _ { t } ,$ where t represents a set of utilized levels in the tree. For example, $H y p e r C A R S – H _ { 3 }$ provides recommendations tailored to more specific contextual situations extracted from level $h _ { 3 }$ of the tree in Figure 4. We can also include more general contextual situations in HyperCARS- $\cdot H _ { 3 + 2 }$ extracted from two levels $h _ { 3 }$ and $h _ { 2 }$ of the tree. These models are different from the general $H y p e r C A R S _ { S e l e c t e d - L e v e l s }$ model in terms of the granularity of the underlying vectors of hierarchical contextual situations because they include only a part of the path of contextual hierarchy.

This concludes the description of the HyperCARS method for modeling contextual information in CARS. Our method constructs latent embeddings of context in the hyperbolic space using a VAE, implements two hierarchical clustering approaches to extract hyperbolic hierarchical contextual situations, and loosely couples the produced situations with the CARS model. The proposed combination of hyperbolic embeddings with hierarchical clustering allows coupling the produced hyperbolic contextual situations with a broad range of previously developed Euclidean-based CARS models. Each of HyperCARS components was designed to accurately represent the hierarchical structure of contextual information. We make the case in the rest of the paper that hyperbolic hierarchical contextual situations generated by HyperCARS lead to better recommendation performance and would be easier interpretable in terms of the original contextual variables.

## 5. Empirical Study

We evaluate the performance of HyperCARS on CARSbased data sets, including Frappe, Gowalla, and Yelp that are described in Table 2. The Frappe data set was collected from the context-aware mobile apps recommendation system (Baltrunas et al. 2015). We apply the HyperCARS method to predict unknown usage frequencies (ratings) using the contextual variables “time of the day,” “day of the week,” “location” (home or work), and “weather.” Gowalla is a location-based socia network platform that enables users to check-in to points of interest (POIs) (Liu et al. 2014). In our study, we used POIs from New York City (NYC) and San Francisco (SF) because their locations are very dense in these areas. We calculated 12 contextual variables, including time-based variables and variables revealing which of the main POI categories were visited within the previous three hours.

Finally, we used Yelp restaurant reviews from Pennsyl vania (Yelp 2023) and used Context Parser (Bauman and Tuzhilin 2022) to extract 12 contextual variables from the texts of user reviews. A detailed description of the data sets and contextual variables is provided in Online Appendix D.

## 5.1. Applying HyperCARS Method

When applying the HyperCARS method, we start by converting categorical contextual variables into sets of dummy variables forming contextual vectors $x _ { j } .$ After that, we follow Step 1 (Section 4.1) and use variationa auto-encoder (VAE) (Kingma and Welling 2014) from Mathieu et al. (2019) to construct latent embeddings $c _ { j }$ of contextual vectors $x _ { j } .$ We select the optimal embedding dimensionality demonstrating the best partitioning performance as described in Online Appendix B.

In Step 2, we construct grouped hierarchical contextual situations from the unique values of the latent contextual vectors $c _ { j } .$ We apply two clustering techni ques to construct the tree hierarchy. First, we use the implementation of AHC, available at scikit-learn.org, with the “complete” linkage that merges clusters based on the maximum distances between all the observations in them. Second, we use the HDBSCAN implementation available at hdbscan.readthedocs.io, which requires tuning only the minimum cluster size parameter. We run this method with various values of that parameter to get all the valuable hierarchical levels selected by HDBSCAN in the range between 2 and 50.

The construction of embeddings in the hyperbolic space and the calculation of the pairwise distances for running hierarchical clustering methods require additional computational costs. In our experiments on the Frappe data set with 967 unique contextual vectors, it took us 15 minutes to construct hyperbolic embeddings (versus 5 minutes for the Euclidean embeddings) and 2 minutes to calculate pairwise distances on a laptop with a 3.1-GHz Dual-Core Intel Core i5 processor. On the larger data sets with more than a million ratings and more than 10K unique contextual vectors, it took us 4.5 hours to construct embeddings (versus 40 minutes for the Euclidean embeddings) and less than 5 hours to calculate all pairwise distances.

## 5.2. Baselines

First, to highlight the advantage of using the proposed hyperbolic embedding approach for modeling contextual information, we compare the produced hierarchical contextual situations with the ones produced in the traditional Euclidean space as described in Section 4 for the proposed HyperCARS method. In particular, we implement the same variational auto-encoder to construct latent embeddings in the Euclidean space for the baselines. We also follow the same procedure as described in Section 5.1 to select the optimal embedding dimensionality. Based on the results presented in Table E2 (Online Appendix E), we select two-dimensional Euclidean space for representing contextual vectors for both considered data sets. Next, we apply AHC and HDBSCAN techniques to build the hierarchical tree structures of contextual situations. We also use the proposed “level-selection” criteria (Section 4.2) to select several granularity levels for the final hierarchical contextual situations in the Euclidean space.

Second, to verify the recommendation performance of the proposed HyperCARS model, we compared it with the following state-of-the-art methods:

• Matrix Factorization (MF) (Koren et al. 2009): Describes users and items with latent vectors inferred from the observed ratings;

• Neural Collaborative Filtering (NCF) (He et al. 2017): A neural CF model for learning the biases of users and items without taking into account contextual information;

• Convolutional Factorization Machines (CFM) (Xin et al. 2019): A deep context-aware model that learns high-order feature interactions; we concatenate the original contextual space as explicit contextual vectors with the user ID and the item ID embeddings and use them as input for CFM;

• Latent Contexts Model (LCM) (Unger et al. 2016): An extension of MF considering Euclidean-based nongrouped latent contextual embeddings that were extracted by an auto-encoder;

• Explicit Context-Aware Model (ECAM (Original)) (Unger et al. 2020): A deep learning model that considers the original contextual space as explicit contextual vectors; we use the NeuMF model extension for recommendations;

• Unstructured Context-Aware Model (UCAM) (Unger et al. 2020): A deep learning model using Euclidean-based nongrouped latent contextual embeddings extracted from an auto-encoder and a NeuMF extension for recommendation purposes;

• Hierarchical Context-Aware Model (HCAM-Original) (Unger et al. 2020): A deep learning model using hierarchical contextual situations extracted from the original contextual space and the NeuMF extension for recommendation purposes.

• Hierarchical Context-Aware Model (HCAM Latent) (Unger et al. 2020): A deep learning model using hierarchical contextual situations extracted from Euclid ean latent contextual embeddings and the NeuMF extension for recommendation purposes. For a fair comparison, we constructed the Euclidean-based latent contextual embeddings using the variational auto-encoder.

• User-Event Graph Embedding Learning (UEG-EL-V) (Liu et al. 2022): A user-event collaborative graph convolution, where the users, items, and original contextual features can all benefit from the information propagation process of graph embedding learning in the Euclidean space through the constructed intent nodes.

We compared these baselines with two variations of the proposed HyperCARS approach: HyperCARS<sub>Selected�Levels</sub> and $H y p e r C A R S _ { C o m p l e t e - T r e e } ,$ as presented in Section 4.

## 5.3. Evaluation Measures

We evaluate the proposed HyperCARS method in three ways. First, we evaluate the potential of the obtained complete tree hierarchies to produce highquality hierarchical contextual situations. In particular, we compare tree hierarchies produced based on the proposed hyperbolic versus Euclidean baseline embedding spaces by analyzing how the clustering quality in terms of the Silhouette and the Dunn Index measures (Dunn 1974) change with the number of contextual situations obtained at each level of the produced clustering tree hierarchies. The details of this evaluation approach are provided in Online Appendix F.

Second, we evaluate the quality of the obtained hierarchical contextual situations in terms of recommendation performance using popular ranking-based metrics Hit@K and MRR@K with values of K � 1, 3 and 5 (Loni et al. 2016, Haruna et al. 2017). In addition, on the Frappe and Yelp data sets with explicit ratings, we also use two rating prediction performance measures, including the root-mean-squared-error (RMSE) and the mean-absolute-error (MAE) (Haruna et al. 2017).

For the Frappe data set, we randomly divided the data into 10 subsets and split them into three parts: 80% for training, 10% for validation, and 10% for testing. The validation set is used for tuning hyper-parameters for the recommendation model training, and the performance comparison is conducted on the test set. The final results are the mean of the 10 experiments. For the Gowalla and Yelp data sets, we applied a time-based cross-validation splitting using 10 equal splits. In particular, a fixed-size time window is moved across the data, and the model is evaluated on each window separately. For each split, we selected the first 80% of data for each user as the training data, the next 10% as the validation, and the remaining 10% as the test data. We use the Wilcoxon signed-rank test to verify that all the improvements are statistically significant.

Our method, called HyperCARS, implements a loose-coupling approach that allows taking advantage of using hyperbolic space for modeling hierarchical structures of contextual information but, at the same time, provides great flexibility to use a broad range of previously developed recommendation algorithms, and not only in the hyperbolic but also in the Euclidean space. We demonstrate that this new hyperbolic-based approach produces superior performance versus the Euclidean case in terms of identifying and clearly specifying groups of hierarchical contextual situations, providing better contextaware recommendations and better interpretability of the resulting contextual situations.

This superior performance can be attributed to better capturing the hierarchical nature of contextual variables in the latent hyperbolic space. Consequently, the advantage of using HyperCARS over Euclidean embeddings appears in cases when an application deals with a large number of contextual situations that have a complex hierarchical organization, which happens in many practical cases. For example, Netflix collects and uses rich contextual information, including time, user location, weather, screen size, and so on (Baltrunas 2019). Each contextual variable introduces a new dimension in the contextual space, and the number of contextual situations grows exponentially with respect to the number of contextual variables. Therefore, with only a few variables, the hierarchical structure of contextual information quickly becomes complex, which provides an impetus to model it in the hyperbolic space. Furthermore, it has been argued in Adomavicius et al. (2021) that many applications dealing with only a few simple contextual variables, in fact, missed additional contextual dimensions, and various novel methods have been developed on how to find and deploy such additional contextual information (Bauman and

Tuzhilin 2022). Therefore, the contextual space is usually quite complex in many important recommendation applications and may benefit from the use of the proposed hyperbolic-based approach.

Besides the CARS literature, our paper contributes to the IS literature by introducing an approach for using hyperbolic embeddings combined with hierarchical clustering for modeling hierarchically struc tured data that can be subsequently used within the traditional models in a loosely coupled fashion. To this extent, we present the Latent Embeddings Representation Framework (Figure 3) that systematically classifies various approaches to model hierarchical data using hyperbolic versus Euclidean space and nongrouped versus grouped methods. In addition to providing a classification of prior work on embeddings, the proposed framework also paves the way to identifying novel research streams exploring the benefits of hyperbolic spaces across IS applications having hierarchical data (not necessarily only recommender systems).

Our experiments were designed to evaluate various latent embedding representation approaches presented in the proposed framework based on the CARS application. First, the comparison of HyperCARS with the ECAM and HCAM-original baselines shows the advantage of using latent embeddings for modeling contextual information. Second, the improvement over the UCAM model demonstrates the advantage of using grouping for modeling context. The performance advantage over HyperCARS with only a single layer from the tree confirms the importance of using hierarchical clustering within HyperCARS. Finally, the comparison with HCAM-Latent demonstrates the advantage of using hyperbolic space for modeling hierarchical contextual information.

The proposed HyperCARS method has the following practical business implications. First, it leads to better recommendations vis-a´-vis the alternative CARSbased approaches. In particular, as shown in the paper, the absolute values of recommendation performance improvement in the Frappe, and Yelp data sets constitute around 12.91%, and 7.34%, respectively, in terms of RMSE, and around 93.41%, 14.99%, and 19.66% in the Frappe, Gowalla, and Yelp data sets in terms of Hit@3, respectively, which are considered to be very strong results in recommender systems (Hastings 2012, Ricci et al. 2022) that can potentially impact business performance metrics (Panniello et al. 2016). As another example of practical implications of more accurate recommendations, Li et al. (2020) demonstrated that a couple of percentage points improvements in accuracy for short video recommendations for more than 500 million customers on the Alibaba-Youku platform leads to significant improvements in business performance metrics. Better interpretability of the HyperCARS method has another practical implication. Managers need to understand what types of recommendations are provided in which contextual situations, which is hard to do in the latent space. Therefore, the interpretability of latent contextual situations is crucial for them.

As reported in Section 5.1, implementation of the hyperbolic embeddings approach comes with increased computational costs for the embedding construction and calculation of the pairwise distances. Online Appendix K examines how HyperCARS scales with the size of the data set. The aforementioned additional calculations should be done only once and do not need to be updated on a regular basis within the loose-coupling paradigm implemented in HyperCARS. Therefore, these several extra hours of calculations is a small “price” to pay to achieve better recommendation performance of the proposed HyperCARS method vis-a´-vis the alternative approaches.

As a future work, we plan to extend the proposed method by studying the hybrid approach that synergistically combines Euclidean and hyperbolic contextual situations. Furthermore, we plan to study other types of explanation methods and labeling the contextual situations for other applications that may require explanation of contextual information. Finally, we plan to study tightly coupled hyperbolic contextual and recommendation methods that use hyperbolic spaces in an end-toend manner for modeling contextual situations and providing superior context-aware recommendations.
