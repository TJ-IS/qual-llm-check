# Understanding Volunteer Crowdsourcing from a Multiplex Perspective

Abstract. Crowdsourcing is about leveraging information technologies to outsource tasks to a large group of people, who can either be paid workers or nonpaid workers. Differing from monetarily incentivized workers, nonpaid workers are more likely to be affected by coworking relationships. To explore the link between the network and volunteering behav ior, we construct dynamic collaboration networks from 827,260 unique volunteers’ participation in 183,445 projects initiated by 74,556 nonprofit organizations over nine years in the capital city of China. Following a multiplex perspective, we allow each type of organization (i.e., school-based, community-based, other-based) to represent a separate network layer. We construct the measures of multiplex ties (i.e., social connections that are linked through multiple layers) and relational pluralism (i.e., involvement diversity in different layers). We find that volunteers with more multiplex ties and a lower level of relational pluralism have higher volunteering continuity and intensity of engagement, guaranteeing the supply of the volunteer labor force. However, they are less likely to explore unfamiliar organizations through interorganization movements. At a macro level, we show how the reduced interorganization movements impeded the development of small and newly established organizations. In addition, we show that incorporating these multiplexity measures improves the prediction of volunteer behavior by 5.390%, 1.624%, and 8.792% for continuity, movement, and engagement, respectively.

## 1. Introduction

Crowdsourcing is “outsourcing of tasks to a large group of people instead of assigning such tasks to an in-house employee or contractor” (Alonso and Lease 2011, p. 1). It is a means to facilitate collaborations for paid workers by for-profit organizations (e.g., corporate open innovation) and nonpaid workers by nonprofit organizations (e.g., open-source software development). Past crowdsourcing studies mostly focus on paid workers’ demographics, knowledge, skills, and behavior in crowdsourcing platforms such as InnoCentive, IdeaStorm, and Amazon Mechanical Turk (Huang et al. 2014, Lee et al. 2018, Zhang et al. 2019, Huang et al. 2020, Jin et al. 2021, Yin et al. 2021). There are also volunteer crowdsourcing platforms that allow nonpaid workers to sign up for volunteering tasks, such as VolunteerMatch, Catchafire, and Myturn. Although the development of volunteer crowdsourcing is still in its infancy, it has great potential to create value for the society that faces critical challenges in volunteer recruitment and management (Alam and

Campbell 2017). Differing from the paid labor market, where the supply of workers is usually higher than the demand, the market of nonpaid workers constantly faces shortages of labor. Each year, the nonprofit sector needs approximately 2 million volunteers (Petru 2014), and 92% of nonprofit organizations are in need of external volunteers to help them achieve their missions (Hoffman 2014).

Despite the lack of monetary return (Ransbotham and Kane 2011), nonpaid workers are motivated by social bonds derived from collaborations to sustain pro sociality. We allow volunteers’ collaboration networks to have multiple layers and focus on two important structural properties that depict network multiplexity. Layers are defined by types of social interactions: Each layer contains all nodes in the network and only one type of social relationship among the nodes. For example, advice, friendship, and cowork relationships among partners and associates of a corporate law firm form a three-layer multiplex network (Lazega et al.

2001). The first important structural property in a multiplex network is tie multiplexity. When two volunteers hold collaboration relationships in multiple network layers, they have a multiplex tie, which likely provides a high level of stability, emotional support, and trust in a collaboration relationship (Li and Piezunka 2020). The second is relational pluralism, or the involvement diversity in different network layers, which arises from a volunteer holding ties in multiple network layers. Relational pluralism is a double-edged sword for volunteers: It provides opportunities for volunteers to learn about volunteering tasks in different network layers (Shipilov et al. 2014) but also creates motivational conflicts and identity ambiguity that add constraints to volunteers’ performance (Zuckerman 1999, 2004).

Our research objective is to understand the following: How volunteers’ decisions regarding (1) whether to volunteer (continued participation), (2) how often to volunteer (intensity of engagement), and (3) where to volunteer (interorganization movement) are linked to their past volunteering collaboration networks documented by the crowdsourcing platform. Nonpaid workers’ continued and intensive engagement is critical to the survival of nonprofit organizations and has been the center of academic attention (Ransbotham and Kane 2011). Volunteers’ decision to move among different nonprofit organizations is even more theoretically intriguing because past studies show that worker movement is key to novel information transmission, the sharing of knowledge and skill (Kane 2009, Singh et al. 2011), and productivity improvement (Ren et al. 2016). Workers’ past participation data (at a societal rather than an organizational level) is uniquely available in crowdsourcing platforms, and we aim to demonstrate the value of this piece of data.

Unlike the homogeneous collaboration relationships (Grewal et al. 2006, Ransbotham and Kane 2011, Ransbotham et al. 2012), the heterogeneous collaboration relationships across social groups (e.g., schools and communities), enabled by a multiplex perspective, pose a unique advantage in understanding individual behavior. The multiplex perspective is rooted in sociology theories on networks and social identities (Verbrugge 1979, Shipilov et al. 2014, Li and Piezunka 2020). Volunteering activities are important facets of individuals’ social identities because a volunteer exhibits multiple identities (e.g., an active student volunteer or a warm-hearted community member) by participating in volunteering activities organized by different types of organizations. The overlap of roles, exchanges, or affiliations in social relationships, which is formally referred to as multiplexity, plays a critical role in behavior (Verbrugge 1979).

To meet our research objectives, we leverage a large archival data set from a volunteer crowdsourcing platform (BV2008.cn). This data set contains volunteering activity logs of 827,260 unique registered individual volunteers. These volunteers engaged in 183,445 different volunteering projects initiated by 74,556 different nonprofit organizations between January 2011 and January 2019. We construct dynamic egocentric collaboration networks for each volunteer and link volunteers behavior to the network features of tie multiplexity and relational pluralism. Our econometric analysis shows that compared with uniplex ties, multiplex ties better facilitate continued participation and intensity of engagement but provide constraints to volunteers’ interorganization movement. In contrast, relational pluralism facilitates movement but provides constraints to continued participation and intensity of engagement.

We further conduct three extended analyses. In the first extended analysis, we aggregate the microlevel activities into a macro-level phenomenon as validations and extensions. We find that as volunteers build more multiplex ties within existing nonprofit organizations, they become less likely to move to small and newly established organizations. Over time, the labor supply of volunteering becomes increasingly concentrated in organizations that are larger and with a longer history instead of organizations that are small and newly established. The second extension is a predictive analysis to demonstrate the value of the multiplexity perspective. It shows that multiplex-related measures can achieve comparable predictive power to traditional measures (experience, network density, and network centrality), and combining multiplex-related measures with traditional measures results in significantly higher predictive power than using traditional measures alone. Specifically, we report an AUC improvement of 5.390% for the decision of continuity, an AUC improvement of 1.624% for the decision of movement, and an root mean square error (RMSE) improvement of 8.792% for the intensity of engagement. The third extended analysis is an online survey study that validates the proposed mechanisms and provides additional insights based on an alternative outcome.

The remainder of this article is organized as follows. First, we summarize the literature on volunteer crowdsourcing and multiplex networks before we develop hypotheses based on these theoretical foundations. Second, we elaborate on the context and data. Third, we present our main analysis at a micro level. Fourth, we present the macro-level analysis, the predictive analysis, the survey analysis, and a battery of robustness checks. Finally, we present our theoretical contribution to the literature on volunteer crowdsourcing and multiplex networks and managerial implications before we discuss our limitations and conclude the study.

## 2. Literature Review

In this section, we first review the literature on volun teer crowdsourcing to note important research gaps.

Second, we review the theories and measurements related to multiplex networks in the literature on information systems (IS), organization science, and social network analysis to lay both theoretical and methodological foundations for our analysis.

## 2.1. Volunteer Crowdsourcing

Crowdsourcing, the practice of leveraging information technologies to outsource tasks to a large group of people, was first developed for commercial activities such as new drug development, photography transactions, and video repackaging (Howe 2006). With the advancement of online crowdsourcing platforms, online workers propose ideas and solutions and compete with each other in crowdsourcing contests to earn monetary rewards. IS researchers have investigated the learning process of paid workers to improve ability and idea quality (Huang et al. 2014), the biases in handling an organizer’s feedback (Lee et al. 2018), competitions and learning from other workers (Zhang et al. 2019, Jin et al. 2021), and the link between online crowdsourcing and macroeconomic downturns (Huang et al. 2020).

As crowdsourcing platforms grow to encompass nonpaid workers, IS researchers started to explore knowledge sharing (e.g., Wikipedia (Ransbotham and Kane 2011)) and open-source software development (e.g., SourceForge (Singh et al. 2011)). Countries including the United States, Canada, China, Philippines, and Japan are developing new applications of volunteer crowdsourcing in response to flooding typhoons, bushfires, tsunamis, and earthquakes (for a review, see Kankanamge et al. (2019)). During the COVID-19 pandemic, the government of California developed a specialized volunteer crowdsourcing platform, Myturn, to facilitate the volunteer workforce for vaccination. The development of such crowdsourcing platforms would greatly benefit from a deeper understanding and better predicting worker behaviors (Alam and Campbell 2017).

This is especially true because the state of volunteer crowdsourcing research is still best described as inchoate, and scholars repeatedly noted nonprofit crowdsourcing initiations as scant in the IS literature (Baruch et al. 2016, Alam and Campbell 2017). Current research on volunteer crowdsourcing in the IS literature focuses on online collaborative activities such as data collection, knowledge sharing, or software development (Ransbotham and Kane 2011, Singh et al. 2011, Alam and Campbell 2017, Kankanamge et al. 2019). The collaboration relationships in these online tasks are mostly homogeneous, unlike the collaboration relationships in offline tasks, which include a much broader range of voluntary activities organized by a diverse set of nonprofit organizations (e.g., healthcare, education, community services, and humanitarian relief). We contribute to the literature by applying the recent development in multiplex networks to model heterogeneous collaboration relationships in dynamic collaboration networks, enabling both theoretical and prescriptive insights. Furthermore, the existing works on crowdsourcing focus on the outcome of the crowdsourcing projects, such as open software development success (Singh et al. 2011, Von Krogh et al. 2012), Wikipedia article quality (Kane 2009), and effectiveness in reducing disaster risks (Kankanamge et al. 2019). To cater to the unique challenge of volunteer shortage, we focus on the outcome of volunteering continuity, intensity of engagement, and interorganizational movement. These outcomes are important from a practical standpoint because high volunteer turnover and low intensity of engagement are the fundamental challenges in volunteer crowdsourcing activities (Alam and Campbell 2017). Additionally, volunteers movement is a critical behavior that helps to facilitate novel information transmission, knowledge and skil sharing (Singh et al. 2011), and productivity improvement (Ren et al. 2016).

## 2.2. Multiplex Networks

The seminal IS research on the network and opensource crowdsourcing has followed the network diagram that contains a single type of links (Grewal et al. 2006, Ransbotham and Kane 2011, Singh et al. 2011, Ransbotham et al. 2012). The limitation of this diagram is the oversimplification of social relationships (Kanawati 2015, Huang et al. 2019), which indicates an underutilization of large-scale data sets that document the rich interactions among people, events, and organizations.

In the multiplex network framework, nodes can connect to other nodes with more than one type of link (Lee et al. 2012). A multiplex network can be defined as a multilayer network, with each layer containing the same set of nodes but different types of links (Kanawati 2015). Figure 1 shows an example of a multiplex network formed by three layers. Among the same set of actors (1–6), different layers can be different contexts through which these actors interact. For example, Layer 1 can represent coworker relationships, Layer 2 can document whether actors go to the same school, and Layer 3 whether actors go to church together. Such a framework allows researchers to handle multirelational, dynamic, and attributed networks and has wide applicability in sociology, political science, and physics (Kanawati 2015, Huang et al. 2019). Here we introduce two network measurements within the multiplex network framework.

2.2.1. Tie Multiplexity. A tie is multiplex when two people have two or more memberships in organizations or peer groups in common (Verbrugge 1979). In a multiplex network, organizations or peer groups correspond to layers that hold distinct relationships (Kanawati 2015). If an alter is linked to the ego in only one layer, we denote the alter as a uniplex connection and

In his seminal work, Granovetter discussed several alternative definitions of tie strength, including the coexistence of different types of social relations, a combination of the amount of time, the intensity of social interactions, and the level of trust and intimacy (mutual confiding) (Granovetter 1973). Multiplex ties, in general, belong to the category of strong ties (Granovetter 1973, Shipilov et al. 2014). They are resistant to changes because a multiplex tie usually involves multiple social roles and obligations. The attempt to change multiplex ties can often create role conflict and confusion (Li and Piezunka 2020). Multiplex ties tend to be more stable, more productive, and less conflict-ridden than uniplex ties because they enable the individuals involved to leverage the knowledge, experience, and trust built in one social group to foster collaboration and reduce conflict in another (Verbrugge 1979, Granovetter 1985, Li and Piezunka 2020). Tie multiplexity, rather than the sheer number of ties (the traditional measure of network cohesion), facilitates volunteer collaboration. For example, the case study of insurgency during the Paris Commune of 1871 shows that, “successful mobilization depended not on the sheer number of ties, but on the interplay between social ties created by insurgent organizations and pre-existing social networks rooted in Parisian neighborhood” (Gould 1991, p. 716). Despite the theoretical importance, empirical studies examining the effect of tie multiplexity are limited, and they predominantly focus on how individuals can benefit from the stability, trust, and cooperation that mul tiplex ties engender (Granovetter 1985, Ingram and Zou 2008). It is unclear whether tie multiplexity has detrimen tal effects on volunteering behaviors.

2.2.2. Relational Pluralism and Degree Entropy. Another important structural property in the multiplex networks is “relational pluralism”, which exists when indi viduals involve in plural network layers and develop multiple identities (a warm-hearted neighbor and a helpful student) (Shipilov et al. 2014). Differing from tie multiplexity, relational pluralism is defined from the perspective of a focal node rather than a dyad. Relational pluralism is closely related to the notion of identity (Reitzes and Mutran 2006), because the identity theory argues that a person possesses as many identities as the groups to which he or she belongs (James 2007). Identities have very important ramifications for individuals’ performance and their future network tie formation (Shipilov et al. 2014).

The essence of relational pluralism is the focal actor’s involvement in different network layers. To represent the extent of one’s involvement in different network layers, degree entropy was used in the literature on multiplex networks (Figure 2). A node’s degree entropy is defined as the entropy of the number of links in each layer and depicts the distribution of a node’s links across different layers. Formally, the degree entropy for a node i is defined as (Kanawati 2015)

$$
d _ {i} ^ {\text {entropy}} = - \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {\alpha} \frac {d _ {i} ^ {[ k ]}}{d _ {i} ^ {[ t o t ]}} \log \frac {d _ {i} ^ {[ k ]}}{d _ {i} ^ {[ t o t ]}},\tag{1}
$$

where there is a total of α layers in the multiplex network; $d _ { i } ^ { [ k ] }$ is the number of alters of the ego i in layer $k ,$ and $\begin{array} { r } { d _ { i } ^ { [ t o t ] } = \sum _ { k = 1 } ^ { \alpha } d _ { i } ^ { [ k ] } } \end{array}$ is the number of alters of node i in all layers. This entropy is zero if all alters of node i are in a single layer. To introduce the concept of degree entropy, we focus on egocentric networks as illustrated in Figure 2. In case (a) of Figure 2, node i only possesses one identity from a single layer (e.g., Layer 1 for case (a) in Figure 2). This measure is maximized when the links are uniformly distributed over different layers (case (c) in Figure 2). In such a case, it is most difficult to determine which social group the individual belongs to. In other words, holding the number of links as constant, the higher the degree entropy, the more ambiguous the individual’s identity appears to an external audience.

Differing from tie multiplexity that tends to stabilize an actor’s relationships and limit their choices (Li and Piezunka 2020), relational pluralism can be an opportunity for enhancing the actor’s behavioral flexibility, because the actor’s multiple identities can increase their chance of being accepted by more than one social group (Shipilov and Li 2014). Relational pluralism, however, can also be a source of constraint. If external audiences have difficulty categorizing the actor with multiple identities, the actor can experience an “illegitimacy discount,” that is, the audiences would be less supportive of the actor (Zuckerman 1999). For example, if a firm’s identity in the stock market (i.e., the industries that the firm belongs to) is ambiguous, the firm would suffer from reduced analyst coverage, lower stock market return, and higher stock price volatility (Zuckerman 1999, 2004).

The origins and consequences of relational pluralism are a fertile area of inquiry. As noted, “it is important to examine how multiple kinds of ties and multiple identities interact in their effect on performance, because of relational pluralism’s simultaneous enabling and constraining nature” (Shipilov et al. 2014, p. 445). This study is among the first to leverage degree entropy to empirically investigate relational pluralism. This measure enables a novel perspective to understand volunteers’ movement across different organizations.

## 3. Theoretical Development

Motivated by the enabling and constraining nature of tie multiplexity and relational pluralism (Shipilov et al. 2014), we develop how multiplex ties and degree entropy link to volunteers’ behaviors regarding continued participation, the intensity of engagement, and interorganization movement.

First, we expect that multiplex ties to facilitate volunteers’ continued participation on the platform. In general, multiplex ties, rather than the sheer number of ties, keep volunteers active in a collective action (Gould 1991). In our context, tie multiplexity would reinforce a volunteer’s broad identity as a volunteer or a member of this volunteer crowdsourcing platform. The sense of belonging leads to cooperation and sustains continued participation on the platform (Shipilov and Li 2014). From a tie strength perspective, as volunteers establish more multiplex ties with other volunteers, they would be under a higher level of informal monitoring from others (Simpson and Willer 2015). Not following the social norm to continually participate may thus lead to informal peer sanctions.

Hypothesis 1a. A volunteer with more multiplex ties is more likely to continuously participate in the volunteer crowdsourcing platform.

Degree entropy, in contrast, can be negatively linked to volunteers’ continued participation. To begin, based on the theories of social identity, social categorization, and relational pluralism (Zuckerman 1999, Shipilov et al. 2014), the higher the degree entropy, the more difficult it is for the external audience (fellow volunteers and nonprofit organization recruiters) to understand the focal volunteer’s identity, and as a result, the more likely the “illegitimacy discount” takes place. Illegitimacy discount results in reduced audience attention and support for the individual when the individual’s identity is ambiguous to the audience (Zuckerman 1999). The focal volunteer, therefore, would find it harder to be accepted by the organizations or other volunteers. For example, when a volunteer not only works for student volunteering associations but also for community-based organizations, a recruiter from a student association would raise concerns regarding this volunteer’s commitment to school-based volunteering activities. Such an overlapping identity leads to an identity crisis as reflected in a diminished sense of belonging and lack of acceptance from others, demotivating the volunteer’s continued participation. On top of the judgment from external audiences, the multiple identities induced by relational pluralism would give rise to internal conflicts for the focal volunteer. Different identities would result in contradictory social obligations, which compete for the volunteer’s time and attention (Shi et al. 2017), creating motivational confusion and leading to the decision to withdraw.

Hypothesis 1b. A volunteer with a higher level of degree entropy is less likely to continuously participate in the volunteer crowdsourcing platform.

Second, we argue that having more multiplex ties (compared with having more uniplex ties) would inhibit volunteers’ movement toward a new organization. As compared with uniplex ties, multiplex ties provide a higher level of stability, emotional support, and trust in the legacy organizations (Li and Piezunka 2020), solidifying the social benefit a volunteer derives from staying in existing organizations (Shipilov et al. 2014). As a result, multiplex ties are useful in volunteer retention (Shi et al. 2017) and uniplex ties, in contrast, motivate an individual’s movement to new organizations (Backstrom et al. 2006, Wang et al. 2013). This is also because uniplex ties that are weak in strength bring novel information on recruitment opportunities (Granovetter 1973). As evidence, uniplex ties are found instrumental in drawing new members for the organization of Nichiren Shoshu of America (Snow 1987) and for the 1964 Mississippi Freedom Summer project (McAdam 1986).

Hypothesis 2a. A volunteer with fewer multiplex ties is more likely to move to new organizations.

In contrast to tie multiplexity, we argue that degree entropy would facilitate volunteers’ movement toward a new organization. First, degree entropy is linked to the focal individual’s identity diversity. With diverse identities, the individual has more flexibility of involving in different groups (Shipilov et al. 2014). Second, a high degree entropy implies that the individual is connected to multiple network layers, which offer complementary sources of volunteering opportunities. Connecting disjoint network layers allows an individual to be situated in rich “structural holes” (Burt 2009), which enable the individual to access nonredundant information and gain autonomy in actions (Burt 1980). Such a network position promotes the formation of new collaborative relations and the disconnection of existing ties (Hanaki et al. 2007). Researchers in supply chain management find that the more existing ties a customer has in different supplier groups, the more opportunities the customer has to link to a new supplier (Shipilov and Li 2012). Third, a high degree entropy is linked to illegitimacy discount, which reduces the focal volunteer’s utility from participation in legacy organizations due to a lower level of acceptance and support (Zuckerman 1999, 2004). Such an illegitimacy discount pushes the volunteer to explore new volunteering opportunities.

Hypothesis 2b. A volunteer with a higher level of degree entropy is more likely to move to new organizations.

Third, we expect volunteers with more multiplex ties to engage more actively in volunteering activities. Past studies show that social norm, emotional support, and trust developed in a multiplex relationship can enhance volunteers’ engagement intensity (Simpson and Willer 2015, Li and Piezunka 2020). In particular, social norm is central to the moral order and social solidarity. It is not only what most others do in the same situation but also a behavioral expectation backed by informal sanctions. As a volunteer develops more multiplex ties in the crowdsourcing platform, this volunteer is more prone to peer monitoring and informal sanction. Also, with more multiplex ties, a volunteer will enjoy more emotional support and trust. Interacting with multiplex ties in voluntary tasks by itself can be enjoyable as well. As such, volunteers will be more likely to actively engage in crowdsourcing tasks to derive a heightened level of utility. Furthermore, with the presence of multiplex ties, volunteers are more likely to seek a reputation as an altruist, because the social benefit of reputation is proportional to the tie strength with the audience and the extent of observability (Van Vugt et al. 2007). An individual would actively engage in volunteering activ ities to pursue a reputation as an altruist.

Hypothesis 3a. A volunteer with more multiplex ties is more likely to have a higher intensity of engagement in the volunteering crowdsourcing platform.

Conversely, diverse involvement in different volunteering groups, implied by a high degree entropy, may adversely affect volunteers’ intensity of engagement. Volunteers with a high degree entropy have less pressure from peer sanctions and social norms. Based on the idea of illegitimacy discount, when volunteers are involved in multiple social groups and hold multiple identities, members in one organization would not fully recognize their legitimacy and commitment to the organization and thus pay less attention to regulating their engagement behaviors. In addition, a high degree of entropy is associated with richer information, which may offer more outside options for the volunteer, such as for-profit job opportunities and recreational activities. Such outside options can distract individuals’ motivation for engaging in volunteering activities. In sum, with less peer pressure, organizational regulation, and more outside options, individuals’ motivation for active engagement in volunteering activities is expected to reduce.

Hypothesis 3b. A volunteer with a higher level of degre entropy is more likely to have a lower intensity of engagement in the volunteering crowdsourcing platform.

## 4. Research Context and Data

We collect our data from BV2008.cn, a volunteering crowdsourcing platform initiated and managed by Beijing Volunteer Service Federation. It is the first and largest volunteering crowdsourcing system in China and documents volunteers’ full voluntary activities in and near Beijing (visualized in Online Appendix A1, Figure A1). Nonprofit organizations post project information on the platform, recruit volunteers, and publicize volunteers’ working hours to document their community service. Each project is a unique volunteering incident with a specific task, time, and location. Volunteers sign up for crowdsourcing projects online and participate offline if they are admitted by the project organizers. In regard to the nature of the projects, 52.7% are for community service (e.g., caregiving for the elderly), 22.6% are for environmental protection, and 18.9% are education related. We collected data on each project from January 2011 to the end of January 2019. For each project, we collected the starting date of the online registration, the launch date of the project, the nonprofit organization that initiated the project, and the list of volunteers who participated in it. On average, a volunteer participated in 6.2 projects initiated by 1.8 nonprofit organizations.

To ensure that we have a minimum time window of one year to observe whether a volunteer is active, we set January 1, 2018, as the cutoff date. If a volunteer participated in a project before the cutoff date but did not participate in any project after the cutoff date (from January 2018 to January 2019), we consider the volunteer no longer active. From our data, we find that the average volunteer participation frequency is about once every 100 days. As a result, the one-year window is sufficient for the judgment of user activeness.<sup>1</sup>

There are several unique and important features of the context. First, all information about volunteers is authentic and reliable. Individuals are required to provide their actual identity (e.g., name, phone number, citizen ID number) when they register themselves on the platform. Second, the platform documents the full records of volunteering activities. Regulated by the government, nonprofit organizations are required to disclose all volunteering projects and participants. Third, the platform is used by all types of nonprofit organizations, that is, school-based (SBOs), community-based (CBOs), and other-based (OBOs) organizations. Consistent with CBO defined in healthcare systems and by the U.S. government,<sup>2</sup> we define CBO as a nonprofit organization that is representative of a community or a significant segment of a community and works to meet community needs. All CBOs are registered at the Ministry of Civil Rights of China. SBOs are different from CBOs in that SBOs are organized by educational institutions (e.g., college, high school) and registered at the

Youth League of China. OBOs are nonprofit organizations that do not belong to CBOs or SBOs, for example, Beijing Red Cross. More examples and discussions of SBOs, CBOs, and OBOs are provided in Online Appendix A2. In the crowdsourcing platform, every volunteer is assigned a unique ID, which we use to track volunteers’ presence in different types of organizations and to construct dynamic collaboration networks.

## 5. Multiplex Network Construction

In empirical research, multiplex relationships are often enumerated and analyzed from the perspective of individuals through the use of egocentric networks, because understanding the structure of an ego’s dyadic relationships is an important precursor to understanding individuals’ behavior and organizational outcomes (Verbrugge 1979). We construct egocentric and dynamic multiplex networks for each volunteer as follows.

First, we identify different types of organizations. To classify all organizations into CBOs, SBOs, and OBOs, we randomly select 3,000 organizations and manually code them into the three categories. We find that CBOs, SBOs, and OBOs account for 59%, 20%, and 21% of the total sample, respectively. Using the sample of 3,000 organizations as a training set, we trained a machine learning model to classify organizations into these three categories according to the names of these organizations. We evaluated different models and feature extraction methods and found that the support vector machine (SVM) model combined with the BERT embeddings as textual features achieved the highest out-of-sample prediction accuracy (see Online Appendix A2 for more details). The out-of-sample F1 score is 96.7%; the high model performance confirms that linguistic features in organization names have high discriminative power. We then use the classification model to sort all the remaining organizations into the three categories for further econometric analysis.

We consider collaboration relationships formed in different types of organizations as different types of links, because they are associated with distinct social and demographic features. People maintain and develop schoolmate relationships through coworking as volunteers in SBOs; they strengthen the relationship of neighbors or members of a community by working together in CBOs that are based on geographic proximity. The accumulated social capital embedded in the relations of schoolmates and neighbors is different (Coleman 1988, Israel et al. 2001, Pooley et al. 2005), because they provide distinct services to an individual (Verbrugge 1979). Intuitively, the volunteer who holds two ties in an SBO is in a different network position from another volunteer who holds one tie in an SBO and another tie in a CBO. A traditional network measure would treat the two volunteers’ positions as the same, whereas the multiplex network framework identifies their differences.

Second, we construct egocentric networks dynamically. For each volunteer (the ego), the egocentric multiplex network contains three layers (i.e., the SBO, CBO, and OBO layers). For example, in Figure 3, the ego has three layers in the multiplex network. Each layer contains the same nodes, that is, the ego and the ego’s neighbors (the alters, that is, Individuals 1, 2, and 3 in Figure 3). If the ego and an alter have a collaboration relationship (coworkership) in the same project of an SBO (CBO/OBO), they will have a link in the SBO (CBO/OBO) layer.<sup>3</sup> Each time the ego participates in a project, new collaboration relationships form, and the egocentric multiplex network evolves. For example, in Figure 3, the ego participates in three different projects. ## 7. Model and Results

We devise two econometric models, with the first being a generalized linear model with fixed effects (the fixedeffects model) and the second being a generalized method of moments (GMM) estimator. The limitation of the fixed-effect model is the lack of control for timevariant unobservables that may affect the outcomes of interest, which we address using the GMM estimator.

## 7.1. Fixed Effects Model

Our first empirical strategy uses the generalized linear model with fixed effects. The model specification is

$$
P r (y _ {i, t + 1} | \boldsymbol {X} _ {i, t}) = F (c _ {i} + \boldsymbol {X} _ {i, t} \boldsymbol {\beta}).\tag{2}
$$

In this model, $y _ { i , t + 1 }$ represents the dependent variable (volunteering behavior), and $X _ { i , t }$ is a vector of independent variables (multiplex network structures and controls). The motivation of this model is that there may exist time-invariant and individual-specific unobservable $c _ { i }$ and omitting it may cause endogeneity; $c _ { i }$ can embody the effects of demographics, personality, the community in which the individual was born, and the individual’s offline social network structure before they joined the platform. Therefore, we collapse these effects into a cumulative unobservable effect $c _ { i }$ and refer to it

## 7.2. GMM Estimator

Our second empirical strategy adopts a generalized method of moments estimator for the following dynamic panel specification (Arellano and Bond 1991):

$$
y _ {i, t + 1} = \alpha_ {0} + \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {K} \alpha_ {k} y _ {i, t + 1 - k} + X _ {i, t} \pmb {\beta} + c _ {i} + \epsilon_ {i, t},\tag{3}
$$

where K is the total number of lags. This specification has been adopted by management researchers in addressing endogeneity in networked panel data (Yoganarasimhan 2012). It accounts for potential endogeneity caused by time-variant unobservables such that $E [ X _ { i , t } \epsilon _ { i , t } ] \neq 0$ . Using this model, we allow for correlations between past shocks $\epsilon _ { i , s } \ ( s \leq t )$ and current network structures (and controls) $X _ { i , t }$ . For example, a volunteer’s offline social network may change significantly after they graduate from high school and start to attend college. Furthermore, heteroskedasticity is allowed for error terms, that is, $E [ \epsilon _ { i , t } ^ { 2 } ] = \sigma _ { i } ^ { 2 }$ . Another benefit of this model specification is that it allows the current realizations of the outcome variable $y _ { i , t }$ to be influenced by past ones $( y _ { i , t + 1 - k } , k \in \{ 1 , 2 , \ldots , K \} )$ . Past outcome variables are also allowed to be correlated with $X _ { i , t }$

The assumptions of a GMM estimator are likely to be met in our context. A GMM estimator assumes that $X _ { i , t }$ is exogenous to future shocks $\epsilon _ { i , s } \ ( s > t )$ . This assumption is plausible because, intuitively, behaviors in the future cannot affect an individual’s current online multiplex network structures. Another assumption of this model is that past outcome variables are sufficiently controlled such that $\epsilon _ { i , t }$ is serially uncorrelated, that is, $E [ \epsilon _ { i , t } \epsilon _ { i , s } ] = 0 \mathrm { i f } t \neq s .$ . This assumption is critical for formulating GMM estimators. Serial correlation in error terms can be alleviated when we incorporate lagged outcome variables to capture past shocks that are persistent over time (Yoganarasimhan 2012). Formally, we confirm the absence of serial correlation using the Arellano-Bond (2) test (Arellano and Bond 1991). We find no evidence showing that error terms are serially correlated for movement when $K = 3$ and engagement when $K = 6 \ ( \mathrm { i . e . } ,$ , the test is not statistically significant, $p > 0 . 6 )$

For participation continuity, $y _ { i , t } \equiv 1$ (otherwise $y _ { i , t + 1 }$ is not defined) and has no variation. Therefore, this specification is not applicable to understanding participation continuity, and we rely on the fixed-effects model for this outcome. We also note that incorporating lagged terms will inevitably shrink the sample size used in estimation.

## 8. Extended Analyses

## 8.1. Evolution of Network Multiplexity and Organizational Ecology

To validate our micro-level findings and demonstrate their implications, we explore the macro-level evolution of volunteer behavior on the crowdsourcing platform. As detailed in Online Appendix A7, this analysis is motivated by and contributes to the literature on organizational ecology (Backstrom et al. 2006, Wang et al. 2013). Following this literature, we focus on the yearly change of the distribution of the organization size, which is a critical feature of the volunteering labor market and has important implications for the sustainable development of nonprofit organization ecology.

Our ecology-level data are constructed at the organization-year level. For every calendar year between 2012 and 2018, we plot the organization size (measured by the number of unique volunteers who participated in associated voluntary activities) for different types of organizations. Here, we classify organizations into the newly established organizations, the top 10% (i.e., large-size) organizations, and the bottom 90% (i.e., small-size) organizations.<sup>9</sup> If an organization started operation in year t, we label the organization as newly established from year t to year t + 1. To avoid an organization being counted as both small-size organizations and new organizations, we exclude new organizations from the category of small organizations.

We find that newly established and small-size nonprofit organizations face larger barriers to attracting participants when volunteers have more multiplex ties and thus become less likely to move to new organizations. Figure 5(a) shows that the top 10% organizations keep increasing in size until the last year of our observation. The bottom 90% organizations grow at a much slower rate. The newly established organizations appear to have an increasingly difficult time acquiring volunteers as time goes by. The graph shows a typical phenomenon of “the rich get richer,” whereby larger and older organizations are more capable of attracting new participation than are newly established and small-size organizations.

The phenomenon can be explained as follows. First, as the collaboration network evolves, more multiplex ties are established (especially after 2014, as shown in Figure 5(b)). Second, multiplex ties are negatively associated with interorganization movement behavior, as our microlevel analysis shows. Figure 5(b) further confirms the negative relationship from a macro-level perspective. Third, more multiplex ties and reduced movement rates negatively affect small and newly established organizations. We combine descriptive analysis and an individual-level multinomial logistic regression model to support this deduction. As detailed in Online Appendix A9, our results show that (1) move ment occurs more frequently from larger nonprofit organizations to smaller and newer ones; and (2) the probability of moving from an established organization to a newly established organization reduces with more multiplex ties.

## 8.2. Predictive Analysis

Complementing the econometric analyses, we conduct a predictive analysis. Practically, managers may be interested in predicting volunteer behaviors for the better design of interventions to achieve managerial goals. As such, predictive analysis could improve the design of recommendation systems, activate volunteer engagement, and facilitate interorganization movement. Theoretically, the analysis will help us understand the extent to which multiplex-related measures can improve the predictive power of a benchmark model (i.e., the model using traditional measures alone).

First, we use the full sample as in the main analysis, and then, for a robustness check, we repeat the analysis on a subsample in which the number of multiplex ties and the value of degree entropy are greater than zero. Second, we choose two machine-learning algorithms, the random forest (RF) and the extreme gradient boost (XGB), because they are considered state-of-the-art and popular in prediction tasks. Furthermore, these two algorithms can produce interpretable feature importance scores, enabling us to compare the predictive power of features. To be objective, we use the default parameters for RF and XGB, proposed by Scikit-learn, the commonly used machine-learning package in Python. RF and XGB are tree-based ensemble learning models, which ensemble the prediction results of multiple decision trees. The default number of trees is 10, and the default maximum depth of each tree is 6. We denote the RF and XGB models that ensemble 10 trees as RF-10 and XGB-10, respectively. For robustness, we also evaluate RF and XGB models that ensemble 100 trees, denoted as RF-100 and XGB-100, respectively. Third, we use the AUC to measure binary classification accuracy for continued participation and whether to move. We use the RMSE to measure prediction accuracy for engagement because it is a continuous variable. Fourth, we construct two feature sets: the traditional feature set and the multiplex-related feature set. The former includes tenure, ego network density, the number of indirect ties, degree centrality, betweenness centrality, and eigenvector centrality. The latter includes the numbers of multiplex and uniplex ties, degree entropy, and proportions of participation in different social layers. We use the traditional feature set to establish a benchmark. Then, we examine whether incorporating multiplex-related measures can offer additional predictive power and outperform the benchmark.

## 8.3. Survey Analysis

Our main analysis relies on observational data. While we conduct extensive econometric analysis to address endogeneity issues, our work can benefit from additional analysis that further accounts for unobservable confounding factors, for example, the development and evolution of people’s offline relationships. Moreover, we aim to derive insight into the underlying mechanisms that drive our findings. Therefore, we performed an online survey analysis. We recruited 157 subjects from the United States and the United Kingdom from Prolific, an online platform for researchers to recruit participants for online surveys. These subjects successfully passed our comprehension checks.<sup>10</sup> They have an average age of 39.6 years, with the youngest being 19 and the oldest being 74. There were 61 males, 92 females, 3 nonbinary, and 1 subject who did not reveal gender information. The compensation for each subject is \$1.50.

Subjects were introduced to an online volunteering platform that allows individuals to sign up for volunteer tasks. The platform documents volunteering information for every volunteer; the volunteering participation information includes the organizer, the project description, and the name of the collaborating volunteer. Volunteering participation information are publicly viewable. The survey is scenario-based. For each scenario, subjects were told to imagine that they had participated in two volunteering projects. We describe the scenarios (A, B, C, and D) in Table 4. To test tie multiplexity, the subjects will be presented with Scenarios A and B. Scenario A corresponds to a highe level of tie multiplexity because Sydney Choi is a multiplex connection. Scenario B corresponds to a lower level of tie multiplexity because there were only uniplex ties. To test degree entropy, subjects will be presented with Scenarios C and D. Scenario C represents a higher degree entropy than Scenario D because the focal volunteer in Scenario C engages in diverse layers (university and community).

Following each pair of scenarios (A versus B or C versus D), we ask subjects to choose which scenario matches higher perceptions of (1) information richness (i.e., whether they anticipate receiving diverse information about volunteering opportunities), (2) informal monitoring (i.e., the perceived pressure from other volunteers regarding keeping engaged in volunteering activities), (3) emotional support (i.e., whether they anticipate care and compassion from contacts), (4) altruistic image (i.e., the motivation of building an altruistic image to contacts), and (5) group identity (i.e., whether they would be recognized as clearly belonging to a certain social group). They were also asked to indicate the hypothetical scenario that leads to higher intention of (1) moving to a new organization, (2) continuously engaging, and (3) providing high-quality service. Subjects are provided with three options following the tie multiplexity question: A, B, and Indifferent between A and B. Following the degree entropy question, subjects were given the options: C, D, and Indifferent between C and D.

## 8.4. Robustness Checks

We perform a battery of robustness checks on our results. First, although the GMM estimator is not applicable for participation continuity, we show that our fixed effects model on participation continuity is robust to the selection of unobservables, following the approach proposed by Oster (2019). In Online Appendix A10, we detail this approach and the results. Second, we consider an alternative definition of continued participation in the main model. In the main analysis, we set January 1, 2018, as the cutoff date: If an individual did not participate in any projects after this date, the individual would be considered not active. We changed the cutoff dates to January 1, 2017, and July 1, 2017, respectively, and the main analysis was performed again. The results remain qualitatively robust (Online Appendix A11). Third, the network was relatively sparse before 2012 because the platform was in its early stages. We repeat our main analysis with the subsample after 2013 as a robustness check for data sparsity. Table A12.1 in Online Appendix A12 shows that the results remain qualitatively consistent. Fourth, one may be concerned about whether the degree entropy measure simply captures the existence of the multiplex ties. If so, the variation in positive-valued degree entropy would not be meaningful. To address this concern, we repeat the model analysis on samples with a degree entropy larger than zero. Table A12.2 in Online Appendix A12 shows that the effects of degree entropy on the three outcomes remain significant and consistent. This result indicates that the entropy measure not only captures the engagement in different layers (the existence of relational pluralism) but also captures the effects of whether such engagement is balanced among different layers (the degree of relational pluralism). According to our theoretical development, balanced engagement also tends to impact a volunteer’s future behavior. Therefore, this analysis provides additional evidence for our theoretical prediction. Fifth, we test the robustness of our results to the choice of lagged outcomes in Online Appendix A13. Table A13.1 shows the results of Arellano-Bond estimators when we slightly deviate from the optimal numbers of lagged outcomes as suggested by the Arellano-Bond (2) test. The results show that our hypotheses are constantly supported when we incorporate fewer or more lagged outcomes in our model.

## 9. Discussion

## 9.1. Summary of Findings

In this study, we leverage a multiplex network perspective to model volunteers’ collaboration networks using 827,260 volunteers’ 183,445 volunteering participations. We find that multiplex ties better facilitate continued participation and intensity of engagement but could impede volunteers’ interorganization movement. At the macro level, more multiplex ties and reduced movement rates are negatively associated with the development of small and newly established organizations, related to a “the rich get richer” phenomenon in the nonprofit organizational ecology. In contrast, relational pluralism stimulates movement and leads to higher service quality but exhibits negative effects on continued participation and intensity of engagement. Practically, incorporating multiplex-related measures can significantly increase the predictive power of a model using traditional measures.

## 9.2. Theoretical Contributions

We make substantial contributions to the IS literature. Given the advancement of information technology, digital platforms are playing a more and more important role in managing nonpaid workers. Existing IS literature on crowdsourcing has predominantly focused on paid workers (Huang et al. 2014, Lee et al. 2018, Zhang et al. 2019, Huang et al. 2020, Jin et al. 2021, Yin et al. 2021). This work contributes to the IS literature of crowdsourcing by looking into nonpaid workers (i.e., volunteers), who are likely motivated by different factors (Baruch et al. 2016, Alam and Campbell 2017). Our study highlights the importance of collaboration relationships in nonpaid workers’ continued participation, intensity of engagement, and interorganization movement. Given our findings, future studies could take into consideration the network structure of collaborations to better understand and predict user behavior of nonpaid workers. Our work further makes a contribution to the IS literature by adapting the multiplex perspective in an empirical context and developing the theoretical foundations for the measurements of tie multiplexity and relational pluralism. By uncovering both the enabling and constraining effects of these two measures and by demonstrating the significant and distinct predictive power behind these novel network measurements, we enhance the understanding of how networks affect behavior. Furthermore, we extend past studies in the literature on multiplexity that predominantly focuses on how individuals can benefit from the stability, trust, and cooperation that multiplex relationships engender (Granovetter 1985, Ingram and Zou 2008) by demonstrating the detrimental impact of tie multiplexity in terms of interorganization movement. Last but not least, IS research has been increasingly focusing on the societal impact of digital platforms. The volunteer crowdsourcing platform could foster collaboration networks at a massive scale (the platform of our context involved 0.8 million volunteers). Our study sheds light on the management of such platforms by uncovering volunteers’ social motives behind their behaviors and by developing network measures that improve the prediction of user behavior.

## 9.3. Practical Contributions

Our study provides a new perspective to improve both organizations’ and individuals’ decision making in an era with increasing availability of integrated social interaction data. We perform both economics studies based on econometrics models and predictive analyses based on machine learning. The substantial increase in predictive power following the incorporation of tie multiplexity and relational pluralism is encouraging for crowdsourcing platform designers and nonprofit organizations, as they could leverage this novel source of information to improve the prediction for user behavior and make preemptive efforts for user retention and activation. Furthermore, crowdsourcing platform designers could develop recommendation systems that recommend volunteering opportunities to prospective volunteers. On the one hand, platforms could improve volunteer engagement and continuity by recommending projects performed by one’s uniplex connections when they branch out to projects in othe layers. On the other hand, platforms could encourage interorganization movements by recommending projects in different social layers.

From a policymaker’s perspective, our work demonstrates the importance of combining volunteers’ activities in different social layers when making managerial decisions. Although CBOs and SBOs are governed by different political entities (Ministry of Civil Rights of China and the Youth League of China, respectively), integrating these two layers using a multiplex perspective likely allows more effective management of volunteers. Policymakers could encourage interorganization movement, especially between layers, to sustain the nonpaid labor force for a diverse set of nonprofit orga nizations. Volunteer movement is not only important for information, knowledge, and skill sharing (Singh et al. 2011) but also key to the development of young and small organizations, according to our findings.

Moreover, these implications could also be applied to a wide variety of circumstances because multiplex networks widely exist in the digital world. For example, a user may be embedded in multiple chat groups on instant messaging platforms with a contact, share multiple common channels on video platforms with another viewer, or contribute to Wikipedia pages belonging to multiple knowledge domains. Future IS researchers can leverage multiplex network measures in these circumstances either for predictive purposes or to derive theoretical implications.

## 9.4. Limitations

This study is not without limitations. First, although we have incorporated a rich set of outcomes that are key to the management of volunteers, we do not observe either volunteers’ satisfaction or the service quality of their volunteering efforts. The survey in our extended analysis touched upon the outcome of service quality, but more systematic analyses are needed to better understand these outcomes. Second, we focus on volunteering in Asian culture in the main analysis and examine volunteering-related perceptions in the

Western world in the survey analysis. Although most of our findings are consistent between these two studies, we did not find supportive evidence of degree entropy on engagement in the online study. Future studies could explore the differences in volunteering behaviors in different cultures. Last but not least, we categorize organizations into CBOs, SBOs, and OBOs. Theoretically, there could be other ways of categorization and each type of organization could be further divided into more granular categories. Future studies could explore other ways to construct the multiplex network and derive more insights.

## 9.5. Concluding Remarks

Our study leveraged multiple methods to understand volunteering behavior from a multiplex perspective. We hope that the network measures we construct and examine could enhance the theoretical understanding of collaboration for volunteering tasks and shed light on the governance and management of volunteers.
