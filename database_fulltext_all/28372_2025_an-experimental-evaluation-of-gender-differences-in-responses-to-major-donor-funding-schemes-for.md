# An Experimental Evaluation of Gender Differences in Responses to Major-Donor Funding Schemes for Crowdfunded Social Ventures

Sofia Bapna,<sup>a,</sup>\* Gordon Burtch<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, Minnesota 55455; <sup>b</sup> Questrom School of Business, Boston University, Boston, Massachusetts 02215

Received: May 27, 2022 Revised: November 5, 2023; June 4, 2024; August 4, 2024 Accepted: September 3, 2024 Published Online in Articles in Advance: October 11, 24

Abstract. Social ventures fundraising through crowdfunding compete for contributions. Thus, they often involve major donors to influence the contributions of smaller donors. We examine what type of major-donor contribution scheme is most likely to attract smaller donors, considering whether and to what degree male and female donors differ in their response to alternative major-donor contribution schemes that are commonly used in practice. In a field experiment, donors were randomly assigned to receive one of three solicitation messages about projects seeking funds through crowdfunding. All donors received identical messages, with the exception that information on major-donor involvement was varied across conditions. The message to the control group made no mention of a major donor. The seed group was informed that a major donor had unconditionally funded the first 50% of the projects’ costs and that the projects were collecting the remaining 50% from other donors; we hypothesize that this scheme is likely to appeal to female donors, who tend to be more uncertainty and risk averse. Finally, the challenge-match group was informed that a major donor would provide the remaining 50% of the projects’ costs after the projects secure the first 50% from other donors; we hypothesize that this scheme is likely to appeal to male donors, who tend to respond more positively to challenges. The major donor in the field experiment—Forschungsfo¨rderungsgesellschaft—is an Austrian government agency that promotes and funds innovation. We find that females in the seed group are \~50% and \~35% more likely to exhibit an interest in contributing as compared with females in the control or challenge-match conditions, respectively. Among males, we do not observe significant differences in interest in contributing across the three groups. Survey results indicate that females are more responsive to the seed scheme because it yields a perception that the organization conducting the project is of high quality, is likely to reach its funding goal, and is likely to achieve its implementation goals. Our study sug gests that social ventures will be more successful at attracting contributions from female donors if a major donor kicks off the campaign with an initial, unconditional contribution.

## 1. Introduction

Fundraising is a significant challenge faced by many social ventures, defined as organizations that seek to address social or societal problems (Dacin et al. 2011).<sup>1</sup> To overcome this challenge, social ventures have recently sought to expand their donor audience by drawing on innovative fundraising approaches, such as online crowdfunding (De Crescenzo et al. 2022), where relatively small contributions are collected from many individuals (backers) through an online platform (Mollick 2014). Crowdfunding platforms benefit social ventures by reducing spatiotemporal and social network-related barriers to fundraising, providing access to a large, diverse, and geographically distributed backer audience.

A lengthy stream of literature on information systems (IS) dealing with crowdfunding identifies factors that influence the crowd’s contributions (e.g., Hong et al. 2018, Geva et al. 2019, Gong et al. 2020). Intuitively, backers will seek to identify social ventures that are not only interesting but also likely to succeed—in terms of both fundraising and project execution. This can raise challenges because crowdfunding is subject to a great deal of information asymmetry. Backers, often characterized as “small,” bear a great deal of uncertainty because they tend to be inexperienced, tend to be geographically dispersed (Agrawal et al. 2014), and may not have prior knowledge of or exposure to the social venture. Collectively, these factors may impede backers’ ability to evaluate or monitor projects.

One strategy that fundraisers could use to mitigate backers’ uncertainty and perceived risk is to leverage the involvement of influential major donors: that is, individuals who commit to large, early contributions during a fundraising campaign. Indeed, in recent years, a burgeoning stream of literature has begun to examine the beneficial effects of major-donor involvement in the context of crowdfunding (Meer 2017, Fang et al. 2021). However, this literature has not dedicated much attention to understanding whether and how major donors influence varies with the nature of their involvement and the characteristics of the donor audience. This latter gap is particularly notable in regard to donor gender given the prevalence with which crowdfunding research highlights gender heterogeneity in contribution decisions (e.g., Greenberg and Mollick 2017, Mohammadi and Shafi 2018, Bapna and Ganco 2021). This gap is doubly surprising given the large body of research in philanthropy that also highlights differences in giving by gender (e.g., Andreoni and Vesterlund 2001, Mesch et al. 2015) and the advice by nonprofit experts that advocates targeting donor appeals based on demographics, like gender (Nichols 1994, 1995). Moreover, unlike many other demographic characteristics, the gender distribution of a donor audience is typically known or easily determined, making targeting by gender a practically viable solution. To our knowledge, no prior work has examined how the nature of major-donor involvement may affect a donor’s decision making conditional on the donor’s gender.

We address that gap here, examining whether and to what degree males and females differ in their responses to major-donor funding schemes that are commonly used in practice—the seed and challenge-match schemes. In the seed scheme, a major donor provides an up-front unconditional contribution. In contrast, in the challengematch scheme, a major donor commits to providing a contribution only if the total contribution of other donors exceeds some threshold.<sup>2</sup> Although the presence of a major donor can reduce small donors’ uncertainty in either scheme, the effects are likely more pronounced in the seed scheme. Because women are known to be more risk averse (Hibbert et al. 2013, Marinelli et al. 2017) and uncertainty averse (Croson and Gneezy 2009) than men, we theorize that women will be more responsive to a project that has secured seed funding relative to a project that has no major donor or that has secured a challenge match. In contrast, because men are more likely to seek out challenges and be concerned with accomplishment (Niederle and Yestrumskas 2008, Sassenberg et al. 2013, von Bieberstein et al. 2020) relative to women, we theorize that men will be more responsive to a project that has secured challenge-match funding relative to a project that has no major donor and also, relative to a project that has secured seed funding.

In Study I, we evaluate these predictions using data from a randomized field experiment in the context of crowdfunding, a setting that many social ventures now commonly leverage for fundraising (e.g., Deutsch et al. 2017, Hong et al. 2018, De Crescenzo et al. 2022). We examine gender heterogeneity in small donors’ response to information indicating differing schemes of majordonor involvement—no mention of a major donor, mention that a major donor has provided seed funding, and mention that a major donor is supporting a challenge match. We find that females are \~50% and \~35% more likely to exhibit an interest in contributing when informed of the presence of seed funding relative to the absence of major-donor involvement or information indicating the presence of a challenge match, respectively. By contrast, we find no evidence that males respond differentially across the three conditions. In Study II, which involves a survey on Prolific, we explore the mechanisms that underlie the results from the field experiment. Finally, in Study III, we replicate the results of our field experiment, revisiting data from an earlier, offline field experiment conducted by Huck et al. (2015).

We make several contributions to the IS literature. First, by providing novel insights into gender heterogeneity around the response to the presence of seed fund ing and challenge matches, we extend a body of work on crowdfunding that identifies important factors that influence the crowd’s contributions (e.g., Hong et al. 2018, Geva et al. 2019, Gong et al. 2020). We also contribute to a broad literature on IS that explores trust in online transactions, a substantial portion of which has considered heterogeneity in trust formation across genders (Gefen and Straub 1997, Van Slyke et al. 2002, Riedl et al. 2010, Chen et al. 2015, Nissen and Krampe 2021). We build on that literature by extending it to the context of online philanthropy and crowdfunding in particular, documenting novel sources of differences across genders in the willingness to transact.

Further, our work contributes to the literature on economics and decision making that examines females’ uncertainty and risk aversion. We identify and empiri cally test a strategy—the use of seed funding—to assuage those concerns in the context of crowdfunding and philanthropy more generally. We also identify specific mechanisms by which seed funding alleviates women’s uncertainty and risk aversion; our survey results demonstrate that (i) females perceive seedfunded projects to be associated with higher-quality organizations and to be more likely to achieve their funding and implementation goals and that (ii) females perceive that any donations that they may make will be more likely to have a significant impact on a project’s ability to meet its fundraising goal and implementation goals if that project has received seed funding.

Lastly, we contribute to the public finance literature, which has found that the seed scheme has a significant, positive effect on fundraising as compared with fundraising without a major donor (e.g., Rondeau and List 2008, Gneezy et al. 2014). Our field experiment results, replicated using data from the offline field experiment conducted by Huck et al. (2015), suggest that the generally superior performance of the seed scheme documented in past work may be attributable to responses specifically among female donors.

In summary, the novelty of our research is that it examines how females and males respond to social ventures that leverage the seed and challenge-match major-donor schemes, which are commonly used in practice. Our findings—that (i) females are more likely to be interested in contributing when a project has obtained a seed contribution versus when a project has obtained a challenge-match commitment from a major donor or when no major donor is involved and (ii) that males exhibit no significant differences in their responses to these alternative conditions—have important practical implications. These findings help inform social ventures about what is likely to be the most effective major-donor scheme for their fundraiser, particularly those ventures whose donor base is known to be predominantly female. Our research suggests that social ventures can maximize interest from their female donor base by employing a seed strategy.

## 2. Theory

## 2.1. Major-Donor Effects in Crowdfunding

As we note earlier, backers in the crowdfunding context experience significant uncertainty in making contribution decisions. This is because they tend to be inexperienced, may not be geographically proximate to the project, and may not have prior knowledge of or exposure to the social project they are considering.<sup>3</sup> One strategy that social ventures use to mitigate donors uncertainty is to leverage the involvement of influential major donors.

An example of recent work on the topic includes that by Fang et al. (2021), who find that crowdfunding campaigns attract increased rates of donation when one-to-one matching is in place (wherein each dollar contributed by a small donor is matched by a third party at a fixed rate, up to a predefined maximum). Another example is the work of Meer (2017), who examines the effect of partner organization grants on fundraising outcomes for teachers’ classroom projects on DonorsChoose.org. He finds that fundraisers attract more donors and donations in the presence of a partner grant. Importantly, in that study, partner grants are not specific to a particular project; they are applied to all projects that meet certain criteria (e.g., all mathematics projects in a certain state). The grants can also be delivered under different schemes, either via one-to-one matching or via an up-front seed grant that covers all but the final \$100 requested. The two schemes are studied in a combined fashion in Meer’s analysis as he sought to understand the value of major-donor contributions generally.

Crowdfunding research has also examined early contributors who are not necessarily “major” (recall that major donors are those who commit large contributions early in the fundraising cycle). As one example, Deutsch et al. (2017) examine the effect of early contributions (e.g., contributions that occurred before projects reach \~55% of their goal) on subsequent contributions in the context of crowdfunding for public goods. That stream of work has also begun to explore sources of heterogeneity around the influence of early donors. For instance, Hassna et al. (2024) examine the effect of early contributions by different types of formal organizations (e.g., a business, a government entity, a nongovernmental organization, etc.) in the context of civic crowdfunding. Additionally, Kim and Viswanathan (2019) examine whether the quality signal provided by early investors varies with the experience of the early investor (e.g., those with development experience and those with application investment experience) in the context of a crowdfunding market for mobile applications. Although these initial works have shed light on various important factors that may moderate the influence of early donors, some likely sources have yet to be examined.

Missing from the literature on major donors in crowdfunding is an investigation of gender-related heterogeneity. This gap is notable given that several studies in the crowdfunding literature have documented heterogeneity in contributions by gender. Greenberg and Mollick (2017) find that female backers in reward-based crowd funding tend to fund women in industry categories where women are underrepresented. Mohammadi and Shafi (2018) conclude that greater risk aversion among females in equity-based crowdfunding makes them less likely to invest in younger and high-tech firms relative to males. Bapna and Ganco (2021) find that female investors with experience as an investor in equity-based crowdfunding exhibit a reduced preference for female founders relative to female investors without such experience. Those authors attribute their finding to the weakening effects of activism and a lower reliance on gender information as a heuristic in investment decisions.

Consistent with the above ideas, research in philan thropy has noted important gender differences in giving. In the public finance literature, research has found differences in general altruism (e.g., Andreoni and

Vesterlund 2001), donation prevalence, and the philanthropic causes that individuals support (Mesch et al. 2015). In line with that body of work, nonprofit experts advocate that fundraisers should demographically target potential donors (Nichols 1994, 1995).

These prior findings related to gender heterogeneity in crowdfunding contributions and patterns of offline giving motivate our research: here, of potential gender heterogeneity in responses to different schemes of major-donor involvement in fundraising for social ventures through crowdfunding. Notably, our work also builds on a lengthy stream of literature on IS, which deals with gender differences in online trust in the context of e-commerce more broadly. For example, Van Slyke et al. (2002) documented early evidence that women were less likely to make purchases via online websites because they were more likely than men to be distrustful of the experience. Subsequently, work by Riedl et al. (2010) and more recently, by Nissen and Krampe (2021) has identified fundamental neurological differences between men and women that affect how each evaluates and assesses trustworthiness in online contexts. Such work suggests that women’s evaluation of online fundraisers and donation opportunities is also quite likely to differ from men’s evaluation in systematic ways. We next discuss our predictions, given the prior literature, of how gender differences may manifest in response to major-donor schemes in crowdfunding.

## 2.2. Predictions Regarding Male and Female Responses to Seed and Challenge-Match Schemes in Crowdfunding

The structure of major donors’ involvement in a fundraiser can affect the response of smaller donors. When an organization communicates to potential small donors that it has received unconditional seed funding from a major donor, this can serve as a signal of project quality, which may motivate small donors to contribute (List and Lucking-Reiley 2002). Unconditional seed funding signals that the major donor has vetted the project and holds a high degree of certainty about its prospects. Seed funding can thus be expected to reduce a small donor’s uncertainty about the project’s quality and the likelihood of the project’s success.

When an organization communicates that it has received a conditional challenge match, smaller donors may provide funds because (i) the match reduces their perceived cost of giving (Karlan and List 2007), that is, smaller donors may feel that their contributions will be amplified by the match, and (ii) the major donor’s involvement may provide a signal of quality (Vesterlund 2003, Karlan and List 2020), albeit one that is likely weaker than that conveyed by unconditional seed funding. Moreover, a challenge match may mobilize donors to share information regarding the fundraiser and invite others to contribute to meet the threshold that triggers the match.

We expect that females and males are likely to respond differently to crowdfunding campaigns for social projects that are backed by seed funding, backed by challenge-match funding, and not backed by a major donor.

Ample research in economics and psychology documents that females are more risk averse (Hibbert et al. 2013, Marinelli et al. 2017) and uncertainty averse (Croson and Gneezy 2009) than males. Byrnes et al. (1999) provide a comprehensive review of 150 studies that compare the risk-taking tendencies of males and females, and they conclude that males are more risktaking. Similarly, recent research in equity crowdfund ing finds that greater risk aversion among females leads them to exhibit different investment patterns (Mohammadi and Shafi 2018). Because seed funding can reduce a small contributor’s uncertainty about a project’s quality and the likelihood of its success (meeting its fundraising and implementation goals), we expect females to be more responsive to a crowdfunding campaign for a social project that has secured seed funding relative to one that has secured challenge match funding or one that has no major donor.

At the same time, prior work has found that males are more likely to seek out a challenge and that they are more likely to be promotion focused (i.e., concerned with advancement, growth, and accomplishment) rather than prevention focused (i.e., concerned with security, safety, and responsibility) (Niederle and Yestrumskas 2008, Sassenberg et al. 2013, von Bieberstein et al. 2020). Considering these factors in tandem with males’ generally lower risk aversion levels, we expect that males will be more interested in contributing to a crowdfunding campaign for a social project that has secured challengematch funding relative to one that has secured seed funding or one that has no major donor.

## 2.3. Broader Literature on Seed and Match Schemes

Given that the seed and match major-donor schemes have a lengthy history in charitable fundraising (Vesterlund 2003), in this section, we summarize findings from research on these schemes in contexts beyond online crowdfunding.

2.3.1. Seed and Match Schemes. Research on the seed scheme (e.g., Rondeau and List 2008, Gneezy et al. 2014) finds that seed money increases the number of people who contribute (donation rate) and the amount they give relative to identical campaigns where no announcement of a major donation is made. Further, as the proportion of seed money increases, the number of contributors increases, and the sizes of their contributions increase as well (List and Lucking-Reiley 2002).

Many studies find that one-to-one matching (also called linear matching)—a popular matching scheme— can cause crowding out, reducing out-of-pocket donations (Rondeau and List 2008, Huck and Rasul 2011);<sup>5</sup> by reducing the price of charitable giving, linear matching leads small donors to make larger donations on the whole (including their own donation and the amount of the match), but their personal, out-of-pocket contribution declines; that is, donors’ out-of-pocket average contribution (excluding the match) drops relative to donors’ average personal contribution in the presence of seed funding (Huck and Rasul 2011).

Given that prior research has identified the superior performance of seed funding relative to matched funding, one might ask why fundraisers do not exclusively rely on seed funding. Huck and Rasul (2011) offer three explanations. First, major donors may have some uncertainty regarding a project’s quality and thus, may prefer to give their gift as a conditional match. By offering conditional matching, a major donor can aggregate the private information that small donors may have about project quality; major donors provide their contribution only if a sufficiently large number of small donors believe the project is worthwhile, to the degree they were willing to make contributions to the project.<sup>6</sup> In contrast, when a major donor is more certain about a project’s quality, they may be willing to make an unconditional, initial commitment in the form of seed money (e.g., an initial commitment of 50% of the project’s goal). Second, small donors might prefer to give to causes where their contributions are matched. This is because matching reduces the small donor’s cost of giving. Third, it is rare to observe a single fundraising organization that experiments with different fundraising schemes. Thus, inertia may play some role in their choices (Adena and Huck 2022). As fundraising organizations may lack feedback about the performance of alternative fundraising schemes, they may continue to apply suboptimal fundraising techniques.

2.3.2. Alternate Forms of Matching and Combining Matching with Targeting. Given the widespread use of matching in practice, research on the topic has continued to explore promising avenues by which it can be improved. Researchers have focused on two paths, altering the design of the matching scheme and the use of matching in combination with targeting, to leverage heterogeneity among donors.

Examples of attempts to innovate on the design of matching schemes are prevalent. One such work considered the efficacy of matching small donations to a focal charity by making contributions to a different charity (Adena and Huck 2017), the idea being to avoid the crowding out of small donations to the focal charity. Within the larger body of literature on alternate matching schemes, studies involving a threshold match (wherein a major donor provides a contribution only once the total contribution of other donors exceeds a threshold) are closest to the matching scheme we examine here.<sup>7</sup> Huck et al. (2015) study the effect of matching individual donations above a e50 threshold. They find that this type of threshold matching leads to higher out-of-pocket contributions than one-to-one matching or fixed gift matching. Charness and Holder (2019) compare three types of matching schemes—one-to-one matching, having people compete individually for matching funds, and having anonymous teams compete for matching funds; in the latter two conditions, contributions above a certain threshold were matched.<sup>8</sup> Those authors found that individual donors’ out-of-pocket contributions were similar for the one-to-one match and the individual competition conditions but were higher for the team competition condition. Gee and Schreck (2018) test the effectiveness of providing a match conditional on a threshold related to the number of donors who contribute. They find that setting a “3 of 10” threshold (3 contributors from a group of 10) increases the donation rate (binary decision of whether one contributes) relative to no matching. Anik et al. (2014) study the effect of matching conditional on the percentage of others who give. Those authors find that conditioning the match on a 75% threshold of other donor participation is most effective at increasing donor involvement.

Our paper is also related to recent research that explores the combination of matching and targeting, with the goal of exploiting heterogeneity among donors. Adena et al. (2024) find that one-to-one matching is an effective scheme among poor populations, which contrasts with the findings for middle- and high-income earners who were the focus of previous research. The authors find (in their context of poorer populations) that the rate of giving is higher in the matched condition relative to the seed condition and that the match does not lead to crowding out (i.e., donation amounts under the match are not significantly lower than those made under the seed). Adena and Huck (2022) test the effectiveness of matching that occurs if individual donors contribute above a personalized threshold (the threshold is based on their past contributions to the focal charity and their personal characteristics). They find that a threshold of about 75% above past donations maximizes out-ofpocket contributions.

## 3. Study I: Field Experiment 3.1. Data and Methods

3.1.1. Institutional Details. This study uses data from a randomized field experiment executed by the Austrian Government’s Research Promotion Agency (in German, Forschungsfo¨rderungsgesellschaft or FFG), a national funding agency that promotes innovation. To test the predictions identified above, FFG piloted a new program under which organizations could apply for funding for their social projects. FFG’s call for applications can be found in Exhibit A1 in the Online Appendix. FFG’s definition of social projects can also be found in Exhibit A1 in the Online Appendix under the sections “What does the FFG understand by a social innovation project” and “Products, services and concepts are developed in the following subject areas.”

If selected through the program, FFG would support half of each project’s costs up to a maximum of e75,000 (the maximum total project cost for any project in the program was e150,000).<sup>9</sup> Our study considers two such projects that were selected for funding, namely Wochenplan.digital and mitwirken.at. Note that no other projects were selected at the same time as these projects.

The subjects in the experiment include all of the 22,744 individuals on FFG’s electronic mailing list. Individuals on the mailing list receive emails covering topics such as calls to apply for funding, workshop information, and field-related updates.<sup>10</sup> All individuals on FFG’s mailing list either voluntarily joined the list or were added by FFG after the individuals registered for and attended an FFG-hosted event (e.g., sessions on proposal writing, shaping cooperation in projects, and funding information among others). Gender data (male or female) were collected at the time of joining the mailing list or at event registration.

The experiment design is similar in spirit to those executed by Bernstein et al. (2017), Bapna (2019), and Bapna and Ganco (2021, 2023). Subjects were randomly assigned to one of three experimental conditions. Subjects in all three conditions received an email that provided information about the two social projects— Wochenplan.digital and mitwirken.at—and invited financial contributions through the crowdfunding platform StartNext.com, which is the largest German language reward-based crowdfunding market.<sup>11</sup> We examined if these projects were representative of the broader set of projects that fundraise through crowdfunding. Our analysis found that the projects in our experiment are comparable with the broader set of projects on StartNext.com on a wide range of observable dimensions (e.g., the campaign categories and campaign descriptions; details are in Section 5 in the Online Appendix). This analysis suggests that the projects in our experiment are not niche, and thus, we expect the findings from the study to generalize more broadly.

The emails that provided information about the two social projects were identical except that subjects in the two treatment conditions received an additional sentence about a major financial contribution (in the form of a seed or challenge-match contribution) provided by FFG to each project. The control condition received an email without the sentence about a major financial contribution. The experimental conditions are described in Section 3.1.2.

By clicking on the “View project” links below each project description in the email, subjects could learn more about the social projects and contribute. Those links, when clicked, directed subjects to the respective project’s crowdfunding campaign page. Our outcome of interest is a subject’s decision to click through on the email to visit the social venture’s crowdfunding campaign page. This outcome measure, which has also been considered in several prior studies related to crowdfunding (e.g., Bernstein et al. 2017, Bapna 2019, Bapna and Ganco 2021), parallels outcome measures considered in the marketing literature, which often treats advertisement click-through as a proxy for ad performance (e.g., Bleier and Eisenbeiss 2015, Liu et al. 2021, Shi and Trusov 2021). In our context, the action of reviewing the email content and deciding to click through to view more information about the social venture and its crowdfunding campaign represents a meaningful expression of interest as it reflects initial screening, arguably the most critical phase of the contribution funnel given that consideration is a necessary precondition for contribution (Bernstein et al. 2017, Bapna 2019). Further, leading crowdfunding platforms have reported that referral traffic generated via email marketing yields higher conversion than any other referral source (Haines 2012), and industry consultants often highlight the central importance of email marketing in crowdfunding campaign success (e.g., Crush Crowdfunding 2021). These points jointly suggest that interest generated via email marketing is likely to be highly associated with ultimate contribution.

The crowdfunding campaign page included information typical of any charity-based crowdfunding campaign with rewards that are gifts or tokens of appreciation (e.g., project description, information about founders, and rewards offered). Importantly, the decision to click through on the email to visit the social venture’s crowdfunding campaign page occurs before the campaign page is viewed. Our outcome, therefore, cannot be affected by the content of the cam paign page. Individuals had the option of contributing (via the campaign page) anonymously or nonanonymously. If contributors chose to be identified, their name would appear on the list of contributors on the campaign page; if they chose to contribute anonymously, their name would be concealed. The campaign page also displayed the total number of anonymous contributions.

3.1.2. Randomized Assignment. As noted above, subjects were randomly assigned to one of three groups: Seed (7,581 subjects), Challenge-Match (7,582 subjects), o Control (7,581 subjects). Exogenous random assignment enables a causal interpretation of treatment-effect estimates by (i) ensuring the absence of reverse causality and (ii) ensuring that treatment variables are unconfounded (i.e., that treatment variables are independent of all other variables that may themselves affect the outcome of interest). The latter is achieved because randomly assigning subjects to the experimental conditions (treatments and control) “balances out potential nuisance variables that might affect outcomes” (Karahanna et al. 2018, p. iv). Moreover, because the experiment was conducted unobtrusively and subjects were not aware that they were part of a study, actor-observer bias was eliminated.

Although the treatment emails sent by FFG included information about FFG’s involvement, there was no mention of FFG’s involvement on the crowdfunding campaign pages, mitigating the potential for information leakage across experimental conditions. It should also be noted that no other emails (apart from those corresponding to the three experimental conditions) were issued by FFG to the subjects regarding the funding scheme or the projects during the period of the experiment. The experiment began when the fundraising solicitation email was sent and ended with the expiry of the crowdfunding campaign deadline. The crowdfunding campaigns associated with mitwirken.at and Wochenplan.digital on StartNext.com lasted 30 days (April 8th to May 7th of 2021) and 31 days (April 8th to May 8th of 2021), respectively.

## 3.1.3. Dependent Variables

3.1.3.1. Interest in Contributing. Following Bernstein et al. (2017) and Bapna (2019), Interest in Contributing is a binary variable that was set to one if a subject clicked on either of the “View project” links in the email from FFG and zero otherwise. ## 4. Study II: Mechanism Exploration 4.1. Mechanisms—Uncertainty, Risk, and Altruism

We conducted a survey study on Prolific using an independent sample of respondents to complement our field experiment and the associated survey of field experiment subjects. Our goal with this Prolific study was to more deeply explore possible mechanisms that may underlie the findings in our field experiment. In this Prolific study, we consider mechanisms related to uncertainty and risk as well as altruism. In Section 7 in the Online Appendix, we describe how we ensured that we received high-quality responses through Prolific (e.g., mitigating subjects’ use of generative large language models in their responses) (Veselovsky et al. 2023). In Exhibit A3 in the Online Appendix, we provide the exact survey text used. Note that the design of this prolific survey was such that all participants saw and answered questions about all matching schemes.

4.1.1. Uncertainty and Risk. The results of our field experiment indicate that females in the Seed condition are \~35% and \~50% more likely to exhibit an Interest in Contributing as compared with females in the Challenge Match or Control conditions, respectively. We theorized (in Section 2.2) that this may be because females are more risk and uncertainty averse than males, and seed funding is more likely to reduce a small contributor’s uncertainty about the project’s quality and the likelihood of its success. Thus, we sought to understand females’ perceptions regarding the Seed pitch (relative to the Control and Challenge-Match pitches) regarding the project quality, its likelihood of fundraising success, and its likelihood of meeting its implementation goals. We provided participants with the Seed, Challenge-Match, and Control pitches. Following the presentation of each pitch’s text, we asked participants to indicate the degree to which they agreed with three statements. (i) The project seems to belong to a high-quality organization, (ii) the project is likely to reach its fundraising goal, and (iii) the project is likely to achieve its implementation goals.

We further sought to understand whether the Seed pitch made females perceive (relative to the Control and Challenge-Match pitches) that their contribution was likely to have an impact on the project reaching its fundraising goal and its implementation goals. Therefore, we asked participants to consider the text in each of the three pitches (Seed, Challenge-Match, and Control) and indicate the degree to which they agreed with two additional statements. (iv) If I was to make a donation to this project, the donation would have a significant impact on the project’s ability to meet its fundraising goal. (v) If I was to make a donation to this project, the donation would have a significant impact on the project’s ability to meet its implementation goals.

Responses to these five questions were recorded on a five-point scale from strongly disagree (one) to strongly agree (five). Responses were recorded in the variables High Quality Organization, Likely to Reach Funding Goal, Likely to Reach Implementation Goals, My Donation Impacts Reaching Funding Goal, and My Donation Impacts Reaching Implementation Goals. Respondent gender was obtained from the demographic data that are made available through Prolific.

Our analyses of female responses are summarized in Figure 3, panel 1 and in Table 5. Relative to the Control and Challenge Match pitches, females felt that the Seed pitch is associated with a greater perception that the project is being undertaken by a high-quality organization (Figure 3, panel 1, section (a) and Table 5, Model (1B)) (the coefficients of Control and Challenge-Match are significant and negative), that the project is more likely to reach its fundraising goal (Figure 3, panel 1, section (b) and Table 5, Model (2B)) (the coefficients of Control and Challenge-Match are significant and negative), and that the project is more likely to reach its implementation goals (Figure 3, panel 1, section (c) and Table 5, Model (3B)) (the coefficients of Control and Challenge Match are significant and negative). Additionally, we observe that female respondents were more likely to report a perception that their donation would have a significant impact on the project’s ability to meet its fundraising goal when evaluating the Seed pitch (Figure 3, panel 1, section (d) and Table 5, Model (4B)) (the coeffi cients of Control and Challenge-Match are significant and negative), and they reported a similar perception related to their donation’s likely impact on the project’s ability to meet its implementation goals (Figure 3, panel 1, sec tion (e) and Table 5, Model (5B)) (the coefficients of Con trol and Challenge-Match are significant and negative).

## 5. Study III: Replication in an Alternative Context to Assess Generalizability

We sought to replicate our main findings for the sake of assessing generalizability. We do so by showing that our finding—that women are more interested in contributing when a major donor contributes using the seed scheme relative to when there is no major donor involved—holds for an organization beyond the specific ones that we study in the field experiment and holds for contributors beyond those on FFG’s mailing list.

To assuage concerns regarding generalizability, we leveraged data from a field experiment previously reported by Huck et al. (2015) involving offline fundraising solicitations for the Bavarian State Opera.<sup>22</sup> Those authors randomly assigned potential donors (operagoers) to one of several conditions, including a seed-funding scheme (very similar to our own), a variety of alternative matching schemes (none of these are directly analogous to our schemes as each involved some form of dollar-for-dollar match), and a control condition that received no information about the major donor. Although Huck et al. (2015) were not interested in gender heterogeneity when studying the effects of their treatments, they did collect details on individuals gender, and this information is available as part of their replication material. We leverage that fact here, revisiting their study’s data to estimate heterogeneous treatment effects around donor gender for their seed and match interventions.

We draw directly on the indicator of the Seed intervention from the original data (treatment T2 in the original study by Huck et al. 2015). As none of the match-related treatments implemented in that study mirror our own challenge-match scheme, we pool all match-related treatments together and define a single dummy, Match, to represent them collectively (treatments T3–T6 in the original study by Huck et al. 2015). Table 8 provides descriptive statistics for the variables of interest. As can be seen, four of every six subjects are assigned to the Match condition (\~66%), whereas one of every six subjects is assigned to the Seed condition (\~17%). The remaining 17% of subjects are assigned to the Control condition. Finally, we see that approximately 48% of potential donors are Female, reflecting that the donations solicitation was issued quite broadly, and we see that our outcome of interest, Donation (in euros), is generally right skewed.

## 6. Conclusion and Contributions

In this research, we seek to identify whether there are gender differences in the responses of small donors to the seed and challenge-match major-donor funding schemes in the context of crowdfunding. We explore this research question using data from a randomized field experiment executed by the Austrian Research Promotion Agency (FFG).

To summarize our main results, (i) we find that females are \~35% and \~50% more likely to exhibit an interest in contributing to social projects in the presence of seed funding relative to the presence of challengematch funding or an absence of major-donor involvement, respectively, and (ii) we do not observe evidence that males’ interest in contributing to social projects changes significantly in the presence of challenge-match funding relative to the presence of seed funding or an absence of major-donor involvement.

We make several contributions to the literature and to practice. First, we contribute to the IS literature on crowdfunding. Several studies have examined factors that drive the creation of projects (e.g., Kim and Hann 2019) and factors that drive contributions from the crowd (e.g., Hong et al. 2018, Geva et al. 2019, Gong et al. 2020). Particularly relevant to our paper is previous research that has focused on major and lead contributors’ influence on others’ contributions (Kim and Viswanathan 2019, Fang et al. 2021, Hassna et al. 2024). We extend this literature by examining gender heterogeneity in donors’ response to two commonly used major-donor funding schemes.

Second, we contribute to a broad literature on IS that speaks to gender differences in the formation of online trust (Van Slyke et al. 2002, Riedl et al. 2010, Nissen and Krampe 2021). We extend that literature to the context of philanthropy and crowdfunding specifically, documenting novel differences across genders in the willingness to contribute.

Third, we contribute to the literature that examines gender differences in uncertainty and risk aversion. Previous literature on crowdfunding suggests that inexperienced female investors—who are likely to feel less confident (more uncertain) about investing than male and female experienced investors—may be more interested in contributing to ventures with female founders because gender homophily may lead to trust and positive perceptions (Bapna and Ganco 2021). We identify and empirically test a strategy—seed funding—that alleviates women’s concerns about risk in the context of crowdfunding. We further contribute to this literature by identifying the mechanisms by which a seed contribution reduces women’s concerns tied to uncertainty and risk aversion. Our Prolific survey results provide suggestive evidence that females perceive that a project that has secured seed funding is more likely to be associated with a high-quality organization and is more likely to reach its funding goal and achieve its implementation goals and also, that their own donation will be more likely to have a significant impact on the project’s ability to meet its fundraising goal and implementation goals.

Fourth, we contribute to the public finance literature that examines the seed scheme. This literature has found that a campaign with a seed scheme (e.g., Rondeau and List 2008, Gneezy et al. 2014) increases the number of people who contribute and the amount they give relative to a campaign where no announcement of a major donation is made. Our field experiment, along with the results of our additional analysis of data collected in an offline context by Huck et al. (2015), suggests that the superior performance of the seed scheme may be in large part because of a response specifically among female donors.

It is worth noting that our paper focuses on the seedfunding and challenge-match schemes because they are particularly relevant in the context of crowdfunding. Seed funding is relevant to online crowdfunding because crowdfunding is characterized by a great deal of information asymmetry and uncertainty (Agrawal et al. 2014). Because campaigns are conducted online, contributors are often geographically distant from the project. Additionally, crowdfunding contributors are often inexperienced and lack prior knowledge of projects to which they contribute. Each of these factors poses challenges for project evaluation and monitoring. In this setting, unconditional seed funding by a major donor may serve as an important signal of quality (Karlan and List 2007), assuaging small contributors’ uncertainty and motivating them to contribute.

Challenge-match schemes are also uniquely relevant to online crowdfunding for at least two reasons. First, challenge matches can prompt a sense of community and a need to coordinate because individual contributors may perceive that they need to “band together” to help the project secure the matching funds. A sense that the community needs to come together is likely weaker in one-to-one matching because every contribution is matched on an individual basis. These coordination and community aspects of the challenge-match scheme are notable because crowdfunding platforms offer features that directly support community building around a fundraising campaign and that help contributors coordinate (e.g., the platform facilitates communication between users and provides real-time information on who has contributed, progress toward the fundraising goal, and time elapsed and remaining until the fundraising deadline) (Solomon et al. 2015). Additionally, crowdfunding platforms often provide social mediabased sharing and peer-referral mechanisms (Burtch et al. 2021), allowing contributors to involve others in their network. These community-building and coordination features can enable contributors to pull together to engage, motivate, and influence one another and to help the fundraising organization reach the match threshold. All of this suggests that a challenge match may be an effective fundraising scheme in crowdfunding. Second, prior work has argued that matching may prove ineffective in fundraising because contributors may be unsure of whether the matching cap has already been surpassed at a given point in time and whether the matching scheme remains active and would apply to a contribution that they might choose to make (Gee and Schreck 2018). In online crowdfunding, however, the provision of real-time information about fundraising progress implies that all contributors will be perfectly informed about how their contribution will relate to that of others’ contributions and whether the matching scheme remains active.

Our research has noteworthy strengths and makes important contributions to practice and to the literature. A particular strength of our paper is that it involves a randomized field experiment, which allows for clean causal inferences and allows us to observe individuals (as opposed to students or participants recruited for a laboratory study) in their natural environment.

From a managerial standpoint, our findings shed valuable light on the efficacy (or lack thereof) of the seed and challenge-match schemes. We find that small female donors are significantly more responsive to the seed scheme than the challenge-match scheme. Thus, our findings indicate that social ventures should take into consideration the gender demographics of their smaller-donor base and then work to attract an appropriate major donor who is willing to contribute through the scheme that is likely to be most effective. If the small donor pool is anticipated to be predominantly female, then the seed scheme is likely to be most effective.

Our paper is not without limitations. First, we are unable to directly analyze the link between interest in contributing and actual contributions because contributions on StartNext.com are often made anonymously. Moreover, subjects who did not contribute anonymously may have nonetheless contributed under a pseudonym or under a name not matchable to FFG’s records. For example, a contributor may have used a nickname or middle name, or someone else in the same household may have made the contribution following a discussion among family members in the household. Each of these factors makes it difficult to reliably match contributions to subjects. Although previous research (e.g., Bapna 2019; Bapna and Ganco 2021, 2023) has shown that campaign email clicks are associated with actual contributions, future work will benefit from studying actual contributions and the link between interest and contributions in the context of the specific schemes that we study here. Second, subjects in our field experiment are individuals on FFG’s mailing list— and thus, are individuals who are interested in research, development, and innovation. More research is needed to study broader audiences in other contexts. Third, it is conceivable that the characteristics of the projects that we study themselves play a role in our findings. Future work would benefit from studying projects in other contexts. That said, our reanalysis of data from the donation experiment of Huck et al. (2015) involving the Bavarian

State Opera—wherein the subjects are operagoers and the outcome is actual donations—yields support for our finding that females respond positively to a seed contribution compared with the absence of a major donor. This result adds confidence that the results of our field experiment are likely generalizable to other contexts and to a broader audience and that they hold for actual contributions. Fourth, we have limited background information on the subjects in the field experiment, so we are unable to further explore heterogeneity based on factors, such as their historical funding behavior. Relatedly, we test and find that females are more responsive to the seed treatment. It is important to note, however, that there may be other unobserved factors that differ systematically between male and female participants, which may contribute to the result. Fifth and last, our treatments are delivered via email solicitations; it would be ideal to also have incorporated the various experimental manipulations into the actual crowdfunding campaign pages (on StartNext.com) such that a subject would only see a version of the campaign web page that corresponds to the experimental condition to which they were randomly assigned (control, seed, or challenge match). Although such a design would likely yield even stronger treatment effects, it would have necessitated the direct involvement of the crowdfunding platform and would have been complicated by challenges of crossdevice and crossbrowser identity tracking.

Our hope is that this research will stimulate future work on major donors in the context of crowdfunding. Researchers could compare the efficacy of different types of matches (e.g., challenge versus one-toone matching). Further, researchers could examine whether the relative efficacy of challenge-match and seed-funding schemes varies depending on the amount committed by the major donor or the fraction of the overall fundraising goal that a major donation constitutes. Finally, future studies could examine which of the interventions that we examine here may be most valuable for re-engaging lapsed contributors (cf. Karlan et al. 2011).
